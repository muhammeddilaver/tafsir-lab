# 48. Sūrat al-Fatḥ

**29 verses.** Medinan. It takes its name from the phrase **فَتْحًا مُّبِينًا** in the first verse.

The sūra opens with one of the strangest namings in the Qurʾān. When a text begins "We have given you a manifest victory", the reader expects a city taken, an army scattered, a border crossed. Not one of these is anywhere in the rest of the sūra.

What is there instead is this: a journey broken off midway (25), sacrificial animals that never reach their place (25), hands withheld (24), a group of people who never set out at all (11), a dream deferred with "not this year" (27). That is: while it recounts **something that did not happen**, the sūra speaks of **a fatḥ that has happened**.

The whole tension of the sūra lies here, and the rest of this commentary will consist largely of investigating what that naming means: **why is something that looks like a step backwards called a "manifest fatḥ"?**

The direction of the answer stands in a sentence at the centre of the sūra:

> **فَعَلِمَ مَا لَمْ تَعْلَمُوا۟** — *"He knew what you did not know."* (48/27)

The sūra ties the difference between what was expected and what happened to a **difference in knowledge**. One side sees the outcome, the other sees only the moment. And the name "fatḥ" is given not to how the event looked at that moment but to the door it opened.

## The name of the sūra

The sūra is also referred to by its opening words, *"Innā fataḥnā"*. In the sources the name **Sūrat al-Fatḥ** is also common; both rest on the same word.

One note: the word *fatḥ* occurs in the sūra **three times** (1, 18, 27), and each time it names something different. The sūra takes its name from a word that is itself under discussion within it.

## Historical frame: what we know, and from where

What is most often confused in this sūra is the boundary between **what the Qurʾān says and what the biographical reports recount**. I follow here the method applied in Sūrat al-Ḥashr: let the boundary be drawn first, and then let us move to the verses.

### What the text of the Qurʾān says

The whole of what may be drawn from the sūra as fact is this:

- A "manifest fatḥ" has been given (1).
- *Sakīna* has been sent down into the hearts of the believers; this happened during a pledge of allegiance, and the pledge was made **beneath a tree** (18).
- There is a group who were not among those who pledged, who are referred to as "those left behind" and who are said to be from among the bedouin; their excuse is "our possessions and our families kept us occupied" (11).
- This group will want to join an expedition for spoils on the return and will be refused (15); another call will be made to them instead (16).
- Entry to the Sacred Mosque was prevented; the sacrificial animals did not reach their place (25).
- In the interior of Mecca, hands were withheld on both sides before fighting began (24).
- In Mecca there are believing men and women who are not yet known as such; the possibility that harm might come to them is named as a ground in the course the event took (25).
- In the hearts of those who disbelieved there was *ḥamiyya* (hot fury); against this, *sakīna* was sent down upon the believers (26).
- There is a **dream**: entering the Sacred Mosque in safety, with heads shaved or hair shortened (27). The dream is true; but its fulfilment has been left to a later stage, with "a near fatḥ" set in between.
- "Many spoils" to be taken soon have been promised (19-20).

### What the Qurʾān does not say

**A settlement is named only once:** *Mecca* (24). The *Sacred Mosque* is also named twice (25, 27) — but that is not a settlement, it is the name of a place. Beyond this there is no place name at all; **the word "Ḥudaybiya" does not occur in the Qurʾān.**

None of the following is in the text: the place where the treaty was made; the clauses of the treaty; its duration; the names of the parties; how many people there were; which year it was; the species of the tree beneath which the pledge was taken; the number of those who pledged; which tribes "those left behind" belonged to; who the "people fierce in war" of verse 16 are.

All of this comes from the biographical and historical reports.

### What the reports recount

In the classical works of exegesis and biography it is widely reported that the sūra came down **on the return from Ḥudaybiya**. The account is transmitted in outline as follows:

It is reported that in the sixth year of the Hijra the Prophet set out towards Mecca with a company about him, intending the lesser pilgrimage (*ʿumra*); that he was halted at a place near Mecca called **Ḥudaybiya**; that envoys passed back and forth; that at one stage, when unfavourable news spread about the envoy sent to Mecca, a **pledge of allegiance** was taken from those with him beneath a tree; and that in the end a treaty was made and they returned that year without performing the *ʿumra*. Among the clauses of the treaty are counted a truce of several years, the postponement of the *ʿumra* to the following year, and — an arrangement that at the time appeared to favour the other side — a provision concerning those who sought refuge. The sources relate that some of those with him were not content with this outcome, and that this sūra came down on the return journey.

**For the pledge beneath the tree** the sources use the name *Bayʿat al-Riḍwān*; this name is derived from the expression *raḍiya'llāh* in verse 18 — that is, the name comes not from the event but from the verse.

### How these two layers should be used

A few measures need to be set down:

**One. The reports are necessary.** The sūra **refers to** an event its audience knows; it does not narrate the event. Only the reports supply the context. For this reason I shall use background information below.

**Two. The reports do not carry the same degree of certainty as the Qurʾān.** On details such as the clauses of the treaty, the wording of what was said, who said what, the sources differ from one another. For this reason I give the details with the note **"this is how it is transmitted"**, and I make no detail a condition of a verse's meaning.

**Three. The strangeness of the sūra does not depend on the reports.** Even with no information brought in from outside, the text says this within itself: entry was not possible (25), the sacrificial animals did not reach their place (25), the dream was deferred (27) — and in spite of this it was called "fatḥ". The reports do not produce this strangeness; they only say in which event it was lived through.

**Four.** Per the method (`STYLE.md`), no wholesale ruling will be issued about any group. The sūra says "those left behind from among the bedouin"; this is the name of a **behaviour**, not of an identity. Indeed the same sūra opens a door to those same bedouin a few verses later: *"if you obey, God will give you a good reward"* (16). What is at issue is not a door closed but a participation deferred.

## The structure of the sūra

| Block | Verses | Subject | What closes the block |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opening** | 1-3 | The proclamation of the fatḥ and its four consequences: forgiveness, the completing of the favour, guidance, help | *naṣran ʿazīzā* |
| **The descent of the sakīna** | 4-7 | The stillness that comes down into hearts; the increase of faith; the separating out of three groups | *ʿazīzan ḥakīmā* |
| **The Messenger and the pledge** | 8-10 | The three attributes of the Messenger; the four acts expected of him; the pledge and "a hand above the hands" | *ajran ʿaẓīmā* |
| **Those left behind I** | 11-14 | The excuse: possessions and family; the split between tongue and heart; ill thought | *ghafūran raḥīmā* |
| **Those left behind II** | 15-17 | The demand for spoils and its refusal; a new call; those with a genuine excuse | *ʿadhāban alīmā* |
| **The pledge beneath the tree** | 18-21 | Good pleasure; the knowing of hearts; sakīna; a near fatḥ and spoils | *qadīrā* |
| **The way of God** | 22-24 | The working that does not change; the holding back of hands on both sides | *baṣīrā* |
| **The analysis of Ḥudaybiya** | 25-26 | The prevention; the believers not known as such; *sakīna* against *ḥamiyya* | *ʿalīmā* |
| **The dream and knowledge** | 27-28 | The confirming of the dream; "He knew what you did not know" | *shahīdā* |
| **The portrait** | 29 | The description of the community and the simile of the crop | *ajran ʿaẓīmā* |

Two things in the structure are worth noticing.

**One. The sūra closes at both ends with the same expression.** The tenth verse ends with *ajran ʿaẓīmā*, and so does the twenty-ninth. Both describe the recompense of an **undertaking**: in the tenth verse that of the one who does not break his pledge, in the last verse that of the one who believes and does good works.

**Two. The sakīna comes down three times** (4, 18, 26), and each time the one who receives it, the moment and the direction change. This is the backbone of the sūra; I shall take it up separately at verse 26.

## Three axes running through the sūra

Before moving to the verses, let me record three threads woven through the sūra; each will be treated separately at the relevant verse.

**First axis: the heart.** The word **قُلُوب** (hearts) occurs five times in the sūra: 4, 11, 12, 18, 26. And each time the heart is named as the place that carries **what cannot be seen from outside**: the sakīna comes down there (4), what does not agree with the spoken word sits there (11), the thought that is made to seem fair is made fair there (12), God knows what is there (18), ḥamiyya settles there (26). Every distinction the sūra draws is drawn at the level of the heart.

**Second axis: the distance between tongue and heart.** *"They say with their tongues what is not in their hearts"* (11). This sentence is the sūra's instrument of diagnosis, and it recurs in different forms in verses 12, 15 and 18.

**Third axis: the hand.** The word *يد* occurs in three places in the sūra: in the pledge (10 — *"the hand of God is above their hands"*), in the withholding of hands (20 — *"He held back the hands of men from you"*), and in the holding back of hands on both sides (24 — *"He held back their hands from you and your hands from them"*). The sūra begins with a clasping of hands and ends with both hands being lowered.

## The basmala

The analysis of the basmala was made at al-Fātiḥa; it is not repeated here. The verse numbers are counted without the basmala.

## 48/1 — إِنَّا فَتَحْنَا لَكَ فَتْحًا مُّبِينًا

*Innā fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā*

"We have opened for you a manifest opening (given you a manifest victory)."

### فتح — root: ف-ت-ح

**The primary sense of the root: to open.** The opening of something that is shut — a door, a lock, a knot, a blocked road, a closed case.

This root was treated at `110-nasr.md` (*naṣru'llāhi wa'l-fatḥ*), at `057-hadid.md` (57/10, "those who spent before the fatḥ") and at `061-saff.md` (*fatḥun qarīb*). Because the word is the name of this sūra I dwell on it here in more detail; rather than repeating what was said there, I build on it.

**Derivatives:**

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **مِفْتَاح** (miftāḥ) | Key — the instrument that opens. Plural *mafātīḥ* |
| **فَاتِحَة** (fātiḥa) | The opening part of a thing |
| **اِفْتِتَاح** (iftitāḥ) | Inauguration, beginning |
| **اِسْتِفْتَاح** (istiftāḥ) | Seeking an opening; seeking judgement, seeking victory |
| **فَتَّاح** (fattāḥ) | The one who opens much, who judges — among the names of God |
| **فُتُوح / فَتْح** | The place that is opened; in the military sense, "territory taken" |

**The two directions of the root**, and the presence of both in the Qurʾān:

**1. To open — concrete.**
- *"Even if We **opened** to them a gate from heaven, so that they kept ascending through it…"* (al-Ḥijr 15/14)
- *"The **keys** of the unseen are with Him"* (al-Anʿām 6/59)
- *"Had the people of those towns believed and been mindful, We would have **opened** upon them the gates of blessing from heaven and earth"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/96)

**2. To judge — to decide between two parties.**
- *"Our Lord! **Decide** between us and our people in truth; You are the best of those who decide"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/89)
- *"He is the **Fattāḥ**, the **ʿAlīm**"* (Sabaʾ 34/26)

The link between the two directions is this: **to give judgement is to open a matter that has stayed shut.** A dispute is a knot; the judge unties it. The same image works in English, where one *opens a case* and brings a matter *out into the open*.

### That fatḥ is not "victory"

This is the first and most important correction in understanding the sūra.

Arabic had ready words for military superiority, and the Qurʾān uses them:

| Word | Root | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| **غَلَبَة** (ghalaba) | غ-ل-ب | Overcoming, subduing — *"The Romans… will be victorious"* (al-Rūm 30/2-3) |
| **ظَفَر** (ẓafar) | ظ-ف-ر | Seizing, taking in the claw (*ẓufr*: claw) — it occurs in verse 24 of this sūra: *aẓfarakum* |
| **نَصْر** (naṣr) | ن-ص-ر | Help, support — that which gives birth to victory (`110-nasr.md`) |
| **فَتْح** (fatḥ) | ف-ت-ح | **Opening** |

Note: **this sūra uses three of the four** — *fatḥ* (1, 18, 27), the verb *ẓafar* (24: *aẓfarakum*), *naṣr* (3: *yanṣuraka naṣran ʿazīzā*). The words are not confused, then; each does its own work. The word in the sūra's title is the one that conveys not overcoming but **opening**.

And how sharp this distinction is here is plain. In the event the reports recount, no city was taken, no army was broken; they even returned that year without being able to perform the *ʿumra*. In spite of this the word is **fatḥ**. The reason lies in the logic of the word itself:

**One. To open is not to create.** What is opened is already there; it is only shut. The one who opens the door does not produce what is behind it, he makes it reachable. With this word the sūra describes what took place in this way: nothing new was built, **what was shut was opened.**

**What supports this reading is the sūra's own description.** What was removed by the treaty the reports recount was not an army but a **state of closure**: the two sides were shut to one another, their meeting meant fighting, an exchange of words between them was not possible. The treaty lifted that closure. This is exactly what the word describes.

**Two. A door is for going through.** The meaning of a door's being opened is that someone will be able to enter. In Sūrat al-Naṣr this was completed: after *fatḥ* came *yadkhulūna* (they enter). Here too the same verb will appear twice within the one sūra: *li-yudkhila'l-muʾminīn* (5), *la-tadkhulunna'l-masjida'l-ḥarām* (27).

**Three. The worth of an opening depends on what passes through it.** I entered this note at `110-nasr.md` and repeat it here: whoever opens a door and lets injustice pass through it has turned the meaning of the word upside down.

### فَتَحْنَا — the perfect

The verb is in the **past tense**: "we opened", "we gave". Done and finished.

This is one of the most debated grammatical points of the sūra. For in the classical commentaries it is transmitted that the sūra came down **on the return** from Ḥudaybiya; yet on that reading the consequences of the thing called "fatḥ" — the entry into Mecca, the performing of the *ʿumra*, people joining in throngs — have not yet happened.

There is a known usage in Arabic that answers to this: **future events whose certainty is being stressed are recounted in the perfect.** Because the thing is as certain as if it had happened, it is spoken of as having happened. The Qurʾān does this openly:

> *"The command of God **has come**; do not seek to hasten it."* (al-Naḥl 16/1)

The remainder of the verse shows that the command has not yet come; the verb is nevertheless in the perfect. The same usage was treated at the verb *jāʾa* (came) in Sūrat al-Naṣr.

**But there is a subtlety here, and the two readings do not exclude one another:**

| Reading | What it says | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **A future in the perfect** | The fatḥ has not yet happened; it was spoken of as having happened for the sake of its certainty | In verse 27 of the sūra "a near fatḥ" is named as something still **ahead** |
| **It has truly happened** | The fatḥ is the treaty itself; it has happened, and the perfect is exactly what it appears to be | The meaning of the word is "opening"; the closure was in fact lifted |

**I record this as my own reading:** the second reading seems more in keeping with the sūra's own choice of word. Had "fatḥ" meant a seizing, the perfect would have had to be a figure of speech. If it means "opening", no figure is needed — the opening has already taken place; passing through the opened door is a separate matter, and the sūra promises that separately in verse 27. I do not impose this as a preference; both explanations have been defended in classical exegesis.

### إِنَّا — "we"

**إِنَّ** is a particle of emphasis, **نَا** a plural of majesty. The sentence could have been built as "a fatḥ has been given to you" or "a fatḥ has occurred"; it is not.

This is the same choice as the one in Sūrat al-Naṣr, and its reverse. There the verb was *jāʾa* — the fatḥ **was coming**, and its agent was not named. Here the agent is named openly: **We opened.** The two sūras say the same fact from the two ends: the side that receives and the side that sends.

And this has a consequence inside the verse: **there is no human act in the sentence at all.** The journey, the waiting, the negotiation, the pledge — all of them will be named in the later verses of the sūra. But at the opening not one of them is there. The subject of the verb is single.

### لَكَ — "for you"

The particle **لِ** here conveys benefit: **in your favour, for you.**

This small particle sets the direction of the sentence. The verse does not say *"fataḥnā Makkata"* (we opened Mecca) or *"fataḥnā ʿalayhim"* (we opened against them). **The object of what is opened is never named**; only the one in whose favour it happened.

This is the first sign of a stance that will hold throughout the sūra: the text **does not make the other side its subject.** There is no defeated party, no place taken, no losses counted. The fatḥ is not an "against" but a "for".

**The address is singular.** This will become still clearer in the second verse. The sūra opens not to an army, a community or a state, but to **a single person** — just as Sūrat al-Naṣr does.

### فَتْحًا — the cognate accusative

The verb *fataḥnā*, and then a verbal noun from the same root: *fatḥan*. In Arabic this is called the **absolute object** (*mafʿūl muṭlaq*), and it does two things: it **reinforces** the verb, and, when it takes an adjective, it states the **quality** of the verb.

Both are present here. The word comes indefinite — *a fatḥ* — and immediately takes an adjective. The indefiniteness here conveys **magnification** (*taʿẓīm*): an opening of a kind that cannot be counted, whose like cannot be specified.

The same pattern will be repeated in the third verse: *yanṣuraka'llāhu **naṣran ʿazīzā***. Two verses apart, that is, the sūra builds the same structure twice: verb + verbal noun from the same root + adjective.

### مُّبِينًا — "manifest"

**Root: ب-ي-ن.** The primary sense is **to part, to open a space between**. *Bayn* — the space between two things. *Bāna* — it parted, it drew away.

From this **bayān** is born: to separate a thing from other things, to fix its bounds — that is, to explain. A thing is understood only when it has been **set apart**; what is mixed together is not understood.

**مُبِين** — the active participle of Form IV. It is read in two directions, and both are valid in Arabic:

| Reading | Meaning | What it means here |
|---|---|---|
| **Intransitive** | That which is itself manifest | The fatḥ is plain in itself, beyond dispute |
| **Transitive** | That which makes something else clear, which separates it out | This fatḥ brings other things to light |

**The second reading has a counterpart within the sūra**, and it needs recording. What happens throughout the sūra is exactly this: the event **separates out** who stands where. Those who pledged and those who stayed behind (10, 11, 18), those whose speech and whose heart are one and those whose are not (11), those upon whom sakīna comes down and those in whom ḥamiyya settles (26). A single event divides a community.

**I record this as my own reading:** this second aspect of the adjective *mubīn* stands like a summary of the sūra's structure. An opening is at the same time a separation — when the door is opened, who goes in and who stays put becomes visible. In the classical commentaries it is predominantly the first sense that is worked out; I offer the second as a reading, not as a transmitted report.

### The sound of the repeated word

The verse consists of four words, two of them from the same root: *fataḥnā… fatḥan*. A short, tight sentence of a single breath. The rest of the sūra is built of long verses; the opening is deliberately kept short.

The verse-ending (the closing sound in -*īn*/-*ā*) will be largely preserved throughout the sūra: *mubīnā, mustaqīmā, ʿazīzā, ḥakīmā, ʿaẓīmā…* This is a consistency not always found in the long Medinan sūras.

## 48/2 — لِّيَغْفِرَ لَكَ ٱللَّهُ مَا تَقَدَّمَ مِن ذَنۢبِكَ وَمَا تَأَخَّرَ وَيُتِمَّ نِعْمَتَهُۥ عَلَيْكَ وَيَهْدِيَكَ صِرَٰطًا مُّسْتَقِيمًا

*Li-yaghfira laka'llāhu mā taqaddama min dhanbika wa mā taʾakhkhara wa yutimma niʿmatahū ʿalayka wa yahdiyaka ṣirāṭan mustaqīmā*

"So that God may forgive you what has gone before of your sin and what has come after, and complete His favour upon you, and guide you on a straight path."

### The structure of the verse: four verbs, one particle

The verse is bound to the first by **لِ** (lām), and then **four verbs** follow in order. Three in this verse, the fourth in the next:

| # | Verb | What is done | Its direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **يَغْفِرَ** | To forgive | Towards the past |
| 2 | **يُتِمَّ** | To complete | Towards what continues |
| 3 | **يَهْدِيَ** | To guide onto a path | Towards the future |
| 4 | **يَنصُرَ** (verse 3) | To help | Towards the future |

All four are **imperfects in the subjunctive** — that is, all of them hang on the same *lām*. The sentence must be read as a single structure: "…We opened so that these things might be."

And the order is meaningful: **first the past is cleared, then the present is completed, then a direction is given, then support comes.** An axis of time has been set up.

### لِ — the function of the lām, and the classical debate

This lām has been much debated in the history of exegesis. The problem is this: **how can a fatḥ be the "cause" of a forgiveness?**

In Arabic this particle has two basic functions:

| Type | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **لام التعليل** | The lām of causation | *so that…* — a real relation of cause and effect |
| **لام العاقبة** | The lām of outcome (*ṣayrūra*) | *…and in the end this came about* — not the cause intended but the result that emerged |

There is a plain example of the second usage in the Qurʾān: *"The family of Pharaoh picked him up, so that he might become an enemy and a sorrow to them"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/8). This was not their aim in picking him up; it was what came of it. The lām states not the intention but the outcome.

**The explanations advanced in classical exegesis:**

| Explanation | How it is built |
|---|---|
| **A causal link** | The fatḥ, together with the thankfulness, patience and striving that come with it, becomes a means to forgiveness |
| **A link of outcome** | The lām states a result; what is being enumerated is the picture that emerged along with the fatḥ |
| **A collective ground** | The *lām* attaches not to the fatḥ alone but to the whole process preceding it — "all of this is for this" |
| **The indication reversed** | The fatḥ is not the cause of the forgiveness; both are two parts of one and the same divine decree, and they are merely set out in sequence |

**I take no side in this debate.** The one thing that needs recording is this: whichever explanation is taken, the verse presents the fatḥ not as a **gain** but as **the beginning of a series of consequences**. And the first item of that series is not an item of victory but an item of **forgiveness**.

This is the same pattern as the one seen in Sūrat al-Naṣr, and it is striking: there too the first thing to follow the victory was the seeking of forgiveness. **In the Qurʾān fatḥ and forgiveness stand insistently side by side.**

### ذنب — root: ذ-ن-ب

The concrete sense of the root is **tail**: **ذَنَب** (*dhanab*) — the tail of an animal, the part of a thing that trails behind.

From the same root:
- **ذَنُوب** (*dhanūb*) — a great bucket full of water; and "share, portion". It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"Those who do wrong have a share too"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/59).
- **أَذْنَاب** (*adhnāb*) — tails; those who trail along behind.

**ذَنْب** (*dhanb*) — sin, offence. The connection the lexicographers record is this: *dhanb* is **an act that drags something along behind it.** Not a deed that ends the moment it is done, but a behaviour that pulls a consequence after it like a tail.

**This shows how the word differs from the other words for sin.** There are several distinct words in the Qurʾān, and each looks at the matter from a different place:

| Word | Root | What it looks at |
|---|---|---|
| **ذَنْب** | ذ-ن-ب | **The consequence** — what it drags behind it |
| **إِثْم** | أ-ث-م | **The slowing down** — the root carries "to weigh down, to hold back" |
| **خَطِيئَة** | خ-ط-أ | **The failure to hit** — missing the target |
| **سَيِّئَة** | س-و-أ | **The evil / the ugliness** — it occurs in verse 5 of this sūra |
| **جُرْم** | ج-ر-م | **The cutting off** — the severing of a bond |

*Dhanb*, that is, brings forward not the moral quality of the act but **the mark it leaves**.

### مَا تَقَدَّمَ مِن ذَنۢبِكَ وَمَا تَأَخَّرَ

One of the most debated expressions in the Qurʾān. Its lexical sense: *"what has gone ahead of your sin and what has stayed behind."*

**Let us look first at what is happening at the level of language.**

- **تَقَدَّمَ** — root ق-د-م: to be in front, to go ahead. *Qadam* (foot), *taqdīm* (bringing forward), *muqaddam* (that which is in front).
- **تَأَخَّرَ** — root أ-خ-ر: to stay behind, to be left to the end. *Ākhir* (last), *taʾkhīr* (postponement), *muʾakhkhar* (that which is left to the end).

The two are an **opposed pair**. In Arabic, and in languages generally, naming the two opposite ends is a way of meaning **everything that lies between them**. English works the same way in *great and small*, *far and wide*, *first and last*. The Qurʾān uses this technique openly: *"He knows what is before them and what is behind them"* (al-Baqara 2/255; Ṭāhā 20/110) — what is meant is not two points but **the whole.**

So the plainest reading of the expression at the level of language is this: **an extent is being stated.** It is not saying "this much was forgiven, this much was left"; no limit is set.

### The classical explanations of the expression

The real issue here attaches to the subject known in theology as **ʿiṣma**: the preservation of the prophets from sin. Because the construction "your sin" occurs in the verse, it has been debated how the expression is to be reconciled with the doctrine of ʿiṣma.

**The disagreement is not concealed. Below, in a table, are the principal explanations worked out in the classical exegetical and theological literature. I transmit them; I adopt no preference among them and present none of them as certainly correct.**

| Explanation | How it is built | What is adduced as its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **A pattern of extent** | "Before and after" is not an enumeration but an expression of **totality**. The verse is not reporting a list of sins, it is making a declaration of forgiveness | The Arabic usage of stating a whole by an opposed pair (al-Baqara 2/255) |
| **Before / after prophethood** | *Taqaddama*: the period before the prophetic office; *taʾakhkhara*: what comes after it | A chronological reading; it is transmitted as one of the most widespread explanations |
| **Zalla / leaving aside the better** | What is meant is not "sin" but **the leaving aside of what would have been more fitting**; the higher the station, the finer the measure | The presence of similar expressions in the stories of the prophets |
| **Ascription to the community** | *Dhanbika* — "the sin of those who are on your account, on your path"; the construction states a relation | That at Muḥammad 47/19 forgiveness is asked for the believers as well |
| **The things laid to his charge** | What are meant are the acts the other side counted as "sin" and laid to his charge | That the context is a context of conflict |
| **Tafwīḍ** | The expression is a **declaration of honour**; its detail is committed to God and is not unfolded | Stopping at the boundary of the text |

**Three notes on this table:**

**One.** Some of these explanations do not exclude one another. The "pattern of extent" reading and the stance of *tafwīḍ*, for instance, can be held at the same time.

**Two.** The Qurʾān itself commands the Prophet to seek forgiveness in more than one place: *"Seek forgiveness for your sin, and for the believing men and the believing women"* (Muḥammad 47/19); *"Seek forgiveness for your sin"* (Ghāfir 40/55); and al-Naṣr 110/3. These verses are plain, and they form the ground of this debate in exegesis. At `110-nasr.md` three views on the same question were set out in a table; no preference was adopted there either.

**Three.** This distinction must be kept: **the question of ʿiṣma is a theological debate, and the schools differ over its extent** — a broad agreement is transmitted regarding preservation from the major sins, and a divergence regarding minor error and forgetting. This commentary takes no theological side.

### What the verse does — independently of the debate

Whatever the views may be, what the verse **does in its word order** is plain, and it can be recorded without entering the debate.

Immediately after a proclamation of victory comes a list of four items, and **the first item of the list is forgiveness.** The text, that is, sets the first meaning of what has been won not as "what is obtained" but as "**what is covered over**".

**I record this as my own reading:** this ordering agrees with the later verses of the sūra. Nothing people do is made a subject of praise anywhere in the sūra — even the pledge is described as being "in reality made to God" (10), the withholding of hands is ascribed to God (24), the sakīna is sent down. If a text pulls the human share this far back, it is consistent that its first item should be a forgiveness.

### وَيُتِمَّ نِعْمَتَهُۥ عَلَيْكَ — the completing of the favour

**تَمَّ** — root ت-م-م: to be complete, to become without deficiency. *Itmām* — completing. *Tatimma* — a supplement that completes.

**The choice of word is meaningful:** *itmām* is not the giving of something that is absent but **the closing of what is lacking in something already there**. The verse, that is, presumes that the favour has begun; what it says is that it **will be brought to its end**.

The Qurʾān uses this construction in several places, and all of them point to the end of a process:

- *"…and so that I may complete My favour upon you"* (al-Baqara 2/150) — in the context of the change of the prayer direction.
- *"Your Lord will choose you… and will complete His favour upon you and upon the family of Jacob (Yaʿqūb)"* (Yūsuf 12/6) — a sentence at the opening of the story that announces its end.
- *"Today I have perfected your religion for you and completed My favour upon you"* (al-Māʾida 5/3).

**The link with al-Māʾida 5/3 is striking.** There the same verb comes in the **perfect**: *atmamtu* — it has been completed. Here it comes in the imperfect: *yutimma* — it will be completed. It is transmitted that al-Māʾida 5/3 came down at the Farewell Pilgrimage; there is thus a distance of some years between the two verses, and the tense of the verb marks exactly that distance.

I record this as an observation; the order of revelation between the two verses rests on transmitted reports, and I do not present it as a proof.

### وَيَهْدِيَكَ صِرَٰطًا مُّسْتَقِيمًا — the path

**هدى** — root ه-د-ي: to show the way, to turn towards the right direction. The detailed analysis of the root was made at `093-duha.md` and `087-ala.md`; I do not repeat it here.

The analysis of the words **صِرَاط** and **مُسْتَقِيم** was made at `001-fatiha.md`: that *ṣirāṭ* is the wide, open, well-travelled main road; that the lexicographers differ over its origin (some of them counting the word as having entered Arabic from outside); that it is always used in the **singular** in the Qurʾān and stands in contrast to the plural *subul* (al-Anʿām 6/153); that *mustaqīm* comes from the root ق-و-م and carries the sense "that does not bend and that holds upright" — all of that was treated there. I do not repeat it.

**There are two points that belong here.**

**One: it comes indefinite.** In al-Fātiḥa it is definite — *ihdinā'ṣ-ṣirāṭa'l-mustaqīm*. Here it is indefinite: *ṣirāṭan mustaqīmā*. In the classical explanations this difference is tied to a distinction between "the whole of a particular road" and "a stretch of that road, a stage of it". I do not present this as a settled ruling; but in the context of the sūra it looks meaningful: what is at issue here is a **stage** — and indeed the verb immediately before it is a verb that says "will be completed".

**Two: the same expression occurs twice in the sūra.** In verse 20 it will be turned this time towards **the community** — *wa yahdiyakum ṣirāṭan mustaqīmā*. What is given to the singular addressee in the second verse is given to the plural in the twentieth.

## 48/3 — وَيَنصُرَكَ ٱللَّهُ نَصْرًا عَزِيزًا

*Wa yanṣuraka'llāhu naṣran ʿazīzā*

"And so that God may help you with an honourable/invincible help."

### The fourth verb

This verse is the continuation of the second — the fourth verb hanging on the same *lām*. Its being a separate verse is not a mark of a break but of **emphasis**: the last item of the series stands on its own.

### نَصْر — root: ن-ص-ر

The full analysis of the root was made at `110-nasr.md`: **to help, to stand behind.** One of the concrete uses of the root has to do with rain (*naṣara'l-ghaythu'l-arḍa* — the rain helped the soil); *anṣār*, *naṣīr*, *manṣūr*, *tanāṣur* are from the same root. I do not repeat it here.

**What belongs here is this:** *naṣr* and *fatḥ* come side by side many times in the Qurʾān, and the division of labour between them is consistent.

| Verse | Expression | Its position |
|---|---|---|
| al-Ṣaff 61/13 | *naṣrun mina'llāhi wa fatḥun qarīb* | **A promise** — not yet come about |
| al-Fatḥ 48/1-3 | *fataḥnā… wa yanṣuraka* | **Realisation + continuation** |
| al-Naṣr 110/1 | *naṣru'llāhi wa'l-fatḥ* | **Completion** |

Three verses use the same two words at three different times: as it is promised, as it is given, as it is completed.

**The difference in the ordering is striking.** In al-Ṣaff and al-Naṣr *naṣr* comes first and *fatḥ* after — help first, opening second. In this sūra it is reversed: first *fatḥ* (1), then *naṣr* (3). I record this as an observation: in this sūra the opening has already happened, and the help is what is to come after the opening. As early as its third verse the sūra says that the matter does not end with the door's being opened.

### عَزِيزًا — root: ع-ز-ز

This root was treated at `059-hasr.md`, `057-hadid.md` and `062-cuma.md` in the context of God's name **al-ʿAzīz**. The primary sense of the root: **that a thing is rare, hard to come by, unwearing and unconquerable.** *ʿIzza* — both honour and hardness. *Aʿazza* — to strengthen. Of ground, *arḍun ʿazāz* — hard ground that resists cultivation.

**The question here is what the word is an adjective to, and this is striking.**

In the Qurʾān *ʿazīz* is almost always **a name of God** or an attribute of a person. Here it has become an adjective to an **act** — more precisely to a verbal noun: *naṣran ʿazīzā*, "an *ʿazīz* help."

In classical exegesis this is explained in two directions:

| Explanation | Its meaning |
|---|---|
| **Invincible help** | A help that cannot be withstood, cannot be blocked, cannot be turned back |
| **Help that confers honour** | A help that makes the one helped honoured; a help that does not lower the one who receives it |

**The second explanation is worth dwelling on**, and I unfold it here as my own reading. Help is not always a comfortable thing for the side that receives it; more often than not it brings a debt, a dependence, a diminishment. The one who helps establishes a claim over the one he helps. The verse puts the adjective at exactly this point: this is a help that **does not make its receiver smaller**.

This reading has a counterpart within the sūra. The event the sūra recounts is, seen from outside, a step backwards, and steps backwards are belittling. In its third line the verse names that step backwards together with the word **honour**.

**A note on the word:** the same root will come in verses 7 and 19 of the sūra as a name of God — *ʿazīzan ḥakīmā*. That is, with three verses in between, the adjective passes from the help to the one who gives it.

## 48/4 — هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنزَلَ ٱلسَّكِينَةَ فِى قُلُوبِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ لِيَزْدَادُوٓا۟ إِيمَٰنًا مَّعَ إِيمَٰنِهِمْ ۗ وَلِلَّهِ جُنُودُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا

*Huwa'lladhī anzala's-sakīnata fī qulūbi'l-muʾminīna li-yazdādū īmānan maʿa īmānihim; wa li'llāhi junūdu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ; wa kāna'llāhu ʿalīman ḥakīmā*

"It is He who sent down the sakīna into the hearts of the believers, so that they might add faith to their faith. To God belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth. God is knowing, possessed of wisdom."

### Context: the shift of address

The first three verses were directed at a **singular** hearer: *laka, dhanbika, ʿalayka, yahdiyaka, yanṣuraka*. With the fourth verse the address opens out to the **plural**: *qulūbi'l-muʾminīn*.

The sūra proceeds, that is, in this way: first a fatḥ was given to a single person (1-3), then what that fatḥ does to a community is recounted (4-7). And the name of what is given to the community has nothing to do with victory: **sakīna.**

This shows the consistency of the sūra's choice of words. If fatḥ is "opening", then what answers to an opening in a human being must be a **loosening**, a settling. And that is exactly what the word is.

### سكينة — root: س-ك-ن

**The primary sense of the root: to stop, to become motionless, to settle in a place.**

This root was treated at `061-saff.md` (*maskan*) and at `059-hasr.md` and `107-maun.md` (*miskīn*). I add here the detail that belongs to this place.

**Derivatives:**

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **سُكُون** (sukūn) | Motionlessness. In Arabic grammar this is also a letter's carrying no vowel — the halting of the sound |
| **مَسْكَن** (maskan) | The place where one comes to rest. Not "house" but **the place where movement ends** |
| **سَاكِن** (sākin) | One who is still, who is settled; both "motionless" and "resident in a place" |
| **مِسْكِين** (miskīn) | The person whom poverty leaves unable to stir — who cannot move |
| **سِكِّين** (sikkīn) | Knife. The explanation the lexicographers give: because it stops the movement of the animal it slaughters |
| **سَكِينَة** (sakīna) | Inward stillness, a state of settledness |

**At the centre of the word there is a single idea: the stopping of movement.** And this idea can be either favourable or unfavourable — in *miskīn* it is a helplessness, in *maskan* a taking of shelter, in *sakīna* a peace. The difference lies in **why** the movement stopped: because it was blocked from outside, or because there was no longer any need for it.

**The pattern: فَعِيلَة.** In Arabic this pattern mostly conveys a **state, a settled quality** (*ṭabīʿa*, *gharīza*, *ṣarīḥa*). *Sakīna*, that is, is not a passing feeling but **a condition that is established**.

### What sakīna is — and what it is not

The word is hard to translate. "Peace", "stillness", "inward ease", "assurance" — each catches one face of it, none of them covers it.

**It is more accurate to say this:** *sakīna* is **the stilling of the thrashing** inside a person. Not the disappearance of fear — a weight that keeps a person from being shifted even while the fear is there. Indeed, in the situation the sūra recounts the thing to be feared has not been removed; what has been removed is the very motion that flings a person about.

**It occurs six times in the Qurʾān, and three of them are in this sūra:**

| Place | To whom | When |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Baqara 2/248** | To a community — "a sakīna from your Lord" coming inside the *tābūt* | When a sign of kingship was demanded |
| **al-Tawba 9/26** | To the Prophet and to the believers | After a moment of rout |
| **al-Tawba 9/40** | To the Prophet — in the context of *"do not grieve, God is with us"* | In the place of refuge, at a moment of being hard pressed |
| **al-Fatḥ 48/4** | **Into the hearts** of the believers | Together with the proclamation of the fatḥ |
| **al-Fatḥ 48/18** | **Upon** those who pledged | During the pledge beneath the tree |
| **al-Fatḥ 48/26** | **Upon** the Prophet and the believers | While there was *ḥamiyya* on the other side |

The common point is this: **each time, the sakīna comes down at a moment of tension.** It is a word not of easy times but of hard-pressed ones.

And the occurrence at al-Baqara 2/248 suggests that the word has a further function of **holding a community together**: there the sakīna is inside the sign that secures the acceptance of a claim to kingship. I record this as an observation; the verse itself says this much.

### أَنزَلَ — "He sent down"

**Root ن-ز-ل.** The use of this verb for things that are not physical was treated in detail at `057-hadid.md` (in the discussion of iron), and exactly this verse was adduced there as evidence: *sakīna* is not a physical object, yet the verb is *anzala*.

The note made there holds here too: **in Arabic *inzāl* conveys a transfer from what is high to what is low, and this transfer may be physical or may be a matter of rank.** The passing of something from what is exalted to what is beneath it.

**What belongs here is this:** the choice of verb says that the sakīna is **not produced**. A person does not build his inward peace by his own effort; it **comes down** to him. The construction of the sentence reinforces this further.

### هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ — a structure of restriction (*ḥaṣr*)

The sentence could have been built as *"God sent down the sakīna"*. It is not. **هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ** — the pattern "it is He who" is used.

In Arabic this pattern conveys **restriction to one**: the doer of the act is **He alone**, and no other. It works as the English cleft does in "it is He who sends it down", where the weight falls on "He".

And this pattern appears **three times** in the sūra — and each time it does the same work:

| Verse | Expression | What it restricts to God |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | *Huwa'lladhī anzala's-sakīnata* | The source of inward stillness |
| **24** | *Wa huwa'lladhī kaffa aydiyahum* | The stopping of the fighting |
| **28** | *Huwa'lladhī arsala rasūlahū* | The sending of the Messenger |

**I record this as my own reading:** the three sentences make up the three components of the event the sūra recounts — the stillness within, the ceasefire without, and the source of the whole matter. All three are bound by the same pattern to the same address. While the sūra does leave human beings a share in the event, it repeats three times **where the decision was taken**.

### فِى قُلُوبِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ — "into the hearts of the believers"

The particle **فِى**: into. The sakīna comes down not *upon* the hearts but **into** them.

This detail matters, because in the sūra's other two occurrences the particle is different: in verse 18 *ʿalayhim* (upon them), in verse 26 *ʿalā rasūlihī wa ʿalā'l-muʾminīn* (upon). **Only here is it said "into the hearts".**

In the classical commentaries this difference is not always worked out separately. I record it as an observation, and I shall compare the three occurrences at verse 26: the fourth verse is the only one that says **where** the sakīna comes down.

### لِيَزْدَادُوٓا۟ إِيمَٰنًا مَّعَ إِيمَٰنِهِمْ — the increase of faith

One of the most debated expressions in the sūra, and one that **needs to be handled carefully.**

**First, the level of language.**

**ازداد** — root ز-ي-د: to increase, to grow more. It is a derivative of Form VIII (*izdāda*; the *dhāl* sound has turned into a *dāl* for the sake of assimilation). Form VIII here conveys an increase **that takes place in the person himself**: "to increase, to gain an increase".

**مَّعَ إِيمَٰنِهِمْ** — "together with their faith", "on top of their faith".

The sentence says this: **there was already a faith; something further was added on top of it.** The function of the sakīna, that is, is described not as bringing faith into being but as **increasing a faith already there**.

### The classical theological debate: does faith increase?

This verse stands at the centre of one of the oldest debates in the history of theology. **I transmit it without concealing the disagreement and without taking a side.**

The source of the debate is a difference of definition: **what is faith?**

| View | The definition of faith | The question of increase | Ground |
|---|---|---|---|
| **It increases and decreases** | Faith consists of assent in the heart, confession with the tongue, and **works** | Since works are a part of faith, faith increases as works increase | This verse; al-Anfāl 8/2; al-Tawba 9/124; al-Muddaththir 74/31; al-Aḥzāb 33/22; Āl ʿImrān 3/173 |
| **It neither increases nor decreases** | Faith is the assent of the heart; works are not a part of faith but a requirement of it | Assent is indivisible; either it is there or it is not | The nature of assent; the frequent **separate** mention of faith and works in the Qurʾān (*āmanū wa ʿamilū'ṣ-ṣāliḥāt*) |

Those who defend the second view explain texts such as this verse as follows:

- **What increases is the object of faith.** Because the revelation came down piece by piece, the number of things to be assented to increases — faith has not weakened, its extent has widened.
- **What increases is the quality of faith.** Its steadiness, its brightness, its unshakeability increase; not the assent itself.
- **What increases is the fruit of faith.** Works and obedience increase; in ordinary speech this is ascribed to faith.

**I take no side in this debate.** Both views have been defended by major scholars from an early period, and the question is a debate about a definition: the two sides call different things "faith" and then argue over whether it increases. Per the method (`STYLE.md`), no theological partisanship is adopted.

**Let us record what the verse says at its own level; these things are independent of the debate:**

**One.** The verse says that faith is **not static**. It is not described as something set up once and then left.

**Two.** What is shown as the **cause** of the increase is not a piece of knowledge but a **state**: the sakīna. The verse does not say "they saw a proof and their faith increased"; it says "they were stilled within and their faith increased".

**Three.** The increase is given in a clause of **purpose** (*li-yazdādū*). What is shown as the aim of the sakīna's coming down, that is, is not relief but **increase**. Stillness is set down not as a place of arrival but as a **possibility**.

**I record this as my own reading:** this third point is the most striking side of the verse. Stillness is generally thought of as an end — "now I am at ease". The verse builds the opposite: stillness is **the ground a thing needs in order to grow.** Nothing collects in a vessel that is being shaken.

### Other verses in the Qurʾān that speak of faith increasing

To see the subject whole, I list the places where the same verb occurs. All of them tie the increase to an event:

- *"When His verses are recited to them, it **increases** their faith"* (al-Anfāl 8/2) — a text that is recited.
- *"Whenever a sūra is sent down… it **has increased** the faith of those who believe, and they rejoice"* (al-Tawba 9/124) — new revelation.
- *"So that those who believe may **increase** in faith"* (al-Muddaththir 74/31) — in the context of the announcing of a number.
- *"This only **increased** them in faith and in submission"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/22) — at a moment of distress under siege.
- *"People said to them, 'They have gathered against you, so fear them'; but this **increased** them in faith, and they said, 'God is enough for us'"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/173) — news of a threat.

**Looking at the list, something becomes visible:** some of the things that set off the increase are good news and some are bad. At al-Aḥzāb 33/22 and Āl ʿImrān 3/173 what causes the increase is a **threat** outright. I record this as an observation: the increase the Qurʾān describes does not depend on conditions improving.

### وَلِلَّهِ جُنُودُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ

**جُنْد** — root ج-ن-د. The lexicographers dwell on the concrete sense of the root: *jund* originally means **hard, stony, compacted ground**; from this, it is said, it passed to the sense "a gathered, packed, close-standing body". The word's sense of "army" is born of that holding-together.

**The word does not mean "soldier".** *Jund* answers to every kind of force gathered for a purpose — it need not be human. The Qurʾān shows this openly: *"We sent against them a wind and **hosts** you did not see"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/9). What is called *junūd* there is the wind.

In this sūra the word occurs **twice**: in verses 4 and 7, in exactly the same wording. I shall take up this repetition at verse 7.

**The function of the sentence here:** it is set immediately behind the sentence about the sakīna. Two things thus stand side by side — **the stillness that comes down within** and **the force standing ready without.** And in naming the second the verse gives the ground of the first: what the sakīna rests on is not optimism but the knowledge of whose the hosts are.

**A note of *tanzīh*:** that "the hosts belong to God" does not mean that He has need of an army. On the contrary — the verse states ownership, not need. Indeed, when the sūra repeats the same sentence in verse 7, it will set the name *ʿAzīz* at its end: the one who cannot be withstood.

### وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلِيمًا حَكِيمًا

**كَانَ** — the perfect tense here conveys no time; on the classical explanation it conveys **the fixedness of the attribute**: "He has always been so, He is so at all times". This note was entered at `110-nasr.md` for *kāna tawwābā*.

**عَلِيم** — root ع-ل-م: to know. The *faʿīl* pattern conveys the settledness of the attribute.
**حَكِيم** — root ح-ك-م: to make a thing firm, to set it in its proper place. From the same root come *ḥikma*, *ḥukm*, and — strikingly — *ḥakama*, a kind of **لِجَام**: the piece of the bridle that turns an animal's head. At the centre of the root lie **holding firm and steering**.

**The two names coming together, and their place here.** The context of the sūra is a decision people **did not understand**: they turned back, they could not enter, the *ʿumra* could not be performed. The two names set at the end of the verse answer to exactly this — **He knows** (what you do not know) and **He sets in its proper place** (an order you cannot see).

This is, in the fourth verse, the seed of the sentence *fa-ʿalima mā lam taʿlamū* in verse 27. The sūra prepares its central sentence at the very beginning with a pair of names.

## 48/5 — لِّيُدْخِلَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ جَنَّٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَا وَيُكَفِّرَ عَنْهُمْ سَيِّـَٔاتِهِمْ ۚ وَكَانَ ذَٰلِكَ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ فَوْزًا عَظِيمًا

*Li-yudkhila'l-muʾminīna wa'l-muʾmināti jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā'l-anhāru khālidīna fīhā wa yukaffira ʿanhum sayyiʾātihim; wa kāna dhālika ʿinda'llāhi fawzan ʿaẓīmā*

"To bring the believing men and the believing women into gardens beneath which rivers flow, there to remain for ever, and to cover over their evil deeds. That, with God, is a great deliverance."

### A new lām

The verse is a second purpose clause hanging on the *li-yazdādū* of the preceding verse. The chain runs on like this: the sakīna came down → so that faith might increase → and in the end so that they might be brought within.

**The verb is once again a verb of "bringing in":** *يُدْخِلَ*, root **د-خ-ل**. This completes the image of *fatḥ* in the first verse. It was recorded at Sūrat al-Naṣr: the meaning of a door's being opened is that someone will be able to enter. Here too a verb of **entering** comes after the opened door — and this time what is stressed is not the one who enters but **the one who brings in**: the verb is Form IV, that is, "to cause to enter".

### ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ — the pairing of men and women

In the preceding verse only *al-muʾminīn* was said. Here the pair comes: *al-muʾminīna wa'l-muʾmināt*.

In Arabic the masculine plural already covers a mixed body; the fourth verse, that is, was not leaving women outside either. The explicit mention here is a **tasrīḥ**: the extent stated over again in so many words.

**And this has a particular place in this sūra.** The sūra sets up this pair in three places:

| Verse | The pair | Context |
|---|---|---|
| **5** | *muʾminīn / muʾmināt* | Recompense — being brought into the gardens |
| **6** | *munāfiqīn / munāfiqāt*, *mushrikīn / mushrikāt* | Recompense — punishment |
| **25** | *rijālun muʾminūn / nisāʾun muʾmināt* | The believers in Mecca who were not recognised |

**The twenty-fifth verse is decisive.** There, believing men and women who were in Mecca and **not yet known** are shown as the ground on which the fighting was held back. The sūra, that is, does not use this pair for decoration: the course an event takes is tied to the people inside that pair.

### وَيُكَفِّرَ عَنْهُمْ سَيِّـَٔاتِهِمْ — "to cover over their evil deeds"

**The verb is striking: يُكَفِّر, root ك-ف-ر.**

The primary sense of this root was analysed at `002-bakara.md` (2/6): **to cover, to close something over.** That the farmer is called *kāfir* because he covers the seed with soil, and that at `057-hadid.md` (57/20) the word *kuffār* is used in the sense of "farmers", were treated there.

**What belongs here is this:** the same root is used here in **Form II** and for a **favourable** act — *takfīr*: to cover, to close over, to wipe away. *Kufr* and *kaffāra*, that is, are from the same root, and both are a covering. The difference lies in **what** is covered: the one covers the truth, the other a fault.

**سَيِّئَة** — root **س-و-أ**: to be bad, to be ugly, to be displeasing. *Sūʾ*, *asāʾa*, *sayyiʾa*. At the centre of the word there is less a moral verdict than a **displeasure**: the thing that sours the face at the sight of it.

The same root will occur **twice** in the next verse: *ẓanna's-sawʾ* and *dāʾiratu's-sawʾ*. The sūra thus uses this root three times, in three separate places — once as the thing that is covered over, and twice as the state of the other side.

### وَكَانَ ذَٰلِكَ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ فَوْزًا عَظِيمًا

**فَوْز** — root **ف-و-ز**: to be saved, to come through to safety; and at the same time "to win, to attain what one sought". The lexicographers record that the root carries the idea of **coming out alive from a dangerous place**; indeed **مَفَازَة** (*mafāza*), from the same root, means desert — a place dangerous to cross, and one from which a man is delivered once he has crossed it.

**This note on the root lights up the verse.** *Fawz* is not a prize taken but **a danger got through**. The English "deliverance" catches this side of it; "success" does not.

**عِندَ ٱللَّهِ** — "with God". This qualification matters, and it bears directly on the whole tension of the sūra.

The verse does not say "this is a great deliverance". It says: **"this, with God, is a great deliverance."** A court of assessment, that is, is being pointed to.

**I record this as my own reading:** the whole of the sūra is an argument about measures. There is an event, and by two different measures it yields two different results — on the human measure a step backwards, "with God" a fatḥ. The qualification *ʿinda'llāh* sets that distinction down once more, and openly. And the same qualification will be built again in verse 27 as a difference in knowledge.

## 48/6 — وَيُعَذِّبَ ٱلْمُنَٰفِقِينَ وَٱلْمُنَٰفِقَٰتِ وَٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ وَٱلْمُشْرِكَٰتِ ٱلظَّآنِّينَ بِٱللَّهِ ظَنَّ ٱلسَّوْءِ ۚ عَلَيْهِمْ دَآئِرَةُ ٱلسَّوْءِ ۖ وَغَضِبَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَعَنَهُمْ وَأَعَدَّ لَهُمْ جَهَنَّمَ ۖ وَسَآءَتْ مَصِيرًا

*Wa yuʿadhdhiba'l-munāfiqīna wa'l-munāfiqāti wa'l-mushrikīna wa'l-mushrikāti'ẓ-ẓānnīna bi'llāhi ẓanna's-sawʾ; ʿalayhim dāʾiratu's-sawʾ; wa ghaḍiba'llāhu ʿalayhim wa laʿanahum wa aʿadda lahum jahannam; wa sāʾat maṣīrā*

"And to punish the hypocritical men and the hypocritical women, and the mushrik men and the mushrik women, who think evil thoughts of God. Upon them is the circle of evil. God is angry with them, has cursed them and has prepared Hell for them. An evil destination!"

### The order in which the two groups are set out

A detail to be noticed: the two groups enumerated are **the hypocrites** and **the mushrikūn**. And the hypocrites come first.

This order is no accident. The context of the sūra is an **internal separation**: who came, who did not, whose speech does and whose does not agree with what is in his heart. Verses 11-16 of the sūra will work through this question from beginning to end. The text puts first not the opposition outside but **the stance taken up inside**.

**And a note:** *munāfiq* is not a name but an **attribute**. The root analysis of the word was made at `002-bakara.md`: from the root **ن-ف-ق** comes *nafaq* — the two-mouthed burrow of the field mouse; the hypocrite is the person who always keeps a second exit tunnel in reserve. Whose attribute this is depends on who carries it; per the method (`STYLE.md`) it is not fastened wholesale onto any community. Indeed the sūra itself names no one.

### ٱلظَّآنِّينَ بِٱللَّهِ ظَنَّ ٱلسَّوْءِ — thinking evil

This construction is given as the **shared attribute** of the two groups. Rather than describing the two separately, that is, the verse joins them under a single quality: thinking evil of God.

**ظَنّ** — root ظ-ن-ن. The analysis of this root was made at `049-hucurat.md` and `075-kiyame.md`: in Arabic the word answers, according to context, both to **a doubtful surmise** and to **a settled conviction**; it carries opposite senses.

**The verse does not say what the evil thought is, but the sūra does.** In the twelfth verse the same expression will be repeated, and this time its content will be given openly:

> *"You thought that the Messenger and the believers would never return to their families… and you thought an evil thought."* (48/12)

The evil thought here, then, **is not a theological conviction but a forecast of an outcome:** "this will not work, these people will not come back, standing on this side is a loss."

**It is important to record this.** The verse does not say "they did not believe in God"; it says "they thought evil of God". What is described is a fault not at the level of belief but at the level of **trust**. The Qurʾān gives one further example of this, and there too the context is the aftermath of a battle:

> *"…thinking of God, without right, **the thought of the age of ignorance**."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/154)

The link between the two verses is striking: there the thought is ascribed to "the age of ignorance", here to "evil". And in verse 26 of this sūra **ḥamiyya** will again be ascribed to "the age of ignorance". The same word qualifies three different things across the same group of sūras: thought, ḥamiyya, judgement (al-Māʾida 5/50), display (al-Aḥzāb 33/33).

### عَلَيْهِمْ دَآئِرَةُ ٱلسَّوْءِ — "upon them is the circle of evil"

**دَائِرَة** — root **د-و-ر**: to turn, to go round about. *Dār* (house — a place enclosed all round), *dawr*, *dāʾira*, *tadwīr*, *mudawwar* (round).

***Dāʾira* is a settled word in Arabic for "the turning of fortune", "the disaster that comes upon a man".** The image is plain: a wheel turns, and what was underneath comes up while what was on top goes under.

**The use of the word here is a sentence that gives back in kind.** The Qurʾān uses the same word in describing exactly this stance:

> *"Among the bedouin is one who counts what he spends as a forced levy and waits for **the turns of fortune to come round against you**. Upon them be the evil **turn**!"* (al-Tawba 9/98)

One side, that is, is waiting for the other side's wheel to come round. The verse turns the same word back: the one who is waiting stands inside the circle himself.

**The word order of the sentence reinforces this.** *ʿAlayhim dāʾiratu's-sawʾ* — the predicate (*ʿalayhim*) has been brought forward. In Arabic this fronting conveys **restriction**: "it is upon them" — and not upon anyone else.

### غَضِبَ … لَعَنَ … أَعَدَّ

Three verbs, three stages:

| Verb | Root | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **غَضِبَ** | غ-ض-ب | He was angry. The root carries the idea of "swelling, growing violent" |
| **لَعَنَ** | ل-ع-ن | He cursed. The primary sense of the root is **to drive far off, to expel** — *laʿīn*: one driven off |
| **أَعَدَّ** | ع-د-د | He prepared. From the same root *ʿadad* (number), *istiʿdād* (readiness) |

**The root sense of the word لعن is worth recording.** "Curse" is heard as an act of abuse, an imprecation. In Arabic the primary sense is **being put far from mercy** — it is a word of distance, not a word of insult.

**The verb أَعَدَّ, for its part, states a time:** made ready, beforehand. The recompense is presented not as a flare of anger but as an ordered outcome.

### وَسَآءَتْ مَصِيرًا

**مَصِير** — root **ص-ي-ر**: to become, to turn into a state, to arrive. *Ṣāra* — it became, it turned into. *Maṣīr* — **the place arrived at**, and at the same time "the state turned into".

The choice of word is meaningful: for "place" Arabic has *makān*, *dār*, *manzil*. *Maṣīr* is the place arrived at as **the result of a becoming** — not the place one goes to but the one that one **turns into**.

And a note on word order: the verse ends with *wa sāʾat* — the root again **س-و-أ**. Within the one verse, then, this root occurs three times: *ẓanna's-sawʾ*, *dāʾiratu's-sawʾ*, *sāʾat*. An evil thought, an evil circle, an evil arrival. **Three stages bound together by a single root:** the thought, the outcome, the place of arrival.

I record this as a weave of sound and sense, not as a claim of miracle.

## 48/7 — وَلِلَّهِ جُنُودُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَزِيزًا حَكِيمًا

*Wa li'llāhi junūdu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ; wa kāna'llāhu ʿazīzan ḥakīmā*

"To God belong the hosts of the heavens and the earth. God is ʿazīz, ḥakīm."

### The repetition is itself a meaning

The first half of this sentence occurred in the fourth verse **in exactly the same wording**. It is repeated with two verses in between. The only thing that changes is **the pair of names at the end**:

| Verse | Sentence | Closing |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | *wa li'llāhi junūdu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* | *wa kāna'llāhu **عَلِيمًا** حَكِيمًا* |
| **7** | *wa li'llāhi junūdu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* | *wa kāna'llāhu **عَزِيزًا** حَكِيمًا* |

**And the difference between them agrees exactly with what is recounted in between.**

- In the fourth verse the sentence came in the context of **the sending down of sakīna upon the believers**. There **ʿAlīm** (the knowing) is placed — because what was spoken of was something that comes down into hearts, and it is He who knows hearts.
- In the seventh verse the sentence comes in the context of **the punishment of the other side**. There **ʿAzīz** (the one who cannot be withstood) is placed — because what is spoken of is the carrying out of a sentence.

**What is common to both is Ḥakīm:** in both directions, what is done is in its proper place.

This is a closing technique often seen in the Qurʾān, and the same fact was dwelt on at `059-hasr.md`: one and the same sentence closes two different pieces of work with two different pairs of names.

### The work the sentence does in the block

The section between four and seven forms a **frame**:

```
4  — the hosts belong to God  →  the believers: sakīna
5  —                             the believers: gardens
6  —                             the other side: punishment
7  — the hosts belong to God
```

The section in which the two groups are separated, that is, has been set between two repetitions of the same sentence. **This is the most regular structural weave in the sūra**, and it says this: both groups are inside the same possession. The separation is not a change of ownership.

**I record this as my own reading**, but the datum of word order is plain: that the sentence is repeated twice, at exactly these two points, stands in the text.

### A note on a number

In this block the pattern *wa kāna'llāhu…* occurred twice (4, 7). Throughout the sūra this pattern — or a variant of it — appears **at the end of nine verses**: 4, 5, 7, 11, 14, 19, 21, 24, 26. The rhythm of the sūra is largely built out of this repetition: each section is sealed with a pair of names.

This is a characteristic feature of the long Medinan sūras, and it was seen at `057-hadid.md` and `059-hasr.md` as well.

## 48/8 — إِنَّآ أَرْسَلْنَٰكَ شَٰهِدًا وَمُبَشِّرًا وَنَذِيرًا

*Innā arsalnāka shāhidan wa mubashshiran wa nadhīrā*

"We have sent you as a witness, a bringer of good news and a warner."

### The repetition of the opening

The pattern of the sentence is the same as that of the sūra's first verse:

| Verse | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **1** | **إِنَّا فَتَحْنَا لَكَ** — We have opened for you |
| **8** | **إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَٰكَ** — We have sent you |

The same particle of emphasis, the same plural of majesty, the same singular addressee. The sūra opens its second block in the same voice as the first.

**And this repetition serves as a reminder.** In between, a community was recounted (4-7): those upon whom sakīna comes down, those who will be brought into the garden, those who will meet punishment. Now the address returns to the singular, and to whom that community is bound is set up again.

### The three attributes

**شَاهِد** — root **ش-ه-د**: to be present, to see, to report what one has seen. The analysis of the root was made at `085-buruc.md` and `050-kaf.md`. At the centre of the word there are two things, and they are inseparable: **being present** and **reporting.** There is no witnessing without seeing; and one who does not report is not counted a witness either.

**مُبَشِّر** — root **ب-ش-ر**: the outer surface of the skin (*bashara*). Good news is the change that shows in a person's face — the news is read not from within but **from the skin**. From the same root *bashar* (the human being — the creature whose skin is visible).

**نَذِير** — root **ن-ذ-ر**: to give notice of a danger in advance. From the same root *nadhr* (a vow — a word bound in advance), *indhār* (warning).

**The order of the three attributes is meaningful, and it is not accidental:**

| Order | Attribute | What it looks to | Its time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Shāhid** | To what is | **Now** |
| 2 | **Mubashshir** | To the good that will be | **The future** |
| 3 | **Nadhīr** | To the evil that may be | **The future** |

First, then, the testimony; then the news that opens in two directions. **And the good news comes before the warning** — in the Qurʾān this order is predominantly preserved.

**The Qurʾān's own parallel:** *"O Prophet! We have sent you as a witness, a bringer of good news and a warner; and as one calling to God by His leave, and as an illuminating lamp"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/45-46). The same three words, in the same order; there two further attributes are added.

**That this sūra is content with three is meaningful.** The subject of the sūra is an argument about an outcome: what name is to be given to what has happened. And at the head of the list stands **shāhid** — that is, *the one who sees what happens and reports it*. Then come the two attributes of announcing: those who report what will happen.

**I record this as my own reading:** the three attributes agree with the question of knowledge at the centre of the sūra. When a community reads what has happened wrongly, what is needed is a witness who sees what has happened rightly. The sūra names this attribute immediately before the very section in which the misreading is recounted.

## 48/9 — لِّتُؤْمِنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ وَتُعَزِّرُوهُ وَتُوَقِّرُوهُ وَتُسَبِّحُوهُ بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا

*Li-tuʾminū bi'llāhi wa rasūlihī wa tuʿazzirūhu wa tuwaqqirūhu wa tusabbiḥūhu bukratan wa aṣīlā*

"So that you may believe in God and His Messenger, and support him, and hold him in honour, and glorify Him morning and evening."

### Four verbs

Four things are set out as the purpose of the Messenger's being sent. And note: **the address is again plural** — *tuʾminū*. The eighth verse to the singular, the ninth to the plural.

| Verb | Root | What is asked |
|---|---|---|
| **تُؤْمِنُوا۟** | أ-م-ن | To believe — to trust |
| **تُعَزِّرُوهُ** | ع-ز-ر | To support, to stand behind |
| **تُوَقِّرُوهُ** | و-ق-ر | To grant weight, to show respect |
| **تُسَبِّحُوهُ** | س-ب-ح | To glorify |

### تعزير — root: ع-ز-ر

The root sense of the word may come as a surprise. **The primary sense of the root: to hinder, to ward off, to forbid.**

From this two directions are born, and both live on in Arabic:

- **To help** — to help someone by warding an enemy off from him. *Taʿzīr*, that is, is not "backing someone from behind" but **clearing an obstacle from in front of him**.
- **To punish** — in law *taʿzīr* is the disciplinary penalty outside the fixed punishments; its name comes from here, because the penalty **holds a person back** from doing it again.

The Qurʾān uses the word in the first sense: *"…if you believe in My messengers and **support** them…"* (al-Māʾida 5/12); *"…those who believe in him, who **support** him, who help him…"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/157).

**The word, then, is not "to love" or "to esteem"; it is an actual protecting.** A work done not with words but by clearing an obstacle away.

### توقير — root: و-ق-ر

**The primary sense of the root: weight.** *Wiqr* — a load, a weight that is carried. *Waqār* — gravity, seriousness.

And there is one further interesting use of the root: **وَقْر** is heaviness in the ear, that is, deafness. The Qurʾān uses this in several places: *"in their ears there is a heaviness"* (al-Anʿām 6/25; al-Isrāʾ 17/46; Luqmān 31/7; Fuṣṣilat 41/44).

The same root, then, names both **respect** and **not hearing**. The common idea: that a thing is heavy. Respect is granting something weight; deafness is the ear's growing heavy.

**The Qurʾān uses this root of God as well:** *"What is the matter with you, that you hope for no **weight** for God?"* (Nūḥ 71/13 — *waqārā*). This verse was treated at `071-nuh.md`.

**Taken together, the two verbs form a pair:** *taʿzīr* is the removing of an obstacle from outside, *tawqīr* the granting of weight from within. The one is actual support, the other the recognition of a standing. Together they describe the two faces of an allegiance.

### To whom the pronouns refer — a classical disagreement

In the verse all three verbs take the pronoun **هُ** (him) at the end: *tuʿazzirū**hu***, *tuwaqqirū**hu***, *tusabbiḥū**hu***. And at the head of the verse **two** parties had been named: God and His Messenger.

**Which of them the pronouns refer to has been debated in classical exegesis. I transmit the disagreement as it stands:**

| Reading | How it distributes them | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **By dividing** | *Taʿzīr* and *tawqīr* → to the Messenger; *tasbīḥ* → to God | Glorification is offered to God alone; support and respect, for their part, have to do with the Messenger's standing |
| **All to God** | All three pronouns refer to God | That the pronouns are identical; that the Qurʾān uses the expression *naṣru'llāh* (helping God) (Muḥammad 47/7; al-Ṣaff 61/14) |
| **All to the Messenger** | All three refer to the Messenger; *tasbīḥ* here means not "declare free of defect and exalt" but "say nothing unseemly about him" | That the context of the sentence is about the Messenger |

**The first reading is the most widespread in classical exegesis.** The second has also been defended, and is supported by the Qurʾān's use of the expression "helping God" — there too what is meant is helping the religion of God.

**The third reading is a minority one and is not adopted in this commentary**, because every use of the verb *tasbīḥ* in the Qurʾān is for God, and the meaning of the word (declaring free of all deficiency) cannot be framed for a human being. This is not a preference but the limit of the word's meaning.

**In this commentary this note is enough:** whichever distribution is adopted, the one to whom *tasbīḥ* is directed is God, and the measure of *tanzīh* is preserved. The remaining distinction is a matter of language; I do not impose a preference.

### بُكْرَةً وَأَصِيلًا — "morning and evening"

**بُكْرَة** — root **ب-ك-ر**: to be early, to bring forward. *Bikr* (firstborn; untouched), *bākir*, *ibtikār* (doing a thing first, invention). *Bukra* — the first part of the day.

**أَصِيل** — root **أ-ص-ل**: root, foundation, bottom. *Aṣl*, *uṣūl*, *aṣīl* (one whose root is sound). And *aṣīl* is the part of the day **between mid-afternoon and evening**.

**The link between these two senses of the word is an explanation the lexicographers record:** the end of the day is the day's "root" — the part that goes down to the bottom. I do not present this as a settled etymological ruling.

**What do the two mean together?** In Arabic, naming the two ends means what lies between them. "Morning and evening" — that is, **continually.** Not two particular hours but the two ends of the day, and so the whole of it.

The Qurʾān uses the same pair in several places: *"Glorify Him morning and evening"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/42); *"Do not drive away those who call upon their Lord morning and evening"* (al-Anʿām 6/52 — there *bi'l-ghadāti wa'l-ʿashiyy*).

**Its meaning as the close of the block:** the block had opened with the proclamation of a fatḥ. What is asked at its close is neither a celebration nor a next objective — it is **continuity**. This is the same pattern as in Sūrat al-Naṣr: what is asked after the outcome is an inward act.

## 48/10 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يُبَايِعُونَكَ إِنَّمَا يُبَايِعُونَ ٱللَّهَ يَدُ ٱللَّهِ فَوْقَ أَيْدِيهِمْ ۚ فَمَن نَّكَثَ فَإِنَّمَا يَنكُثُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِۦ ۖ وَمَنْ أَوْفَىٰ بِمَا عَٰهَدَ عَلَيْهُ ٱللَّهَ فَسَيُؤْتِيهِ أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

*Inna'lladhīna yubāyiʿūnaka innamā yubāyiʿūna'llāh; yadu'llāhi fawqa aydīhim; fa-man nakatha fa-innamā yankuthu ʿalā nafsih; wa man awfā bimā ʿāhada ʿalayhu'llāha fa-sa-yuʾtīhi ajran ʿaẓīmā*

"Those who pledge allegiance to you are in truth pledging allegiance to God. The hand of God is above their hands. Whoever breaks his pledge breaks it only against himself. And whoever fulfils what he has covenanted with God, He will give him a great reward."

### بيعة — root: ب-ي-ع

**The primary sense of the root: to sell — and to buy.**

The root was analysed at `060-mumtehine.md` (60/12, the verse of the pledge) and at `062-cuma.md` (62/9, *dharu'l-bayʿ*). I recall briefly what was recorded there and build on it:

- The root is counted among the **aḍdād** (words carrying opposite senses): the same verb conveys both selling and buying. The reason lies in the structure of the thing — a sale **is a single transaction**, it has two sides, and the word names the transaction itself.
- The concrete source of *bayʿa* is that in Arab custom a sale was made final by the parties' **striking hands**. The pledge is that striking of hands.

**What belongs here and needs underlining is this: in its origin the pledge is not an act of worship or of veneration but the act of a commercial contract.**

This is very far from the word's present-day associations. "To pledge allegiance" mostly calls up submission, the handing over of one's will, an unquestioning attachment. The logic of the word itself says the opposite:

| The property of a sale | Its counterpart in the pledge |
|---|---|
| **There are two parties** | Not a one-way surrender |
| **Its content is defined** | What is given and what is taken can be counted |
| **There is a consideration** | Not a transfer without return |
| **Breaking it is a violation** | It produces a legal consequence, not a moral one |

The Qurʾān uses this commercial language openly in the context of the pledge:

> *"God has **bought** from the believers their lives and their possessions in return for the Garden… This is a true promise binding upon Him in the Torah, the Gospel and the Qurʾān. And who is more faithful to his covenant than God? So rejoice in the **bargain** you have made."* (al-Tawba 9/111)

The same root, the same logic, the same words: *ishtirāʾ* (buying), *bayʿ* (sale), *ʿahd*.

### يُبَايِعُونَ — the mufāʿala form

The verb is of the **مُفَاعَلَة** form, and this is the most important grammatical feature of the word.

In Arabic the *mufāʿala* form (Form III) conveys an act with **two sides**: *qātala* (fought one another), *kātaba* (corresponded), *ʿāhada* (made a mutual covenant). One-sided verbs do not enter this form.

**The very pattern of the verb, then, says that the pledge has two sides.** The word itself does not convey a one-sided obedience; had it done so, another form would have been used.

And the verse names these two sides openly: on one side those who pledge, on the other — differently from what would be expected — **God.**

### إِنَّمَا يُبَايِعُونَ ٱللَّهَ — "they are in truth pledging allegiance to God"

**إِنَّمَا** — in Arabic this is a particle of **restriction**: "only, solely, in truth". It restricts a judgement to one place and leaves every other place outside.

And it occurs **twice** in the verse: *innamā yubāyiʿūna'llāh* and *innamā yankuthu ʿalā nafsih*. The verse, that is, is built upon two sentences of restriction:

| Restriction | What it says |
|---|---|
| **The first** | The real party to the contract is **God** |
| **The second** | The harm of breaking the contract falls **on oneself** |

Each requires the other: if the other party is He, a violation can do the other party no harm.

### What the sentence does

This is one of the most extraordinary sentences in the sūra, and it must be read carefully.

What happens concretely is this: a group of people take hold of one person's hand. This is an entirely human, entirely physical act, performed thousands of times in history.

The verse does not **cancel** this act; it **renames** it. It does not say "do not pledge allegiance to him"; it says "in pledging allegiance to him you are in truth pledging allegiance to God".

**And this has a consequence that runs in two directions — both of which must be recorded:**

**One. It increases the weight of the allegiance.** The word given is not bounded by the person to whom it was given.

**Two. And to just the same degree, it takes the address of the allegiance away from the human being.** If the real party to the contract is God, then the person in between is not a **party** but the **place** at which the contract is made. This is a note that cuts off every reading which makes allegiance to a person absolute.

**I record this as my own reading:** this second direction is often passed over, and the sentence is read only in its first direction. But the particle of restriction works in both directions: to restrict a thing to one place is to take it away from **every other place**. This is an interpretation, not a transmitted report.

**Another place where the Qurʾān builds the same structure** supports this reading:

> *"When you threw, it was not you who threw, but God who threw."* (al-Anfāl 8/17)

The same logic: the act was really done, the man really threw; but another is shown as the owner of the outcome.

### يَدُ ٱللَّهِ فَوْقَ أَيْدِيهِمْ

The sentence is one of the **mutashābih** expressions of the Qurʾān (those whose meaning cannot be fixed with certainty). Let us set the measure first, and then give the explanations.

### The note of tanzīh

**This expression carries no corporeal meaning. No hand, no limb, no direction, no place and no contact may be ascribed to God. This is not an interpretive preference but a boundary the Qurʾān sets itself:**

> **لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِۦ شَىْءٌ** — *"There is nothing like Him."* (al-Shūrā 42/11)

This boundary was applied at `057-hadid.md` in two separate places — at the expression *istiwāʾ* in 57/4, and at the expression *bi-yadi'llāh* ("bounty is in the hand of God") in 57/29. At `002-bakara.md` the same care was kept at the expression *wajhu'llāh* in 2/115: what was recorded there was that the verse does not state **a direction in which God is**.

**The same note holds here too, and is repeated exactly.**

There is also a datum of language that must not be neglected: **in Arabic the word *yad* is already figurative at the level of idiom.** *Bi-yadi fulān* means "in his hand, at his disposal, within his authority", and it is used in that sense in everyday speech. English is the same: "the matter is in your hands", "my hands are tied", "to have the upper hand". None of these expressions states an organ.

### The classical explanations

**The disagreement is not concealed. In the classical commentaries the following are the principal explanations worked out for this construction. I transmit them; I do not impose a preference.**

| Explanation | What it says | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Power** | "Hand" is in the sense of disposal and power: God's power is above their power | The settled idiomatic sense of *yad* in Arabic; the withholding of hands in the same sūra at al-Fatḥ 48/24 |
| **The firmness of the contract** | The pledge is made by a clasping of hands; the expression states who the real party to the contract is, and therefore how firm the contract is | That the sentence comes immediately after the expression of the pledge |
| **Favour / benefaction** | "Hand" is in the sense of a bounty conferred: it is He who gave them the opportunity of this pledge | The use of *yad* in Arabic in the sense of "kindness, favour" (*lahū ʿalayya yadun*) |
| **Help and protection** | The hand stands over those who pledge as a protection | The sūra's context of sakīna and help |
| **Tafwīḍ** | The wording is accepted as it stands; the manner of it is committed to God and is not unfolded | Stopping at the boundary of the text; the stance ascribed to the majority of the early generations |
| **Taʾwīl** | The expression is interpreted figuratively as one of the senses above | The boundary set by al-Shūrā 42/11 |

**The stance in this commentary is this**, and it is the same as the stance at `057-hadid.md`: **no theological partisanship is adopted.** The debate between *tafwīḍ* and *taʾwīl* is one of the oldest theological divisions in exegesis, and it is not a question to be settled here.

**What needs to be recorded:**

**One.** All sides meet at the boundary set by al-Shūrā 42/11: whatever is understood, no likeness to created things may be drawn.

**Two.** The context narrows what the question is. The sentence comes immediately after a sentence about a **contract**, and what follows speaks of **the breaking of the contract**. Where the expression stands, that is, the subject is not the description of a limb but a table of **the parties to an undertaking**.

**Three.** The word *فَوْق* (above) is likewise open to non-spatial uses in Arabic: *"He is the absolute master over His servants"* (al-Anʿām 6/18, 61 — *qāhiru fawqa ʿibādih*). Being above may state a degree and not a position.

### فَمَن نَّكَثَ — root: ن-ك-ث

**The concrete sense of the root: to undo a spun and twisted thread, to unravel it.** In the dictionaries *nikth* is used of wool or hair that has been unravelled: spun, and then picked apart again.

The word for "breaking a covenant", then, carries an image of **spinning** inside the language. To give one's word is to spin a thread; to break it is to unpick the thread that was spun.

**And this image is worth recording:** the unpicked thread does not go back to what it was before. Its crimp remains, its strength is gone. An undertaking that has been broken is not the same as an undertaking never given — it leaves something behind.

The Qurʾān uses the root in the context of covenants: *"If they **break** their oaths after their treaty and revile your religion…"* (al-Tawba 9/12).

### فَإِنَّمَا يَنكُثُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِۦ

**عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ** — "against himself". The particle *ʿalā* here states the direction of the harm.

The logic of the sentence is this: if the other party to the contract is God, the violation can do that other party no harm. The harm stays with the one who commits it and nowhere else.

**This is a recurring pattern in the Qurʾān**, and its counterparts were treated at `059-hasr.md` and `002-bakara.md`: *"Whoever gives thanks gives thanks for his own sake"* (Luqmān 31/12); *"Whoever does a good deed, it is for himself; and whoever does evil, it is against himself"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/46; al-Jāthiya 45/15).

**I record this as my own reading:** the practical meaning of this pattern is that it changes the **mechanism of enforcement** of an undertaking. If the binding force of a promise depends on whether the other party keeps watch over it, then for anyone able to escape that watch the promise was never binding at all. The verse cuts the binding force loose from being watched and sets it somewhere else.

### وَمَنْ أَوْفَىٰ بِمَا عَٰهَدَ عَلَيْهُ ٱللَّهَ

**أَوْفَىٰ** — root **و-ف-ي**: to give a thing complete and in full. *Wafāʾ* — completing. *Īfāʾ* — payment. *Wafāt* — the completing of a lifetime (that this word is used for death is a naming of life as a "completion" rather than a "diminution").

In Form IV (*awfā*) the sense is reinforced: **to fulfil exactly and in full.**

**عَاهَدَ** — root **ع-ه-د**: to watch over a thing, to turn back and look at it, to keep it. *ʿAhd* — a word given, a treaty; but in the root there is the idea of **continual watching over**. A covenant, that is, is not a thing said once and finished but a thing that has to be watched over continually. From the same root *muʿāhada* (a mutual covenanting — the *mufāʿala* form again) and *maʿhūd* (known, kept in view).

**A note on the reading.** It is recorded that in the transmission of Ḥafṣ the pronoun *عَلَيْهُ* in this verse is read with **ḍamma**; it is one of the few places in the Qurʾān where this pronoun is read in that way. **It does not change the meaning;** I record it as a feature of the reading.

### The close of the block: أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

The block closes with "a great reward". And the sūra too will close with the same expression (29).

**The link between the two closings is striking:** in the tenth verse the reward is promised to **the one who keeps his word**; in the twenty-ninth to **the one who believes and does good works**. The sūra closes with works the ledger it opened with an undertaking.

## 48/11 — سَيَقُولُ لَكَ ٱلْمُخَلَّفُونَ مِنَ ٱلْأَعْرَابِ شَغَلَتْنَآ أَمْوَٰلُنَا وَأَهْلُونَا فَٱسْتَغْفِرْ لَنَا ۚ يَقُولُونَ بِأَلْسِنَتِهِم مَّا لَيْسَ فِى قُلُوبِهِمْ ۚ قُلْ فَمَن يَمْلِكُ لَكُم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ شَيْـًٔا إِنْ أَرَادَ بِكُمْ ضَرًّا أَوْ أَرَادَ بِكُمْ نَفْعًۢا ۚ بَلْ كَانَ ٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرًۢا

*Sa-yaqūlu laka'l-mukhallafūna mina'l-aʿrābi shaghalatnā amwālunā wa ahlūnā fa'staghfir lanā; yaqūlūna bi-alsinatihim mā laysa fī qulūbihim; qul fa-man yamliku lakum mina'llāhi shayʾan in arāda bikum ḍarran aw arāda bikum nafʿā; bal kāna'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna khabīrā*

"Those of the bedouin who were left behind will say to you: 'Our possessions and our families kept us occupied; ask forgiveness for us.' They say with their tongues what is not in their hearts. Say: Who then has any power for you against God, if He wills you harm or wills you benefit? No — God is aware of what you do."

### سَيَقُولُ — said before it is said

The verb comes with the prefix **س** (sa-): **the future tense.** "They will say."

The verse, that is, records a sentence that has not yet been said. This sets the tone of the sūra in this block, and it will be repeated in the fifteenth verse: *sa-yaqūlu'l-mukhallafūn*.

**This has two consequences:**

**One.** The excuse is **answered** before it is made. The force of an excuse often comes from the unpreparedness of the moment at which it is first uttered; the verse takes that unpreparedness away.

**Two.** The sentence has the character of a report of the unseen. I record this not as a proof but as a fact at the text's own level: the verse writes a piece of speech in advance, in the future tense.

### ٱلْمُخَلَّفُونَ — "those who were left behind"

**One of the most important word analyses in the sūra is here.**

**Root: خ-ل-ف.** The full analysis of the root was made at `002-bakara.md` (2/30, *khalīfa*) and at `057-hadid.md` (57/7, *mustakhlafīn*): **to come after, to take another's place behind him.** *Khalaf*, *khilāfa*, *ikhtilāf*, *khilāf* are from the same root. I do not repeat it.

**Here the question is not the meaning of the root but the pattern.**

Step by step:

1. **خَلَفَ** (khalafa) — he stayed behind, he came after
2. **خَلَّفَ** (khallafa) — Form II: **he left someone behind.** The form adds transitivity here: the doer of the act is someone else
3. **مُخَلَّف** (mukhallaf) — the **passive participle** of that form: **one who has been left** behind. **Passive.**

**The word does not mean "those who stayed behind"; it means "those who were left behind".**

The difference looks small, but it is not. Most English renderings say "those who stayed behind", and at that moment all the tension of the word is lost. One who *stays behind* has stayed by his own decision; one who is *left behind* has undergone another's act.

**Who, then, left them?** The agent is not named. And its not being named makes the word readable in two directions:

| Reading | Who the agent is | Its meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Divine decree** | God | It was not decreed that they should set out; they were left |
| **Their own stance** | They themselves | They put themselves into the state of being left; the outcome is a passive position |
| **Simple description** | — | The word is a settled term used for those who remain behind an army |

**In the classical commentaries the first and second readings are predominantly worked out together**, and they do not exclude one another.

### Two passive participles: مُسْتَخْلَف and مُخَلَّف

And now I record a contrast that emerges across the index of this commentary.

At `057-hadid.md` the word **مُسْتَخْلَفِين** (*mustakhlafīn*) in verse 57/7 was analysed: from the same root **خ-ل-ف**, the **passive participle** of Form X, that is, "one who **has been made** a successor". That the word was passive was recorded there as "the heart of the matter": a person is not the owner of what is in his hand but one who has been **put in another's place**.

Here there is another word from the same root, **passive** in the same way: **مُخَلَّف** — "one who has been left behind".

| Word | Form | Meaning | Position |
|---|---|---|---|
| **مُسْتَخْلَف** (al-Ḥadīd 57/7) | Form X | **Made** a successor | Brought forward, entrusted |
| **مُخَلَّف** (al-Fatḥ 48/11) | Form II | **Left** behind | Remaining at the back, outside |

**The same root, the same passivity, two opposite positions.** And in neither is the agent named.

**I record this as my own reading, not as a transmitted report:** the root itself already carries an idea of sequence — the one in front and the one who comes after. The two words name the two ends of that sequence. And what they share is their passivity: a person is the subject neither of his being brought forward nor of his being left behind. This is recorded not in order to open a debate about destiny but to show that the grammar of the two words is the same.

### مِنَ ٱلْأَعْرَابِ — "of the bedouin"

**The word ٱلْأَعْرَاب does not mean "the Arabs".** This was recorded in detail at `049-hucurat.md` (49/14):

| Word | Its meaning |
|---|---|
| **عَرَب** (ʿArab) | Arab — the name of a people |
| **أَعْرَاب** (Aʿrāb) | **The bedouin** — communities living the unsettled life of the desert |

*Aʿrāb* is not the plural of *ʿArab*; it is a separate word, and it states **a way of living**. A town-dwelling Arab is *ʿArab* as far as language goes, but he is not *aʿrābī*.

**And the note recorded there holds here exactly as it stands:** the Qurʾān issues no wholesale ruling about this community. Two verses stand side by side in the one sūra:

> *"**The bedouin** are more given to disbelief and hypocrisy…"* (al-Tawba 9/97)
> *"And among **the bedouin** there are also those who believe in God and the Last Day…"* (al-Tawba 9/99)

**In this verse too there is a qualification, and it must not be overlooked:** the verse does not say *"the bedouin"*; it says ***"those of the bedouin who were left behind"***. The ruling, that is, is directed not at a people but at those within that people who show a particular behaviour. The particle *min* here conveys the **partitive** (the separating off of a part).

Per the method (`STYLE.md`), no wholesale ruling is issued here about any community either. What the verse describes is an **attribute**; whoever carries that attribute is included in it — in the desert or in the town.

### شَغَلَتْنَآ أَمْوَٰلُنَا وَأَهْلُونَا — the structure of the excuse

**شَغَلَ** — root **ش-غ-ل**: to occupy someone with a task, to keep them busy, to leave no time for anything else. *Mashghūl*, *ishtighāl*, *mashghūliyya* are from the same root — words that live on as loanwords in Turkish.

**The choice of word shows the structure of the excuse.** The verse does not report an obstacle of the kind "we could not come", "we were not able", "we were not given leave". What is reported is **occupation** — that is, being full of something else.

**And the word order gives the excuse by making it the agent.** The subject of the sentence is *amwālunā wa ahlūnā*: **our possessions and our families kept us occupied.** The speaker, that is, puts himself not as the subject of the verb but as its **object**. He does not say "I was occupied"; he says "they occupied me".

**This is a universal feature of the language of excuses, and I record it as my own reading:** an excuse frames a sentence that makes its speaker passive. And the word itself (*mukhallafūn* — those left behind) is already passive too. There are thus two layers of passivity in the verse: the naming is passive, and so is the excuse.

### Two excuses: possessions and family

What the two things enumerated are matters.

**أَمْوَال** — root **م-و-ل**. *Māl* in Arabic is everything that is owned; it is recorded that the root carries the sense of **inclining** — the thing towards which a person inclines.

**أَهْلُون** — root **أ-ه-ل**: the people of a place or of a thing, its owner, its intimates. *Ahl al-bayt* (the household), *ahliyya* (fitness for a task), *āhil* (married). The plural **أَهْلُون** is a pattern that occurs rarely in the Qurʾān, and means "families".

**That these two words are named together is a pattern in the Qurʾān** — and what is striking is that the same pair occurs both as an **excuse** and as a **trial**:

- *"Your possessions and your children are only a trial"* (al-Taghābun 64/15; al-Anfāl 8/28).
- *"O you who believe! Do not let your possessions and your children divert you from the remembrance of God"* (al-Munāfiqūn 63/9 — there the verb is *tulhikum*: to divert, to occupy and hold back).
- In verse 12 of this sūra the real cause of the excuse will be traced to somewhere else.

**The link with al-Munāfiqūn 63/9 is particularly worth attention**, and it was treated at `063-munafikun.md`: there the "holding back" of a person by these same two things came as a **warning**. Here the same thing is offered as an **excuse**.

What the verse says, then, is true — possessions and family do genuinely occupy a man. **The trouble with the excuse is not that it is false but that it is insufficient.** The Qurʾān has already granted that this occupation exists, and has even warned against it. To offer as an excuse a thing one has been warned about is to void the excuse itself.

### فَٱسْتَغْفِرْ لَنَا — the request for forgiveness

**The structure of the request is striking.**

The analysis of the root of *istighfār* was made at `110-nasr.md`: **غ-ف-ر** — to cover, to close over; *mighfar* (helmet) is from the same root. Forgiveness is not counting what happened as not having happened, but **covering** it.

**The question here is from whom the request is made.** The speakers do not ask forgiveness of God directly; they ask **the Prophet to ask it for them**.

And the verse does not answer this request directly. In place of an answer it delivers a **diagnosis**: *"they say with their tongues what is not in their hearts."*

**We may read it this way** — and I record this as my own reading: the request for forgiveness is the continuation of the excuse. If a person says both "I had an excuse" and "have me forgiven", his two sentences give each other the lie. If the excuse holds, there is nothing to forgive; if forgiveness is needed, the excuse does not hold. The verse does not state this contradiction separately; it only records that the sentence stayed in the tongue and did not come down into the heart.

### يَقُولُونَ بِأَلْسِنَتِهِم مَّا لَيْسَ فِى قُلُوبِهِمْ

**This is the sūra's sentence of diagnosis, and its recurring theme.**

**The subtlety in the word order:** the sentence does not say "they are lying". What it says is finer and more exact: **there is a mismatch between the word and the heart.**

The difference matters. A "lie" is a sentence that does not agree with the world outside it. What is described here is a sentence that does not agree with **its own owner**. What is said may even be objectively true — the possessions may really have occupied them. The fault lies in the speaker's not believing his own sentence.

**بِأَلْسِنَتِهِم — "with their tongues".** *Lisān*, root **ل-س-ن**: tongue (the organ) and tongue (speech). The particle **بِ** here conveys **instrument**: the tongue is the tool with which speech is produced.

**In the Qurʾān there are two variants of this pattern, and the difference between them is worth recording:**

| Verse | The word used | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Fatḥ 48/11** | **بِأَلْسِنَتِهِم** — with their tongues | They say with their **tongues** what is not in their hearts |
| **Āl ʿImrān 3/167** | **بِأَفْوَاهِهِم** — with their mouths | They say with their **mouths** what is not in their hearts |
| **al-Māʾida 5/41** | **بِأَفْوَاهِهِم** | "They said with their mouths, 'We believe'; but their hearts did not believe" |

The *mouth* is further out — the opening the sound comes through. The *tongue* is an organ, and it is the thing that **shapes** the speech. I record this as an observation; I do not claim that there is a settled difference of meaning between the two words. But this much is plain: in both patterns **the organ of speech is set over against the heart.**

### The sūra's recurring theme

This distinction is set up in more than one place in the sūra, and it is useful to see it in a table:

| Verse | What is on the tongue | What is in the heart |
|---|---|---|
| **11** | "Our possessions kept us back; ask forgiveness for us" | Something else |
| **12** | (Unspoken) | *"They will never return"* — an evil thought |
| **15** | "Let us follow you" | The expectation of spoils |
| **18** | (The word: the pledge) | *"He knew what was in their hearts"* — **in agreement** |
| **26** | (Unspoken) | *Ḥamiyya* — hot fury |

**And the sūra's measure lies exactly here.** In the eighteenth verse the good pleasure is tied not to the pledge's having been given but to **what is in the heart being known**: *fa-ʿalima mā fī qulūbihim*. The same outward act — giving one's word — yields two different results in two different places, and what settles the difference is not the part that is visible.

### قُلْ فَمَن يَمْلِكُ لَكُم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ شَيْـًٔا

The answer comes in the form of a question, and it aims directly at what the excuse rests on.

**يَمْلِكُ** — root **م-ل-ك**: to own, to hold the power of disposal over a thing. *Mulk*, *malik*, *mālik*, *mamlūk*.

**The logic of the question is this:** the calculation behind the excuse is the calculation "we would have been safer had we not gone" — possessions kept safe, family kept safe, harm avoided. The verse asks after the foundation of that calculation: **who is it that determines harm and benefit?**

**ضَرًّا … نَفْعًا** — harm and benefit. The two are named as an opposed pair, and they cover every possibility that lies between.

**There is a subtlety in the word order:** the sentence says *"if He wills harm or wills benefit"* — **harm first.** Yet the usual ordering runs from good to bad or the reverse. The order here is arranged to fit the hearer's anxiety: the calculation of those who stayed behind is a calculation of avoiding harm, and the verse names that first.

**And a note:** the verse does not condemn the love of possessions and family, and does not reproach the wish to be safe. All it asks is **in whose hand it lies**. This is the same as the question that runs the length of the sūra at `057-hadid.md`: "is what is in your hand yours?"

### بَلْ كَانَ ٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرًا

**بَلْ** — in Arabic this is a particle of *iḍrāb*: it cancels the preceding sentence and puts something else in its place. "No, it is not so; the real matter is this."

**خَبِير** — root **خ-ب-ر**. The lexicographers dwell on the concrete sense of the root: *khabara'l-arḍa* — to plough the ground, to break it open, to turn what is beneath to the top. From the same root **خَبَار** — soft ground, hollow underneath.

**From this the meaning of the word is born:** *khabīr* is one who knows **the inside** of a thing. Not one who sees from outside, but one who has ploughed the ground and seen what lies under it.

This is the most fitting name to be placed at the end of a verse that recounts a matter of the heart. The verse has recounted the difference between tongue and heart; at the close it names the attribute that can see that difference.

The difference between *ʿAlīm* and *Khabīr* is recorded in the classical sources in this way: *ʿilm* is general, whereas *khibra* is the knowledge of **what is hidden**. I transmit this distinction as a tendency; there is no firm agreement among the lexicographers.

## 48/12 — بَلْ ظَنَنتُمْ أَن لَّن يَنقَلِبَ ٱلرَّسُولُ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِيهِمْ أَبَدًا وَزُيِّنَ ذَٰلِكَ فِى قُلُوبِكُمْ وَظَنَنتُمْ ظَنَّ ٱلسَّوْءِ وَكُنتُمْ قَوْمًۢا بُورًا

*Bal ẓanantum an lan yanqaliba'r-rasūlu wa'l-muʾminūna ilā ahlīhim abadan wa zuyyina dhālika fī qulūbikum wa ẓanantum ẓanna's-sawʾ wa kuntum qawman būrā*

"No! You thought that the Messenger and the believers would never return to their families, and that was made to seem fair in your hearts. You thought an evil thought, and you became a people gone to ruin."

### The shift in the direction of address — iltifāt

**Those named as "they" in the preceding verse suddenly become "you" here.**

*Sa-yaqūlu laka'l-mukhallafūn* (11) → *bal **ẓanantum*** (12).

In Arabic rhetoric this is called **iltifāt** (a shift in the direction of address). And its function here is plain: the matter stops being the passing on of information and becomes **a direct confrontation**.

**The word order takes this one step further.** In the eleventh verse the words they will say were reported; here what they do not say is said. The order, then, is this:

1. The sentence they will say (11)
2. That the sentence has no counterpart in their hearts (11)
3. **What** is in their hearts (12)

In three steps the text opens up what lies behind the excuse.

### أَن لَّن يَنقَلِبَ — the real calculation

**The content of the evil thought is given here**, and the expression *ẓanna's-sawʾ* that occurred in the sixth verse is thereby defined.

The calculation is this: **"These people are never coming back."** That is: those who went will be finished, the matter will be over, standing on that side will be a loss.

**This is not a question of belief but a calculation of risk.** And for precisely that reason it is worth recording: the verse describes the staying behind not as laziness or cowardice but as a **forecast**. What is wrong is not the attitude but the prediction.

**لَنْ** — in Arabic this is the particle of **absolute negation** directed at the future: "will never…". And after it comes **أَبَدًا** (ever). Two reinforcements one upon the other.

**I record this as my own reading:** these two reinforcements show how certain the prediction was taken to be. The speakers are not weighing probabilities; they take themselves to know the outcome. And the sūra's central sentence (27: *"He knew what you did not know"*) stands directly opposite this stance. On one side those who do not know that they do not know; on the other, the one who knows.

### يَنقَلِبَ — root: ق-ل-ب

**This word sets up one of the most striking root connections in the sūra.**

**The primary sense of the root: to turn a thing over, to exchange its top for its bottom, to turn it round.**

- **قَلْب** (qalb) — **the heart.** The organ is named from this root: the thing that *turns without ceasing, that passes from state to state*.
- **اِنْقِلَاب** (inqilāb) — a turning, a turning over; and from this, "revolution".
- **تَقَلُّب** (taqallub) — passing from one state into another.
- **قَالَب** (qālib) — a mould: the thing that takes, and gives, the shape of whatever is poured into it; it lives on in Turkish as *kalıp*.

**"Heart", then, and "to turn" are from the same root.** The heart is given this name because it is **not fixed**.

**And now look at the verse.** There are two words in the one verse: *yanqaliba* (will return) and *qulūbikum* (your hearts). The same root, two words, one sentence.

**I record this as my own reading, not as a transmitted report:** the verse speaks of the **turning place inside** those who thought "they will not return". On one side a physical return is denied; on the other, something is turning in the heart. This is a weave of sound and root; I do not offer it as a claim of miracle, but it stands in the text.

The Qurʾān uses this root in exactly this sense elsewhere too: *"…We turn their hearts and their eyes about"* (al-Anʿām 6/110 — *nuqallibu afʾidatahum*).

### وَزُيِّنَ ذَٰلِكَ فِى قُلُوبِكُمْ — "it was made to seem fair in your hearts"

**زَيَّنَ** — root **ز-ي-ن**: to adorn, to make a thing look fine. *Zīna*, *tazyīn*, *muzayyan*.

**The verb comes in the passive:** *zuyyina* — "it was made to seem fair"; the one who adorned it is not named.

**This is a consistent usage in the Qurʾān.** Where an evil thing's looking fine is recounted, the agent is most often either left unnamed or given differently in different places:

- *"Satan made what they were doing seem fair to them"* (al-Anʿām 6/43; al-Anfāl 8/48; al-Naml 27/24).
- *"He whose evil deed has been made to seem fair to him, so that he sees it as good…"* (Fāṭir 35/8 — passive here too: *zuyyina lahū*).
- *"The love of desires — for women, for sons… — has been made to seem fair to people"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/14 — again passive).

**That the agent is not named is meaningful.** The work of adorning is done sometimes by a suggestion from outside, sometimes by a person's own desire; the two cannot always be told apart. Leaving the verb in the passive preserves that uncertainty.

**The real work of the word here is this:** the evil thought was adopted not because it looked bad but **because it looked good**. Nobody takes his own decision under the description "wrong but easy"; he takes it under the description "right and sensible". In a single word the verse gives the psychological mechanism of an excuse.

### وَكُنتُمْ قَوْمًۢا بُورًا — root: ب-و-ر

**بُور** — the sense of the root: **to spoil, to become useless, to come to nothing.**

The concrete use of the root is agricultural: **أَرْضٌ بَائِرَة** — unsown, untilled ground, left unproductive. The word *bâyir* (uncultivated land), borrowed into Turkish from Arabic, is from this root.

The word **بُور** is used here not as a singular adjective but as a **verbal noun**; this is why it can qualify a whole people in the plural. Its meaning: "brought to ruin", "come to nothing", "unproductive".

**The root's other uses in the Qurʾān:**

- *"…and brought their people down to **the abode of ruin**"* (Ibrāhīm 14/28 — *dāra'l-bawār*).
- *"…they hope for a commerce that **will never fail**"* (Fāṭir 35/29 — *tijāratan lan tabūr*).

**The link with Fāṭir 35/29 is striking.** There the same root occurs in the context of **commerce**, and under a **negation**: "a trade that will not fail". In this sūra, two verses earlier, the pledge was described as a trade (10). Now the word used of those who did not enter that trade is the word meaning "come to nothing".

I record this as a verbal link; I do not claim that there is a deliberate allusion between the two verses.

**And the agricultural image links up with the end of the sūra:** in the twenty-ninth verse the community will be described as a **crop** — a crop that puts out its shoot, thickens, and stands upright on its stalk. Here the word names **unsown ground**. Two extremes: ground that yields and ground left empty.

## 48/13 — وَمَن لَّمْ يُؤْمِنۢ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ فَإِنَّآ أَعْتَدْنَا لِلْكَٰفِرِينَ سَعِيرًا

*Wa man lam yuʾmin bi'llāhi wa rasūlihī fa-innā aʿtadnā li'l-kāfirīna saʿīrā*

"And whoever does not believe in God and His Messenger — We have prepared for the disbelievers a blazing fire."

### The subtlety in the structure of the sentence

The verse opens with a conditional clause: *"Whoever does not believe…"* And in the answering clause, in place of the expected pronoun, comes an **explicit noun**: *"…We have prepared a fire for **the disbelievers**."*

The sentence does not say *"We have prepared a fire for him"*; it says *"for the disbelievers"*.

**In Arabic rhetoric this is called putting an explicit noun in place of a pronoun, and it does two things:**

**One. It states the ground of the ruling.** The punishment is attached to a person not because of who he is but because of **the attribute he carries**. The reason has been set inside the sentence.

**Two. It leaves the door open.** The sentence does not say "to these people", it says "to those who carry this attribute". If the attribute changes, the ruling changes too.

**This is one of the clearest examples in the text of the distinction between name and attribute set out in `STYLE.md`.** The verse does not place the group named in the eleventh verse directly inside this ruling; it describes an attribute and attaches the ruling to that. Indeed, two verses later **a new call** will be made to the same group (16) — which is the proof that the ruling is attached to the attribute and not to persons.

### سَعِير — root: س-ع-ر

**The sense of the root: to make blaze, to kindle, to make hot.** *Saʿara'n-nāra* — he made the fire blaze up.

**سَعِير** — the *faʿīl* pattern: a fire made to blaze, a fire in flames.

**An interesting word from the same root:** **سِعْر** (*siʿr*) — price. In Turkish it is one of the Arabic-derived words used for "price". The link the lexicographers give: prices too **grow hot**, rise, flare up. The same image is at work today when a market is said to be heating up.

This word occurs in sūras that carry descriptions of Hell, such as `067-mulk.md` and `074-muddessir.md`; the detailed analysis of the root belongs there.

### أَعْتَدْنَا — "we have prepared"

**Root: ع-ت-د.** *ʿAtīd* — ready, made ready. The Qurʾān uses it: *"beside him is a watcher ready at hand"* (Qāf 50/18 — *raqībun ʿatīd*).

The verb is in the **perfect**: prepared, beforehand. This answers to the verb *aʿadda* in the sixth verse — two verses saying the same thing with two different roots: the recompense is not a reaction of the moment but an order already established.

## 48/14 — وَلِلَّهِ مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ يَغْفِرُ لِمَن يَشَآءُ وَيُعَذِّبُ مَن يَشَآءُ ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا

*Wa li'llāhi mulku's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ; yaghfiru li-man yashāʾu wa yuʿadhdhibu man yashāʾ; wa kāna'llāhu ghafūran raḥīmā*

"To God belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills. God is forgiving, merciful."

### The place of the sentence in the sūra

**This sentence is a variant of the sentence in the fourth and seventh verses:**

| Verse | Sentence |
|---|---|
| 4 and 7 | *wa li'llāhi **جُنُودُ** 's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — **the hosts** |
| 14 | *wa li'llāhi **مُلْكُ** 's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — **the kingdom** |

In the first block **force** is named, in the second **ownership**. And this suits the subject of each block: in the first block an outcome has been realised, in the second a calculation is under debate. Force settles an outcome; ownership settles a calculation.

The analysis of the root **مُلْك** was made at `067-mulk.md`, and it was treated the length of the sūra at `057-hadid.md`. I do not repeat it.

### يَغْفِرُ لِمَن يَشَآءُ وَيُعَذِّبُ مَن يَشَآءُ — the subtlety in the word order

**There is a difference of particle between the two verbs, and it should not be missed:**

- *yaghfiru **لِ**man yashāʾ* — the verb of forgiving comes with the particle **لِ** (for)
- *yuʿadhdhibu man yashāʾ* — the verb of punishing takes its object **directly**, with no particle

**In Arabic the verb *ghafara* is generally used with *li-***; this can be explained as a technical difference of usage. But the particle has a meaning as well: **لِ** conveys benefit — "He covers **in his favour**".

I record this as an observation and draw no ruling from it; to claim that the difference of particle is a deliberate emphasis here would be to disregard the ordinary usage of the language.

**But this datum of word order is beyond dispute:** the sentence **begins with forgiveness**, goes on with punishment, and **closes with two names of forgiveness**: *ghafūrun raḥīm*.

Although it states a ruling running in two directions, that is, the verse is tied at both its ends to the side of mercy. This is not a balancing but a **distribution of weight**.

### وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ غَفُورًا رَّحِيمًا — the close of the block

**The block 11-14 begins with the rejection of an excuse and ends with a proclamation of forgiveness.**

This order is striking, and I record it as my own reading. What is said through the block is heavy: the invalidity of the excuse, the evil thought in the heart, "a people gone to ruin", the fire. And at the close two names come: *ghafūr* and *raḥīm*.

**The text does not shut the door.** And in the next block this openness will take concrete form: a new call will be made to the same group (16), and the excuse of those who genuinely have one will be accepted (17).

**غَفُور** — the *faʿūl* pattern, an intensive form: one who covers much.
**رَحِيم** — root ر-ح-م: the same root as *raḥim* (the womb). The analysis of the root was made at `001-fatiha.md`.

## 48/15 — سَيَقُولُ ٱلْمُخَلَّفُونَ إِذَا ٱنطَلَقْتُمْ إِلَىٰ مَغَانِمَ لِتَأْخُذُوهَا ذَرُونَا نَتَّبِعْكُمْ ۖ يُرِيدُونَ أَن يُبَدِّلُوا۟ كَلَٰمَ ٱللَّهِ ۚ قُل لَّن تَتَّبِعُونَا كَذَٰلِكُمْ قَالَ ٱللَّهُ مِن قَبْلُ ۖ فَسَيَقُولُونَ بَلْ تَحْسُدُونَنَا ۚ بَلْ كَانُوا۟ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

*Sa-yaqūlu'l-mukhallafūna idha'nṭalaqtum ilā maghānima li-taʾkhudhūhā dharūnā nattabiʿkum; yurīdūna an yubaddilū kalāma'llāh; qul lan tattabiʿūnā kadhālikum qāla'llāhu min qabl; fa-sa-yaqūlūna bal taḥsudūnanā; bal kānū lā yafqahūna illā qalīlā*

"When you set out to take spoils, those who were left behind will say: 'Let us follow you.' They wish to change the word of God. Say: You shall never follow us; God said so before. Then they will say: 'No, you are envious of us.' No! They understand very little."

### The second "they will say"

There was a *sa-yaqūlu* in the eleventh verse; here comes the second. **And this time the qualifier has been cut short:** there it said *al-mukhallafūna **mina'l-aʿrāb***, here only *al-mukhallafūn*.

This is not an abbreviation but a **settling**: the word is no longer a description but has become a name. The text stated whom it meant at the first occurrence, and at the second it sees no need to state it.

### The change of scene

**What needs to be noticed is the difference in time between the two utterances.**

- **In verse 11** the words spoken are an excuse offered **before setting out**.
- **In verse 15** the words spoken are a request made **when the spoils are about to be shared out**.

**The same people, that is, frame two different sentences at two different moments.** And the difference between them is a further proof of what the stance is: at the moment of risk an excuse, at the moment of gain a wish to take part.

**I record this as my own reading:** the force of the verse comes not from a moral accusation but from **setting the two sentences side by side**. The text delivers no verdict; it places the two sentences one after the other and leaves the verdict to the reader. This is a method the Qurʾān often uses.

### مَغَانِم — root: غ-ن-م

**The concrete sense of the root is surprising: غَنَم (*ghanam*) — sheep, small livestock.**

From this **غَنِيمَة** (*ghanīma*) is born: what is obtained, what falls into one's hands. The link the lexicographers record is this: in a nomadic life the typical form of what falls into one's hands is a **flock**; the word was generalised from there.

**The word has a second aspect, and it must be recorded:** in Arabic *ghunm* also carries the sense "obtained without payment or unexpectedly". Indeed the maxim well known in legal theory, *"al-ghunmu bi'l-ghurm"* (gain is the counterpart of risk), uses the word in exactly this sense: the one who is entitled to the gain is the one who bears the risk.

**And the logic of the verse is exactly this.** Those who did not share the risk want to share the gain. The verse does not say this in a moral idiom; it merely records who was standing where.

**The word occurs three times in this sūra:** 15, 19, 20. The sūra, that is, takes up the question of spoils openly, and says two different things to two different groups.

### ذَرُونَا نَتَّبِعْكُمْ — the language of the request

**ذَرُوا۟** — "let us". The word has an interesting side to it: in Arabic the perfect of this verb is not used; only the imperative and the imperfect are. This is a feature of usage recorded by the lexicographers.

**نَتَّبِعْكُمْ** — root **ت-ب-ع**: to go after, to follow. *Tābiʿ*, *ittibāʿ*, *tabaʿa*.

**The subtlety in the language of the request:** it is not "let us come too" but "**let us follow you**". The speakers, that is, put themselves from the outset in the position of followers; they are not claiming a right, they are asking a leave.

**And the answer turns that very word back on them:** *lan tattabiʿūnā* — "you shall never follow us." The same verb, negated and reinforced with the particle of certainty.

### يُرِيدُونَ أَن يُبَدِّلُوا۟ كَلَٰمَ ٱللَّهِ — the sūra's key connection

**Root: ب-د-ل** — to put something else in the place of a thing, to change it. *Badal*, *tabdīl*, *istibdāl*, *mubādala*.

**And this root occurs twice in the sūra. The second occurrence is at verse 23:**

| Verse | Expression | Who does it |
|---|---|---|
| **15** | *yurīdūna an **yubaddilū** kalāma'llāh* | People — they wish to |
| **23** | *wa lan tajida li-sunnati'llāhi **تَبْدِيلًا*** | No one — you will not find it |

**The two verses answer one another.** In the fifteenth a group **wishes** to change the word of God; the twenty-third states that no change **can be found** in God's way of working.

**I record this link as my own reading**, but the shared root in the two verses is a datum that can be verified, and that the sūra should use the same word eight verses apart with two different subjects is worth attention.

### What does "the word of God" mean?

In the classical commentaries what the expression **كَلَٰمَ ٱللَّهِ** points to here is debated, and the disagreement must be recorded:

| View | What it points to |
|---|---|
| **An earlier ruling** | A ruling given that the spoils of this expedition belong only to those who took part |
| **The promise in verses 19-20** | That the spoils had been promised to those who pledged |
| **A general principle** | The principle that gain depends on participation |

**The text does not explain this**, and I impose no preference either. The verse says only *kadhālikum qāla'llāhu min qabl* — "God said so before". What was said, and when, is not stated.

### بَلْ تَحْسُدُونَنَا — the third sentence

**The verse gives the exchange in three stages**, and this is one of the Qurʾān's narrative techniques:

1. The request: *"Let us follow you"*
2. The answer: *"You shall never follow"*
3. **The counter-accusation**: *"No, you are envious of us"*

**The third stage is the most striking.** Met with a refusal, the other side does not dispute the ground of it; it disputes the **motive**. It does not say "this decision is wrong", it says "you took this decision out of envy".

**حَسَد** — root **ح-س-د**: to wish that a blessing another has should perish. The lexicographers dwell on the concrete sense of the root: some connect the word with a worm that gnaws the heartwood of a tree. I do not offer this as a settled etymology.

**I record this as my own reading:** this three-stage structure shows how an argument changes direction. When a decision is refused, it is hard to refute the ground of the decision; it is easy to accuse the one who made it. The verse records that shift and gives no answer — because an accusation has no addressee; an accusation is not a ground.

### بَلْ كَانُوا۟ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا

**فقه** — root **ف-ق-ه**: to understand a thing **by penetrating into it**. The lexicographers tie the root to the idea of "splitting, opening the inside of". This is why *fiqh* is not merely knowledge but **grasp**: reaching the logic behind the information.

**The choice of word is meaningful.** The verse does not say "they do not know" (*lā yaʿlamūn*). There is something they do know: they know the spoils will be shared out, they know how the process works. What is missing is **grasp** — what all this means.

**إِلَّا قَلِيلًا — "except a little".** And this qualification must not be overlooked.

The verse does not make an absolute negation; it sets an **exception**. It does not say "they understand nothing", it says "they understand little". This is, within the text itself, the counterpart of the prohibition on wholesale rulings set out in `STYLE.md`: the Qurʾān itself limits the negative ruling it has just framed.

**There are two readings of what the exception attaches to:**

| Reading | Its meaning |
|---|---|
| **An exception of quantity** | They have understanding, but it is little |
| **An exception of persons** | A few among them do understand |

Both readings are found in the classical commentaries; I impose no preference. Both arrive at the same result: the ruling is not framed against the whole body.

## 48/16 — قُل لِّلْمُخَلَّفِينَ مِنَ ٱلْأَعْرَابِ سَتُدْعَوْنَ إِلَىٰ قَوْمٍ أُو۟لِى بَأْسٍ شَدِيدٍ تُقَٰتِلُونَهُمْ أَوْ يُسْلِمُونَ ۖ فَإِن تُطِيعُوا۟ يُؤْتِكُمُ ٱللَّهُ أَجْرًا حَسَنًا ۖ وَإِن تَتَوَلَّوْا۟ كَمَا تَوَلَّيْتُم مِّن قَبْلُ يُعَذِّبْكُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

*Qul li'l-mukhallafīna mina'l-aʿrābi sa-tudʿawna ilā qawmin ulī baʾsin shadīdin tuqātilūnahum aw yuslimūn; fa-in tuṭīʿū yuʾtikumu'llāhu ajran ḥasanā; wa in tatawallaw kamā tawallaytum min qablu yuʿadhdhibkum ʿadhāban alīmā*

"Say to those of the bedouin who were left behind: You will be called against a people of great might in war; you will fight them, or they will submit. If you obey, God will give you a good reward. But if you turn away as you turned away before, He will punish you with a painful punishment."

### The opening of the door

**This verse is the place where the moral balance of the sūra is established, and it must be read carefully.**

In the preceding verse a request was refused, and the refusal was absolute: *lan tattabiʿūnā* — "never". Now, immediately afterwards, **a new call** comes.

What was refused, that is, was not the **persons** but **that particular request to take part**. The text does not extend a past choice into the future.

**This is the practical counterpart of the distinction between name and attribute recorded at verse 13.** And it is the textual support for the principle in `STYLE.md` that no wholesale ruling is issued about any group: the Qurʾān itself attaches the ruling to a behaviour rather than to persons, and because behaviour can change, the ruling stays open to change too.

### سَتُدْعَوْنَ — "you will be called"

The verb is **passive** and in the **future**: "you will be summoned". Who will call is not said.

**And this sets up a symmetry with the word.** The name of this group is *mukhallafūn* — "those who were **left**", passive. Now the opportunity given them comes in a passive verb too: "you **will be called**". The same passivity, this time in their favour.

I record this as an observation about the word order.

### قَوْمٍ أُو۟لِى بَأْسٍ شَدِيدٍ — who they are

**بَأْس** — root **ب-أ-س**: violence, hardship, difficulty; and might in war. From the same root *baʾsāʾ* (hardship), *bāʾis* (severe), and **also**: *lā baʾsa* — "no harm done" (a common usage in Arabic).

*Ulī baʾsin shadīd* — "those possessed of a fierce might".

**Who this people are is not stated in the Qurʾān. In classical exegesis there is more than one view, and the disagreement must not be concealed:**

| View | Who |
|---|---|
| Those who would take part in a later expedition | A particular tribe |
| Great struggles that came about later | More distant periods |
| It is deliberately left indefinite | The verse gives no name on purpose |

**Various names are mentioned in the classical commentaries; I do not give these names**, because the reports differ from one another and none of them is certain. Per the method (`STYLE.md`) I do not write an ascription of which I am not sure.

**What the text says amounts to this:** a situation will come in which a fierce might is met, and this group will be called at that time. Neither its name nor its date is given.

### تُقَٰتِلُونَهُمْ أَوْ يُسْلِمُونَ

**Two possibilities are set out, and both are given as outcomes:** either there is fighting, or the other side submits.

**أَوْ** — "or". This particle **separates** possibilities; it commands neither of them. The verse, that is, does not frame a command to fight; it describes a situation, and in that situation participation is asked.

**يُسْلِمُونَ** — root **س-ل-م**: to be sound, to be without flaw, to submit. *Islām*, *salām*, *salāma*, *muslim* are from the same root. At the centre of the root lie **wholeness and submission**.

**A note:** the verse does not say which of the two possibilities is preferred. And in view of the sūra as a whole this is striking — for the event the sūra recounts is itself an event in which fighting was **stopped** (24). The sūra names fighting as an option, not as an objective.

### فَإِن تُطِيعُوا۟ … وَإِن تَتَوَلَّوْا۟

**Two conditions, two outcomes.** The structure is symmetrical.

**أَطَاعَ** — root **ط-و-ع**: to do something willingly, without being forced. The primary sense of the root is **willingness**; the *ṭawʿ* in the expression *ṭawʿan wa karhan* (willingly and unwillingly) is this. *Ṭāʿa*, that is, is at root a word of **consent**, not of compulsion.

**تَوَلَّىٰ** — root **و-ل-ي**: to be near, to be adjoining; *walī*, *mawlā*, *walāya*. In Form **تَفَعُّل** (*tawallā*) the sense becomes **to turn away, to turn one's back**.

**This is an interesting behaviour of the root, and it must be recorded:** the same root means both "to be near" and "to turn one's back". The link is this: *walāʾ* states a turning towards; and the man who turns his back has also turned in a **direction** — only in another one. The English "to turn away" works on the same logic: the face that is turned does not turn into empty space but towards some other side.

**كَمَا تَوَلَّيْتُم مِّن قَبْلُ** — "as you turned away before". The past is named, but not as a verdict — as a **measure**: if it is repeated, it will have its consequence.

### أَجْرًا حَسَنًا — the change of adjective

The "reward" is named several times in the sūra, and its adjectives differ:

| Verse | Expression | To whom |
|---|---|---|
| **10** | *ajran **عَظِيمًا*** — great | To the one who keeps his pledge |
| **16** | *ajran **حَسَنًا*** — good | To those left behind, if they now take part |
| **29** | *ajran **عَظِيمًا*** — great | To those who believe and do good works |

**The difference of adjective is striking.** The reward promised to those left behind is "good"; the one promised to those who pledged, and to the community of the last verse, is "great".

**I record this as my own reading and do not present it as a settled ruling:** the difference of adjective seems to accord with the measure treated at `057-hadid.md` on verse 57/10 — *"Those of you who spent and fought before the fatḥ are not equal…"*. It was recorded there that there is a **threshold**, and that what is done before that threshold and what is done after it are not of the same weight. The same measure may be at work here. But this must be recorded too: in both verses **a good reward** is promised to the one who joins later; the door has not been shut.

## 48/17 — لَّيْسَ عَلَى ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ حَرَجٌ وَلَا عَلَى ٱلْأَعْرَجِ حَرَجٌ وَلَا عَلَى ٱلْمَرِيضِ حَرَجٌ ۗ وَمَن يُطِعِ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥ يُدْخِلْهُ جَنَّٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ ۖ وَمَن يَتَوَلَّ يُعَذِّبْهُ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

*Laysa ʿalā'l-aʿmā ḥarajun wa lā ʿalā'l-aʿraji ḥarajun wa lā ʿalā'l-marīḍi ḥaraj; wa man yuṭiʿi'llāha wa rasūlahū yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtihā'l-anhār; wa man yatawalla yuʿadhdhibhu ʿadhāban alīmā*

"There is no constraint upon the blind, no constraint upon the lame, no constraint upon the sick. Whoever obeys God and His Messenger, He will bring him into gardens beneath which rivers flow. And whoever turns away, He will punish him with a painful punishment."

### The place of the verse in the block

**This verse completes the moral measure of the block, and its place in the sequence is highly meaningful.**

Look at the order:

| Verse | What is done |
|---|---|
| **11** | A false excuse is reported: "our possessions and our families" |
| **12-14** | The real calculation behind the excuse is opened up |
| **15-16** | The request is refused; a new door is opened |
| **17** | **Genuine excuses are enumerated and accepted** |

**Immediately after refusing the false excuse, that is, the sūra says that the genuine excuse holds.**

**This is one of the most striking moral moves in the text, and I record it as my own reading.** When a text sets about criticising excuses, the natural tendency is to make every excuse suspect — the stance of "everybody finds a pretext". The verse does not do this. Having refused the false one, it takes the genuine one separately and explicitly under protection.

### حَرَج — root: ح-ر-ج

**The concrete sense of the root: narrowness, being pressed tight.** *Ḥaraj* is a thing's being squeezed, growing too tight, becoming impassable.

The concrete use the lexicographers record is more vivid still: **حَرَجَة** — a dense thicket in which the trees press together until it cannot be got through. In the root, that is, there is an image of **impassability**.

**The Qurʾān uses the root in exactly this sense:**

- *"God has laid upon you no **constraint** in religion"* (al-Ḥajj 22/78).
- *"Whomever God wishes to guide, He opens his breast to Islam; and whomever He wishes to let go astray, He makes his breast **narrow and tight** — as though he were climbing up into the sky"* (al-Anʿām 6/125 — *ḍayyiqan ḥarajā*).

**Before it is a legal term, then, *ḥaraj* is a word of space:** being pressed tight, having no room left to move.

And the verse uses it exactly where it belongs. The three people named are people who in fact **cannot move**. To say to them "there is no narrowness" agrees with the word's own sense: no further narrowness is laid upon one who is already hemmed in.

### The three cases

| Word | Root | Case |
|---|---|---|
| **ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ** | ع-م-ي | The blind. From the same root *ʿamā* (blindness), *aʿmā*. The Qurʾān uses the word for blindness of heart as well: *"It is not the eyes that go blind, but the hearts within the breasts"* (al-Ḥajj 22/46) |
| **ٱلْأَعْرَج** | ع-ر-ج | The lame. The root was treated at `057-hadid.md`: *yaʿruju* (to climb up a sloping way), *miʿrāj*, *maʿārij* are from the same root. **In the root there is "movement that is not level"** — lameness and ascent come from the same word |
| **ٱلْمَرِيض** | م-ر-ض | The sick. The Qurʾān uses this word figuratively too: *"in their hearts is a sickness"* (al-Baqara 2/10) |

**What the three words have in common:** all three name **an incapacity, permanent or temporary**, and all three are used in the Qurʾān in both a physical and a spiritual sense. Here the physical sense is meant; there is no disagreement about that.

### On the scope of the verse

**A legal note:** in classical law this verse is counted among the principal proofs on the question of who is released from the obligation to take part in fighting. The same three cases are enumerated at al-Tawba 9/91 as well: *"There is no blame upon the weak, upon the sick, or upon those who find nothing to spend — provided they are sincere towards God and His Messenger."*

**I issue no legal ruling;** the schools differ in the details, and that is a separate subject. What must be recorded here is the verse's **measure**.

### The measure: not an enumeration but a principle

**Are the three cases enumerated in the verse a closed list?**

In classical exegesis the predominant view is that the list is **illustrative**: what is named are the clearest instances of the category "one who in fact cannot". This reading is supported by the sense of the word itself — *ḥaraj* is narrowness, and the causes of narrowness are not limited to three.

**There is also a proof from within the text:** at al-Tawba 9/91, in the same context, a **fourth** case is enumerated (those who find nothing to spend). The lists in the two verses are not identical; this suggests that the lists are not closed.

**I offer this as a reading, not as a legal ruling.**

### The second half of the verse: the measure preserved

Having accepted the excuses, the verse repeats the general ruling: for the one who obeys, the gardens; for the one who turns away, the punishment.

**This repetition is not a severity but a balance.** That an excuse is accepted does not mean the measure is lifted. The verse acknowledges the excuse but leaves the ruling exactly as it stands for those who have none.

**And note: the verb here too is *tawallā*** — the word that occurred in the preceding verse. The measure, that is, does not change; what is stated is only who falls outside it.

## 48/18 — لَّقَدْ رَضِىَ ٱللَّهُ عَنِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ إِذْ يُبَايِعُونَكَ تَحْتَ ٱلشَّجَرَةِ فَعَلِمَ مَا فِى قُلُوبِهِمْ فَأَنزَلَ ٱلسَّكِينَةَ عَلَيْهِمْ وَأَثَٰبَهُمْ فَتْحًا قَرِيبًا

*Laqad raḍiya'llāhu ʿani'l-muʾminīna idh yubāyiʿūnaka taḥta'sh-shajarati fa-ʿalima mā fī qulūbihim fa-anzala's-sakīnata ʿalayhim wa athābahum fatḥan qarībā*

"God has indeed been pleased with the believers when they pledged allegiance to you beneath the tree. He knew what was in their hearts, sent down the sakīna upon them, and gave them a near fatḥ."

### لَّقَدْ — the weight of the oath

The verse opens with **لَ** (the lām of the oath) and **قَدْ** (the particle of verification). The two together make one of the strongest reinforcements possible in Arabic: "assuredly, beyond all doubt, truly".

And this reinforcement is here for a reason. The event the sūra recounts is transmitted as having ended in disappointment for some of those who took part. The verse comes down on top of that disappointment with the heaviest pattern of reinforcement there is: **He was pleased.**

### رَضِىَ — root: ر-ض-ي

**The sense of the root: to be well pleased, to have one's heart settle on a thing, to accept.** *Riḍā*, *riḍwān*, *marḍī*, *tarāḍī* (mutual consent).

This root was treated at `057-hadid.md` (57/20, *riḍwān*) and at `098-beyyine.md`. It was recorded there that **riḍwān** is an intensive verbal noun — a great good pleasure; and that the Qurʾān sets it **above** the description of the Garden, as something higher than it.

**There are two points that belong here.**

**One: the tense of the verb.** *Raḍiya* — the perfect. The good pleasure, that is, had **already come about** at the moment the verse came down. It is not a promise but a declaration.

**Two: the particle.** *Raḍiya **عَنْ*** — "was pleased *with*". When *riḍā* comes with this particle in Arabic it carries the sense of being pleased with the person **himself**, not with the deed.

**The Qurʾān's strongest expression of good pleasure is reciprocal:** *"God is pleased with them, and they are pleased with Him"* (al-Bayyina 98/8; al-Tawba 9/100; al-Māʾida 5/119). In this verse there is no reciprocity; it runs one way. I record this as an observation.

**A note on a name.** In the sources the pledge beneath the tree is called **Bayʿat al-Riḍwān**. This name is derived directly from this verse; the name of the event, that is, comes not from the event itself but from the verse that came down about it. This is like a fact recorded at `110-nasr.md`: there too one of a sūra's names arose not from its content but from the meaning laid upon it.

### إِذْ يُبَايِعُونَكَ — the past, in the present tense

**There is a fine point in the word order.**

**إِذْ** is an adverb of past time: "when…". What one expects after it is a verb in the perfect. But the verb that comes is in the **imperfect**: *yubāyiʿūnaka* — "while they are pledging", "as they pledge".

In Arabic this is a technique for **bringing an event before the eye**: a past scene is made living with the present tense. It is exactly what we do in English when we say "and that day he arrives there and sees this".

**The verse, that is, is not recounting the event; it is rebuilding the scene.** And at the centre of the scene there is a clasping of hands.

### تَحْتَ ٱلشَّجَرَةِ — beneath the tree

**شَجَرَة** — root **ش-ج-ر**: tree. The root has an interesting aspect that must be recorded: the same root carries the sense of **interlacing, becoming entangled, branching**. *Tashājara* — they became entangled with one another, they quarrelled. The Qurʾān uses this: *"until they make you judge in what has become entangled between them…"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/65 — *shajara baynahum*).

A tree is called *shajara*, then, because **its branches interlace**. And the same word is used of a tangled dispute between people.

**The Qurʾān does not say what kind of tree it was. Nor does it say where.** Only "al-shajara" — a particular tree, but undefined.

**The layer of reports, and a note.** It is transmitted in the sources that in later years the Companions differed as to which tree it was, and that its place could not be determined with certainty. It is also reported that ʿUmar had it cut down so that it should not turn into a place of visitation and veneration. The details of these reports vary; **I do not present them as certain knowledge.**

**But the Qurʾān's own stance must be recorded, and that stance is plain:** the verse **does not identify** the tree. It gives no place name, no species, no mark. What is of worth in the event is not the tree but what happened beneath it.

**I record this as my own reading:** this silence of the text appears deliberate. We have already recorded that throughout the sūra the Qurʾān refrains from giving names of places and persons (only *Mecca* occurs, 24). This is precisely the moment most open to the making of a sacred tree, a sacred stone, a sacred place of visitation — and at precisely this moment the text leaves no mark at all.

### فَعَلِمَ مَا فِى قُلُوبِهِمْ — the measure itself

**The measure of the sūra is in this sentence.**

**Attend to the word order.** The verse could have been built like this: *"they pledged, and God was pleased."* It is not. A verb comes in between:

```
they were pledging
      ↓
God KNEW what was in their hearts
      ↓
He sent down the sakīna
```

**The good pleasure, that is, is attached not to the pledge itself but to what stood behind the pledge.** The clasping of hands is the visible part; the verse makes the invisible part the measure.

**And this is the exact counterpart of the eleventh verse:**

| Verse | What is visible | What is in the heart | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **11** | "Ask forgiveness for us" | Something else — *"they say what is not in their hearts"* | The excuse does not hold |
| **18** | The pledge — the clasping of hands | The same thing — *"He knew what was in their hearts"* | Good pleasure |

**Two verses apply the same measure in two directions.** And the measure itself does not change anywhere: the act visible from outside does not settle the outcome by itself.

**One more note:** the verse does not say **what** was in their hearts. It says only "He knew". This silence shows that the source of the ruling is not human observation. No one can look into another's heart and apply this measure.

**I record this as my own reading:** this is the most important side of the measure. The principle "the intention in the heart is what matters" turns into its opposite the moment it becomes an instrument of judgement between people, putting everyone in the position of ruling on someone else's intention. The construction of the verse does not permit this: the knowing subject is stated explicitly, and that subject is not a human being.

### فَأَنزَلَ ٱلسَّكِينَةَ عَلَيْهِمْ — the second descent of the sakīna

**The comparison of the sakīna's three occurrences in the sūra will be made at verse 26.** Here I record two points.

**One: the difference of particle.** In the fourth verse it was *fī qulūbi'l-muʾminīn* (**into** their hearts); here it is *ʿalayhim* (**upon** them). The two particles build two different images: the one a filling from within, the other a covering.

**Two: the order.** Here the sakīna comes down **after** the hearts are known. The sequence, that is, is this: there was something within → it was known → it was sent down upon them. In the fourth verse there was no such sequence; there the sakīna came down directly.

### وَأَثَٰبَهُمْ — root: ث-و-ب

**The primary sense of the root: to return, to come back, to revert to what one was.**

And the words born from it are striking:

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **ثَوَاب** (thawāb) | Recompense. In the logic of the root: the part of what one does that **comes back** |
| **ثَوْب** (thawb) | **Garment.** The lexicographers' explanation: thread, by being woven, returns to what it properly is (cloth) |
| **مَثَابَة** (mathāba) | **A place returned to.** The Qurʾān uses this of the Kaʿba: *"We made the House a place of return for people, and a safety"* (al-Baqara 2/125) |

**The link with al-Baqara 2/125 is particularly meaningful in this verse**, and I record it as an observation: the same root names the Kaʿba in one place as "the place returned to"; here it names the recompense given to a body of people who could not reach the Kaʿba and turned back. I do not claim that there is a deliberate allusion between the two verses; the shared root is a datum that can be verified.

**The choice of verb is meaningful too.** *Athāba* — "he recompensed, he made it come back". What is given, that is, is not a gift but a **return**: the coming back of something that was done.

### فَتْحًا قَرِيبًا — the second fatḥ

**The second occurrence of the word *fatḥ* in the sūra.** And note: the fatḥ of the first verse is **mubīn** (manifest), this one is **qarīb** (near).

| Verse | Expression | Adjective | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | *fatḥan mubīnā* | Manifest | Done (verb in the perfect) |
| **18** | *fatḥan qarībā* | **Near** | Given, but near |
| **27** | *fatḥan qarībā* | **Near** | Set in between |

**What the "near fatḥ" is has been debated in classical exegesis. I transmit the disagreement:**

| View | Which event |
|---|---|
| **Khaybar** | The expedition transmitted as having taken place shortly after the treaty; that "many spoils" are named in verse 19 is adduced in support of this view |
| **Mecca** | That the absolute use of the word most often calls this to mind |
| **The treaty itself** | That the fatḥ is the opening itself |

**In classical exegesis the first view is widely transmitted**, because the very next verse speaks of spoils. **But I impose no preference**; the Qurʾān gives no name to the event.

**What must be recorded is this:** the fatḥ of the first verse is "manifest", this one is "near". The two adjectives say different things — the one has to do with **visibility**, the other with **time**. And the adjective *qarīb* states that the opening is not yet complete, that it continues.

The same construction was treated at `061-saff.md`: *fatḥun qarīb* (al-Ṣaff 61/13) occurred there as a **promise**, and it was recorded that "a note of time is being set upon the promise". Here the same construction occurs as a **recompense**.

## 48/19 — وَمَغَانِمَ كَثِيرَةً يَأْخُذُونَهَا ۗ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَزِيزًا حَكِيمًا

*Wa maghānima kathīratan yaʾkhudhūnahā; wa kāna'llāhu ʿazīzan ḥakīmā*

"And many spoils that they will take. God is ʿazīz, ḥakīm."

### The continuation of the preceding verse

The verse is a second object attached to the verb *athābahum* (He recompensed them) in verse 18: **He gave them a near fatḥ and many spoils that they will take.**

**And the choice of word is meaningful.** In the fifteenth verse *maghānim* was the thing those left behind **asked for**; here the same word is what is **given** to those who pledged. The same word stands in two different places four verses apart: in one it is asked for, in the other it is given.

**The verb is in the imperfect:** *yaʾkhudhūnahā* — "that they will take", "that they are taking". Something that has not yet happened is recounted as though it were happening.

### وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَزِيزًا حَكِيمًا

This pair of names comes for the second time in the sūra (7 and 19). And in both places the context is **the carrying out of a decision**: in the seventh verse a punishment, here the realisation of a division of spoils.

**And its place here is particularly meaningful.** To whom spoils are to be given is a question of division, and divisions give rise to dispute — indeed it is exactly this dispute that the sūra recounts (15). The verse closes the division with two names: **ʿAzīz** (whose decision cannot be turned back) and **Ḥakīm** (whose decision is in its proper place).

## 48/20 — وَعَدَكُمُ ٱللَّهُ مَغَانِمَ كَثِيرَةً تَأْخُذُونَهَا فَعَجَّلَ لَكُمْ هَٰذِهِۦ وَكَفَّ أَيْدِىَ ٱلنَّاسِ عَنكُمْ وَلِتَكُونَ ءَايَةً لِّلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَيَهْدِيَكُمْ صِرَٰطًا مُّسْتَقِيمًا

*Waʿadakumu'llāhu maghānima kathīratan taʾkhudhūnahā fa-ʿajjala lakum hādhihī wa kaffa aydiya'n-nāsi ʿankum wa li-takūna āyatan li'l-muʾminīna wa yahdiyakum ṣirāṭan mustaqīmā*

"God has promised you many spoils that you will take. He has hastened this one to you and held back the hands of men from you — so that it may be a sign for the believers, and that He may guide you on a straight path."

### The address changes again

**In the nineteenth verse it was "they":** *yaʾkhudhūna**hā***.
**In the twentieth it is "you":** *taʾkhudhūna**hā***, *waʿada**kum***, *lakum*, *ʿankum*, *yahdiya**kum***.

One verse apart, the text moves from the third person to the second. This is a technique frequent enough in the sūra that one could count the occurrences — **iltifāt**, the shift in the direction of address.

**Its function here is plain:** the preceding verse was a statement; this verse is an **address**. The one to whom the spoils belong is now not the party recounted but the party spoken to.

### فَعَجَّلَ لَكُمْ هَٰذِهِۦ — "He has hastened this one to you"

**عَجَّلَ** — root **ع-ج-ل**: to hurry, to bring forward. *ʿAjala*, *taʿjīl*, *ʿājil*, *ʿājila* (what is at hand, this present life). The Qurʾān uses the pair *ʿājila* and *ākhira*: what is at hand and what comes after (al-Isrāʾ 17/18; al-Qiyāma 75/20).

**هَٰذِهِ** — "this one". A demonstrative, pointing to what is near.

**The verse thus draws a distinction:** many things have been promised; **this** is the one **brought forward** among them. Not the whole of the promise but the part paid in advance.

**The place of this distinction within the sūra matters**, and it will continue in verse 21: a part has been given, a part still stands encircled.

### وَكَفَّ أَيْدِىَ ٱلنَّاسِ عَنكُمْ — "held back their hands from you"

**كَفَّ** — root **ك-ف-ف**: to hold a thing, to keep it back, to draw it back. The concrete sense of the root is the **palm**: **كَفّ** (*kaff*) — the flat of the hand, the palm. From this come "to hold, to grasp, to prevent". From the same root *kifāya* (sufficiency — the hand's being filled), *kaffa* (the pan of a balance — because it is shaped like a palm).

**And this verb occurs twice in the sūra:**

| Verse | Whose hand | From whom |
|---|---|---|
| **20** | *aydiya'n-nās* — **the hands of men** | From you |
| **24** | *aydiyahum* and *aydiyakum* — **their hands and your hands** | From each other |

**In the twentieth verse it runs one way, in the twenty-fourth both ways.** The twentieth verse is a protection; the twenty-fourth is a ceasefire. The sūra uses the same verb first in one party's favour, then reciprocally.

### وَلِتَكُونَ ءَايَةً لِّلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

**ءَايَة** — root **أ-ي-ي**. The sense of the word: **a sign, a mark, a proof.** In the Qurʾān it is used both for "verse" (a unit of the text) and for "sign" (a proof in the created order). The common idea: **that which points to something.**

**The subtlety in the word order of the sentence:** *li-takūna* — feminine. That is, "**it** may be a sign". What it is that will be a sign has been debated: the spoils brought forward, the withholding of hands, or the event as a whole?

All three possibilities are mentioned in the classical commentaries. **I impose no preference.**

**But what the verse does is plain:** the event is named as a **proof**. What happened, then, is not merely an outcome but also a sign that has to be read.

**I record this as my own reading:** this connects to the sūra's central question. Throughout the sūra one and the same event is being read in two different ways by two different readers — some see it as a step backwards, others as a fatḥ. That the verse says "sign" shows that what is at issue is less the event itself than **the reading of it**. A sign is a sign only if there is someone to read it.

### وَيَهْدِيَكُمْ صِرَٰطًا مُّسْتَقِيمًا — the second occurrence

**This expression is word for word the same as the last clause of the second verse** — the only difference is in the pronoun:

| Verse | Expression | Addressee |
|---|---|---|
| **2** | *wa yahdi**يَكَ** ṣirāṭan mustaqīmā* | **Singular** — the Prophet |
| **20** | *wa yahdi**يَكُمْ** ṣirāṭan mustaqīmā* | **Plural** — the believers |

**In terms of the structure of the sūra this is a closing.** In the opening block this was one of the four things given to the singular addressee. Now the same thing is given to the community.

I record this as an observation about the word order: what the sūra says to one person at the beginning it carries over to the community in the middle.

## 48/21 — وَأُخْرَىٰ لَمْ تَقْدِرُوا۟ عَلَيْهَا قَدْ أَحَاطَ ٱللَّهُ بِهَا ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرًا

*Wa ukhrā lam taqdirū ʿalayhā qad aḥāṭa'llāhu bihā; wa kāna'llāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīrā*

"And others, over which you have had no power — God has encompassed them. God is qadīr over all things."

### One root, two ends: ق-د-ر

**The whole force of the verse lies in the repetition of a single root.**

- **لَمْ تَقْدِرُوا۟** — "you had no power" — root **ق-د-ر**
- **قَدِيرًا** — "having power over all things" — the same root

**The same root, in the same verse, with two opposite subjects.** What the sentence says is exactly this: where your *qudra* ends, His *qudra* is.

**The sense of the root:** **to measure, to determine the extent of a thing** — and from this, **to have power over.** *Qadar* (measure, determination), *miqdār*, *taqdīr*, *qadr* (worth, measure). The detailed analysis of the root was made at `097-kadr.md`.

**And this link is striking:** the same root means both "to measure" and "to be able". For to have power over a thing is for it to lie **within your measure**. What exceeds your measure you have no power over.

The verse uses this exactly: *lam taqdirū ʿalayhā* — those things lay **outside your measure**.

### أَحَاطَ — root: ح-و-ط

**The sense of the root: to surround, to enclose, to encompass.**

From the same root:
- **حَائِط** (*ḥāʾiṭ*) — a wall; and a garden (a place walled about). The idea behind the English word *enclosure*.
- **إِحَاطَة** (*iḥāṭa*) — encompassing; and figuratively "a full grasp of a subject".
- **مُحِيط** (*muḥīṭ*) — the one who encompasses. It occurs in the Qurʾān as an attribute of God: *"God encompasses all things"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/126).

**The use of the verb here is exactly right.** The verse does not say "God owns them" or "He will give them to you". It says **"He has encompassed them"**.

**The image is this:** a thing whose surroundings have been closed off can no longer escape. It has not yet been taken, but the ring around it has been shut. **The verse states not the outcome, that is, but the inevitability of the outcome.**

**I record this as my own reading:** this verb does the same work as the perfect tense in the sūra's first verse. There a thing not yet complete was recounted as having happened; here a thing not yet in hand is recounted as "encompassed". Both are expressions of the same stance: that the outcome is not up for debate.

### The place of the verse in the block

The twentieth verse recounts **what has been given in advance**, the twenty-first **what has not yet been given**. The two form a pair:

| Verse | What | State |
|---|---|---|
| **20** | *hādhihī* — "this one" | Brought forward, given |
| **21** | *ukhrā* — "others" | Encompassed, not yet taken |

**And this pair sums up the whole logic of the sūra.** From beginning to end the sūra works on the tension between what is in hand and what is not yet: a mosque that could not be entered, a dream not yet fulfilled, a fatḥ near but not yet come. These two verses set that tension out in its simplest form: part of it in your hand, part of it not — but both under the same hand.

## 48/22 — وَلَوْ قَٰتَلَكُمُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ لَوَلَّوُا۟ ٱلْأَدْبَٰرَ ثُمَّ لَا يَجِدُونَ وَلِيًّا وَلَا نَصِيرًا

*Wa law qātalakumu'lladhīna kafarū la-wallawu'l-adbāra thumma lā yajidūna waliyyan wa lā naṣīrā*

"Had those who disbelieve fought you, they would surely have turned their backs and fled. Then they would have found neither protector nor helper."

### لَوْ — a condition that did not come about

**لَوْ** in Arabic is the particle of **imtināʿ**: it states a condition that did not come about, together with the consequence that therefore did not come about. "Had it been…, it would have been…"

**And this is the most important point of the verse: there was no fighting.**

In the event the sūra recounts, no fighting broke out. The verse sets this down not as a deficiency but as a **datum**, and builds a ruling upon it: had there been fighting, the outcome would have been this.

**This is like the stance recorded at `056-vakia.md` for the pattern *law nashāʾu*:** the verse speaks not of something that happened but of **what could have happened and did not**.

**What is the function of the sentence?** I record this as my own reading: the sūra shuts off the possibility that the turning back should be read as an **incapacity**. The treaty did not come out of an inability to fight. The verse says this without opening it for debate, in a single conditional sentence.

**And in saying it, it frames no boast.** The subject of the sentence is the other side (*la-wallaw* — they would have turned); it does not say "you would have won". The difference is fine, but it agrees with the tone of the sūra: the outcome is tied not to whose strength was greater but to who was left without support.

### لَوَلَّوُا۟ ٱلْأَدْبَٰرَ — "they would have turned their backs"

**وَلَّىٰ** — root **و-ل-ي**. The two directions of this root were recorded at verse 16: both "to be near, to be a friend" and "to turn away".

**دُبُر** — root **د-ب-ر**: the back, the end of a thing. *Tadbīr* is from the same root — to think through the **end** of a matter. *Mudabbir* (the one who manages a matter), *idbār* (turning back), *dābir* (root, hindmost part).

*Wallā'd-dubr* is a settled idiom in Arabic for "to flee": to turn one's back.

### ثُمَّ لَا يَجِدُونَ وَلِيًّا وَلَا نَصِيرًا — the repetition of a root

**And now the point of word order in the verse.**

- *la-**وَلَّوُا۟** * — they turned their backs — root **و-ل-ي**
- *lā yajidūna **وَلِيًّا** * — they would find no **walī** — **the same root**

**The same root, in the same verse, in two different senses.** The word for what they do in fleeing is the same as the word for what they cannot find.

**I record this as a weave of words;** not as a claim of miracle. But the effect in the word order is plain: for the one who turns his back, no side is left to turn to.

**وَلِيّ and نَصِير — two different kinds of support.**

| Word | Root | What kind of support |
|---|---|---|
| **وَلِيّ** | و-ل-ي | Support arising from **nearness** — kinship, protection, friendship |
| **نَصِير** | ن-ص-ر | **Actual help** — coming to the rescue |

The Qurʾān often uses these two as a pair (al-Baqara 2/107, 120; al-Tawba 9/116). **Together they close off the two sources of support:** neither the protection that comes from a structural bond, nor help that actually arrives.

It was recorded at `110-nasr.md` that the concrete sense of the root *naṣr* has to do with rain — what comes from above onto dry ground and brings it to life. To have no *naṣīr* is for that descent not to happen.

## 48/23 — سُنَّةَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلَّتِى قَدْ خَلَتْ مِن قَبْلُ ۖ وَلَن تَجِدَ لِسُنَّةِ ٱللَّهِ تَبْدِيلًا

*Sunnata'llāhi'llatī qad khalat min qabl; wa lan tajida li-sunnati'llāhi tabdīlā*

"The way of God, which has run its course before. And you will find no change in the way of God."

### سنة — root: س-ن-ن

**The concrete sense of the root has to be dwelt on, because the word's common use has drifted far from its root.**

**The verb at the centre of the root: سَنَّ — to make a thing even, flowing, smooth.**

Uses:

| Use | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **سَنَّ الحديدةَ** | He **whetted** the iron — sharpened it by rubbing |
| **سَنَّ الماءَ** | He **made the water flow** — poured it along an even channel |
| **مِسَنّ** (*misann*) | **A whetstone** — the stone on which whetting is done |
| **سِنَان** (*sinān*) | **A spear point** — a whetted, sharpened tip |
| **سِنّ** (*sinn*) | **A tooth** — and from this "age" (age being told by the teeth) |
| **مَسْنُون** | Made even, given shape. It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"moulded clay"* (al-Ḥijr 15/26 — *ḥamaʾin masnūn*) |

**Two ideas meet in the root: smoothing by friction, and flowing along an even channel.**

**From this *sunna* is born:** a road **opened** by much repetition, made smooth, its track worn plain. The English expressions *to blaze a trail*, *a beaten path*, *a well-worn track* carry the same image.

**So *sunna* is not "tradition" or "custom".** In the logic of the root there is a **repetition** and a **channel** opened by that repetition. If water always runs in the same place it cuts a bed; after that it does not run elsewhere — because it has a bed.

### سُنَّةَ ٱللَّهِ — the concept

**The construction occurs in several places in the Qurʾān, and in all of them it conveys the same thing: a working that does not change.**

| Verse | Expression |
|---|---|
| **al-Aḥzāb 33/62** | *"The way of God concerning those who went before… and you will find no change in the way of God"* |
| **Fāṭir 35/43** | *"You will find in the way of God neither change nor turning aside"* |
| **Ghāfir 40/85** | *"The way of God that has run its course among His servants…"* |
| **al-Isrāʾ 17/77** | *"The way concerning the messengers We sent before you… and you will find no change in Our way"* |
| **al-Fatḥ 48/23** | This verse |

**And the word occurs in the plural too:** *"Ways have passed away before you; so travel the earth and see how the end of those who denied came about"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/137).

**Āl ʿImrān 3/137 defines the concept.** There the ways are presented as things that **can be observed in history**: "travel the earth and see". *Sunnat Allāh*, that is, is not a hidden law but **a regularity that can be traced**.

### What the working is

**Which way is spoken of in this verse is understood by looking at the preceding one:**

> *"Had those who disbelieve fought you, they would surely have turned their backs and fled. Then they would have found neither protector nor helper."* (22)
> *"The way of God, which has run its course before."* (23)

**The way recounted, then, is this:** a structure that persists in an unjust position is in the end left without support, and breaks up. This is not an event that happened once but a working that repeats.

**And the verse says that this has happened before:** *allatī qad khalat min qabl* — "which has passed away before".

**خَلَتْ** — root **خ-ل-و**: to be emptied, to pass away. *Khalā* (it was empty), *khālī* (empty), *khalwa* (being alone). Used of time it means "it has passed, it is behind us". The Qurʾān uses it often: *"That was a community that has passed away"* (al-Baqara 2/134, 141).

### سُنَّةَ ٱللَّهِ — its being in the accusative

The word comes in the **accusative**: *sunnat**a**'llāh*. This is a point the grammarians dwell on, and it is explained in two ways:

| Explanation | Its meaning |
|---|---|
| **Absolute object** | It hangs on an implied verb: *"God has made this His way as a way"* |
| **Specification / calling to notice** | Drawing attention: *"look at the way of God!"* |

**Both explanations bring the sense to the same place** and require no preference between them: the sentence is not a piece of news but the **statement of a rule**.

### وَلَن تَجِدَ لِسُنَّةِ ٱللَّهِ تَبْدِيلًا

**لَنْ** — absolute negation directed at the future: "you will never find".

**تَجِدَ** — a **singular** address. This is striking: the preceding verse addressed a plural (*qātalakumu*), and here it returns to the singular. The rule is being stated to a single person — or, on one of the classical explanations, "you" here answers to any person at all: *"whoever looks will not find it."*

**تَبْدِيل** — root **ب-د-ل**, which occurred in the fifteenth verse.

**And the link between the two verses is one of the strongest weaves in the sūra:**

| Verse | Sentence | Who does what |
|---|---|---|
| **15** | *yurīdūna an **يُبَدِّلُوا۟** kalāma'llāh* | A group **wish to change** the word of God |
| **23** | *wa lan tajida li-sunnati'llāhi **تَبْدِيلًا*** | No one **can find a change** |

**The same root eight verses apart, at two opposite poles.** In one a human will, in the other the limit of that will.

**I record this as my own reading**, but the shared root and the position of the two verses are data that can be verified.

### On the meaning of the concept

**The concept of *sunnat Allāh* is the core of the Qurʾān's view of history, and it calls for careful speech.**

**What the concept says:** events are not random; particular behaviours have particular consequences, and that relation is not arbitrary.

**What the concept does not say:** which event is the application of which way. The Qurʾān gives examples for the past; but it grants no authority to name an event happening now.

**This distinction must be preserved.** For this is where the concept is most often abused: looking at the outcome of an event and framing a retrospective verdict — "so the way of God worked thus, so that side must have been in the wrong". This is the very manner of thinking the sūra itself **rejects**.

**For the event the sūra recounts is exactly the opposite:** the outcome as it appears is a step backwards, yet the sūra calls it a fatḥ. By its own subject matter, that is, the sūra shows that judging by outcomes can go wrong.

**I record this as my own reading:** *sunnat Allāh* is a measure given for understanding the past; it is not an authority given for judging the present. The command in Āl ʿImrān 3/137 is "travel and **see**" — not "pass judgement".

**And this commentary does not enter current politics.** The application of this concept to contemporary events is a field that `STYLE.md` explicitly leaves outside.

## 48/24 — وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى كَفَّ أَيْدِيَهُمْ عَنكُمْ وَأَيْدِيَكُمْ عَنْهُم بِبَطْنِ مَكَّةَ مِنۢ بَعْدِ أَنْ أَظْفَرَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرًا

*Wa huwa'lladhī kaffa aydiyahum ʿankum wa aydiyakum ʿanhum bi-baṭni Makkata min baʿdi an aẓfarakum ʿalayhim; wa kāna'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna baṣīrā*

"It is He who held back their hands from you, and your hands from them, in the heart of Mecca, after He had given you the upper hand over them. God sees what you do."

### The symmetry

**At the centre of the verse there is an exact symmetry:**

```
aydiya-HUM   ʿan-KUM     — their hands  from you
aydiya-KUM   ʿan-HUM     — your hands   from them
```

The same verb (*kaffa*), two objects, pronouns crossing each other. **And the same thing is done to both sides.**

**This is one of the most striking sentences in the sūra, and for this reason:** the ceasefire is not recounted as a one-sided favour. One side is not being protected; **both sides are being stopped.**

**I record this as my own reading:** the verse could have said "We protected you from them" — indeed in verse 20 it had said just that, in one direction (*kaffa aydiya'n-nāsi ʿankum*). The construction chosen here runs both ways. And this means that the stopping of the fighting is not presented as the gain of one side only. The stopping of the fighting is an outcome for both.

### مِنۢ بَعْدِ أَنْ أَظْفَرَكُمْ عَلَيْهِمْ

**أَظْفَرَ** — root **ظ-ف-ر**. The concrete sense of the root: **the nail.** **ظُفْر** (*ẓufr*) — nail, claw.

From this *ẓafar* is born: to take a thing **in the claw**, to seize it. In Turkish the borrowed word *zafer* means simply "victory", and the image in its root has been lost entirely.

**And now the distinction made at the head of the sūra falls exactly into place.** It was recorded at the first verse: though Arabic had words such as *ghalaba* and *ẓafar* for military superiority, the Qurʾān chose the word *fatḥ* (opening).

**And here the root *ẓafar* occurs exactly once in the sūra, exactly here** — and it states no "winning"; it states **a superiority that was not used**.

| Word | Where | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| **فَتْح** | 1, 18, 27 | **Opening** — the name of the sūra |
| **ظفر** | 24 (a single occurrence) | **The opportunity to seize** — unused |

**The logic of the sentence is this:** the superiority was there, but it was not used; and its not being used is not a weakness but a **decision** — the subject of the verb is God.

**This is the most concrete expression of the sūra's understanding of fatḥ.** A fatḥ is not the closing of the claw but the opening of the door. The verse records that where both were possible at the same time, the second was chosen.

**A note:** which event the "being given the upper hand" points to is recounted in different ways in the reports — the beginning of a skirmish, a position obtained against one group. The details are disputed, and the Qurʾān does not narrate the event; **I do not present them as certain knowledge.**

### بِبَطْنِ مَكَّةَ — in the heart of Mecca

**بَطْن** — root **ب-ط-ن**: belly, interior. The analysis of the root was made at `057-hadid.md` (in the context of the name *al-Bāṭin*), and a note of *tanzīh* was entered there. Here the word is entirely in its concrete sense: **the inner part of a place, its hollow.**

*Baṭn* is used in geography for the floor of a valley, the low ground between two rises.

**مَكَّة — and a note.** This word occurs **only here in the Qurʾān**. It is known that the city is also named once in the Qurʾān in the form **بَكَّة** (Bakka): *"The first House established for people is the one at Bakka"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/96).

The relation between the two names has been debated among the lexicographers: some say they are two names for the same place, others that *Bakka* answers to the narrow area in which the Kaʿba stands while *Makka* answers to the whole city. **I do not speak with certainty;** this is a disagreement among lexicographers.

**And this must be recorded:** a single place name occurs in the sūra, and it occurs as the name of the place where a fight **stopped**.

### وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرًا

**بَصِير** — root **ب-ص-ر**: to see; and **baṣīra** — inward sight, discernment. From the same root *baṣar* (the eye, sight), *ibṣār*, *tabaṣṣur*, *mubṣir* (seeing, giving light).

**In the eleventh verse the same pattern closed with *khabīr*:** *wa kāna'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna **خَبِيرًا***. Here it is *baṣīr*.

| Verse | Name | What it looks to |
|---|---|---|
| **11** | **خَبِير** | **Inward** — to the gap between tongue and heart |
| **24** | **بَصِير** | **Outward** — to what actually happens |

**Two names, two blocks, two distinct questions.** What was recounted in the eleventh verse was an inward mismatch; what is recounted here is a behaviour that actually took place — the lowering of hands. The names are chosen to fit their subjects.

I record this as an observation about the word order.

## 48/25 — هُمُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ وَصَدُّوكُمْ عَنِ ٱلْمَسْجِدِ ٱلْحَرَامِ وَٱلْهَدْىَ مَعْكُوفًا أَن يَبْلُغَ مَحِلَّهُۥ ۚ وَلَوْلَا رِجَالٌ مُّؤْمِنُونَ وَنِسَآءٌ مُّؤْمِنَٰتٌ لَّمْ تَعْلَمُوهُمْ أَن تَطَـُٔوهُمْ فَتُصِيبَكُم مِّنْهُم مَّعَرَّةٌۢ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ ۖ لِّيُدْخِلَ ٱللَّهُ فِى رَحْمَتِهِۦ مَن يَشَآءُ ۚ لَوْ تَزَيَّلُوا۟ لَعَذَّبْنَا ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ مِنْهُمْ عَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

*Humu'lladhīna kafarū wa ṣaddūkum ʿani'l-masjidi'l-ḥarāmi wa'l-hadya maʿkūfan an yablugha maḥillah; wa lawlā rijālun muʾminūna wa nisāʾun muʾminātun lam taʿlamūhum an taṭaʾūhum fa-tuṣībakum minhum maʿarratun bi-ghayri ʿilm; li-yudkhila'llāhu fī raḥmatihī man yashāʾ; law tazayyalū la-ʿadhdhabnā'lladhīna kafarū minhum ʿadhāban alīmā*

"They are the ones who disbelieved and barred you from the Sacred Mosque, and kept the sacrificial animals from reaching their place of offering. Had it not been for believing men and believing women whom you did not know, whom you might have trampled underfoot, so that harm might have come upon you from them without your knowing… so that God might bring into His mercy whom He wills. Had they been separated from one another, We would surely have punished those of them who disbelieved with a painful punishment."

### The unfinished sentence

**The most striking grammatical feature of the verse is this: the answer to the conditional clause is not stated.**

The answer to the clause beginning *lawlā…* (*"…We would have done such and such"*) is not in the text. In Arabic this is called **the suppression of the apodosis**, and it is a deliberate technique: what is left unsaid is already clear from the context, and leaving it unsaid makes it stronger.

**The missing sentence here is understood from what precedes the verse:** "…permission to fight would have been given", "…the hands would not have been held back".

The **ground of the withholding of hands** recounted in the twenty-fourth verse, that is, is here. And the ground is astonishing.

### وَصَدُّوكُمْ — root: ص-د-د

**The sense of the root: to prevent, to turn aside, to turn away.** *Ṣudūd* (turning away), *ṣadad* (direction, intent — it lives on in Turkish in the phrase *bu sadette*).

The Qurʾān uses this verb particularly in the context of the Sacred Mosque: *"Those who disbelieved and barred others from the way of God and from the Sacred Mosque…"* (al-Ḥajj 22/25).

### وَٱلْهَدْىَ — root: ه-د-ي

**And here is one of the finest verbal connections in the sūra.**

**هَدْي** (*hady*) — the sacrificial animal. And the root of the word is **ه-د-ي** — that is, the same root as **hidāya**, guidance.

The sense of the root: **to show the way, to direct a thing towards a place, to send it on.** And a *hady* is in the fullest sense the animal **that is sent to its place**: sent on ahead by its owner, and having a particular place to reach.

From the same root comes **هَدِيَّة** (*hadiyya*): a thing **directed** towards someone. The word lives on in Turkish as *hediye*, and it still carries the idea of "sending" that is in its root.

**And now let us see the link with the second verse.** In the second verse one of the four verbs was this: *wa **يَهْدِيَكَ** ṣirāṭan mustaqīmā* — "and **guide** you on a straight path". The same root.

**I record this as my own reading, not as a transmitted report:** the sūra uses the same root at two ends. In one place a human being is shown the way; in the other the way of an animal already sent out is cut off. The shared idea of the word is the same in both: **having been directed towards a place.** This is a weave of words; I do not claim there is a deliberate allusion.

### مَعْكُوفًا — root: ع-ك-ف

**The sense of the root: to stay put in a place, to attach oneself to a thing and not leave it, to be held back.**

From the same root comes **اِعْتِكَاف** (*iʿtikāf*): staying in a mosque without leaving it. The Qurʾān uses the verb in the context of idolatry too: *"They came upon a people **devoting themselves** to idols of theirs"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/138 — *yaʿkufūna ʿalā aṣnāmin lahum*) — at the centre of the word lies **not leaving the side of something**.

**Here the word is a passive participle:** *maʿkūf* — **held back, kept waiting.**

**The image is extremely concrete:** the sacrificial animals were set on the road, but they were stopped in the middle of it. They can neither go back nor go forward. A state of waiting.

### أَن يَبْلُغَ مَحِلَّهُۥ — root: ح-ل-ل

**مَحِلّ** — root **ح-ل-ل**: to come down in a place, to halt; the untying of a knot; and **to become lawful.**

**These three senses of the root are bound to one another, and the word here carries all of them at once:**

- **The halting place:** the place the sacrificial animal must reach.
- **The untying:** the loosing of a bond — the untying of the bond of consecration by the animal's being sacrificed.
- **Becoming lawful:** the moment at which sacrificing it becomes permitted.

**The Qurʾān uses the same expression in another place, and that verse lights this one directly:**

> *"…**If you are prevented**, then send whatever sacrificial animal is within your means. And do not shave your heads **until the sacrificial animal reaches its place of offering**."* (al-Baqara 2/196)

**The two verses use the same words:** *uḥṣirtum* (being prevented), *hady* (sacrificial animal), *yablugha'l-hadyu maḥillah* (the animal's reaching its place), *taḥliqū ruʾūsakum* (shaving the head).

And **the shaving of the head** will occur again in verse 27 of this sūra: *muḥalliqīna ruʾūsakum*. Two verses — al-Baqara 2/196 and al-Fatḥ 48/25-27 — thus share the same cluster of words.

**I record this as an observation:** this section of the sūra speaks in the language of the rulings of pilgrimage and *ʿumra*. What is recounted is not a military event but **an act of worship left unfinished**.

### وَلَوْلَا رِجَالٌ مُّؤْمِنُونَ وَنِسَآءٌ مُّؤْمِنَٰتٌ — the ground

**This is the most astonishing sentence in the sūra.**

What is shown as the **ground for stopping** a fight is neither a strategy, nor a balance of forces, nor a political calculation. What is shown is this: **inside the other side there are believers whose identity is not known.**

**لَّمْ تَعْلَمُوهُمْ — "you did not know them."**

**And this expression is the first half of the sūra's central sentence.** Two verses later will come:

> *"**He knew what you did not know.**"* (48/27 — *fa-ʿalima mā lam taʿlamū*)

**Here what is unknown is given concretely:** people inside the other side. In the twenty-seventh verse this will turn into a general principle.

**The sūra, that is, does not content itself with saying "there was something you did not know"; it also says what that something was.** And what it says bears directly on the way people sort one another into categories: when a body of people is looked at as a whole, the single human beings inside it become invisible.

### أَن تَطَـُٔوهُمْ — root: و-ط-ء

**The sense of the root: to tread with the foot, to trample, to set one's foot upon.** It is said that *waṭan* (the place one settles upon) comes from the same root; and *muwāṭaʾa* (agreement — setting foot in the same place).

**The choice of word is mercilessly concrete.** It does not say "that you might harm them" or "that you might kill them"; it says **"that you might trample them"**.

**And this is a word that describes how trampling happens:** the one who sets his foot down does not see what he treads on. He does not look, does not pick out, does not distinguish. **Trampling is something done without awareness** — and indeed the sentence says so separately: *bi-ghayri ʿilm*, "without knowing".

### فَتُصِيبَكُم مِّنْهُم مَّعَرَّةٌۢ بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ

**مَعَرَّة** — root **ع-ر-ر**. The senses the lexicographers record: a harm that clings, a defect, a disgrace, a blameworthy condition. As the concrete sense of the root the dictionaries mention a disease that clings to the skin (*ʿurr*); I do not present this connection as a settled etymology.

**The shared idea of the word: contamination.** A stain that passes onto one without one's wanting it.

**بِغَيْرِ عِلْمٍ — "without knowing".**

**And now attend to the moral structure of the verse.** What is at issue is not a deliberate wrong. The verse speaks of something that would be done **unknowingly**, and says that it would give rise to a *maʿarra*.

**I record this as my own reading:** the measure the verse sets is this — **not knowing does not undo the outcome.** A person trampled unknowingly has still been trampled; and the stain that clings has still clung. For this reason the remedy is not to say "we did not know" but that **the act should not be done at all**. This is exactly the ground for the withholding of hands.

This reading is supported by the word order of the verse: the expression *bi-ghayri ʿilm* is set down not as an excuse but as **the character of the outcome**.

### لِّيُدْخِلَ ٱللَّهُ فِى رَحْمَتِهِۦ مَن يَشَآءُ

A parenthetical sentence. The purpose behind the preceding ground is stated: **so that God might bring into His mercy whom He wills.**

**The verb is once again a verb of "bringing in":** *yudkhila* — for the third time in the sūra (5, 17, 25). And here the place into which they are brought is not a garden but **mercy**.

**The function of the sentence, as my own reading, is this:** the withholding of hands is not only for the protection of the believers already known but to leave open a possibility for people about whom it is **not yet known who will be included**. A fight turns everything not yet settled into something settled. Stopping it preserves what is unsettled.

And the sūra has a continuation that supports this reading: Sūrat al-Naṣr recounts people entering in throngs. It is transmitted in the reports that this entering quickened in the period after the treaty. **But this is a historical assessment; I do not present it as a claim of the verse.**

### لَوْ تَزَيَّلُوا۟ — root: ز-ي-ل

**The sense of the root: to part, to be separated from one another, to withdraw from between.** *Zāla* (it departed, it passed away), *tazayyul* (separating from one another), *izāla* (removal).

*Law tazayyalū* — "had they been separated from one another".

**What the sentence says is plain, and it completes this whole section of the sūra: the two groups were mixed in together.** Those who believed and those who did not were in the same city, in the same houses, in the same families. They were not separated out.

**And the verse makes their not being separated a variable of the outcome:** had they been separated, the outcome would have been different.

**I record this as my own reading:** this is one of the most practical measures in the sūra. What stands in the way of applying a collective treatment to a body of people is that the body is **not homogeneous**. And real communities never are. The verse uses this fact as a ground and draws a consequence from it: intermixture makes collective treatment impossible.

**A note:** this verse is one of the clearest supports within the Qurʾān for the principle in `STYLE.md` that no wholesale ruling is issued about any group. The text itself sets, in the way of taking a community as a whole, the unknown people inside that community.

## 48/26 — إِذْ جَعَلَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ فِى قُلُوبِهِمُ ٱلْحَمِيَّةَ حَمِيَّةَ ٱلْجَٰهِلِيَّةِ فَأَنزَلَ ٱللَّهُ سَكِينَتَهُۥ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِۦ وَعَلَى ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَأَلْزَمَهُمْ كَلِمَةَ ٱلتَّقْوَىٰ وَكَانُوٓا۟ أَحَقَّ بِهَا وَأَهْلَهَا ۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمًا

*Idh jaʿala'lladhīna kafarū fī qulūbihimu'l-ḥamiyyata ḥamiyyata'l-jāhiliyyati fa-anzala'llāhu sakīnatahū ʿalā rasūlihī wa ʿalā'l-muʾminīna wa alzamahum kalimata't-taqwā wa kānū aḥaqqa bihā wa ahlahā; wa kāna'llāhu bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīmā*

"When those who disbelieved put in their hearts that hot fury — the hot fury of the age of ignorance — God sent down His sakīna upon His Messenger and upon the believers, and bound them to the word of God-consciousness; and they were more worthy of it, and its rightful people. God is knowing of all things."

### The sūra's strongest contrast

**This verse is built upon a comparison, and the two sides of the comparison are two words:**

```
On the one side:  الحمية   —  ḥamiyya   —  HEATING UP
On the other:     السكينة  —  sakīna    —  SETTLING DOWN
```

**And the contrast is at the level of the root**, not at the level of the word. The verse does not set "anger" against "patience", or "hatred" against "mercy". What is set against each other are **two states of motion**: growing hot and growing still.

Before setting this out in detail, let us look at the two roots separately.

### حمية — root: ح-م-ي

**The sense of the root: to grow hot, to be heated, to warm up.** *Ḥamiya'sh-shayʾu* — the thing grew hot, it became very warm.

**Derivatives:**

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **حَامِيَة** (*ḥāmiya*) | Burning hot, heated. In the Qurʾān of fire: *"a blazing fire"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/4; al-Qāriʿa 101/11) |
| **حُمَّى** (*ḥummā*) | **Fever** — the rise of the body's heat. It lives on in Turkish as *humma* |
| **حِمَى** (*ḥimā*) | **A protected pasture** — an area made inviolable, which it is forbidden to enter |
| **حِمَايَة** (*ḥimāya*) | Protection. It lives on in Turkish |
| **حَمِيَّة** (*ḥamiyya*) | Hot fury; and "the feeling of guarding something jealously". It lives on in Turkish as *hamiyet*, but there its sense has drifted to the favourable |

**And here a note must be entered.** When this root was treated at `101-karia.md` the following was stated: **the lexicographers differ over whether the same root letters gather the sense-clusters of "heat" and "protection" under one root, or whether these are two separate homonymous roots.** That disagreement was not entered into there, and no meaning was drawn from the shared sound.

**I keep the same stance here.** There are explanations by which the two clusters of sense may be linked — "one grows hot over what one protects" — but I do not present this as a settled ruling about the language.

**The sense the verse itself uses, however, is beyond dispute:** *ḥamiyya* here means **hot fury, anger springing from pride, the determination not to give a step**. The verse qualifies this further: *ḥamiyyata'l-jāhiliyya*.

### ٱلْجَٰهِلِيَّة — root: ج-ه-ل

**The two senses of the root were given in a table at `049-hucurat.md`**, and they are decisive here:

| Sense | What it is the opposite of | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Not knowing** | The opposite of *ʿilm* | Ignorance |
| **Rashness** | The opposite of ***ḥilm*** | Acting in anger, without thinking, without self-restraint |

And it was recorded there that on one view *jāhiliyya* is not "the age of ignorance" but **"the age of not being able to hold oneself back"**.

**This verse directly supports the second sense.** For what is ascribed to *jāhiliyya* in the verse is not an ignorance but a **condition of feeling**: hot fury. What is described is a fault having to do not with knowledge but with self-restraint.

### How "jāhiliyya" is used in the Qurʾān

**The word occurs four times in the Qurʾān, and in all four not on its own but as part of a genitive construction:**

| Verse | Construction | What it qualifies |
|---|---|---|
| **Āl ʿImrān 3/154** | *ẓanna'l-jāhiliyya* | **Thought** — a conviction |
| **al-Māʾida 5/50** | *ḥukma'l-jāhiliyya* | **Judgement** — a verdict |
| **al-Aḥzāb 33/33** | *tabarruja'l-jāhiliyyati'l-ūlā* | **Tabarruj** — a manner of display |
| **al-Fatḥ 48/26** | *ḥamiyyata'l-jāhiliyya* | **Ḥamiyya** — hot fury |

**What this table says is important and must be recorded:** the Qurʾān does not use *jāhiliyya* on its own as the name of a period. Every time, it makes it qualify a **behaviour** or an **attitude**.

**The word names a stance, that is, not a stretch of history.** A thought may be *jāhilī*, a judgement may be *jāhilī*, a fury may be *jāhilī*. And these can be met with at any time.

**This is another instance of the distinction between attribute and name in `STYLE.md`:** the verse names not a community but a state.

### The contrast of the two roots: heating and settling

**Now let us come to the point of word order at the centre of the verse.**

| | **حَمِيَّة** | **سَكِينَة** |
|---|---|---|
| **Root** | ح-م-ي | س-ك-ن |
| **The idea of the root** | **Warming, heating, fever** | **Stopping, becoming motionless** |
| **Where** | *fī qulūbihim* — into their hearts | *ʿalā rasūlihī wa ʿalā'l-muʾminīn* — upon them |
| **Verb** | *jaʿala* — they put, they placed | *anzala* — He sent down |
| **Subject of the verb** | **People** — *alladhīna kafarū* | **God** |
| **Its result** | (The verse does not say) | *alzamahum kalimata't-taqwā* |

**The contrast has four layers, and it is complete in each of them:**

**One. The layer of sense.** The one is heating, the other settling. In language heat is bound to motion and stillness to its absence — in Arabic as in English. The expressions *one's blood boils*, *a heated argument*, *to keep a cool head* carry this link. I record this as an observation about the language, not as a claim about physics.

**Two. The layer of direction.** *Ḥamiyya* is **put in**: *jaʿala fī qulūbihim* — they placed it in their hearts; the subject of the verb is themselves. *Sakīna* is **sent down**: *anzala* — it comes from above; the subject of the verb is God.

**Three. The layer of lodging.** *Ḥamiyya* settles **inside** the heart. *Sakīna* here comes down not into the heart but **upon** the person. (In the fourth verse it came down into the heart — I shall take up this difference below.)

**Four. The layer of outcome.** The outcome of *ḥamiyya* is not stated in the verse — but the event the sūra recounts is plain: a barring, a turning back. The outcome of *sakīna* is stated openly: being bound to a word.

**I record this as my own reading, not as a transmitted report:** this is one of the strongest points of word order in the sūra. The difference between the two sides is framed in the verse not by a claim of rightness but by **two physical states**. One side has grown hot, the other has grown still. And the verse does not even recount what either of them did — it records only their inward states.

**Why this matters may be put like this:** the side that grows hot feels itself, at that moment, to be in the right. Anger produces its own justification and generally speaks in the idiom of values — honour, pride, something that must be defended. Indeed in Turkish the word *hamiyet* has taken on a favourable sense. The verse does not remove the favourable-looking face of the word; it simply sets an adjective before it: **the fury of the age of ignorance.**

### The three occurrences of the sakīna — a comparison

**The sakīna comes down three times in the sūra. Set the three side by side and none of them turns out to be the same as another:**

| | **48/4** | **48/18** | **48/26** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wording** | *al-sakīna* — definite | *al-sakīna* — definite | ***sakīnata-hū*** — "His own sakīna" |
| **To whom** | *al-muʾminīn* | To those who pledged (*ʿalayhim*) | To ***His Messenger*** and to *al-muʾminīn* |
| **Particle** | **فِى** *qulūbihim* — **into** | **عَلَىٰ** *him* — **upon** | **عَلَىٰ** … and **عَلَىٰ** … — **upon, twice** |
| **When** | With the proclamation of the fatḥ | During the pledge | Facing *ḥamiyya* |
| **What precedes it** | — | *"He knew what was in their hearts"* | *"They put the fury in their hearts"* |
| **Its result** | *li-yazdādū īmānā* — that faith may increase | *wa athābahum fatḥan qarībā* — a near fatḥ | *wa alzamahum kalimata't-taqwā* — being bound to a word |
| **Is the Prophet named** | No | No (he is present as the addressee, "pledge to you") | **Yes — and first** |

**Three observations follow from the comparison:**

**One. Only in verse 4 does the sakīna come down *into* the heart.** In the others it comes *upon*. The two particles build two different images: the one a filling, the other a covering. In the fourth verse an **increase** is the aim (*li-yazdādū*) — and for increase one must get inside. In the other two what is at issue is a **being protected**.

**Two. Only in verse 26 is the sakīna ascribed to God:** *sakīnata**-hū***. In the others it comes with the definite article. The possessive stresses the source in particular — and this is exactly the verse in which the difference of source is the subject: one side has put something into its own heart, and to the other side **someone else** has sent something down.

**Three. Only in verse 26 is the Prophet named separately, and first.** And this accords with another verse: *"Then God sent down His sakīna upon His Messenger and upon the believers"* (al-Tawba 9/26) — there too the order is the same.

**These three observations are my own reading;** the data on roots and particles can be verified, but the meanings I draw from them are mine.

### وَأَلْزَمَهُمْ كَلِمَةَ ٱلتَّقْوَىٰ

**أَلْزَمَ** — root **ل-ز-م**: to cleave to a thing, not to part from it, to be necessary. *Lāzim*, *luzūm*, *mulāzama*, *iltizām*. In Form IV: **He made it inseparable, He fastened it on.**

**The choice of verb is meaningful.** It does not say "He gave", "He taught", "He commanded". It says **"He fastened it on"** — the word conveys a thing's becoming inseparable from a person.

The Qurʾān uses this verb in one further striking place: *"We have **fastened** every person's deed upon his neck"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/13 — *alzamnāhu ṭāʾirahū fī ʿunuqih*). The same verb, the same idea of inseparability.

### كَلِمَةَ ٱلتَّقْوَىٰ — "the word of taqwā"

**What this construction is has been debated in classical exegesis. I transmit the disagreement:**

| View | What it is |
|---|---|
| **The word of tawḥīd** | *Lā ilāha illā'llāh* — the view most widely transmitted in classical exegesis |
| **Steadfastness and loyalty** | Standing by the word given; carrying out what the pledge requires |
| **Acceptance of the treaty** | Abiding by the decision of that moment; not giving way to fury |
| **General: the word that leads to taqwā** | The construction states not a particular wording but a quality |

**In the classical commentaries the first view is widely transmitted. I impose no preference;** the Qurʾān does not give the wording.

**But the structure of the construction can be dwelt on, and that is independent of the debate.**

**تَقْوَىٰ** — root **و-ق-ي**: to guard, to protect oneself, to keep a thing from harm. From the same root *wiqāya* (protection), *ittiqāʾ*, *muttaqī*. The analysis of the root was made at `049-hucurat.md`, `057-hadid.md` and in other sections: **taqwā is not fearing but guarding oneself** — a person's putting a distance between himself and harm.

**And now see the link inside the verse.** Two words stand side by side:

- **حِمَى** (*ḥimā*) — the word associated with the root of *ḥamiyya*: **the protected pasture**, the inviolable area.
- **تَقْوَى** — the sense of the root: **guarding oneself.**

**Both are words of protection. The difference lies in what is protected:** the one guards an area against what comes from outside, the other guards a person against his own harm.

**I record this as an observation, and I limit it by recalling the note at `101-karia.md`:** whether the root ح-م-ي genuinely covers the "protection" cluster of senses is disputed among the lexicographers. For this reason I draw no ruling from the observation here.

### وَكَانُوٓا۟ أَحَقَّ بِهَا وَأَهْلَهَا

**أَحَقّ** — root **ح-ق-ق**: to be established, to be real, to deserve. An **elative**: "more worthy, having more right".

**أَهْل** — root **أ-ه-ل**: the people of a thing, one close to it, one belonging to it.

**And this word occurs for the second time in the sūra.** The first was in verse 11:

| Verse | Word | Context |
|---|---|---|
| **11** | *wa **أَهْلُونَا*** — "our families" | **An excuse**: "they kept us back" |
| **26** | *wa **أَهْلَهَا*** — "its people" | **A quality**: the rightful people of the word of taqwā |

**The same root, at two ends.** In one place the ground for staying behind, in the other the name of being worthy of a word.

**I record this as my own reading:** in the root of the word there lies "belonging to a thing, being close to it". At the first occurrence people stayed behind by counting themselves as belonging to their families; at the second, others are counted as belonging to a word. The verse does not set up this contrast; the shared word is an observation.

**Why two adjectives together?** *Aḥaqqu bihā* states a **degree** (more worthy); *ahlahā* states a **belonging** (its people, its holders). The first is comparative, the second absolute. Together they establish both the comparison and the ownership.

### وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمًا

**The close agrees exactly with the subject of the verse.** What is recounted through the verse is what goes on **inside hearts**: on one side a fury put there, on the other a sakīna sent down. Neither can be seen.

And the verse closes with the name "knowing of all things". **بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ** — "of all things": the extent given in its widest form.

**And this name prepares the central sentence of the next verse:** *fa-ʿalima mā lam taʿlamū* — "He knew what you did not know." The twenty-sixth verse establishes the extent; the twenty-seventh states the difference.

## 48/27 — لَّقَدْ صَدَقَ ٱللَّهُ رَسُولَهُ ٱلرُّءْيَا بِٱلْحَقِّ ۖ لَتَدْخُلُنَّ ٱلْمَسْجِدَ ٱلْحَرَامَ إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ ءَامِنِينَ مُحَلِّقِينَ رُءُوسَكُمْ وَمُقَصِّرِينَ لَا تَخَافُونَ ۖ فَعَلِمَ مَا لَمْ تَعْلَمُوا۟ فَجَعَلَ مِن دُونِ ذَٰلِكَ فَتْحًا قَرِيبًا

*Laqad ṣadaqa'llāhu rasūlahu'r-ruʾyā bi'l-ḥaqq; la-tadkhulunna'l-masjida'l-ḥarāma in shāʾa'llāhu āminīna muḥalliqīna ruʾūsakum wa muqaṣṣirīna lā takhāfūn; fa-ʿalima mā lam taʿlamū fa-jaʿala min dūni dhālika fatḥan qarībā*

"God has indeed fulfilled His Messenger's vision in truth: you shall certainly enter the Sacred Mosque, if God wills, in safety, your heads shaved or your hair shortened, without fear. He knew what you did not know, and set, short of that, a near fatḥ."

### The centre of the sūra

**This is the verse that resolves the whole tension of the sūra.** And within it stands the answer to the question we asked in the sūra's opening lines: *why is something that looks like a step backwards called a fatḥ?*

The answer is in six words: **فَعَلِمَ مَا لَمْ تَعْلَمُوا۟** — "He knew what you did not know."

Let us open up the rest of the verse first, and then come back to this sentence.

### لَّقَدْ صَدَقَ ٱللَّهُ رَسُولَهُ ٱلرُّءْيَا

**صَدَقَ** — root **ص-د-ق**: to be true, to stand by one's word. The analysis of the root was made at `049-hucurat.md` and `057-hadid.md`; it was recorded there that the root carries the idea of **soundness and durability** (a spear being called *ṣadq* for being hard and straight).

**The verb's use here is striking:** **it takes two objects.**

*Ṣadaqa'llāhu **rasūlahu** **'r-ruʾyā*** — "God confirmed the vision to His Messenger."

The verb takes both the person and the content as its objects. In Arabic this structure gives the sense "to tell someone truly about something / to make a word come true".

**The Qurʾān uses the same verb in one other place, in the context of a vision:**

> *"O Abraham! **You have fulfilled the vision.**"* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/105 — *qad ṣaddaqta'r-ruʾyā*)

**The difference between the two verses is meaningful and must be recorded:**

| Verse | Agent | Who confirms |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Ṣāffāt 37/105** | *ṣaddaqta* | **A human being** — he did what the vision required |
| **al-Fatḥ 48/27** | *ṣadaqa'llāh* | **God** — He brought the vision about |

In one place a man confirms the vision, in the other God does. **A vision is presented as something that can be "confirmed" from either side.**

### بِٱلْحَقِّ — "in truth"

**حَقّ** — root **ح-ق-ق**: to be fixed and real; not to be shifted, not to change. As the concrete sense of the root the lexicographers record "a thing's being firm and in its proper place".

**Its function in the sentence:** the confirming of the vision was not a coincidence or a construction placed upon it; it happened **in accordance with what is real**. And this qualification prepares the ground for what will be said at the end of the verse: the vision is true, but **the time of its fulfilment** is not part of the vision.

### ٱلرُّءْيَا — root: ر-أ-ي

**The Qurʾān uses a particular word for a vision in sleep, and the distinction must be recorded:**

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **رُؤْيَة** (*ruʾya*) | Seeing — awake, with the eye |
| **رُؤْيَا** (*ruʾyā*) | **A dream** — what is seen in sleep |

Both are from the root **ر-أ-ي**; the difference lies in the pattern. In Arabic the *ruʾyā* pattern is reserved for what is seen in sleep.

**The Qurʾān uses this word in several contexts:** in the story of Joseph (Yūsuf) (12/43, 100), in the story of Abraham (37/105), and *"We made the vision We showed you only a trial for people"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/60).

**The content of the dream is given in the verse** — which is not always the case in the Qurʾān: entering the Sacred Mosque in safety.

**And the verse does not say when the dream was seen.** It is transmitted in the reports that it was seen before setting out and recounted to the company; **I do not present this as certain knowledge.**

### لَتَدْخُلُنَّ — the strongest reinforcement

The verb comes with the strongest pattern of reinforcement Arabic has: **لَ** (the lām of the oath) at its head and **نَّ** (the nūn of emphasis) at its end. The two together mean "you shall certainly, absolutely, beyond all doubt enter".

**And note: the verb is plural.** *La-tadkhulunna* — "**you** shall enter". The one who saw the dream is a single person; but the addressee of the promise is the community.

**And the verb is once again a verb of "entering".** The root *d-kh-l* for the fourth time in the sūra: 5 (bringing into the gardens), 17 (bringing into the gardens), 25 (bringing into mercy), 27 (entering the mosque). **This is the counterpart of the image of "opening" in the first verse:** one goes in through the door that is opened.

### إِن شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ — a reservation

**This expression is one of the most dwelt-upon points of the verse.**

**The question is this:** why, in stating His own promise, is the reservation "if God wills" entered? If the promise is His own, what is the condition a condition of?

**The explanations worked out in classical exegesis:**

| Explanation | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Instruction** | The expression teaches the hearers to enter this reservation in every statement about the future |
| **A reservation of extent** | Those who will enter are those who live to see that day; not everyone. The condition concerns the persons |
| **A reservation of time** | The entering is certain; **when** it will happen is what is reserved |
| **The wording of the dream** | The expression is the transmission of the dream's own wording |
| **Emphasis** | In Arabic this pattern sometimes conveys not a condition but a reinforcement |

**I impose no preference;** in classical exegesis several of these explanations have also been defended together.

**But this must be recorded, and it is independent of the debate:** the Qurʾān sets this reservation down as a command as well:

> *"And do not say of anything, 'I shall do that tomorrow' — without adding, 'if God wills'."* (al-Kahf 18/23-24)

**And in the context of the verse this reservation is entirely apt.** What the sūra recounts is exactly this: a body of people supposed that something would happen **that year**, and it did not. The reservation "if God wills" set inside the promise stands like a mark against the repeating of that same mistake.

**I offer this as a reading, not as a classical transmitted report.**

### ءَامِنِينَ — root: أ-م-ن

**And here there is a connection of root.**

**أَمْن** — safety, freedom from fear. And from the same root comes **إِيمَان** (*īmān*): **to trust, to give safety, to make secure.**

**"Āminīn" (in safety), then, and "muʾminīn" (those who believe) are from the same root.**

This link bears directly on the fourth verse: there *sakīna* was coming down upon the believers — an inward stillness. Here *amn* is promised to them — an outward safety.

| Verse | Word | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | *sakīna* | **Inside** — in the hearts |
| **27** | *āminīn* | **Outside** — in the Sacred Mosque |

**I record this as my own reading:** the sūra gives the inward safety first and leaves the outward safety for later. And the order itself says something: outward safety may be delayed, but the inward stillness was not.

### مُحَلِّقِينَ رُءُوسَكُمْ وَمُقَصِّرِينَ

Two states, describing the last stage of the rites of *ʿumra*.

**حَلَقَ** — root **ح-ل-ق**: to shave. From the same root come **حَلْقَة** (a ring — a round shape) and **حَلْق** (the throat). What is mentioned as the shared idea is "roundness, going all the way round".

**قَصَّرَ** — root **ق-ص-ر**: to shorten, to be short. From the same root come *qaṣīr* (short), *qaṣr* (the shortening of the prayer), and **قَصْر** (a palace — a structure that is shortened, bounded; or one whose limits are fixed). The link between the word's two senses is disputed among the lexicographers; I do not speak with certainty.

**That the two options are named together is striking.** The verse does not impose a single practice; it names both. **I issue no legal ruling;** the conditions of these two options, and the preference between them, differ in detail among the schools.

**But one link must be recorded:** at al-Baqara 2/196 it was said, in the case of being prevented, *"do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its place of offering"*. Here, in the case where the prevention has been lifted, the shaving is named **inside the promise**. The two verses show two states of the same act: its forbidden state and its accomplished state.

### لَا تَخَافُونَ — "you will not fear"

**The clause comes after the word *āminīn*, and appears to repeat it.** To be in safety already means not to be afraid.

**The repetition is a reinforcement**, but the two words do not say the same thing:

- **أَمْن** is a **condition**: the absence of danger outside.
- The absence of **خَوْف** is an **inward state**: that condition having its counterpart in the person.

**I record this as my own reading:** the two are not always together. A person may be afraid in a safe place; he may be unafraid in a dangerous one. The verse states both separately — the surroundings will be safe, and no fear will be left within.

And this is the completion of the *sakīna* of the fourth verse: there a stillness coming while fear was present was recounted; here the lifting of fear itself is promised.

### فَعَلِمَ مَا لَمْ تَعْلَمُوا۟ — the sūra's central sentence

**Six words. And the whole structure of the sūra rests on this sentence.**

**The subtlety in the word order:** the sentence begins not with *wa ʿalima* but with ***fa*-ʿalima**. In Arabic the particle **فَ** conveys **sequence and consequence**: "…and thereupon". The knowing, that is, is set down as an explanation following the promise.

**What the sentence builds is a contrast, and the contrast is built with a single root:**

- *fa-**عَلِمَ*** — He knew
- *mā lam **تَعْلَمُوا۟*** — what you did not know

**The same root (ع-ل-م), in the same sentence, with two subjects.** This is exactly like the repetition of *ق-د-ر* in the twenty-first verse: there power, here knowledge.

| Verse | Root | Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **21** | ق-د-ر | *lam **taqdirū** ʿalayhā … ʿalā kulli shayʾin **qadīrā*** |
| **27** | ع-ل-م | *fa-**ʿalima** mā lam **taʿlamū*** |

**Two verses build the same structure in two fields: the limit of power and the limit of knowledge.**

### What the sentence says

**The verse ties the difference between what was expected and what happened to a difference in knowledge. This is the thesis of the sūra.**

Consider it this way. A body of people has set out, it has an objective, and it has turned back without reaching it. Someone looking at this picture can say one of three things:

1. **The objective was wrong.** — The verse rejects this: the vision was confirmed **in truth**.
2. **They lacked the strength.** — The verse rejects this: verse 22 said what the outcome would have been had there been fighting; verse 24 recorded that the upper hand had been obtained.
3. **Knowledge was lacking.** — This is what the verse says.

**And what the missing knowledge was had been given two verses earlier:** inside the other side there were believers whose identity was unknown (25). What was "unknown", that is, was not an abstract secret; **it was, concretely, people.**

**I record this as my own reading:** the verse frames the difference in knowledge not as a claim of superiority but as an **explanation**. The hearer is not blamed — it does not say "you should have known". Only the existence of a difference is stated.

**The Qurʾān builds this structure elsewhere too:**

- *"…It may be that you dislike a thing and it is good for you; and it may be that you love a thing and it is bad for you. **God knows and you do not know.**"* (al-Baqara 2/216)
- *"…**I know what you do not know.**"* (al-Baqara 2/30 — to the angels)
- *"And how could you bear patiently with what you have not encompassed in knowledge?"* (al-Kahf 18/68)

**The link with al-Baqara 2/216 is particularly close.** There too the subject is the outcome of a situation that is disliked; there too a difference in knowledge is set down as the ground. **The two verses say the same principle on two different scales:** the one as a general principle, the other about a concrete event.

### The limit of the measure — a note

**A note needs to be entered on how this sentence is to be used, and I write it as my own assessment.**

The sentence *"He knew what you did not know"* is **an explanation looking back at what has happened.** The verse came down after the event, and it is naming an outcome.

**This sentence is not an instrument of decision.** That is, it cannot be used to avoid deciding, thinking or calculating on the grounds that "there must be some wisdom in it anyway". The sūra itself shows this: the community set out, made preparations, negotiated, decided. At no stage was it said "we do not know, so let us not act".

**What the verse does is to change the measure by which an outcome is assessed**; it does not cancel the process of deciding.

### فَجَعَلَ مِن دُونِ ذَٰلِكَ فَتْحًا قَرِيبًا

**مِن دُونِ ذَٰلِكَ** — this construction is read in two ways, and both are valid in Arabic:

| Reading | Its meaning |
|---|---|
| **Time** | "**Before** that" — before the fulfilment of the vision |
| **Degree** | "**Short of / below** that" — something that does not reach the real objective but comes near it |

**Both readings are found in classical exegesis, and they do not exclude one another.** Combined, the sense that emerges is this: **a stage that brings one nearer to the real objective, before it is reached.**

**And this gathers the whole logic of the sūra into a single construction.**

The following picture emerges:

```
THE DREAM     →   [ FATḤ (near) ]     →   THE DREAM FULFILLED
(the promise)      SET IN BETWEEN            (the Sacred Mosque)
```

**A stage has been set in between.** And that stage has been given the sūra's name: **fatḥ.**

**I record this as my own reading:** the reason for the naming in the sūra's first verse lies here. A fatḥ is not the objective itself but the opening of the door that leads to it. And a door is opened **earlier** than the objective is reached. That the verse says *min dūni dhālika* says exactly this: this is not the arrival but the opening that precedes it.

**And the choice of word in the first verse now falls exactly into place.** Had "fatḥ" meant a seizing, this verse would have produced a contradiction — for nothing was seized. If it means "opening", there is no contradiction: the door was opened, and the entering was left for later.

## 48/28 — هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَرْسَلَ رَسُولَهُۥ بِٱلْهُدَىٰ وَدِينِ ٱلْحَقِّ لِيُظْهِرَهُۥ عَلَى ٱلدِّينِ كُلِّهِۦ ۚ وَكَفَىٰ بِٱللَّهِ شَهِيدًا

*Huwa'lladhī arsala rasūlahū bi'l-hudā wa dīni'l-ḥaqqi li-yuẓhirahū ʿalā'd-dīni kullih; wa kafā bi'llāhi shahīdā*

"It is He who sent His Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth — so that He may make it prevail over all religion. And God suffices as a witness."

### The third هُوَ ٱلَّذِى

**It was recorded at the fourth verse: this pattern occurs three times in the sūra** (4, 24, 28), and each time it restricts something to God.

| Verse | What it restricts |
|---|---|
| **4** | The source of inward stillness |
| **24** | The stopping of the fighting |
| **28** | The sending of the Messenger |

**And the third, at the end of the sūra, sets up the widest frame.** The sūra opened with an event, analysed an event, and at its close names the whole within which the event stands.

### A sentence that occurs three times in the Qurʾān

**This sentence occurs in three places in the Qurʾān, in almost exactly the same wording:**

| Verse | Sentence | Its close |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Tawba 9/33** | *huwa'lladhī arsala rasūlahū bi'l-hudā wa dīni'l-ḥaqqi li-yuẓhirahū ʿalā'd-dīni kullih* | *wa law kariha'l-mushrikūn* — "however much the mushrikūn dislike it" |
| **al-Ṣaff 61/9** | The same | *wa law kariha'l-mushrikūn* |
| **al-Fatḥ 48/28** | The same | ***wa kafā bi'llāhi shahīdā*** — "and God suffices as a witness" |

**The difference at the close is striking, and it agrees with the tone of this sūra.**

In the other two places the sentence closes with a sense of "in spite of an opposition": "however much they dislike it". Here the other side is not touched on at all; the close is a sentence of **witness**.

**I record this as my own reading:** this is the stance we have seen throughout the sūra. It was recorded at the first verse: a fatḥ is not an "against" but a "for"; the other side has not been made the subject. One of the sūra's last sentences continues the same stance — a sentence that occurs three times comes, in this sūra precisely, with a close that does not touch on the other side.

### بِٱلْهُدَىٰ وَدِينِ ٱلْحَقِّ

**Two things: guidance and the religion of truth.**

In the classical explanations the distinction is put like this: *hudā* is the side of **knowledge** — the showing of the way; *dīn* is the side of **practice** — the order walked in on that way.

The root analysis of the word **دِين** was made at `107-maun.md` (**د-ي-ن**: debt, submission, receiving what is due, a working order) and recalled at `110-nasr.md`. I do not repeat it.

**دِينِ ٱلْحَقِّ** — what is decisive in the construction is that the religion is ascribed to **truth**. It was recorded for the construction *dīnu'llāh* (the religion of God) in Sūrat al-Naṣr: the owner of the thing being entered is named, and it is not "the religion of the winning side". A similar note applies here: the religion is ascribed not to a community but to **the truth**.

### لِيُظْهِرَهُۥ عَلَى ٱلدِّينِ كُلِّهِۦ

**أَظْهَرَ** — root **ظ-ه-ر**. The concrete sense of the root: **the back.** *Ẓahr* — the back. From this two directions are born, and both are found in the Qurʾān:

- **To be visible** — *ẓāhir*: what comes out, what shows. (The analysis of this name was made at `057-hadid.md`.)
- **To prevail** — *ẓahara ʿalā*: to overcome a thing. The image: laying an opponent on his back, or getting **on top of** something.

**The verb is in Form IV** (*yuẓhira*): "to make visible" or "to make prevail".

**Which of the two senses is meant? There is disagreement in classical exegesis:**

| Reading | Its meaning |
|---|---|
| **To make prevail** | Prevailing over the others |
| **To make visible** | Bringing to light, making known — not a supremacy but a spreading |
| **Superiority in proof** | Prevailing at the level of argument |

**I impose no preference.** But this must be recorded: *ʿalā'd-dīni **kullih*** — "over all religion". The construction is **singular** (*al-dīn*), not plural. In classical exegesis this is explained in two ways: either as a generic noun covering all religions, or as answering to the concept "religion" itself.

**And one further note:** this verse does not describe a historical event; it frames a clause of **purpose** (*li-yuẓhira* — "so that He may make it…"). Clauses of purpose give no information about the time or the manner of fulfilment.

**I do not enter current politics and I draw no contemporary programme from this verse;** this is the field `STYLE.md` leaves outside.

### وَكَفَىٰ بِٱللَّهِ شَهِيدًا

**كَفَىٰ** — root **ك-ف-ي**: to suffice, to be enough. The pattern is a particular one in Arabic: *kafā bi-* — "suffices as…". The additional particle **بِ** is what the grammarians call a reinforcing *bāʾ zāʾida*.

**شَهِيد** — root **ش-ه-د**, treated at the eighth verse: to be present, to see, to report what one has seen.

**And here a frame of the sūra closes:**

| Verse | Word | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **8** | *shāhidā* | **The Messenger** — sent as a witness |
| **28** | *shahīdā* | **God** — suffices as a witness |

**In the eighth verse the first attribute given to the Messenger was *shāhid*. In the twenty-eighth the same root is used of God.** The word that opened the block becomes one of the words that close the sūra.

**And this agrees with the sūra's subject.** A question of testimony has been worked through the length of the sūra: who saw what, who did not know what, what was in the hearts. At the close, the witness of all this is named.

## 48/29 — مُّحَمَّدٌ رَّسُولُ ٱللَّهِ ۚ وَٱلَّذِينَ مَعَهُۥٓ أَشِدَّآءُ عَلَى ٱلْكُفَّارِ رُحَمَآءُ بَيْنَهُمْ ۖ تَرَىٰهُمْ رُكَّعًا سُجَّدًا يَبْتَغُونَ فَضْلًا مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَرِضْوَٰنًا ۖ سِيمَاهُمْ فِى وُجُوهِهِم مِّنْ أَثَرِ ٱلسُّجُودِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ مَثَلُهُمْ فِى ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةِ ۚ وَمَثَلُهُمْ فِى ٱلْإِنجِيلِ كَزَرْعٍ أَخْرَجَ شَطْـَٔهُۥ فَـَٔازَرَهُۥ فَٱسْتَغْلَظَ فَٱسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِۦ يُعْجِبُ ٱلزُّرَّاعَ لِيَغِيظَ بِهِمُ ٱلْكُفَّارَ ۗ وَعَدَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ مِنْهُم مَّغْفِرَةً وَأَجْرًا عَظِيمًۢا

*Muḥammadun rasūlu'llāh; wa'lladhīna maʿahū ashiddāʾu ʿalā'l-kuffāri ruḥamāʾu baynahum; tarāhum rukkaʿan sujjadan yabtaghūna faḍlan mina'llāhi wa riḍwānā; sīmāhum fī wujūhihim min athari's-sujūd; dhālika mathaluhum fi't-Tawrāh; wa mathaluhum fi'l-Injīli ka-zarʿin akhraja shaṭʾahū fa-āzarahū fa'staghlaẓa fa'stawā ʿalā sūqihī yuʿjibu'z-zurrāʿa li-yaghīẓa bihimu'l-kuffār; waʿada'llāhu'lladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū'ṣ-ṣāliḥāti minhum maghfiratan wa ajran ʿaẓīmā*

"Muḥammad is the Messenger of God. Those who are with him are firm against the disbelievers, merciful among themselves. You see them bowing, prostrating, seeking bounty from God and good pleasure. Their mark is on their faces from the trace of prostration. That is their likeness in the Torah. And their likeness in the Gospel is like a crop that puts out its shoot, then strengthens it, then grows thick, and then stands up straight upon its stalk, delighting the sowers — so that through them He may enrage the disbelievers. God has promised those of them who believe and do good works forgiveness and a great reward."

### The longest verse of the sūra

**The sūra closes with the portrait of a community.** And the portrait has two layers: first a **moral and practical** description (the first half), then a **simile of growth** (the second half).

Because the verse is long I shall proceed piece by piece.

### مُّحَمَّدٌ رَّسُولُ ٱللَّهِ

**The name "Muḥammad" occurs in four places in the Qurʾān:** Āl ʿImrān 3/144, al-Aḥzāb 33/40, Muḥammad 47/2, and here.

**And all four are part of a statement**, not of an address. When the Qurʾān speaks to the Prophet it almost always uses patterns of attribute — "O Prophet", "O you wrapped in your cloak"; when it names him it does so in the **third person**, inside a sentence that states something.

**There is disagreement about the parsing of the sentence, and it must be recorded:**

| Reading | Structure | Its meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **The first** | *Muḥammadun* subject, *rasūlu'llāh* predicate | "Muḥammad is the Messenger of God." A new sentence then begins |
| **The second** | *Muḥammadun* subject, *rasūlu'llāh* an adjective/appositive, the predicate being *ashiddāʾu…* | "Muḥammad the Messenger of God and those with him… are firm" |

**Both readings are found in classical exegesis; I impose no preference.** The difference in sense is small: on the first an affirmation of the messengership stands as a separate sentence, on the second the description flows as a single whole.

### وَٱلَّذِينَ مَعَهُۥ — "those who are with him"

**The construction is striking.** The verse does not say "his Companions", "his community", "those who believe in him". It says **"those who are with him"**.

**مَعَ** — the adverb of accompaniment. This word was treated at `057-hadid.md` (57/4, *wa huwa maʿakum*), and it was recorded there that *maʿiyya* is used for more than physical side-by-sideness.

**Two readings are possible here, and both are found in classical exegesis:**

| Reading | Who |
|---|---|
| **Physical accompaniment** | Those who were with him at that moment — those who took part in the event the sūra recounts |
| **Accompaniment of position** | Those who stand on his side; independently of time |

**The two do not conflict.** And the sentence at the end of the verse (*"those of them who believe and do good works"*) shows that the description is attached to an attribute.

### أَشِدَّآءُ عَلَى ٱلْكُفَّارِ رُحَمَآءُ بَيْنَهُمْ

**Two adjectives, two particles. And the particles say more than the adjectives.**

**أَشِدَّاء** — the plural of *shadīd*. Root **ش-د-د**: to bind, to tighten, to make firm. The **shadda** of Arabic grammar (the mark that doubles a letter) is from the same root — the mark that "tightens" the letter.

**At the centre of the root there is not harshness but firmness.** *Shadd* is the tying of a knot fast. The Qurʾān uses this: *"and **strengthen** my back through him"* (Ṭāhā 20/31 — *ushdud bihī azrī*).

**رُحَمَاء** — the plural of *raḥīm*. Root **ر-ح-م**: the same root as *raḥim* (the womb); its analysis was made at `001-fatiha.md`.

**A point to note:** *raḥīm* is among the names of God. Here it is used of human beings, in a plural pattern. The Qurʾān does this elsewhere too: *"…towards the believers he is **raʾūf** and **raḥīm**"* (al-Tawba 9/128) — there too the same two names are used of a human being.

**Now let us look at the particles — this is the finest point of the verse:**

| Adjective | Particle | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| *ashiddāʾu* | **عَلَى** — "against" | A stance turned in one direction, outward |
| *ruḥamāʾu* | **بَيْنَ** — "among themselves" | An inward-facing, reciprocal relation |

**عَلَى runs one way**; upon someone, against someone. **بَيْنَ is reciprocal**; between two parties.

**The verse, that is, describes two different kinds of relation: the one a stance, the other a bond.**

### How this description is not to be read — a note

**Here, per the method (`STYLE.md`), I am obliged to enter an explicit note.**

**One. The verse looks at a position, not at an identity.** The root of the word *al-kuffār* was analysed at `002-bakara.md`: **ك-ف-ر — to cover.** And it was recorded there that the word is used within the same Qurʾān in the sense of "farmer" (`057-hadid.md`, 57/20). The word states an attribute: one who covers, who closes over the truth. Whoever carries that attribute is included in it; it is not the name of an ethnic, national or religious group.

**Two. The word itself frames no command of behaviour in this verse.** The verse is a **description**, not a command. The sentence does not say "be firm"; it says "they are so". Grammatically it is a statement.

**Three. And the Qurʾān itself blocks the turning of this description into a general ruling of enmity:**

> *"God does not forbid you from being good to those who have not fought you over religion and have not driven you from your homes, or from dealing justly with them. God loves those who deal justly."* (al-Mumtaḥina 60/8)

This verse was cited at `059-hasr.md` for the same purpose, and it holds here too.

**Four. And this sūra itself is the plainest proof.** The event the sūra recounts is an event in which **a treaty was made** with the other side. The same sūra records the mutual withholding of hands as a favour (24); it makes the unknown people inside the other side a ground (25). This sūra, that is, is the strongest text standing in the way of any reading that turns the attribute of "firmness" into a programme of conflict.

**I record this as my own reading:** looked at in terms of the root's sense, the adjective *ashiddāʾ* states a **firmness** — not bending easily, not losing one's position. And the verse balances that firmness with a mercy in the same sentence. In describing a community the Qurʾān gives the two sides together; to take one and leave the other is to cut the sentence in half.

### تَرَىٰهُمْ رُكَّعًا سُجَّدًا

**تَرَىٰ** — "you see". The address is **singular**, and this is the same pattern as *lan tajida* ("you will not find") in verse 23 of the sūra: any person who looks.

**رُكَّع** and **سُجَّد** — the plurals of *rākiʿ* and *sājid*, on the **فُعَّل** pattern. In Arabic this pattern is used for plurals stating a **state**: those engaged in an act at that moment.

**رَكَعَ** — root **ر-ك-ع**: to bend, to bow. The concrete use of the word: the bending of an old man's back.
**سَجَدَ** — root **س-ج-د**: to bow down, to lower oneself, to submit. In Arabic *sajada'n-nakhl* — the palm tree bent under the weight of its fruit.

**And the word order: there is no conjunction between the two.** *Rukkaʿan sujjadan* — "bowing, prostrating". In Arabic a sequence without a conjunction states that the two things form **a single picture**: one uninterrupted movement.

### يَبْتَغُونَ فَضْلًا مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَرِضْوَٰنًا

**This construction occurs in one other place in the Qurʾān, in exactly the same wording:**

> *"…for the poor emigrants who were driven from their homes and their possessions, **seeking bounty from God and good pleasure**, and helping God and His Messenger…"* (al-Ḥashr 59/8)

**This construction was treated at `059-hasr.md`:** it was recorded there that *faḍl* carries the sense "what is over and above, what is added on top of what is deserved", and that the word answers both to material gain and to divine favour.

**What the two verses have in common is striking:** both describe a community, and both put at the centre of the description not what they have but **what they are seeking**.

**اِبْتِغَاء** — root **ب-غ-ي**: to seek, to want, to go after. Form VIII conveys an **effort** in the seeking.

**The verb is in the imperfect:** *yabtaghūn* — "they seek", continually. What is described is not something found but a search still under way.

**رِضْوَان** — root **ر-ض-و**, treated at verse 18: an intensive verbal noun, a great good pleasure. And it was recorded at `057-hadid.md`: the Qurʾān most often sets *riḍwān* last, even above forgiveness.

**A link within the sūra:** in verse 18 *raḍiya'llāh* — God was pleased. Here *yabtaghūna… riḍwānā* — they seek good pleasure. The sūra names the good pleasure once as given and once as being sought.

### سِيمَاهُمْ فِى وُجُوهِهِم مِّنْ أَثَرِ ٱلسُّجُودِ

**سِيمَا** — a mark, a token, a sign on the face.

**The lexicographers differ over the root of the word**, and this was recorded at `055-rahman.md`: some attach it to the root **و-س-م** (to brand; it became *sīmā* by a transposition of letters), others to the root **س-و-م**. **I do not speak with certainty.**

**The word was treated at `002-bakara.md` on verse 2/273**, and what was recorded there is decisive here:

> *"One who does not know would take them for rich because of their restraint. **You will know them by their mark.** They do not beg of people insistently."* (al-Baqara 2/273)

**The note entered there was this:** *"recognising them calls not for an enquiry but for attention."* And, more importantly: the people described in that verse were people who **do not display themselves** — those who do not ask, who are taken for rich because of their restraint.

**This is the ground needed for reading the *sīmā* here.** In one of the two clearest places where the Qurʾān uses this word, the bearers of the mark are people **unaware that they carry it**.

**A third use was treated at `055-rahman.md`:** *"The guilty are known by their marks"* (al-Raḥmān 55/41). The word works in both directions.

### What "the trace of prostration" is — a classical disagreement

**What this expression points to has been debated in classical exegesis. I transmit the disagreement as it stands:**

| View | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Light / gravity on the face** | The stillness, brightness and composure that appear on the face of one who worships much |
| **Humility and modesty** | The moral trace prayer leaves in a person; a mark showing in behaviour |
| **A physical trace** | A mark formed on the forehead by prostration |
| **A mark on the Day of Judgement** | That the sign will appear in the hereafter |

**In the classical commentaries the first and second views are widely worked out.** I impose no preference.

**But two notes must be entered, and they are independent of the debate.**

**One. The wording of the verse does not say "a callus on the forehead".** *Sīmā* means "sign", and *fī wujūhihim* is "on their faces" — not the forehead but the **face.** The face is where the whole of a person's expression shows.

**Two — and this is more important:** in the logic of the word itself, a *sīmā* is something **another person reads**, not something its bearer puts on show. When the verse at al-Baqara 2/273 says "you will know them by their mark", those people **were not trying to carry** a mark; the mark was their state showing through.

**I record this as my own reading:** producing a sign in order to display it stops the sign being a sign. This is something the Qurʾān warns of openly elsewhere — the criticism of worship performed for show (treated at `107-maun.md`). The sentence of the verse is a **statement of fact**: there is such a trace in them. It is not a recommendation: it does not say "acquire such a trace".

### ذَٰلِكَ مَثَلُهُمْ فِى ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةِ ۚ وَمَثَلُهُمْ فِى ٱلْإِنجِيلِ

**مَثَل** — root **م-ث-ل**: to resemble, to be the equal of a thing. *Mithāl*, *timthāl*, *tamthīl*, *mumāthil*. In Arabic the word means both "simile" and **"description, portrayal"**.

**There is disagreement about the parsing of the sentence, and it affects the sense:**

| Reading | How it divides |
|---|---|
| **The first** | *"That is their likeness in the Torah."* Full stop. *"And their likeness in the Gospel is: like a crop…"* — **two separate descriptions** |
| **The second** | *"That is their likeness in the Torah and in the Gospel."* The simile of the crop then begins as a new sentence — **one description + a separate simile** |

**Both readings have been defended in classical exegesis. I impose no preference.** The first reading appears to agree better with the marks of pause and is widely preferred; but this is not binding.

**A note.** The verse does not say **which passages** in the texts now in hand these descriptions correspond to, and I make no such matching. A matching of that kind would require a separate discussion about the history of the texts we have, and that is not the subject of this commentary. **I record what the verse says and no more: the description of this community is found in the earlier books as well.**

### كَزَرْعٍ — the simile of the crop

**And now we come to the last and most visual part of the sūra.**

**زَرْع** — root **ز-ر-ع**. And this choice of word settles the whole sense of the simile.

**The root was analysed at `056-vakia.md` (56/63-64), and the distinction drawn there is decisive here:**

| Word | Root | Whose work |
|---|---|---|
| **حَرْث** (*ḥarth*) | ح-ر-ث | **The human being's** — to plough the soil, to cast the seed, to water |
| **زَرْع** (*zarʿ*) | ز-ر-ع | **Not the human being's** — what goes on inside the seed: splitting, taking root, greening |

It was recorded there that Sūrat al-Wāqiʿa hands over the human share in full (*mā taḥruthūn* — "what you sow"), and then draws the line: *"Is it you who make it grow, or are We the ones who make it grow?"* **The sowing is yours; the growing is not.**

**And this verse chooses exactly that word for its simile: *zarʿ*.**

**I record this as my own reading, not as a transmitted report:** the simile is built with the word that names the work the human being does not do. The growth of a community, that is, is likened not to the act of sowing but to the act of **coming up**. This agrees with the stance the sūra has kept from the beginning: in the first verse the fatḥ was not ascribed to any human being, in the fourth the sakīna was sent down, in the twenty-fourth the hands were held back. The last simile looks from the same place.

**And there is this too:** the verse also uses the word *zurrāʿ* (sowers) in the same sentence — the human share is not written off, it is simply positioned as the party that **watches the growing**.

### The four stages

**The simile is built with four verbs, and all four are joined to one another by the conjunction فَ (fāʾ). In Arabic this conjunction conveys succession and swift transition: no sooner has one finished than the next comes.**

| # | Verb | Root | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **أَخْرَجَ شَطْأَهُۥ** | ش-ط-أ | **It put out its shoot** |
| 2 | **فَـَٔازَرَهُۥ** | أ-ز-ر | **It strengthened / supported it** |
| 3 | **فَٱسْتَغْلَظَ** | غ-ل-ظ | **It grew thick** |
| 4 | **فَٱسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِۦ** | س-و-ي / س-و-ق | **It stood up straight on its stalk** |

**Let us now open the four one by one.**

### 1. شَطْء — the shoot

**Root: ش-ط-أ.** *Shaṭʾ* is the **new shoot** that comes out at the side of the crop's main stem. Sister shoots springing from beside a grain, from the base of the first shoot.

**A relative of the word is striking:** **شَاطِئ** (*shāṭiʾ*) — **the bank, the edge, the shore.** It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"from the right **bank** of the valley…"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/30).

**The shared idea is "edge":** *shāṭiʾ* is the edge of water, and *shaṭʾ* the shoot that comes out at the edge of the plant.

**I record this connection made by the lexicographers as an observation**, not as a settled etymological ruling.

**And the meaning of the image is this:** the shoot comes out **from the side**. It is not the continuation of the main stem but something new springing from its base. One grain is sown, and more than one shoot appears around it.

**I record this as my own reading:** the first stage of the simile is a **multiplying**, and the multiplying happens at the side. This is the closest agricultural counterpart to a picture that begins with one person and spreads. At the head of the verse there is a name — *Muḥammadun rasūlu'llāh* — and immediately after it comes *"those who are with him"*.

### 2. آزَرَهُۥ — it strengthened

**Root: أ-ز-ر.** The sense of the root: **strength, power, the loins, the back.**

- **إِزَار** (*izār*) — the cloth tied about the waist, a waist-wrapper. It is known in Turkish as *izar*.
- **أَزْر** (*azr*) — strength, power, the strength of the back.

**The Qurʾān uses this word in a prayer, and that place lights up this verse:**

> *"And appoint for me a helper from my family: my brother Aaron (Hārūn). **Strengthen my back through him**, and make him a partner in my task."* (Ṭāhā 20/29-32 — *ushdud bihī **azrī***)

**The same root, the same idea: strengthening someone's back, someone's loins.**

**And the subject of the verb here is striking.** *Fa-āzarahū* — "it strengthened it". Who strengthens whom?

In the classical explanations there are two readings:

| Reading | Who strengthens whom |
|---|---|
| **The shoot the crop** | The shoots coming out at the side strengthened the main plant |
| **The crop the shoot** | The main plant fed and strengthened the shoots |

**The first reading is the more widely transmitted**, and it agrees with agricultural observation: sister shoots widen the root system and hold the plant up.

**I record this as my own reading:** on the first reading the picture that emerges is this — **the one who comes later strengthens the one who came first.** This agrees with verse 16 of the sūra: there a door was opened to those who would join later.

### 3. ٱسْتَغْلَظَ — it grew thick

**Root: غ-ل-ظ.** Its sense: **to be thick, coarse, hard.** *Ghalīẓ* — thick, dense.

**In Form X** (*istaghlaẓa*): "it grew thick, it took on thickness". The function of this form here is to state **a passage into a state**.

**The Qurʾān uses the root figuratively too, and there the sense becomes plain:** *"They have taken from you **a solemn covenant**"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/21 — *mīthāqan ghalīẓā*); the same construction occurs at al-Aḥzāb 33/7.

**The root, then, conveys not "coarseness" but a firmness that amounts to unbreakability.** For a word given to be *ghalīẓ* is for it to be impossible to undo.

**The meaning of the image:** the slender shoot has now thickened past the point of breaking. This is the most critical stage of the simile — for the most fragile moment in a plant's life is the passage from shoot to stalk.

### 4. ٱسْتَوَىٰ عَلَىٰ سُوقِهِۦ — it stood up straight on its stalk

**اِسْتَوَىٰ** — root **س-و-ي**: to be even, to be level, **to straighten up**, to be complete. This root was taken up at `057-hadid.md` (57/4, *istiwāʾ*); a note of *tanzīh* was entered there because it was used of God. **Here the word is entirely in its concrete sense and is speaking of a plant; that debate has no bearing on this place.**

**The sense of the word here: to be upright, to stand balanced by its own weight.**

**سُوق** — the plural of **سَاق** (*sāq*). Root **س-و-ق**: to drive, to urge on. *Sāq* — leg, trunk, stalk.

- The Qurʾān uses *sāq* of the human leg: *"…she bared her legs"* (al-Naml 27/44); *"leg is wound about leg"* (al-Qiyāma 75/29).
- From the same root comes **سُوق** (market) — the place to which goods are **driven**, or where feet walk.

**That the plural is used is meaningful:** *ʿalā sūqihī* — "upon its stalks", not the singular. Not one stalk but several; all the shoots that came out at the first stage are now standing upright.

### What the four stages say

**Let us look at the simile as a whole.**

```
seed → shoot → support → thickening → standing upright
       (at the side) (mutual) (durability) (independence)
```

**I record this as my own reading and do not present it as a transmitted report:**

**One. The process is by stages.** A *fāʾ* has been set between the four verbs — succession. No stage is skipped. The growth of a community is recounted not as something that happens at a stroke but as **a process with phases**.

**Two. And the process cannot be reversed.** A stalk that has thickened does not become a shoot again. The simile says that past a certain point the fragility is over.

**Three. And this agrees with the name of the sūra.** Fatḥ is "opening"; and the splitting of a seed is an opening too. It was recorded for the word *falāḥ* at `002-bakara.md`: **"to break through an obstacle, to crack the husk, to come to the point of bearing."** The sūra begins with an opening and ends with a growing.

**Four. And in the simile nothing is destroyed.** A text that could be read as a sūra of war builds an agricultural scene in its last sentence. Growth, not destruction; putting down roots, not fighting.

### يُعْجِبُ ٱلزُّرَّاعَ — and a point about a word

**زُرَّاع** — the plural of *zāriʿ*: **sowers, farmers.**

**And something happens here that must be recorded.**

It was treated at `057-hadid.md` (57/20): there the same scene — a plant delighting the farmer — is recounted, and the word used for the farmers is **كُفَّار**. Because the root *ك-ف-ر* means "to cover", and the farmer is the one who covers the seed with soil.

**In this verse, however, the farmers are called *zurrāʿ*. And the reason comes immediately after:**

> *yuʿjibu'z-**زُرَّاعَ** li-yaghīẓa bihimu'l-**كُفَّارَ***

**There are two words in the one sentence:** *zurrāʿ* (sowers) and *kuffār* (the disbelievers). Had *kuffār* been used for the farmers too, the same word would have occurred twice in one sentence in two different senses.

**I record this as my own reading, not as a transmitted report:** here the Qurʾān chooses another word for the farmer, and the sentence does not become confused. This is an instance showing that the choice of word is made according to context. I do not present it as a claim of miracle; the choice of word in the two verses is a datum that can be verified.

**أَعْجَبَ** — root **ع-ج-ب**: to be astonished, to wonder; and to please. From the same root *ʿajīb*, *taʿajjub*, and — note — ***ʿujb*** (self-admiration). The root names "the reaction to something unexpected".

### لِيَغِيظَ بِهِمُ ٱلْكُفَّارَ

**غَيْظ** — root **غ-ي-ظ**: anger seething within, fury. The lexicographers record this difference between *ghayẓ* and *ghaḍab*: *ghaḍab* is anger that shows outwardly, while *ghayẓ* is anger **held in, seething within**. I transmit this distinction as a tendency; there is no firm agreement.

**And now another frame of the sūra closes.**

| Verse | Word | Root | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **26** | **حَمِيَّة** | ح-م-ي | *fī qulūbihim* — in their hearts |
| **29** | **غَيْظ** | غ-ي-ظ | (Within them) |

**Both words name a state that seethes within.** The sūra names the inward state of the other side once in the twenty-sixth verse and once again in the twenty-ninth — and what the two words have in common is that both are words of **heat**.

**And in both places what is set against it is the same:** in the twenty-sixth verse *sakīna* (settling), in the twenty-ninth **the silent growing of a crop**.

**I record this as my own reading:** the sūra closes by describing the two sides at two different temperatures. On one side a seething, on the other a growing. And the growing does not produce the seething **on purpose**; the verse says *li-yaghīẓa* (so that they may be enraged), but the cause of the anger is not a provocation, it is simply **the growing itself**.

This needs to be limited by a note: whether the particle *lām* here conveys causation or outcome is the same question we discussed at the second verse. On the reading of outcome the sentence becomes: "and in the end this enrages them." **I impose no preference.**

### وَعَدَ ٱللَّهُ … مَغْفِرَةً وَأَجْرًا عَظِيمًا

**The sūra's last sentence. And it closes the sūra's frame.**

| Verse | The promise |
|---|---|
| **2** | *li-**yaghfira** laka'llāhu…* — **forgiveness** |
| **29** | *…**maghfiratan** wa ajran ʿaẓīmā* — **forgiveness** |

**The sūra opens with forgiveness and closes with forgiveness.** At the opening to a single person, at the close to a community.

**This is the most striking structural feature of the sūra, and I record it as my own reading:** a sūra of "fatḥ" is framed at both its ends by a declaration of forgiveness. Not by victory but by covering over. This is the same pattern we saw in Sūrat al-Naṣr — there too what was asked after the victory was the seeking of forgiveness.

### مِنْهُم — a particle much dwelt on

**There is a *min* in the sentence:** *alladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilū'ṣ-ṣāliḥāti **مِنْهُمْ***.

**The function of this particle has been debated in classical exegesis, and the disagreement must be recorded:**

| Reading | Its name | Its meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **The first** | *min* **explicative** | "They being those who believe and do good works" — it qualifies the whole and does not divide it |
| **The second** | *min* **partitive** | "Those of them who believe and do good works" — it points to a part of them |

**I impose no preference in this commentary.** In Arabic the particle *min* serves both functions, and which it is in this verse cannot be settled with certainty from the context.

**And this must be recorded plainly:** this grammatical question has been used through history in disputes between schools. **This commentary does not enter that dispute and issues no ruling for either side.** Both readings are possible in the language, and the matter is no more than the function of a particle.

**What can be recorded independently of the dispute is this:** the sentence attaches the promise to an **attribute** — *āmanū wa ʿamilū'ṣ-ṣāliḥāt*, "to believe and to do good works". Whichever reading is taken, what the promise is attached to is not an identity but an attribute. This is the stance the sūra has kept from the beginning (13, 16).

### أَجْرًا عَظِيمًا — the third and last

The expression *ajran ʿaẓīmā* occurs twice in the sūra: 10 and 29. And in verse 16 there was *ajran ḥasanā*.

**In the tenth verse the reward was promised to the one who keeps his word. Here, to the one who believes and does good works.** The sūra closes with works the ledger it opened with an undertaking.

# The sūra as a whole: a few weaves

Read from beginning to end, the sūra shows several weaves. I record them separately.

## One: the three occurrences of the word "fatḥ"

**The word that gives the sūra its name occurs three times in it, and each time it names something different.**

| Verse | Expression | What it names | Its adjective |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | *fataḥnā laka fatḥan mubīnā* | What happened — the treaty, the opening | **Manifest** |
| **18** | *wa athābahum fatḥan qarībā* | The recompense given to those who pledged | **Near** |
| **27** | *fa-jaʿala min dūni dhālika fatḥan qarībā* | The stage set between the dream and its fulfilment | **Near** |

**And read together, the three make plain what the word means.**

In the first verse something is named "a manifest fatḥ". In the twenty-seventh the same thing is described as a stage set **before** arrival at the objective. **A fatḥ, that is, is not the arrival but the opening of the door that stands before it.**

**This is the answer to the question we asked in the sūra's opening lines.** That something looking like a step backwards should be called a "fatḥ" is because the word means not "winning" but "opening". No place was won by the treaty; but something that had been shut was opened.

## Two: three hands

The word *yad* occurs in three places in the sūra, and the three build a story:

| Verse | The hand | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **10** | *yadu'llāhi fawqa aydīhim* | A clasping of hands — a contract is made |
| **20** | *kaffa aydiya'n-nāsi ʿankum* | One side's hand is held back |
| **24** | *kaffa aydiyahum ʿankum wa aydiyakum ʿanhum* | **Both sides' hands are held back** |

**The sūra begins with a clasping of hands and ends with both hands being lowered.**

**I record this as my own reading:** this is the most concrete expression of the sūra's understanding of "fatḥ". There are two things a hand does: grasp and strike. The sūra establishes the first and stops the second.

## Three: the heart

The word **قُلُوب** occurs five times in the sūra, and each time as the place where something unseen is lodged:

| Verse | What is in the heart | Who put it there |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | *Sakīna* | **It was sent down** |
| **11** | What does not agree with what is said | (Not stated) |
| **12** | An adorned thought | **It was made to seem fair** — passive |
| **18** | Something known | (Not stated — it says "He knew") |
| **26** | *Ḥamiyya* | **They put it there** — themselves |

**And all the sūra's distinctions are drawn at this level.** The visible acts — pledging, setting out, turning back — nowhere settle the outcome by themselves.

**And a limit to this is set openly in the verses:** the subject who knows the hearts is in no verse a human being. A measure is set down, but the authority to apply the measure is not given to human beings.

## Four: the axis of knowledge

**Throughout the sūra the verb "to know" forms an axis:**

| Verse | Expression | Who knows / does not know |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | *kāna'llāhu **ʿalīman** ḥakīmā* | It is He who knows |
| **11** | *kāna'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna **khabīrā*** | It is He who knows the inside |
| **15** | *kānū **lā yafqahūna** illā qalīlā* | A lack of grasp |
| **18** | *fa-**ʿalima** mā fī qulūbihim* | The knowing of hearts |
| **25** | *lam **taʿlamūhum*** | People not known |
| **25** | *bi-ghayri **ʿilm*** | Harm that would be done unknowingly |
| **26** | *kāna'llāhu bi-kulli shayʾin **ʿalīmā*** | The declaration of the extent |
| **27** | *fa-**ʿalima** mā lam **taʿlamū*** | **The difference stated** |

**Eight occurrences gathered into one sentence: *"He knew what you did not know."***

And the sūra frames this difference not as an accusation but as an **explanation**. The hearer is not told "you should have known".

## Five: the relation with Sūrat al-Naṣr

**This sūra and Sūrat al-Naṣr (110) share the same two words, and read together they trace an arc.**

It was recorded at `110-nasr.md`: the words *naṣr* and *fatḥ* come side by side in the Qurʾān at three different times, in three different states.

| Sūra | Expression | In what state |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Ṣaff 61/13** | *naṣrun mina'llāhi wa fatḥun qarīb* | **A promise** — not yet come about |
| **al-Fatḥ 48/1-3** | *fataḥnā… wa yanṣuraka* | **The opening** — done, but continuing |
| **al-Naṣr 110/1** | *naṣru'llāhi wa'l-fatḥ* | **Completion** |

**And this was recorded at `110-nasr.md`:** *"It is widely said in the classical commentaries that the verse al-Fatḥ 48/1 came down for the treaty of Ḥudaybiya. No city had been taken there… This is the clearest proof that the word means not 'taking a city' but 'a door opening' — for what was opened at Ḥudaybiya was not territory but contact."*

**In this commentary that note has been confirmed.** What the sūra says within itself — verses 24, 25, 27 — arrives at the same conclusion.

**The two sūras have one further pattern in common:**

| | **al-Fatḥ 48** | **al-Naṣr 110** |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening** | The declaration of a fatḥ | The declaration of a help and a fatḥ |
| **The other side** | Not made the subject | Not named at all |
| **Human act** | Absent at the opening | Absent everywhere |
| **First promise / first command** | **Forgiveness** (2) | **Seeking forgiveness** (110/3) |
| **Close** | **Forgiveness** (29) | **Tawwāb** — the one who answers a turning |

**I record this as my own reading:** the two sūras say the same thing about what is to be done after a victory. The one in the imperative (*wa'staghfirh*), the other in the indicative (*li-yaghfira laka*). And in neither is there a single sentence of celebration, of boasting, or directed at the other side.

## Six: the neighbours in the codex

The sūra stands between **Muḥammad (47)** and **al-Ḥujurāt (49)**. This is the order of the codex, not the order of revelation; even so, the picture that emerges is striking.

- **Sūrat Muḥammad** speaks in the context of fighting, and contains the verse *"Seek forgiveness for your sin, and for the believing men and the believing women"* (47/19) — a sentence directly related to the second verse of this sūra.
- **Sūrat al-Fatḥ** recounts the moment at which the fighting **stops**.
- **Sūrat al-Ḥujurāt** establishes a community's **internal order**: verifying news, making peace, not mocking, guarding against suspicion. And it contains a verse directly connected to verse 11 of this sūra: *"The bedouin say, 'We believe.' Say: You do not believe; say rather, 'We have submitted'"* (49/14).

**Three sūras in a row trace a sequence:** the fighting, the stopping of the fighting, and the order to be established once it has stopped.

**I record this as an observation about the arrangement**; it is not a claim about the order of revelation.

## Seven: the sūra's texture of sound and closing

Throughout the sūra the verse-endings close largely with a long vowel plus *alif*, in forms such as **-īmā / -īrā / -īzā / -īlā**: *mubīnā, mustaqīmā, ʿazīzā, ḥakīmā, ʿaẓīmā, maṣīrā, nadhīrā, aṣīlā, khabīrā, būrā, saʿīrā, raḥīmā, qalīlā, alīmā, qarībā, qadīrā, naṣīrā, tabdīlā, baṣīrā, ʿalīmā, shahīdā…*

This consistency is not always found in the long Medinan sūras. The sūra's long verses, carrying rulings and analyses, have been held together by a regular closing sound.

**I record this not as a claim of miracle**, but as an observation about the sound-character of the text.

# What this says to the present

**One. The problem of naming an outcome.**

This is the sūra's most general lesson, and it requires no religious context: **the meaning of an event lies not in the event itself but in what it opens.**

A meeting ends without result, an offer is turned down, a plan is cut short. The picture visible at that moment is a loss. But that picture is not the **whole** of the event — only its visible part.

What the sūra does is to show that the same event can be given two names: a step backwards and an opening. And which name is the right one becomes clear not then but later.

**Its practical counterpart is this:** in assessing an outcome, the question to ask may be not "what did I gain?" but **"what was opened?"** If a closed relationship has become one that can be talked about, if a party is no longer seen as an enemy, if a door has been left ajar — there is something to be entered in the column of gains, and most of the time it is never counted at all.

**Two. Knowing what one does not know.**

The human counterpart of the sentence *"He knew what you did not know"* is not a call to submission; it is a **measure**.

The information in our hands when we take a decision is not the whole of the information that will bear on that decision. This is as ordinary a truth as it is a theological one: every decision is taken with incomplete information. And the outcomes of decisions most often turn on a variable that was not visible at the moment of deciding.

**The conclusion is not: "then do not decide."** The community the sūra recounts set out, made preparations, negotiated. The conclusion is this: **confidence in a decision and confidence in an outcome are not the same thing.** The ground of a decision may be sound; the outcome may still turn out other than expected. And that does not show the decision to have been wrong.

**Three. The structure of an excuse.**

The eleventh verse describes how an excuse is framed, and the description holds for today as well:

- **An excuse makes the person passive:** "our possessions kept us occupied" — the subject is the possessions, not the person.
- **The content of an excuse is most often true:** possessions and family do genuinely occupy a man.
- **And the trouble with an excuse is not that it is false but that it is insufficient.**

**And the verse's measure lies here:** the excuse is assessed by looking at whether what is said with the tongue agrees with what is in the heart. This measure cannot be applied to another person — the verse ascribes it to God. But it is a measure a person can apply to **himself**, and that is probably its only right use.

**Four. Protecting the genuine excuse.**

And immediately afterwards, the seventeenth verse: *"There is no constraint upon the blind, no constraint upon the lame, no constraint upon the sick."*

**This sequence is important, and it is a direct measure for today.** When a community begins to notice false excuses, its greatest risk is this: **treating every excuse as suspect.** The sentence "everybody finds a pretext" crushes the person who genuinely cannot.

The verse prevents this: immediately after the criticism of excuses, it takes those with a genuine obstacle separately and explicitly under protection. **Whoever sets a measure is obliged to define those who fall outside it as well.**

**Five. Heat and stillness.**

The contrast of the twenty-sixth verse — *ḥamiyya* against *sakīna* — is perhaps where the sūra looks most directly at the present.

In an argument, a negotiation, a disagreement, two inward states are possible: **growing hot** and **growing still.** And the side that grows hot feels itself, at that moment, to be in the right — anger produces its own justification, and it generally speaks in a language of honour.

**What the verse does is not to criticise anger in a moral idiom; it is to name it as a *state*.** *Ḥamiyya* — a heating. That naming stops the anger from being a ground.

**And what is set against it is not patience or endurance but *sakīna*** — that is, **stopping.** What is won in an argument is most often what the calmest side wins; and that is not a matter of virtue but of **state**.

**Six. The people who are not seen.**

The measure of the twenty-fifth verse is perhaps the most concrete of all for today.

What is shown as the ground for stopping a fight is the **unknown people** inside the other side. And the verb chosen is *to trample* — without looking, without seeing, without distinguishing.

**What this says to the present is this:** when a body of people is looked at as a whole, the single human beings inside it become invisible. And the verse makes that invisibility a **ground for stopping** an action.

**And one further note:** the verse says that harm done "unknowingly" also produces a consequence. "I did not know", that is, is not an excuse but **a reason for precaution.**

**Seven. Growth by stages.**

The four verbs of the last verse — shoot, support, thickening, standing upright — describe a process in which no stage is skipped.

**Its counterpart is plain:** things that last do not grow fast, and at every stage of their growing they are fragile. And the most critical moment is the one at which the shoot becomes a stalk — still slender, but no longer in the soil.

**And the simile has one further side:** *āzarahū* — the shoots that come out later strengthen the main plant. The one who comes later holds up the one who came first. The measure of a structure is not whether its founders can carry it but whether **those who come after** can.

**Eight. Forgiveness after victory.**

The sūra is framed at both ends by a declaration of forgiveness (2 and 29). In Sūrat al-Naṣr the same pattern stood as a command.

The observation recorded at `110-nasr.md` holds here too and is worth repeating: **success tends to justify, retrospectively, the methods that led to it.** When the outcome turns out well, everything done along the way is counted as right.

That the Qurʾān insistently sets fatḥ and forgiveness side by side cancels exactly that automatic endorsement.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

- **That the sūra came down on the return from Ḥudaybiya** is widely transmitted in the classical sources, and in this commentary it was given in the idiom of "it is transmitted". The clauses of the treaty, its place, the names of the parties and the wording of what was said were not presented as certain knowledge, **because they are not in the Qurʾān**. The only place name that occurs in the Qurʾān is **Mecca** (24).
- **The classical explanations of the expression *mā taqaddama min dhanbika wa mā taʾakhkhar* at 48/2** were given in **a table of six items**. The disagreement was not concealed, **no preference was imposed**, and no speculation was made. It was recorded openly that the question of the prophets' *ʿiṣma* is a **theological debate** and that the schools differ over its extent.
- **Whether the *lām* at 48/2 conveys causation or outcome** is disputed; four explanations were given, and no preference was adopted. The same debate was recorded for *li-yaghīẓa* in verse 29.
- **Whether faith increases or not** (48/4) is one of the oldest debates in the history of theology. **The two views and their grounds were given in a table, and no side was taken.** It was recorded that the question arises from a **difference of definition**.
- **At the expression *yadu'llāhi fawqa aydīhim* (48/10) a note of *tanzīh* was entered openly**, and al-Shūrā 42/11 was set as the boundary. The same care was kept as in the notes entered at `057-hadid.md` (57/4 *istiwāʾ* and 57/29 *bi-yadi'llāh*) and at `002-bakara.md` (2/115 *wajhu'llāh*). **Six classical explanations were given in a table; no side was taken in the debate between *tafwīḍ* and *taʾwīl*.**
- **To whom the pronouns at 48/9 refer** is disputed; three readings were given. The third reading (all of them referring to the Messenger) was not adopted, but it was stated that this is **not a preference but the limit of the meaning of the word *tasbīḥ***.
- **On who the "people fierce in war" at 48/16 are**, different names are mentioned in classical exegesis; **no name was written down, because the reports differ.**
- **On the tree at 48/18**: the Qurʾān gives neither its species nor its place. The reports that its place could not be determined in the later period and that ʿUmar had the tree cut down were transmitted **in the idiom of "it is transmitted"** and **were not presented as certain knowledge.**
- **Which event the "near fatḥ" at 48/18 and 48/27 is** is disputed; three views were given in a table, and no preference was imposed.
- **What the *kalimat al-taqwā* at 48/26 is** is disputed; four views were given, and no preference was adopted. It was recorded that the Qurʾān does not give the wording.
- **Whether the root ح-م-ي gathers the sense-clusters of "heat" and "protection" under one root** is disputed among the lexicographers. The note entered at `101-karia.md` was kept here too: **no meaning was drawn from that shared sound.**
- **On the parsing of 48/29** (the predicate of *Muḥammadun rasūlu'llāh*; the division of the Torah and Gospel descriptions) two readings were given in a table; no preference was imposed.
- **On *sīmāhum… min athari's-sujūd* at 48/29**, four views were given in a table, and no preference was adopted. On the root of the word *sīmā* (و-س-م / س-و-م) **no certainty was claimed**; the note at `055-rahman.md` was kept.
- **Whether the particle *minhum* at 48/29 is partitive or explicative** is disputed. Two readings were given, **no preference was imposed**, and it was stated that this grammatical question has been used in disputes between schools — **which dispute was not entered.**
- **On which passages in the texts now in hand the Torah and Gospel descriptions at 48/29 correspond to, no matching whatever was made.**
- **The expression *ashiddāʾu ʿalā'l-kuffār* at 48/29** was read as a **description** and not as a command; that the root ك-ف-ر states an attribute and is not the name of an ethnic or religious group was recorded on the basis of `002-bakara.md`; al-Mumtaḥina 60/8 was cited as the boundary verse. **No wholesale ruling was issued about any group.**
- **That the word *aʿrāb* means not "the Arabs" but "the bedouin"** was recorded on the basis of `049-hucurat.md`; it was shown that the particle *min* conveys the partitive and that the ruling is attached to a behaviour and not to a people.
- **The application of the concept of *sunnat Allāh* to contemporary events was not entered into**; that the concept is a measure for understanding the past and not an authority for judging the present was recorded **as my own assessment**. Current politics was not entered.
- **The following readings are mine and were not presented as transmitted reports:** that the "separating" aspect of the adjective *mubīn* sums up the structure of the sūra; that the perfect tense in the first verse needs no figure of speech; the reading of *naṣran ʿazīzā* as "a help that does not make its receiver smaller"; that the sakīna is not a place of arrival but a ground; the contrast between *mukhallaf* and *mustakhlaf*; that an excuse makes its speaker passive; the weave of roots between *yanqaliba* and *qulūbikum*; the ب-د-ل link between 15 and 23; that the mutual withholding of hands is not presented as a one-sided favour; that the word *zarʿ* names the work the human being does not do; the choice of word between *zurrāʿ* and *kuffār*; that *ḥamiyya* and *ghayẓ* are the words of heat at the two ends of the sūra; and that the sūra is framed by forgiveness. **The textual data they rest on (roots, patterns, particles, the order of verses) were shown separately in each case.**
