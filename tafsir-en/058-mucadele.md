# 58. Sūrat al-Mujādala

Twenty-two verses. Medinan. It takes its name from the verb **تُجَادِلُكَ** (she disputes with you) in the first verse.

The name of the sūra is read in two ways in the sources: **al-Mujādala** (the dispute) and **al-Mujādila** (the woman who disputes). Both are different vocalisations of the same word; the first names the event, the second the person. It is also reported that it is called "the sūra of ẓihār" — from the subject of the first four verses.

## Why the opening of the sūra is without parallel

There is no other instance in the Qurʾān of a sūra that opens upon the complaint of a single unnamed person.

The sūra begins thus: *"God has heard the words of the woman who disputes with you about her husband and lays her complaint before God."*

To see what this sentence does, one must first look at what it does not do. The sentence announces no ruling. It gives no command. It tells no story. The only thing it does is **to record that something was heard.**

And the owner of the voice that was heard is this: a woman; left in suspense by her husband with a formula counted irreversible in the law of that period; therefore neither married nor divorced; able neither to build a new life nor to return to the old one. That is, someone standing in the legally weakest position there is.

This person comes to the Prophet, does not accept the answer she is given, and disputes. And the result is this: **a sūra of the Qurʾān opens with her sentence.**

There is no need to overstate the matter here, because the text itself already speaks clearly enough. But it must also be given its due. This is a measure of how open a legal order is to the voice of the individual, and the measure lies here: the owner of the voice is not powerful. The voice of the powerful is heard in every order; the character of an order shows itself in what it does with the voice of the powerless.

And a second point to be noticed: the woman **disputes with the Prophet.** The text does not soften this; it does not say "she asked", "she consulted", "she submitted". *Tujādiluka* — in a pattern that conveys mutual contention, "she disputes with you". The answer the Prophet gave at that moment was not a ruling; and the woman did not stop at that answer. The sūra places this insistence on record and does not disapprove of it.

## The structure of the sūra

The sūra consists of four sections, and the sections are fastened to one another by verbal links:

| Verses | Subject | The word that links the section |
|---|---|---|
| **1-4** | Ẓihār: the annulling of a formula, and the expiation | *ḥudūd Allāh* (4) |
| **5-6** | Those who stand on the opposite side, and the day of reckoning | *yuḥāddūna* (5) — the same root as the *ḥudūd* of the preceding verse |
| **7-13** | *Najwā*: secret talk, the etiquette of the assembly, private audience with the Prophet | the *najwā* chain |
| **14-22** | The two sides: *ḥizb al-shayṭān* and *ḥizb Allāh* | *yuḥāddūna* (20) — a repetition of the fifth verse |

The same word in the fifth and the twentieth verses takes the second half of the sūra into a frame. And the root of that word is the same as that of *ḥudūd Allāh* in the fourth verse: **ح-د-د.** Boundary. The sūra begins with the drawing of a boundary and ends with the description of those who stand on the far side of the boundary.

## An observation: *Allāh* in every verse

There is an observation frequently recorded about this sūra: **it is the only sūra in the Qurʾān in which the name of majesty (the name *Allāh*) occurs in every single verse without exception.** I counted all twenty-two verses one by one; the observation is correct — in some verses it occurs once, in some three times.

This must be recorded not as a game with numbers but as a stylistic choice; per the method (`STYLE.md`) I keep clear of claims resting on letter and number reckonings. But the choice itself is meaningful, and it overlaps with the subject of the sūra: from beginning to end the sūra is about **concealment** — a word spoken behind a closed door, the whisper of three people, a sentence passed through the mind, an oath sworn falsely. And in every verse the same name is repeated. The form of the text carries the claim of the text: there is nowhere hidden.

## جدل — the root itself

**Root: ج-د-ل.** The original sense given by the lexicographers is **to twist hard, to plait tightly**. *Al-jadīl* means a tightly plaited strap or rope; *majdūl* is that which has been plaited.

How the step to *jadal* (dispute) was made from here is plain: two people disputing twist the word between them. Each pulls one end of the rope towards himself. In the logic of the root, disputing is a work of **pulling and twisting**.

Some lexicographers attach the word to *al-jadala* (hard ground, earth); on this account *mujādala* is to throw one's opponent to the ground in wrestling. Both derivations are found in the sources; on which of them is the original I do not speak with certainty. But both give the same picture: *jadal* is a work in which two sides exert force upon one another.

**Pattern: Form III (*mufāʿala*).** Not *jadala* but *jādala*. This form conveys reciprocity (*qātala*: he fought with another). That is, the woman does not speak alone and fall silent; there is a two-sided conversation here, going back and forth. The word in the next clause, *taḥāwurakumā* (the conversation of you two), will say this outright.

The Qurʾān does not always blame *jadal*. In one place it commands it openly: *"And dispute with them in the best way"* (al-Naḥl 16/125). In another it counts it the most marked characteristic of man: *"Man is, above all else, given to disputation"* (al-Kahf 18/54). That is, *jadal* is an act that takes its value from its subject and its manner. The *jadal* at the opening of this sūra is not praised, but neither is it blamed — its outcome was a revelation.

## The occasion of revelation: the reports and their limits

What the text of the Qurʾān says is this: there was a woman; she disputed with the Prophet about her husband; she laid her complaint before God; God heard this conversation. Then the ruling on ẓihār comes.

**What the Qurʾān does not say:** the woman's name, her husband's name, how many years they had been married, whether they had children, where the dispute took place, what exactly the woman said.

All of this comes from the reports. In the reports the woman's name is generally given as **Khawla** (in some transmissions Huwayla) bint Thaʿlaba, and her husband's name as **Aws b. al-Ṣāmit**. Even in the name itself there is variation between the transmissions — Khawla/Huwayla, and in the father's name variants such as Thaʿlaba/Mālik b. Thaʿlaba.

The main line of the narrative is transmitted thus: an elderly man, in a moment of anger, speaks the ẓihār formula to his wife; then he regrets it; the woman comes to the Prophet and asks whether this formula has ended the marriage; the Prophet, since no ruling had yet been sent down, gives an answer to the effect of "I know nothing except that you have become forbidden to him"; the woman is not satisfied with this and goes on pressing, saying what will become of her children and their livelihood; and at this point the verses come down.

This narrative is widespread in the classical sources and is consistent with the first verses of the sūra. At the level of detail, however, the reports part company with one another, and for that reason I give all of them here with the note "so it is transmitted".

**And this point must be dwelt on: the Qurʾān gives no name.** This has two consequences, and both are important.

The first: had the verse said *"He heard the words of Khawla"*, the event would have been closed upon one person. When it says *"He heard the words of the woman who disputes with you"*, the mould stays open. Whoever is in that position, the verse is speaking of her.

The second: that no name is given shows that the record is made of the **voice**, not of the personality. What is recorded is not a fame but a having-been-heard.

## Its being Medinan

There is no disagreement that the sūra came down in the Medinan period, and the text itself supports this: a regulation of family law such as ẓihār, the command of zakāt (13), the ordering of the assembly, the presence of the hypocrites, and the Prophet's standing in a position where a private audience is requested of him as of a head of state — all of these are elements of Medina.

It is not possible to give a definite year. The relation between hypocrite and ally described from the fourteenth verse onward shows an affinity with the picture in al-Ḥashr, the sūra that follows; this suggests that the two sūras may have come down in periods close to one another. But I record this as a conjecture, not as a dating.

## The basmala

The word-by-word analysis of the basmala was made in Sūrat al-Fātiḥa; I do not repeat it here. There is a point particular to this sūra: the first sentence of a text that opens with the names al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm is the hearing of a complaint. Mercy, in the sense that lies in the root of the word (*raḥim* — the womb), is an act of enfolding and protecting. The first scene of the sūra is the hearing of the voice of someone whom no one is enfolding and protecting.

## 58/1 — قَدْ سَمِعَ ٱللَّهُ قَوْلَ ٱلَّتِى تُجَٰدِلُكَ فِى زَوْجِهَا وَتَشْتَكِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ وَٱللَّهُ يَسْمَعُ تَحَاوُرَكُمَآ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ سَمِيعٌۢ بَصِيرٌ

*Qad samiʿa'llāhu qawla'llatī tujādiluka fī zawjihā wa tashtakī ila'llāh, wa'llāhu yasmaʿu taḥāwurakumā, inna'llāha samīʿun baṣīr*

"God has heard the words of the woman who disputes with you about her husband and lays her complaint before God. God hears the conversation of you two. Surely God is hearing, seeing."

### The first word of the sentence

For its first word after the basmala, the sūra chooses **قَدْ**.

*Qad*, when it comes at the head of a perfect (*māḍī*) verb, conveys **taḥqīq** (assertion of certainty): it has happened, it is certain, it is done. English has no exact equivalent; "indeed", "truly", "in fact" can carry it, but none sits quite right. The nearest thing is to put an emphasis at the end of the sentence: "He heard — He really did hear."

The function of this particle is to cut off in advance a doubt that might arise. What was the doubt? The woman's own situation. When a person voices a complaint that no one is listening to, the first thing that comes to mind is that it has gone unheard. *Qad* is placed at exactly this point.

### سَمِعَ — "He heard"

**Root: س-م-ع.** To hear.

That the verb is in the **perfect** matters. Not "God hears" (a general statement of an attribute) but **"He heard"** — a hearing that is particular, that has taken place, that has a history. Not an attribute but an event is announced.

And the verb comes at the head of the sentence. In Arabic it is ordinary for the verb to stand first in a verbal sentence; but what is striking here is **what the first declarative sentence of the sūra is**. The sūra does not open with "God has commanded such-and-such" or "We have sent down this ruling". The first thing done is the recording of an act of listening.

**Why "He heard" and not "He knew"?** There is a difference between the two, and the difference builds the whole of the sūra:

- **Knowing** happens at a distance. Knowledge requires no contact with what is known.
- **Hearing** demands nearness. The one who hears is within the distance the voice reaches. Hearing implies that speaker and hearer share the same space.

Had the verse said "He knew", it would have said something true and nothing would have changed. When it says "He heard", a third party enters the room in which the woman is speaking.

And this choice runs on through the sūra: in the seventh verse, where three people whisper together, it will say "He is the fourth of them". The same idea, the same nearness.

### قَوْلَ ٱلَّتِى — "the words of the woman"

**قَوْل** — the word that is spoken. The object of the verb is not the woman herself but **her words.** That is, the verse does not say "He heard that woman", it says "He heard her words". What is recorded is not a person's existence but **the content of what she said.**

**ٱلَّتِى (*allatī*)** — the feminine singular relative pronoun: "she who". There is no name. What this does we discussed above.

A grammatical note: the relative pronoun itself conveys definiteness — that is, not "a woman" but "the woman". The one addressed knows who it is. The text points to a known person without giving her name. This can also be read as a regard for privacy: the event goes on record but the person is not exposed.

### تُجَٰدِلُكَ — "she disputes with you"

The verb is in the **imperfect** (*muḍāriʿ*). The preceding verb (*samiʿa*) was perfect. This transition is meaningful: the hearing is complete, whereas the dispute **is still going on.** The imperfect keeps the scene alive — the reader finds the event not as a story that has ended but as a conversation still in progress.

**Form III (*mufāʿala*).** We discussed it above: reciprocity. And the one addressed is directly the Prophet: *tujādilu-**ka*** — "with you".

A balance has to be struck here. To draw from this verse a general conclusion such as "disputing with the Prophet is good" is to force the text; indeed the Qurʾān elsewhere explicitly forbids going ahead of the Prophet and raising the voice (al-Ḥujurāt 49/1-2). But this too stands in the text: **this dispute is not blamed.** No warning comes to the woman, no correction of her behaviour. On the contrary, the sūra carries her name.

What determines the difference must be the **subject** of the dispute. The woman speaks not for a privilege of her own but for the resolution of a situation left null. This is an inference; I do not present it as a firm report.

### وَتَشْتَكِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ — "and she complains to God"

The finest point of the verse is here, and the translations most often flatten it.

**Two verbs, two distinct addressees:**

| Verb | Addressee | What is being done |
|---|---|---|
| *tujādilu-**ka*** | The Prophet ("you") | Dispute, objection, insistence |
| *tashtakī **ila'llāh*** | God | Complaint, the pouring out of what is within |

The woman disputes with the Prophet but does not direct her complaint at him. The complaint is to God. That is, this is not a complaint against the Prophet — it is a complaint against the existing situation itself. The person she disputes with and the authority she complains to are separate.

This distinction also explains the attitude of the text towards the woman. One who directs her complaint to God has not rejected the seat of judgment; **she is conveying to the highest authority that she is not satisfied with the ruling itself.**

**Root: ش-ك-و.** The lexicographers attach this root to *al-shakwa*: a small waterskin, a leather vessel. On this account *shikāya* (complaint) is the pouring out of what is inside the vessel. At the centre of the word lies "emptying" — the bringing out of something that has collected within.

This derivation is mentioned in the classical lexicons; one must be cautious about its certainty, but it agrees with the usage of the word. A complaint is not the giving of information; it is the emptying of something that has overflowed.

**Pattern: Form VIII (*iftiʿāl*) (تَشْتَكِى).** This form turns the verb back upon the subject and adds inwardness and effort. That is, not a simple "she said"; she brought out into the open something she drew from within herself.

### وَٱللَّهُ يَسْمَعُ تَحَاوُرَكُمَآ

The sentence turns once more here and the verb of hearing is repeated — this time in the **imperfect**: *yasmaʿu*, "He hears". The first verb was perfect (He heard that conversation); this one is general (He hears every conversation).

**تَحَاوُر** — one of the most beautiful words of the sūra.

**Root: ح-و-ر.** The original sense of the root is **to turn, to turn back**. *Ḥāra yaḥūru*: he turned. From here *muḥāwara*: the **going and coming** of speech between two sides. Conversation, in the word's own logic, is a movement of departure and return; a word is thrown, it comes back, it is thrown again.

The most striking use of this root in the Qurʾān is in Sūrat al-Inshiqāq: *"He supposed that he would never return"* — **إِنَّهُ ظَنَّ أَن لَّن يَحُورَ** (al-Inshiqāq 84/14). There "returning" is the return to the hereafter; here, to speech. The same root names two returns, one cosmic and one everyday.

From the same root are also derived **ḥurr/ḥawar** (the sharp distinction between the white and the black of the eye) and, disputably, **ḥawārī**. I record these as a possibility; the lexicographers differ on how these branches of the root connect to one another.

**Pattern: Form VI (*tafāʿul*).** One step beyond Form III: a reciprocity that **continues** between the parties. *Taḥāwur* is not a single exchange of words but a conversation in progress.

**كُمَآ — the dual.** Arabic has a separate inflection for the dual, and it is used here: "of you two". This is not a grammatical necessity but a choice — "your conversation" (plural) could also have been said.

The dual pattern frames the two sides as **equals**. In the sentence one is a prophet, the other a woman whose name is not given; but the grammar puts the two into a single dual. This is not a ruling but an observation about the word order; still, the observation stands in the text.

### إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ سَمِيعٌۢ بَصِيرٌ

The verse closes with two names, and the choice of the two is no accident.

**سَمِيع (*Samīʿ*)** — in the intensive form (فَعِيل): the one who hears constantly and completely. Three times through the verse the verb of hearing occurred; at the close it becomes an attribute.

**بَصِير (*Baṣīr*)** — seeing. But why? Nowhere in the verse did a verb of seeing occur.

In the classical commentaries this explanation is given, and it fits the text: **words are heard, condition is seen.** The woman spoke — her words were heard. But there was something more that words could not convey: her face, her age, the state of her children, the condition of her house, the look of her helplessness. The complaint is the part that comes into speech; **the condition is the part that does not come into speech.** The two names take in both these layers at once.

From another angle: hearing depends on the one who speaks — if she does not say it, it is not heard. Seeing does not depend on the one who speaks. When the two are named together, the possibility "you are known only as far as you can tell it" is closed off.

### Context — how this verse builds the sūra

This verse lays the ground for all the rulings that come after it, and the ground is this: **the ruling is built upon a situation that has been heard.**

The order is striking. First the complaint, then the ruling. The Qurʾān could have opened the matter of ẓihār as an abstract article of law: "Whoever says such-and-such to his wife shall do such-and-such." It does not. First it records a woman's voice, then it gives the ruling. That is, the ruling is an answer to a problem; not a rule hanging in the air.

And this sequence is preserved through the rest of the sūra as well: in the eleventh verse the matter of the assembly arises from a situation, in the twelfth the alms condition from a behaviour, in the fourteenth the description of the hypocrite from a concrete alliance.

### What this says to the present

**One. The legitimacy of complaint.** The side of the verse most easily passed over is this: the one who complains is not criticised. Given how often "complaint" is treated in religious language as a weakness, an impatience, a lack of manners, this note matters. The Qurʾān says something similar through the mouth of Jacob (Yaʿqūb): *"I complain of my grief and my sorrow only to God"* (Yūsuf 12/86). That is, there is a forbidden form of complaint — the kind directed at people and turned into a settling of scores; and there is a legitimate form — the kind directed at its source.

**Two. The recording of a voice.** The most concrete thing this verse says to the present is where the quality of an order is to be measured: in what happens to the weakest person who objects. The objection of the powerful is listened to everywhere. The measure is not there.

**Three. That the answer did not come at once.** According to the reports, when the woman came there was no ruling to hand and the Prophet was left having to say "I do not know". This is visible in the text itself as well — the ruling comes in the second verse, not in the first. To be able to say that one does not know is the condition for the right answer coming afterwards. One who has no answer in hand, so long as he does not invent an answer, has kept the matter open.

## 58/2 — ٱلَّذِينَ يُظَٰهِرُونَ مِنكُم مِّن نِّسَآئِهِم مَّا هُنَّ أُمَّهَٰتِهِمْ إِنْ أُمَّهَٰتُهُمْ إِلَّا ٱلَّٰٓـِٔى وَلَدْنَهُمْ وَإِنَّهُمْ لَيَقُولُونَ مُنكَرًا مِّنَ ٱلْقَوْلِ وَزُورًا وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَعَفُوٌّ غَفُورٌ

*Alladhīna yuẓāhirūna minkum min nisāʾihim mā hunna ummahātihim, in ummahātuhum illa'llāʾī waladnahum, wa innahum la-yaqūlūna munkaran mina'l-qawli wa zūrā, wa inna'llāha la-ʿafuwwun ghafūr*

"Those among you who make ẓihār against their wives — those women are not their mothers. Their mothers are only those who gave them birth. They are indeed uttering an abhorrent and baseless word. And surely God is much pardoning, much forgiving."

### ظهار — a form of divorce from the Jāhiliyya

**Root: ظ-ه-ر.** The back. From the same root: *ẓāhir* (what is outward, what is visible — the back being turned outward), *ẓuhūr* (to come into view), *ẓahīr* (one who backs another, a supporter).

*Ẓihār* is, in the Arabia of the Jāhiliyya, a man's saying to his wife: **"You are to me as my mother's back."** Once this formula was spoken, in that order the woman was counted forbidden to her husband forever.

**Why "the back"?** Several explanations are given in the classical sources; none of them is certain:

| Explanation | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| A metaphor from the back of a mount | An allusion in Arabic connected with "riding"; the back is what is ridden | The existence of the allusion is not certain |
| Naming the part and meaning the whole | A part of the body is named and the whole intended | It does not explain why "the back" was chosen |
| An indirect expression out of covering and decency | Instead of saying it directly, the most neutral limb was named | Plausible, but not resting on transmission |

I adopt no firm preference. But this is striking: the root of the word means **the face that shows**. Ẓihār is a likeness in appearance — and the sūra will say exactly that appearance does not change reality.

### What is the regulation doing?

I issue no legal ruling here; I set out the logic of the regulation.

The consequence of ẓihār in the Jāhiliyya was this: the woman was left **neither divorced nor married.**

- The marriage bond legally continued — hence the woman could not marry another.
- But the husband could not approach her — hence the marriage was in fact over.

This is a position that leaves the woman in an indefinite suspense. She can neither go forward nor turn back. And what builds this suspense is a single sentence — a sentence, moreover, most often spoken in anger. Her maintenance, her children, her future, all freeze inside that sentence.

**The operation the Qurʾān performs has three steps:**

1. **It refutes the truth-claim of the formula** (this verse): "those women are not their mothers."
2. **It characterises the formula**: *munkar* and *zūr* — that is, the formula is not a legal disposition but an abhorrent and baseless piece of speech.
3. **But neither does it leave the formula without consequence** (verses 3-4): it attaches an expiation to it.

The third step calls for particular attention. The Qurʾān does not say "this formula is void, forget it and be done". The spoken word comes to have a price. Why?

Because to leave the formula entirely without consequence makes it **free of charge.** What is free is repeated. The expiation, while it makes the formula retractable, also makes the retraction cost something. That is, the regulation does two things at once: **it takes the woman down from suspense, and it loads a price onto the one who spoke.**

The consequence of this is: a sentence a man builds in a moment of anger can no longer lock another person's life. The lock climbs onto the man's own back.

### مَّا هُنَّ أُمَّهَٰتِهِمْ — "they are not their mothers"

**A point of grammar.** The **مَا** here is a particle of negation that governs as *laysa* does in the Ḥijāzī dialect: it puts its subject in the nominative and its predicate in the accusative. Accordingly *ummahātihim* is read not as genitive but as accusative (*ummahātihim* — its kasra appearance is due to the construct).

The best-known instance of the same structure in the Qurʾān: *"This is no mortal"* — **مَا هَٰذَا بَشَرًا** (Yūsuf 12/31). The same pattern, the same function: to declare in a decisive manner that a thing is not a thing.

**The function of the sentence.** This sentence is not a ruling but a **statement of fact**. It does not say "do not count them as mothers"; it says "they are not mothers". The difference matters: the first would have been a prohibition, and prohibitions can be broken; the second is a declaration of a fact, and facts cannot be broken.

### إِنْ أُمَّهَٰتُهُمْ إِلَّا ٱلَّٰٓـِٔى وَلَدْنَهُمْ

**A structure of restriction (*ḥaṣr*).** The pattern *in … illā* conveys limitation in Arabic: "only and only". That is, the definition of motherhood is closed: **those who give birth.**

What is being done here is the fixing of the source of a definition. What is motherhood? A status established by a word, or an event that has taken place? The verse says the second: motherhood comes about by the event of **giving birth**. It cannot be established by a sentence; and therefore neither can it be imitated by a sentence.

**وَلَدْنَ** — the verb, feminine plural perfect: "they gave birth". A concrete, physical event with a history.

### That the word does not change reality

This is one of the most basic principles the sūra builds, and it is treated with the same logic elsewhere in the Qurʾān.

The clearest parallel is **al-Aḥzāb 33/4**, and that verse already takes ẓihār as its subject too:

> *"God has not made two hearts inside a man. He has not made your wives, against whom you make ẓihār, your mothers. Nor has He made your adopted sons your own sons. These are words you say with your mouths. But God speaks the truth and guides to the way."*

That verse sets three examples side by side, and all three have the same structure:

| Example | The claim | The reality |
|---|---|---|
| **Two hearts** | There can be two centres inside a person | There cannot — a person is not divided |
| **Ẓihār** | A word makes a wife a mother | It does not — motherhood is giving birth |
| **Adoption** | A word makes an adopted son an own son | It does not — lineage is established by birth |

And the ruling common to all three is in a single sentence: **ذَٰلِكُمْ قَوْلُكُم بِأَفْوَاهِكُمْ** — "this is what you say with your mouths."

The note "with your mouths" is important. Words are said with the mouth in any case; to state this separately looks like a repetition. But the repetition does a job: it says that the word **stays in the mouth**, that it does not pass over into reality. The word comes out of the mouth and ends there; it produces no counterpart in the world.

This principle is the Qurʾān's general attitude towards false realities built with language. To give a thing a name does not make it that thing. The same logic is seen elsewhere too: of the names given to idols, *"They are nothing but names you and your fathers have given"* (al-Najm 53/23); of those who count usury as trade, *"They said: trade is just like usury"* (al-Baqara 2/275) — and the verse rejects this equation.

Its side facing the present is quite concrete: giving a practice a new name does not change it. Naming orders not reality but perception.

### مُنكَرًا مِّنَ ٱلْقَوْلِ وَزُورًا — two characterisations

The formula is characterised with two words, and the two say different things.

**مُنكَر (*munkar*)** — **Root: ن-ك-ر.** Not to recognise, to find odd, to find foreign. The exact opposite of *maʿrūf* (the recognised, the known, the accepted).

This is an important pair of words in the language of ethics. The Qurʾān frequently uses the words *maʿrūf* and *munkar* for good and evil — that is, "familiar" and "foreign". *Maʿrūf* is what human nature and common reason recognise; *munkar* is what they do not recognise, what they find odd. The ẓihār formula is characterised as *munkar*: this word is **foreign**, foreign to the relations a person has himself built.

The note *mina'l-qawl* is meaningful too: "a *munkar* of speech". That is, *munkar* has kinds, and this is one of them — the kind that belongs to the class of speech.

**زُور (*zūr*)** — **Root: ز-و-ر.** The original sense of the root is **crookedness, inclination, a swerving to one side**. *Azwar* is a person whose chest is crooked.

The concrete use of the root in the Qurʾān appears in Sūrat al-Kahf: of those in the cave, *"You would see the sun, when it rose, **inclining** away from their cave to the right"* — **تَّزَٰوَرُ** (al-Kahf 18/17). That is, the light of the sun does not come straight; it slides to one side.

From the same root comes **ziyāra** (visiting) as well: to turn towards someone, to incline towards him. At the centre of the root lies "a swerving from the straight, an inclining in one direction".

From here *zūr*: **a word bent away from the truth.** Not exactly a lie — a lie (*kidhb*) is to say the contrary of a known truth. *Zūr* is a word that makes an angle with reality, that does not correspond to it. The English word "baseless" stands close to it.

The other uses of *zūr* in the Qurʾān confirm this sense: *"Shun the word of falsehood (*qawl al-zūr*)"* (al-Ḥajj 22/30); *"They do not bear witness to *zūr*"* (al-Furqān 25/72).

**What do the two words do together?** *Munkar* characterises the **social** side of the word, *zūr* **its relation to reality**. That is, the word is both something to be found odd and not true. A word may possess only one of these two — abhorrent but true, or fine but false. The ẓihār formula carries both at once.

### وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ لَعَفُوٌّ غَفُورٌ

Immediately after a heavy accusation come two names of forgiveness. This is a structure the Qurʾān uses often, and it has a function: to weight the ruling without closing the door.

**عَفُوّ (*ʿAfuww*)** — **Root: ع-ف-و.** The concrete sense of the root is **to wipe out, to erase the trace**; as the wind erases footprints on the sand. *ʿAfw* is a thing's leaving no trace.

**غَفُور (*Ghafūr*)** — **Root: غ-ف-ر.** To cover. *Mighfar* (helmet) is from the same root — what covers the head. *Maghfira* is the covering over of a sin.

**The difference between the two** is put thus in the classical sources: *ghufrān* covers, *ʿafw* erases. What is covered is there but not seen; what is erased is no longer there. I convey this distinction as a point about the language; the exegetes generally take the coming together of these two names as emphasis.

**Intensive forms.** *ʿAfuww* is on the فَعُول pattern, and *ghafūr* is on the same pattern. Both convey abundance and continuity.

**Their place.** That the names come at the end of the verse is striking in that this is **before** the verses of expiation. The order, that is, runs thus: the word is abhorrent and baseless → but God is pardoning → now hear the expiation. The possibility of forgiveness is stated before the obligation. The expiation is set down not as a punishment but as the threshold of a door that has been opened.

### What this says to the present

**One. The power of the word over life, and its limit.** The sūra says two things at once, and the two do not contradict: the word does not change reality (verse 2); but the word binds the one who speaks it (verses 3-4). That is, the power of the word lies not in shaping the world but in **placing the speaker under obligation.**

**Two. Sentences built in anger.** The typical context of ẓihār is anger. What the regulation does is to prevent a sentence built in a moment of anger from producing a permanent legal consequence. This is a principle still valid today: a momentary anger is not allowed to lock another person's life indefinitely.

**Three. Leaving someone in suspense.** The real problem this regulation solves is the woman's position of being "neither married nor single". Uncertainty is often heavier than an open negative — because no decision can be made, no plan laid, nothing new built. Here the Qurʾān ends the uncertainty: either the expiation is given and the marriage continues, or the paths separate. The scope of this principle is wider than ẓihār; to hold a relationship in suspense, neither ending it nor carrying it on, is a wrong done to the other side.

## 58/3 — وَٱلَّذِينَ يُظَٰهِرُونَ مِن نِّسَآئِهِمْ ثُمَّ يَعُودُونَ لِمَا قَالُوا۟ فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ مِّن قَبْلِ أَن يَتَمَآسَّا ذَٰلِكُمْ تُوعَظُونَ بِهِۦ وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

*Wa'lladhīna yuẓāhirūna min nisāʾihim thumma yaʿūdūna limā qālū fa-taḥrīru raqabatin min qabli an yatamāssā, dhālikum tūʿaẓūna bihi, wa'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna khabīr*

"Those who make ẓihār against their wives and then go back on what they said must set free a neck before the two of them touch one another. You are being admonished by this. God is aware of what you do."

### ثُمَّ يَعُودُونَ لِمَا قَالُوا۟ — the centre of the disagreement

This expression is the most disputed place in the sūra, and the schools of law part company here. What does "going back on what they said" mean?

Even rendering it into English forces a choice, because the pattern *ʿāda li-* can be pulled in two directions at once: "he returned to what he said" (he repeated the formula) or "he went back on what he said" (he retracted it).

| View | What it understands | Its consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Ḥanafī** | To count again as lawful what he had made unlawful to himself by the ẓihār formula, that is, to turn towards continuing relations with his wife | The expiation becomes due with the will to return to the relationship |
| **Shāfiʿī** | Not to divorce the woman after the ẓihār but to keep her in his marriage for a time | The expiation becomes due by not divorcing |
| **Mālikī** | To resolve, to intend, to be with his wife | The expiation becomes due with the resolve |
| **Ẓāhirī tendency** | To repeat the formula itself | The expiation is due only if the formula is repeated |

**Here I adopt no preference** and I issue no legal ruling; per the method (`STYLE.md`) the views of the schools are conveyed, not decided between. But to show **why** the disagreement arose is the business of exegesis:

The source of the disagreement is a single letter: **لِ (lām).** Had it said *ʿāda ilā*, it would have been "he returned to it" and the debate would largely have ended. Had it said *ʿāda ʿan*, it would have been "he turned back from it". The *lām* stands between the two, and in Arabic it can convey both direction towards and implication.

And an observation: the practical result of the disagreement comes out at the same place on the first three views — if the marriage is to be continued, the expiation is required. The differences concern **at which moment** the expiation becomes obligatory. That is, the disagreement is not over the existence of the ruling but over its timing.

**A verbal link within the sūra.** This pattern — *yaʿūdūna limā* — comes once more in the eighth verse: *"Then they return to what they were forbidden"* (**ثُمَّ يَعُودُونَ لِمَا نُهُوا۟ عَنْهُ**). The same verb, the same letter, the same relative pronoun.

In both places there is a "returning", but one is closed with an expiation and the other with Hell. Where is the difference? The return in the third verse is a turning **back** from a wrong word; the one in the eighth is a going **back** to a forbidden behaviour. The same verb, two opposite directions. By using the same pattern twice, the sūra shows that returning is in itself neither good nor bad, and that the direction is what decides.

### فَتَحْرِيرُ رَقَبَةٍ — the loosing of a neck

**تَحْرِير — Root: ح-ر-ر.** *Ḥurr*: free. *Taḥrīr*: to make free, to set at liberty.

The same root has an interesting branch: *taḥrīr* is also used in Arabic in the sense of "writing" (setting a text down in the pen). The lexicographers dwell on how the two senses join; the common core may be "to purify a thing, to bring it to a pure state, to set it right". I do not speak with certainty.

There is one more use of the same root in the Qurʾān: when the wife of ʿImrān dedicates her child, *"I have vowed to You what is in my womb, **set free**"* — **مُحَرَّرًا** (Āl ʿImrān 3/35). There too there is a "loosing from everything else": the child is freed from worldly services and dedicated to the temple.

**رَقَبَة — Root: ر-ق-ب.** The neck. From the same root *raqīb* (the one who watches — who cranes his neck to look), *murāqaba* (surveillance).

The verse does not say "to set a slave free". It says **"to set a neck free"**. This is a metaphor (naming the part and meaning the whole), and not an accidental metaphor: **the captive and the slave are bound by the neck.** The word carries within it the physical image of bondage.

The Qurʾān uses this word consistently in this context:

- *"And for (the freeing of) necks"* — **وَفِى ٱلرِّقَابِ** (al-Baqara 2/177 and al-Tawba 9/60): both in the definition of goodness and among the places where zakāt is spent.
- *"It is the loosing of a neck"* — **فَكُّ رَقَبَةٍ** (al-Balad 90/13): the first step of the steep pass.

The verb *fakk* (to loose, to open a bond) in Sūrat al-Balad brings out plainly the image of the bond in the word. The neck is bound; the work to be done is to open the bond.

**And now look at the logic of the regulation.** A man put a woman into suspense with a sentence. What is his penalty? **To loose someone else's bond.**

This is an unusual form of penalty. The penalty is not the infliction of a pain measured against the offence; it is a compelling to do **a good of the same kind** as the offence. The one who locks shall open. The one who binds shall loose. The structure of the offence determines the structure of the expiation.

This symmetry is not much emphasised in the sources; I record it as a reading, but it stands in the text: on both sides what is at issue is a human being's inability to move.

### مِّن قَبْلِ أَن يَتَمَآسَّا — "before the two of them touch one another"

**يَتَمَآسَّا** — **Root: م-س-س.** To touch. **Form VI (*tafāʿul*) and the dual:** before the two touch one another mutually.

Two grammatical notes:

**First, the dual.** The verb is dual: *yatamāssā* — "the two of them". That is, the condition of the expiation is not attached to the man alone; both sides of the relationship are named together. It is the man who makes the ẓihār; but the sentence that carries the condition makes the two of them subject together.

**Second, the Form VI pattern.** It conveys reciprocity. This takes the word out of being a one-sided act.

**The function of the timing condition.** The note "before" closes off the possibility of postponing the expiation. The expiation is not a debt to be paid later; it is **the threshold before the door.** If the man wants to return to his marriage, he will pay the price first.

The practical consequence of this carries the whole effect of the regulation: the word ceases to be something easily said. Saying it has a price, and the price is paid in advance.

### ذَٰلِكُمْ تُوعَظُونَ بِهِۦ — "you are being admonished by this"

This short sentence names what the expiation is, and the word chosen is striking.

**وَعْظ — Root: و-ع-ظ.** Admonition, counsel; speech that softens the heart and recalls consequences.

The verse does not call the expiation a "punishment" (*ʿuqūba*). It calls it "admonition". That is, the purpose of the obligation is not to inflict pain but **to remind.**

**تُوعَظُونَ** — passive: "you are being admonished". Who does the admonishing is not said — but it is clear from the context. The passive brings forward not the source of the admonition but its addressee.

And note: **ذَٰلِكُمْ** carries the plural pronoun of address — "you all". The verse speaks not to the man who made the ẓihār but to the whole community. That is, the expiation is positioned not as one person's private matter but as a practice that everyone sees and takes a lesson from.

### وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

**خَبِير (*Khabīr*)** — **Root: خ-ب-ر.** The concrete sense of this root is worth dwelling on: *khabr/khibr* carries the sense of ploughing the soil, turning what is underneath to the top; *al-khabīr* is the farmer. From here *khabar*: to know the inside of a thing, to be aware of its inner face.

That is, *Khabīr* is not one who knows from the outside but **one who knows the inside**. And it fits this verse exactly: because whether the expiation is required depends on the man's inner intention — "going back on what he said" is not a visible act but a turning. The verse closes with the name of the one who knows this inner turning.

The choice of verse-ending thus ceases to be an accident: if the ruling has been tied to an inner intention, the name to be mentioned at the end of the ruling will be the one who knows the inside.

## 58/4 — فَمَن لَّمْ يَجِدْ فَصِيَامُ شَهْرَيْنِ مُتَتَابِعَيْنِ مِن قَبْلِ أَن يَتَمَآسَّا فَمَن لَّمْ يَسْتَطِعْ فَإِطْعَامُ سِتِّينَ مِسْكِينًا ذَٰلِكَ لِتُؤْمِنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ وَتِلْكَ حُدُودُ ٱللَّهِ وَلِلْكَٰفِرِينَ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

*Fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu shahrayni mutatābiʿayni min qabli an yatamāssā, fa-man lam yastaṭiʿ fa-iṭʿāmu sittīna miskīnā, dhālika li-tuʾminū bi'llāhi wa rasūlih, wa tilka ḥudūdu'llāh, wa li'l-kāfirīna ʿadhābun alīm*

"Whoever cannot find this shall fast two consecutive months before the two of them touch one another. Whoever is not able to do that shall feed sixty needy people. This is so that you may believe in God and His Messenger. These are the bounds of God. For those who deny, there is a painful punishment."

### Three degrees

The expiation is divided into three steps, and the steps **descend according to means:**

| Degree | Obligation | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | To set a neck free | The default |
| 2 | Two consecutive months of fasting | *lam yajid* — the one who cannot find |
| 3 | To feed sixty needy people | *lam yastaṭiʿ* — the one who is not able |

**The logic in the order of the degrees.** At first glance there is something odd here: setting free, the heaviest obligation, stands first; feeding, which seems the lightest, stands last. Whereas logically one would expect a movement from easy to hard.

What determines the order is not difficulty but **the kind of means**:

- **Setting free**: it demands property. Whoever has property does this.
- **Fasting**: it demands the body. Whoever has no property pays with his body.
- **Feeding**: it demands property again, but far less of it.

That is, the second degree is a passage between property and body; and the third degree is a door opened for the one whose body too falls short (the sick, the aged, the one who cannot fast). And at the third degree property is again demanded, but in a broken-up form — sixty meals, to sixty separate people.

**And the point to notice: at no degree does the obligation lapse.** The poor man too does something. Whoever spoke the ẓihār formula pays a price: with his body if he has no property, and in small pieces if his body does not suffice. The expiation varies according to means, but it does not disappear.

### مُتَتَابِعَيْنِ — "consecutive"

**Root: ت-ب-ع.** To go after, to follow. *Tābiʿ*, *tabaʿa*, *ittibāʿ* are from the same root.

**Pattern: Form VI (*tafāʿul*), and the dual** (*mutatābiʿayni* — for the two months).

*Tatābuʿ* means an uninterrupted following of one upon the other. That is, the two months cannot be kept separately, with a gap set between them.

**The function of the condition.** Making continuity a condition determines not the length of the fast but its **difficulty**. Between keeping sixty days at intervals and keeping them without a break there is no difference in the total number of days; the difference lies in a person's capacity to carry it. What is unbroken, once broken, is begun again from the start — hence it demands attention, it demands planning, it demands a continuous wakefulness.

This too shows the character of the expiation: the obligation is not an amount that is paid off and done with, but something that goes on being remembered for two months. The sentence "you are being admonished by this" was describing exactly this.

### سِتِّينَ مِسْكِينًا — sixty needy people

**Why the number sixty?** The verse does not say. But there is a plain relation: two months is roughly sixty days. That is, an equivalence has been set up between the second and the third degree: **for every day you cannot keep yourself, feed one person.**

This equivalence makes the third degree the counterpart of the second. If you cannot go hungry for a day, feed one person for a day. The expiation is converted from hunger into feeding.

**مِسْكِين** — the analysis of the word was made in Sūrat al-Māʿūn: **Root: س-ك-ن**, immobility, stillness. The *miskīn* is the person whom poverty has brought to a state of not being able to stir; what he has lost is not money but **the capacity to move**.

I do not repeat it here, but one point must be added: the choosing of the poor as the recipient of the expiation **takes the expiation out of being a closed circuit.** The man made a mistake; he pays the price of the mistake not to God but to sixty people who have nothing to do with it. The penalty does not close between the wronged party and the wrongdoer; it is made to flow down to the lowest layer of society.

This is a shared feature of the expiation regulations in Islam, and it is striking: the expiation for an oath, the expiation for breaking a fast, violations of the pilgrimage — in almost all of them the price is directed to those in need. That is, the counterpart of the sin is not burnt up somewhere; it is **transferred** somewhere.

### ذَٰلِكَ لِتُؤْمِنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ — the purpose written out openly

This sentence is the most striking piece of the verse, and it is generally passed over.

**لِ (the *lām* of purpose)** — the particle conveying cause/purpose: "so that…".

That is, the Qurʾān says **why** it has laid down the expiation it lays down. This is a rare thing in legal texts; a rule is generally given without a ground.

**And the ground given is surprising: "so that you may believe in God and His Messenger."**

It might have been said that the expiation arises out of a faith. The verse says the reverse: the expiation is **in order to lead to faith.** That is, the sequence is not "you have believed, therefore do this"; it is "do this so that you may believe."

One has to think about what this means. What is meant here is not that someone who does not believe should come to believe — those addressed are believers already. What is meant must be the **renewal** of faith and its settling into place. You understand what a sentence does only when you have paid its price. The expiation makes this price concrete and so turns an abstract acceptance (that God's ruling binds) into a lived experience.

A similar logic is seen elsewhere in the Qurʾān as well: the act of worship bringing a person into a state (for the prayer, al-ʿAnkabūt 29/45), the sacrifice conveying not meat but *taqwā* (al-Ḥajj 22/37). The deed is positioned not only as the result of faith but also as its **carrier**.

This reading is a preference; it is not binding. But that the verse has built a sentence giving the ground is the text's own choice.

### وَتِلْكَ حُدُودُ ٱللَّهِ — the bounds of God

**حُدُود — Root: ح-د-د.** The concrete sense of the root is **edge, tip, sharp boundary**. From the same root:

- **حَدِيد (*ḥadīd*)** — iron. By reason of its sharpness and hardness.
- **حَادّ (*ḥādd*)** — sharp (of a knife).
- **حَدّ (*ḥadd*)** — the line where one thing ends and another begins. The "*ḥadd*" of a definition is the boundary that separates it from other things.
- **حِدَاد (*ḥidād*)** — mourning; the period in which ordinary life is bounded.

At the centre of the word lies **the line that separates two things from one another**. A *ḥadd* has two sides; and it is this duality that makes it a *ḥadd*.

**And this prepares the second half of the sūra.** In the next verse **يُحَادُّونَ** will come — the same root, in Form III. Its meaning: "to draw a boundary against God, to stand on the opposite side, to position oneself on the far shore."

This link between the two verses is no accident, and it shows the architecture of the sūra:

| Verse | Word | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **58/4** | *ḥudūd Allāh* | The bounds God has drawn |
| **58/5** | *yuḥāddūna'llāh* | Those who draw a boundary against God |
| **58/20** | *yuḥāddūna'llāh* | (a repetition — it closes the section) |

The same root, with two different agents. When God draws the boundary it is called *ḥudūd*; when the servant draws it, it is called *muḥādda*. And what *muḥādda* is is exactly this: **not to recognise the boundary another has drawn, and to draw one's own.**

This opens a side of the expression "the bounds of God" that is often used but little thought about. *Ḥudūd* is not "an area not to be approached" but **"a line not to be stepped outside of"**. The inside is wide; what is bounded is not the inside but the edge.

The Qurʾān mostly uses this word in the context of family law (al-Baqara 2/229-230, al-Ṭalāq 65/1) — that is, in the field where people are most able to wrong one another and which can least be supervised. This choice is meaningful: the boundary is set where no one sees.

### وَلِلْكَٰفِرِينَ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

The close of the verse is severe. And it raises this question: does the one who does not carry out the expiation become a "denier"?

The classical exegetes generally meet this thus: the *kāfirūn* here is not the believer who neglects the expiation but **the one who rejects the ruling itself**. That is, the matter is not one of practice but of recognition. The same people as the *muḥāddūn* who will be described in the second half of the sūra.

The sense in the root of the word (ك-ف-ر: to cover) supports this reading: *kufr* is the covering over of something known. What is covered here is the boundary that has just been drawn.

I record this as a preference; it is not binding. But that the verse comes immediately after the sentence *ḥudūd Allāh* supports this reading from the text.

## 58/5 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يُحَآدُّونَ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥ كُبِتُوا۟ كَمَا كُبِتَ ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ وَقَدْ أَنزَلْنَآ ءَايَٰتٍۭ بَيِّنَٰتٍ وَلِلْكَٰفِرِينَ عَذَابٌ مُّهِينٌ

*Inna'lladhīna yuḥāddūna'llāha wa rasūlahu kubitū kamā kubita'lladhīna min qablihim, wa qad anzalnā āyātin bayyināt, wa li'l-kāfirīna ʿadhābun muhīn*

"Those who draw a boundary against God and His Messenger have been brought low, as those before them were brought low. We have sent down clear signs. For those who deny, there is a humiliating punishment."

### يُحَآدُّونَ — to stand on the opposite side

We treated the root in the preceding verse. Here it is the **pattern** that must be dwelt on.

**Form III (*mufāʿala*).** It conveys reciprocity. But how does reciprocity work in this verb? Does God too draw a boundary from the other side?

The explanation of the classical lexicographers is this: Form III sometimes conveys not reciprocity but **the doing of the act in one direction** and the effort within it. *Muḥādda* is "to set one's own boundary opposite someone's boundary". That is, the act is one-sided but requires two sides — a boundary can only be drawn against another boundary.

**The picture is this:** there is a piece of ground, and one side has drawn its line. The other side comes and draws a different line and says "this side is mine". This is what opposition is in the word — not a rejecting, but **a declaring of a separate sphere of sovereignty.**

This allows us to distinguish the kinds of opposition. Not to understand a ruling, to find a ruling hard, to have difficulty with a ruling — none of these is *muḥādda*. *Muḥādda* is to position oneself as a rival authority.

**The imperfect:** a continuous, settled state. Not a momentary attitude but a permanent position.

### كُبِتُوا۟ — they were brought low

**Root: ك-ب-ت.** The senses given by the lexicographers: to make abject, to bring down into contempt, to drive back leaving the anger stuck in the throat, to turn back before the goal is reached.

Some lexicographers attach the word to the root **ك-ب-د** (liver) and explain *kabt* as "to strike at the liver, to break the inside"; on this account there has been a sound change between *tāʾ* and *dāl*. This derivation is mentioned in the sources but is not certain; I record it as a possibility.

The other use of the word in the Qurʾān is in the context of Uḥud: *"…or that He should **crush** them so that they turn back in despair"* — **أَوْ يَكْبِتَهُمْ** (Āl ʿImrān 3/127). There too the sense is the same: a side that comes with a plan turning back with the plan come to nothing.

**The particular sense the word carries:** *kabt* is not to kill or to defeat. It is **to turn back by belittling**. That is, the other side is not destroyed; its attempt comes to nothing, and that coming to nothing is itself a humiliation.

This is the natural counterpart of the act of "drawing a boundary": the one who drew a boundary had made a claim to sovereignty. His penalty is that the claim goes unanswered.

### The point of the tense: a future spoken in the past

**كُبِتُوا۟ — perfect and passive.**

This is a choice that must be noticed. The verse says not that those who commit *muḥādda* will be brought low but that they **have been** brought low. Yet the people spoken of are still standing.

In Arabic it is a widespread style to express with the perfect something that will certainly take place in the future, and it conveys **taḥaqquq** (realisation): it is so certain that it is counted as already done. The best-known example of this in the Qurʾān is the sentence *"The command of God has come"* (al-Naḥl 16/1).

There is a further function here: the sentence continues "as those before them were brought low". That is, there is an instance realised in the past. The perfect tense brings the two events onto the same temporal plane: what befell those before and what will befall these are named with the same verb and the same tense. History is presented as a repeating pattern.

The **passive**, for its part, conceals the agent. Who brought them low? It is not said. The effect of this is: the being brought low stands not like an outside intervention but like the natural consequence of the attempt itself.

### وَقَدْ أَنزَلْنَآ ءَايَٰتٍۭ بَيِّنَٰتٍ

**قَدْ** is here again — the same particle as the sūra's first word. In the first verse *"qad samiʿa"* (He heard), here *"qad anzalnā"* (We have sent down). Two assertions of certainty: that the voice was heard and that the proof was sent are both certain.

**بَيِّنَات — Root: ب-ي-ن.** This root was treated in Sūrat al-Bayyina: **to open a space between** two things, to separate; *bayān* is to make what is confused clear by separating it. A *bayyina* is a proof that is itself clear and that makes another thing clear as well.

**The function of the sentence.** This parenthetical closes off an excuse. The one who draws a boundary cannot say "I did not know, it was not clear"; because the signs were sent down as *bayyināt*.

This is a basic condition in the Qurʾān's understanding of accountability: responsibility depends on knowledge having arrived. *"We do not punish until We have sent a messenger"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/15).

### عَذَابٌ مُّهِينٌ — a humiliating punishment

**Root: ه-و-ن.** Lightness, worthlessness, insignificance. *Hawn*: ease, gentleness (al-Furqān 25/63: *"they walk upon the earth **in humility**"* — **هَوْنًا**). *Hawān*: humiliation, loss of standing. *Muhīn*: the one who humiliates.

The same root carries both a positive sense (gentleness, humility) and a negative one (humiliation). What separates them is whether the lightness is **chosen** or **imposed**. He who keeps himself light is humble; he who is kept light has been humiliated.

**The choice of the adjective is meaningful.** The punishment could have been characterised as "severe" (*shadīd*) or "great" (*ʿaẓīm*), and the Qurʾān so characterises it elsewhere. Here it is called **humiliating**.

Why? Because the offence itself was a **claim to rise**. The one who committed *muḥādda* had positioned himself as a rival authority. The character of the penalty is derived from the character of the offence: the one who wants to rise is brought low.

The same symmetry was present in Sūrat al-Fajr too: there *ṭughyān* (overflowing) was paired with *sawṭ ʿadhāb* (the scourge). In the Qurʾān the penalty most often carries the shape of the act.

## 58/6 — يَوْمَ يَبْعَثُهُمُ ٱللَّهُ جَمِيعًا فَيُنَبِّئُهُم بِمَا عَمِلُوٓا۟ أَحْصَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ وَنَسُوهُ وَٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ شَهِيدٌ

*Yawma yabʿathuhumu'llāhu jamīʿan fa-yunabbiʾuhum bimā ʿamilū, aḥṣāhu'llāhu wa nasūh, wa'llāhu ʿalā kulli shayʾin shahīd*

"On the day when God raises them all up and informs them of what they did. God has counted it, and they have forgotten it. God is witness to everything."

### أَحْصَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ وَنَسُوهُ — two verbs, one object

The centre of the verse is here, and the structure is very tight: two clauses, ending with the same pronoun, carrying opposite verbs.

**أَحْصَىٰ — Root: ح-ص-ي.** The concrete sense of the root is **pebble** (*ḥaṣā*). *Iḥṣāʾ* is to count with pebbles.

This is the oldest and most concrete form of counting. Before the invention of writing, before the numeral even, a man counted his flock or his crop with pebbles: one stone for each sheep. The operation of counting rested not on an abstract number but on **a tangible counterpart**.

That the word comes from this root separates *iḥṣāʾ* from ordinary knowing. To know a thing is one matter; **to count it one by one and set aside a counterpart for each** is another. *Iḥṣāʾ* is an inventory in which nothing is passed over.

The Qurʾān uses this word insistently in the context of deeds: *"What a book this is! It has left out nothing, small or great, but has **counted** it all"* (al-Kahf 18/49) — there too the bewilderment of the guilty is directed not at the existence of the knowledge but at its **completeness**.

**نَسُوهُ — Root: ن-س-ي.** To forget.

And the pronoun is the same: *aḥṣā-**hu*** / *nasū-**hu***. Both refer to the same thing: what was done.

**The force of the sentence lies in the asymmetry of the contrast.** The two sides stand in opposite positions with regard to the same event — one has counted every detail of it, the other has forgotten the whole of it. And this asymmetry shows that something which looks to be in a person's favour is in fact against him: forgetting is the easiest way of counting a thing as not having happened.

It is worth recalling here the distinction between *sahw* and *nisyān* in Sūrat al-Māʿūn: *nisyān* is the complete departure of knowledge from the mind. That is, these people are not suppressing what they did, not denying it — they genuinely do not remember. And it is exactly for this reason that the verb *fa-yunabbiʾuhum* (He will inform them) is used: as though **a new piece of information** were being given to them.

**نَبَّأَ — Root: ن-ب-أ.** *Nabaʾ* means important and new news (it was treated in Sūrat al-Nabaʾ). That is, a person's own deeds will be presented to him as **news**. His own life will become a story told to him.

This image is disturbing, and it looks as though its being disturbing is intended: a person's own deed carries news value for himself.

### يَوْمَ يَبْعَثُهُمُ ٱللَّهُ جَمِيعًا

This sentence occurs **twice** in the sūra: here and at 58/18, in exactly the same wording.

This is an important sign for the architecture of the sūra. Two separate groups in the sūra — those who commit *muḥādda* (5-6) and the hypocrites (14-19) — are brought onto the same stage. Both sections are tied to the day of raising by the same sentence.

**جَمِيعًا — "all together".** Root ج-م-ع. This word turns up in an interesting way both in this sūra and in the next (al-Ḥashr 59/14: *taḥsabuhum jamīʿan*). Here a real gathering; there a false appearance of togetherness.

**شَهِيد — Root: ش-ه-د.** The one present, the one who sees, the one who bears witness. At the centre of the root lies *being present*: the *shāhid* is the one who was there at the moment of the event.

The choice of verse-ending again suits the verse: the verse is built upon forgetting, and at the close it says "witness to everything". The witness is the one who does not forget — because he was there.

And the link with the first verse: there we had *Samīʿ* and *Baṣīr*. Here *Shahīd*. All three are different faces of the same idea: what was spoken was heard, the condition was seen, what took place was recorded by being present.

## 58/7 — أَلَمْ تَرَ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ مَا يَكُونُ مِن نَّجْوَىٰ ثَلَٰثَةٍ إِلَّا هُوَ رَابِعُهُمْ وَلَا خَمْسَةٍ إِلَّا هُوَ سَادِسُهُمْ وَلَآ أَدْنَىٰ مِن ذَٰلِكَ وَلَآ أَكْثَرَ إِلَّا هُوَ مَعَهُمْ أَيْنَ مَا كَانُوا۟ ثُمَّ يُنَبِّئُهُم بِمَا عَمِلُوا۟ يَوْمَ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

*A-lam tara anna'llāha yaʿlamu mā fi's-samāwāti wa mā fi'l-arḍ, mā yakūnu min najwā thalāthatin illā huwa rābiʿuhum, wa lā khamsatin illā huwa sādisuhum, wa lā adnā min dhālika wa lā akthara illā huwa maʿahum ayna mā kānū, thumma yunabbiʾuhum bimā ʿamilū yawma'l-qiyāma, inna'llāha bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm*

"Have you not seen that God knows what is in the heavens and what is in the earth? There is no secret talk of three but He is the fourth of them; nor of five but He is the sixth of them; nor of fewer than that nor of more, but He is with them wherever they are. Then on the day of resurrection He will inform them of what they did. Surely God knows everything."

### نجوى — what the root shows

**Root: ن-ج-و.** The derivatives of this root in Arabic look surprisingly scattered, but they meet at a single centre:

- **نَجَا (*najā*)** — he was saved. *Najāt*: deliverance.
- **نَجْوَة (*najwa*)** — a raised place, a mound, a rise the floodwaters do not reach.
- **نَجْوَى (*najwā*)** — secret talk, whispering together.
- **نَجِيّ (*najiyy*)** — a confidant, the one with whom one speaks alone.

**The common core: to separate, to come out of the main mass, to draw aside.**

The picture is this: a *najwa* is a rise — a place separated from the flat, standing apart. *Najāt* is to be separated from the place of danger and saved. And *najwā* is **two or three people separating from the crowd and drawing aside**.

This changes what the word says. **Najwā is in the first place not a matter of sound but a matter of space.** The lowering of the voice is secondary; what is essential is a group's separating from the main group.

And this explains the later verses of the sūra. In the tenth verse it will say "secret talk is from Satan, in order to **grieve** those who believe". The one who is grieved is not the one who does not hear what is said — he is **the one left outside**. The harm is not in the words but in the separation.

One use of the same root in the Qurʾān completes this picture: when the brothers of Joseph (Yūsuf) could not decide, *"they drew aside to talk among themselves"* — **خَلَصُوا۟ نَجِيًّا** (Yūsuf 12/80). The verb is *khalaṣū*: they extricated themselves, they separated. The act of drawing aside is used together with the word *najiyy*.

And of Moses (Mūsā): *"We brought him near as a **najiyy**"* (Maryam 19/52) — there the separation is positive: being alone together. That is, *najwā* is not bad in itself; the sūra will say this openly in the ninth verse.

### The succession of the numbers

The most striking side of the verse is the way it lists the numbers:

> three → **fourth** • five → **sixth** • fewer → **with them** • more → **with them**

Several points are worth dwelling on.

**First: why does it begin from three?** The minimum number for whispering together is two. But the verse begins from three. One of the classical explanations: a conversation between two is not counted as *najwā* at all, it is talk; *najwā* takes on meaning when it is a small group separating out from within a community. Another: three is the smallest real number for "a minority group".

**Second: why is four skipped?** The sequence runs 3, 5; 4 is not named on its own (it occurs only as "the fourth of three"). This has an effect: **the listener cannot keep track of the count.** Had it gone 3, 4, 5, 6, the mind would build a list and expect the list to end somewhere. When the numbers are given with gaps, the logic of the list is broken and the mind asks "how far is this going to go?"

And at exactly that moment the verse abandons the list: **وَلَآ أَدْنَىٰ مِن ذَٰلِكَ وَلَآ أَكْثَرَ** — "nor fewer than that nor more". The counting is dropped and all the numbers are closed off in a single stroke.

That is, the structure of the verse does this work: first it makes the thing concrete by counting (it calls up a room with three people in it in the reader's mind), then it generalises by abandoning the count. To begin from the concrete and rise to the all-inclusive is a way the Qurʾān often uses.

**Third: the expression "the fourth of them".** The verse does not say *"He is beside them"*; it says **"He is the fourth of them"**. That is, He is included in the count. A conversation of three is in fact a conversation of four.

This says something different from saying "you are being watched". The watcher is outside; the fourth person is **inside.** The scene itself changes: a thing called secret talk cannot be set up at all, because in every closed room there is one person too many.

### إِلَّا هُوَ مَعَهُمْ — the question of being-with

After abandoning the numbers the verse says **مَعَهُمْ** (with them). This expression has been debated in the history of theology, and one must be honest here.

**A summary of the classical record:** the great majority of the exegetes and the theologians explain the being-with here as **maʿiyya ʿilmiyya** (a being-with in knowledge); that is, no presence in respect of essence and place is intended. The ground is given from within the verse itself:

- The verse opens with the verb **يَعْلَمُ** (He knows).
- The verse closes with **بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ** (knower of everything).
- That is, the sentence about "being-with" is placed between two expressions of knowledge.

This indication is strong, and it is not the imposing of an interpretation but the text's own frame.

Into the debates that go beyond this — the nature of God's attributes, their relation to place — I do not enter here; these are the subject not of exegesis but of theology, and they exceed the bounds of this commentary.

But neither should the **emotional** effect of the verse be wiped out. The text could have been content to say "He knows"; it says "He is with them". This choice preserves the difference between knowing from a distance and nearness. The sūra did the same thing in the first verse: instead of saying "He knew" it said "He heard".

### أَيْنَ مَا كَانُوا۟ — "wherever they are"

The note of place is closed off too. The number was closed; now the place is closed. What is left? Nothing — neither the smallness of the company nor the remoteness of the place offers a refuge.

### Context — why is this verse here?

In respect of the structure of the sūra this verse is a turning point, and the transition is very skilfully built.

The first six verses spoke of **a word that was spoken**: the ẓihār sentence, and the woman's complaint. Now the subject passes to **a word that is spoken but not heard**: the whisper.

The link is this: in the first verse a woman's voice was heard from a place no one was attending to. In the seventh verse the whisper of three people is heard from the place where they are trying to conceal it. **The same hearing works in two opposite directions:** for one it is an assurance, for the other a warning.

This is one of the most elegant transitions in the sūra. Being heard becomes, according to position, a consolation or a threat.

### أَلَمْ تَرَ — what the pattern does

**"Have you not seen?"** This pattern is often used in the Qurʾān and was treated in Sūrat al-Fīl and Sūrat al-Fajr. In brief: the seeing here is not seeing with the eye; it means "are you not aware, do you not grasp?"

There is a particular subtlety in this verse: the pattern is used for something **not seen**. The subject is concealment, and the verb is seeing. This is the clearest proof that the pattern does not intend seeing with the eye — because the thing in question is by definition not visible.

And the pattern will be repeated in two more verses of the sūra (58/8 and 58/14). Three times *a-lam tara*. Each time the attention of the one addressed is drawn to something that stays hidden: to those whispering, to the twisted greeting, to the secret alliance.

### What this says to the present

In building the present-day counterpart of this verse one must not force anything. The verse does not speak of a surveillance technology, and to read it so would be an injustice to the text.

But it is fitting to say this: what the verse builds is **an inner supervision** that works where outer supervision cannot reach. And the difference between these two matters. Outer supervision changes the behaviour of the supervised for the duration of the supervision; when the supervision is lifted the behaviour returns. For the person who believes what the verse describes, there is no such category as "a moment outside supervision".

The practical consequence of this comes out at the same place as the measure we discussed in Sūrat al-Māʿūn: what a person really is is measured by what he does at the moment when no one is looking. The seventh verse removes that moment.

## 58/8 — أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى ٱلَّذِينَ نُهُوا۟ عَنِ ٱلنَّجْوَىٰ ثُمَّ يَعُودُونَ لِمَا نُهُوا۟ عَنْهُ وَيَتَنَٰجَوْنَ بِٱلْإِثْمِ وَٱلْعُدْوَٰنِ وَمَعْصِيَتِ ٱلرَّسُولِ وَإِذَا جَآءُوكَ حَيَّوْكَ بِمَا لَمْ يُحَيِّكَ بِهِ ٱللَّهُ وَيَقُولُونَ فِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ لَوْلَا يُعَذِّبُنَا ٱللَّهُ بِمَا نَقُولُ حَسْبُهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ يَصْلَوْنَهَا فَبِئْسَ ٱلْمَصِيرُ

*A-lam tara ila'lladhīna nuhū ʿani'n-najwā thumma yaʿūdūna limā nuhū ʿanhu wa yatanājawna bi'l-ithmi wa'l-ʿudwāni wa maʿṣiyati'r-rasūl, wa idhā jāʾūka ḥayyawka bimā lam yuḥayyika bihi'llāh, wa yaqūlūna fī anfusihim lawlā yuʿadhdhibuna'llāhu bimā naqūl, ḥasbuhum jahannamu yaṣlawnahā fa-biʾsa'l-maṣīr*

"Have you not seen those who were forbidden secret talk and then return to what they were forbidden, and whisper together about sin, enmity and disobedience to the Messenger? When they come to you, they greet you with a word with which God has not greeted you, and they say within themselves: 'Why does God not punish us for what we say?' Hell is enough for them; they will enter it. What an evil destination!"

### The three subjects of the whispering

**بِٱلْإِثْمِ وَٱلْعُدْوَٰنِ وَمَعْصِيَتِ ٱلرَّسُولِ** — three words are listed, and the three point in different directions.

**إِثْم (*ithm*) — Root: أ-ث-م.** The original sense given by the lexicographers: **to make heavy, to delay, to hold back.** *Nāqa athūm*: a slow-walking she-camel. On this account *ithm* is what holds a person back from good, what makes his advance heavy.

That the word comes from this root defines sin not as a "breach of a rule" but as a **burden**. Sin is a weight that slows the person down.

As to its field: *ithm* concerns in the first place **the person himself**. Of wine and gambling the Qurʾān says *"in both of them there is great **ithm**"* (al-Baqara 2/219); there too the harm is in the first place to the person himself.

**عُدْوَان (*ʿudwān*) — Root: ع-د-و.** The original sense of the root is **to pass, to cross over, to go beyond**. *ʿAdā*: he ran, he passed. *ʿAduww* (enemy): the one who crosses the boundary and comes upon you. *Iʿtidāʾ*: the overstepping of a limit.

As to its field: *ʿudwān* concerns **another person**. If a boundary is being crossed, the boundary crossed is someone else's.

**مَعْصِيَت (*maʿṣiya*) — Root: ع-ص-ي.** From the same root comes **عَصَا (*ʿaṣā*)** — the staff. The lexicographers set up the relation thus: the *ʿaṣā* is what is held in the hand, what one leans on; *ʿiṣyān* is a **separating** from the community and from obedience — as in the expression "to separate one's staff from the congregation".

As to its field: *maʿṣiya* concerns **the order**. Not the person's relation with himself or with another, but the breakdown of his relation with the structure he belongs to.

**The order of the three:**

| Word | Field | Whom the harm falls on |
|---|---|---|
| *ithm* | Inward | On himself |
| *ʿudwān* | Horizontal | On another |
| *maʿṣiyat al-rasūl* | Vertical | On the order / on the authority |

The order widens from the inward outwards. And this triad will be repeated in the ninth verse **in exactly the same wording** — this time as a prohibition.

### يَتَنَٰجَوْنَ — the change of pattern

In the seventh verse *najwā* was in the form of a noun. Here it is a verb, and in **Form VI (*tafāʿul*)**: *yatanājawn*. A reciprocal and continuing whispering. The pattern shows that the thing is not a one-off, that it is a continuous conversation circulating among them.

### حَيَّوْكَ بِمَا لَمْ يُحَيِّكَ بِهِ ٱللَّهُ — the twisted greeting

One of the finest observations of the sūra is here.

**تَحِيَّة — Root: ح-ي-ي.** Life. *Taḥiyya* is originally **"to wish life"**: *ḥayyāka'llāh* — "may God give you life". In Arabic, to greet is to wish the other person long life.

This origin explains what a greeting is. A greeting is not a formula of politeness; it is **to want the other person to go on existing**. When two people meet, the first thing done is to confirm one another's existence.

And exactly for this reason, the twisting of it becomes so heavy a thing.

**What the reports say.** According to what is transmitted, some people, when they came to the Prophet, said *"al-sāmu ʿalayka"* instead of *"al-salāmu ʿalayka"*, bending the sound slightly. **السَّام (*sām*)** in Arabic means **death**.

The difference between the two words is a single letter: the *lām*. Said quickly, it is hard to make out. For the one who says it, an insult; for the one who hears it, an ordinary greeting.

This report has a wide place in the ḥadīth corpus, and it is transmitted that ʿĀʾisha noticed it and gave a sharp answer, that the Prophet counselled her to gentleness, and that he himself answered with *"and upon you"*. There are variations in the details of the report; its main core recurs in the sources.

**The subtlety in the verse's manner of expression.** The verse does not say "they insulted you". It says: **"they greeted you with a word with which God has not greeted you."**

This is an unusual manner of expression. What is set as the measure is **God's greeting**. In the Qurʾān there are greetings directed to the prophets (*"Peace be upon Noah"*, al-Ṣāffāt 37/79; *"Peace be upon Abraham"*, 37/109). The verse recalls this measure: what the right greeting is, is known, and this lies outside it.

Moreover, the expression **does not name the insult directly.** The word spoken is not taken into the text. This can be read as a choice of decorum: the text records the ugly word without repeating it.

### The link with al-Baqara 2/104: the same mechanism, a different solution

This verse must be read together with the 104th verse of Sūrat al-Baqara, because the two take **the same linguistic mechanism** as their subject.

In al-Baqara 2/104 the situation was this: the Companions were saying *"rāʿinā"* (attend to us) to the Prophet — an innocent expression in Arabic. But the same sequence of sounds, with a slight bending, or in another language, could carry a demeaning sense. The Qurʾān's solution: abandon the word, say *"unẓurnā"* instead.

The situation here is the other face of the same mechanism: a word is used in such a way as to bear two senses; the speaker intends one sense, the hearer understands the other.

**But the solution of the two verses differs, and the difference is instructive:**

| | **al-Baqara 2/104** | **al-Mujādala 58/8** |
|---|---|---|
| Who uses the word | The believers (in good faith) | The other side (in bad faith) |
| Who does the bending | Someone from outside | The speaker himself |
| What is commanded | Abandon the word | (No command to the believers) |
| Outcome | A move to a synonymous word | The reckoning is left to God |

In al-Baqara the believers change their own word — that is, the responsibility is loaded onto the speaker: *how a word may be understood is also the speaker's field.*

In al-Mujādala, on the other hand, it is the other side that does the bending, and no command about behaviour is given to the believers. What is given is a piece of information: this is being done, it is known, it has a counterpart.

**These two together establish a measure:** you are responsible for your own word; not for what someone else does with your word. Al-Baqara says the first, al-Mujādala the second.

There is a third link as well: in al-Baqara 2/58-59 the **changing in bad faith** of a word that had been given was recounted. The three verses together give three states of the same matter: the changing of the word in good faith (2/104), its changing in bad faith (2/59), and its bending in bad faith (58/8).

### لَوْلَا يُعَذِّبُنَا ٱللَّهُ بِمَا نَقُولُ — counting the delay of the penalty as a proof

**فِىٓ أَنفُسِهِمْ** — "within themselves". That is, this sentence is not spoken, it is thought. And the verse records that too. The principle of the seventh verse, "He is the fourth of them", is applied here: not even a whisper, but a thought with no sound at all.

**لَوْلَا** — here a particle conveying incitement/urging: "if only it were so", "why is it not happening". That is, the sentence carries this logic: *"If what we are saying were really something bad, something would have befallen us. Nothing does. So there is nothing to it."*

This is one of the most widespread inferences in human history, and as logic it carries a weakness: **taking the delay of a consequence for the absence of the consequence.**

The Qurʾān records this objection in many places:

- *"They ask you to hasten the evil before the good"* (al-Raʿd 13/6).
- *"O God, if this is the truth from You, then rain down stones upon us from the sky"* (al-Anfāl 8/32) — here the same logic is even plainer: the punishment's not coming is counted as the refutation of the claim.

**And the answer comes in an interesting form: حَسْبُهُمْ جَهَنَّمُ.**

**حَسْب — Root: ح-س-ب.** To count, to reckon. *Ḥasbuhum*: "it is enough for them". The word carries the idea of "the amount that will close the account".

That is, what is asked for is not answered, but neither is the request rejected. The man says "let Him punish us then"; the verse says "there is a punishment, and enough of it". What is in dispute is not the existence of the punishment but **its timing.** The verse does not dispute the timing; it only says that the amount is sufficient.

This is the Qurʾān's general attitude on the question of a deferred penalty: that a respite has been given does not mean the ruling has been lifted.

**يَصْلَوْنَهَا — Root: ص-ل-ي.** To enter the fire, to be roasted in the fire. The concrete sense of the root is holding meat to the fire and cooking it (*shiwāʾ*, *maṣliyy*). The word is a verb of contact: not beside the fire but inside it.

**ٱلْمَصِير — Root: ص-ي-ر.** *Ṣāra*: he became, he was transformed, he came into a state. *Maṣīr*: the place arrived at, the state turned into.

The choice of the word is meaningful: not "the place one goes to" (*makān*) but **"the thing one becomes"**. The root conveys **transformation** more than place. That is, arrival is as much a coming into a state as a reaching of a place.

## 58/9 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ إِذَا تَنَٰجَيْتُمْ فَلَا تَتَنَٰجَوْا۟ بِٱلْإِثْمِ وَٱلْعُدْوَٰنِ وَمَعْصِيَتِ ٱلرَّسُولِ وَتَنَٰجَوْا۟ بِٱلْبِرِّ وَٱلتَّقْوَىٰ وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ ٱلَّذِىٓ إِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū idhā tanājaytum fa-lā tatanājaw bi'l-ithmi wa'l-ʿudwāni wa maʿṣiyati'r-rasūl, wa tanājaw bi'l-birri wa't-taqwā, wa'ttaqu'llāha'lladhī ilayhi tuḥsharūn*

"O you who believe! When you speak secretly, do not speak of sin, enmity and disobedience to the Messenger; speak of goodness and *taqwā*. And be mindful of God, to whom you will be gathered."

### What the prohibition is not

The structure of the verse must be read carefully, because it is very easily misunderstood.

The verse begins with **إِذَا تَنَٰجَيْتُمْ**: "when you speak secretly". This conditional clause takes secret talk as a **given** — it is assumed that it will happen; it is not forbidden.

Then two commands come:

1. *fa-lā tatanājaw bi-* … — do not speak about these subjects
2. *wa tanājaw bi-* … — speak about these subjects

**The second command is as important as the first, and it is generally passed over.** Had the verse forbidden secret talk, it could not have given the second command. It says "speak" — and it does so using the same verb, in the same pattern.

That is, what is regulated is not privacy but **what is placed inside privacy.** People's speaking alone together is legitimate; the sūra's concern is what these conversations serve.

This distinction matters, because the opposite reading — "every secret conversation is bad" — is both contrary to the text and heavy in its consequences. To remove from a community the possibility of speaking alone together means the complete disappearance of privacy; the verse does not want that.

### Two lists, asymmetric

| Forbidden | Commanded |
|---|---|
| *ithm* (sin) | *birr* (goodness) |
| *ʿudwān* (enmity) | *taqwā* |
| *maʿṣiyat al-rasūl* (disobedience to the Messenger) | — |

**Three bad, two good.** The asymmetry is striking, and it may be explained thus: *birr* and *taqwā* are already two very wide words; wide enough to leave no need for a third.

**بِرّ — Root: ب-ر-ر.** The original sense of this root is **breadth**; from the same root *barr* — dry land, a wide open expanse (the opposite of the sea). *Birr*, accordingly, means "wide goodness", a goodness that does not fit into one mould.

The Qurʾān defines this word explicitly at al-Baqara 2/177, and the definition is surprisingly wide: the articles of faith + giving property to six groups despite loving it + the prayer and zakāt + keeping a covenant + patience in three situations. That is, *birr* is not a single behaviour but a sum of behaviours.

**تَقْوَىٰ — Root: و-ق-ي.** To protect oneself, to take oneself into protection. *Wiqāya*: a protective covering.

**The pairing of the two words establishes a balance:** *birr* is an expansion outwards (goodness that reaches another), *taqwā* is a protection inwards (holding oneself back). The Qurʾān pairs these two in the same way elsewhere as well: *"Help one another in goodness and taqwā; do not help one another in sin and enmity"* (al-Māʾida 5/2) — and there too the two words on the other side are *ithm* and *ʿudwān*. The two verses use the same four words.

### How the subject of a conversation is determined

The measure the verse gives is practical and works directly today as well: to understand what a conversation serves, it is enough to look at **what it does to whom.**

- If a plan by which someone will be harmed comes out of the conversation: *ʿudwān*.
- If the conversation puts those speaking under a burden: *ithm*.
- If the conversation aims to wear down an established order from within: *maʿṣiya*.
- If the conversation will serve someone's good: *birr*.
- If the conversation protects those speaking from something: *taqwā*.

What the verse does is to protect secrecy as a right of privacy while preventing hiding behind secrecy. The two are different things: privacy is the not-being-visible of something that serves no one's interest; hiding is the not-being-visible of something that will harm someone.

### ٱلَّذِىٓ إِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ

The verse closes with the name of the next sūra: **تُحْشَرُونَ** — "you will be gathered". The root **ح-ش-ر** is the name of Sūrat al-Ḥashr.

We shall dwell on the meaning of the root there. Here it is enough to say this much: *ḥashr* is to gather together and **to drive from one place and dispatch to another**.

And the function of the verse-ending is plain: secret talk was an act of **separating** (the root of *najwā*: to draw aside). The verse closes by reminding those who separate that they will be gathered. The corner drawn into is temporary; the gathering is permanent.

## 58/10 — إِنَّمَا ٱلنَّجْوَىٰ مِنَ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ لِيَحْزُنَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَلَيْسَ بِضَآرِّهِمْ شَيْـًٔا إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ وَعَلَى ٱللَّهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ

*Innama'n-najwā mina'sh-shayṭāni li-yaḥzuna'lladhīna āmanū, wa laysa bi-ḍārrihim shayʾan illā bi-idhni'llāh, wa ʿala'llāhi fal-yatawakkali'l-muʾminūn*

"That secret talk is only from Satan, in order to grieve those who believe. Yet it cannot harm them at all except by God's leave. Let the believers rely on God alone."

### لِيَحْزُنَ — the aim: to grieve

The most striking side of the verse is how it names the purpose of *najwā*.

What one would expect is doing harm: laying a plan, preparing a trap, getting ready for an attack. The verse does not say these things. The only thing it says is: **to grieve.**

**حُزْن — Root: ح-ز-ن.** The concrete sense of the root is **hard, broken, stony ground**. *Al-ḥazn* is hard earth that is difficult to walk on (the opposite of *sahl*, which is soft and level).

From here *ḥuzn* (grief): **the hardening of the terrain within.** Its becoming unwalkable. The word describes sorrow not as an emotion but as **a deterioration of the ground**.

This clarifies the picture the sūra builds. What the whispering does is not to do actual harm to the other side; it is **to make his inside unwalkable.** A group of people draw aside and talk, and the person left outside does not know what is being said. That unknowing does more work than a real harm: the person produces possibilities in his own mind, and those possibilities work against him.

**And the verse names this as a technique: مِنَ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ.**

One has to think about what this means. The verse does not say that those whispering are Satan; it says that the **source** of the whispering comes out there. That is, this is the naming of a form of behaviour.

Satan's basic work in the Qurʾān is *waswasa* — that is, not the giving of information but **the dropping in of doubt.** This is what is described in Sūrat al-Nās as well: the one who whispers into breasts. And the function of whispering is not to say something untrue but to leave behind something whose truth or untruth cannot be known.

*Najwā* does exactly the same work. Nothing is said to the person left outside; a door is simply closed and something is spoken behind it. The rest the person produces himself.

### وَلَيْسَ بِضَآرِّهِمْ شَيْـًٔا — the distinction between grief and harm

The second half of the verse balances the first, and there is a very fine distinction here.

**The verse does not deny the grief.** It does not say "you will not be grieved"; it says "it is in order to grieve" — that is, the intent is real, and in all likelihood effective. But then: **it cannot harm.**

That is, two things are separated:

| | **Grief** | **Harm (*ḍarar*)** |
|---|---|---|
| Its reality | Real, it is being lived | It does not come about without God's leave |
| Where | Inside | Outside |
| Who produces it | The person's own mind included | Only outward events |

This distinction is both true and useful. A conversation that takes a person as its target can grieve that person — and to be grieved is not a fault. But grief and actual harm are not the same thing. The real effect produced by the whispering group is not so much a real harm as **the shadow of an expected harm**. The verse separates the shadow from the body.

The note **إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ** too is not a formula of consolation but a frame: whether the harm comes about or not is not in the hands of the one who plans it. That is, the power of the other side is smaller than it estimates.

### فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ — to rely

**Root: و-ك-ل.** *Wakīl*: the person to whom a matter is committed. *Tawakkul* (Form V, *tafaʿʿul*): to commit a matter to an agent, to rely on him.

The legal origin of the word matters: *wakāla* in Arabic is the transfer of the authority to carry out a matter. That is, *tawakkul* is not an emotional relief but a **transfer of authority** — the acceptance that the outcome is not in one's own hands.

And it fits this verse exactly: what the whispering group is planning is not known. The only thing that can be done about something unknown is to stop tracking it.

**فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ** — a command to the third person with the *lām* of command: "let them rely". And a point of word order: **عَلَى ٱللَّهِ** has been brought forward. In Arabic the ordinary order would be *fal-yatawakkali'l-muʾminūna ʿala'llāh*. The bringing forward conveys **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*): "on God alone".

This detail is the very same structure treated in al-Fātiḥa: *iyyāka naʿbudu* — the object is brought forward and a limitation is established.

## 58/11 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ إِذَا قِيلَ لَكُمْ تَفَسَّحُوا۟ فِى ٱلْمَجَٰلِسِ فَٱفْسَحُوا۟ يَفْسَحِ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمْ وَإِذَا قِيلَ ٱنشُزُوا۟ فَٱنشُزُوا۟ يَرْفَعِ ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ مِنكُمْ وَٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْعِلْمَ دَرَجَٰتٍ وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū idhā qīla lakum tafassaḥū fi'l-majālisi fafsaḥū yafsaḥi'llāhu lakum, wa idhā qīla'nshuzū fanshuzū yarfaʿi'llāhu'lladhīna āmanū minkum wa'lladhīna ūtu'l-ʿilma darajāt, wa'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna khabīr*

"O you who believe! When you are told 'Make room in the assemblies', make room, and God will make room for you. And when it is said 'Rise', rise. God raises by degrees those of you who believe and those who have been given knowledge. God is aware of what you do."

### تَفَسَّحُوا۟ / فَٱفْسَحُوا۟ — to make room

**Root: ف-س-ح.** Breadth, spaciousness, openness. *Fasīḥ*: wide. *Fusḥa*: a wide space, an opening.

**Two different patterns are used, and the difference is meaningful:**

- **تَفَسَّحُوا۟** — Form V (*tafaʿʿul*). An effort upon oneself: "press yourselves together, widen the space among you". That is, those seated gathering themselves in.
- **فَٱفْسَحُوا۟** — Form I. Directly: "make room".

When the command is spoken, the courteous and indirect pattern (*tafassaḥū*); when obedience is commanded, the direct pattern (*ifsaḥū*). A small nuance, but it stands in the weave of the text.

**The scene is very concrete:** a crowded assembly. There are newcomers, and there is nowhere to sit. Some must press together so that another can sit.

One might think this a small matter. That the Qurʾān regulates it with a separate verse has everything to do with the fact that it looks small: **great principles are tested in the places no one pays attention to.** To hold on to the place where one is sitting is one of the most automatic of human behaviours.

### يَفْسَحِ ٱللَّهُ لَكُمْ — the recompense given with the same root

The recompense is given with the verb's own root: *ifsaḥū* → *yafsaḥi'llāhu*. "You make room, and God will make room for you."

**And note: what He will make room in is not said.** The object has been dropped. The verse does not say "your provision", "your breast", "your place in the garden" — only "may He make room for you".

This gap is deliberate, and its effect is this: the recompense is not obliged to be of the same kind as what was given. What will be given to the person who makes a hand's breadth of room is left open.

This is a technique the Qurʾān often uses in its verses of recompense. A promise of a definite recompense turns the transaction into a trade; a recompense left indefinite keeps the transaction open.

### ٱنشُزُوا۟ — to rise

**Root: ن-ش-ز.** The concrete sense of the root is **a place higher than the ground, a mound, a swelling**. *Nashaz*: a piece of ground standing higher than its surroundings.

From here the verb: to rise from one's place, to lift oneself, to straighten up.

**The other use of the same root in the Qurʾān is in a very different context:** for discord between spouses the word **نُشُوز** is used (al-Nisāʾ 4/34 and 4/128). There the word means one party's "rising" from her place in the relationship, lifting herself, going out of line.

When the two uses are set side by side, the root is seen to be neutral: *nushūz* is a rising; where the rising is directed and against what determines its value. Here rising is commanded; there it is blamed. The word is the same; the context differs.

**What is the context?** Two possibilities are mentioned in the reports: rising from the assembly to give a newcomer a place, or rising and leaving when the assembly breaks up (so as not to take up the Prophet's time unnecessarily). Both fit the text; the Qurʾān does not specify.

**The structure of the command is striking:** the verse regulates both situations — pressing together while seated, and rising and going when needed. That is, both making room and leaving one's place. Both are part of the same thing: **not counting the place where you sit as your own property.**

### يَرْفَعِ ٱللَّهُ … دَرَجَٰتٍ — the raising

And here the architecture of the verse appears.

The command: *inshuzū* — **rise** (the root ن-ش-ز, "height").
The recompense: *yarfaʿi'llāh* — **God raises** (the root ر-ف-ع).

The same idea, with two different roots and two different agents. A person rises from his place of his own will; God raises him. That is, the matter of the seating order in the assembly is tied to the matter of degrees.

What this link says is plain: **giving up a place is positioned as the way to rise to a place.** To own the seat in an assembly and to own a degree work in opposite directions.

**دَرَجَٰت — Root: د-ر-ج.** A step, the step of a stair; and *daraja*, *tadrīj* (advancing step by step), *idrāj* (placing a thing into a sequence) are from the same root.

The word comes as an **indefinite plural**: "degrees" — how many is not said. The indefiniteness here conveys magnification (*taʿẓīm*).

### Two groups, or one?

The expression **وَٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْعِلْمَ** raises a grammatical question, and the exegetes differ:

| Reading | Its meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Two separate groups** | God raises those who believe, and He raises those given knowledge too | The conjunction (*wa*) ordinarily adds a new element |
| **The second from within the first** | Those who believe, and especially those given knowledge | The note *minkum* has determined the community; the second group is selected from it |

On the second reading two degrees are established: faith one degree, knowledge added to faith a second degree. This reading is widespread in the classical commentaries.

Here I adopt no firm preference; both readings are grammatically possible. But this must be stated: the second reading does not count knowledge as a cause of rising **independent** of faith. Knowledge is something added on top of faith. This also accords with the verse's place within the sūra — throughout the sūra the measure is not knowledge itself but what is done with knowledge.

### أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْعِلْمَ — "those who have been given knowledge"

**The structure is passive.** The verse does not say "those who acquire knowledge", "those who learn knowledge", "the learned". It says **"those who have been given knowledge"**.

This choice is important and is consistent in the Qurʾān. The same pattern occurs many times in Sūrat al-Baqara: **أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ** — "those who have been given the Book". And in that sūra what this expression does was treated at length: to have been given the Book is not a privilege but a **burden**; and it is possible to be crushed beneath that burden.

The same structure is built for knowledge too. Knowledge is named not as an acquisition but as a trust. This has two consequences:

1. **The ground for boasting falls away.** One does not boast of something given.
2. **Responsibility arises.** An account is asked for a trust.

And this balances the verse's promise of "He raises". The rising is not the automatic consequence of knowledge; it is something that comes about when the person who has been given knowledge makes room, when he rises, that is, when he **does not make his knowledge an instrument of position.**

Indeed the verse-ending confirms this: **وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ** — "He is aware of what you do". Not of your knowledge, but **of what you do.**

### The reports on the occasion of revelation

Several narratives are transmitted in the sources: that there was a shortage of room in the Prophet's assembly; that when those who had taken part at Badr came, no room was made for them; that the People of the Bench could find nowhere to sit.

These reports differ in detail, and which of them is the direct occasion of the verse has not been settled. I give all of them here with the note "so it is transmitted".

But the following general picture is the common point of the reports and is plausible: in Medina the Prophet's assembly had grown crowded and the seating arrangement had become an issue. When a community grows, who sits where produces a mark of hierarchy of itself. The verse breaks that mark.

### What this says to the present

**One. Sharing your place.** This is the most concrete form of the verse, and it has not aged at all. In a community the place one sits in, the turn to speak, the share of attention — all of these are limited resources, and once seized they are not willingly given up. The verse counts giving them up as a cause of rising.

**Two. The converting of knowledge into rank.** When the promise that those given knowledge will be raised stands beside the verse-ending (aware of what you do), a measure emerges: knowledge does not carry its owner upward automatically. The person who turns knowledge into an instrument of position is already not doing the verse's first command — making room.

**Three. Leaving the recompense indefinite.** That the sentence "may God make room for you" does not say what will be made room in takes the recompense of a good deed out of being calculable. A calculable recompense turns a good deed into an investment.

## 58/12 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ إِذَا نَٰجَيْتُمُ ٱلرَّسُولَ فَقَدِّمُوا۟ بَيْنَ يَدَىْ نَجْوَىٰكُمْ صَدَقَةً ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ وَأَطْهَرُ فَإِن لَّمْ تَجِدُوا۟ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū idhā nājaytumu'r-rasūla fa-qaddimū bayna yaday najwākum ṣadaqa, dhālika khayrun lakum wa aṭhar, fa-in lam tajidū fa-inna'llāha ghafūrun raḥīm*

"O you who believe! When you are to speak privately with the Messenger, give alms before your private talk. That is better for you and purer. If you cannot find the means, then surely God is forgiving, merciful."

### The most unusual ruling of the sūra

This verse is the clearest instance in the Qurʾān of a ruling that is laid down and then **lifted after a very short time**. The next verse will lift the ruling.

First one must understand what the ruling is: whoever wishes to have a **private audience** with the Prophet is to give alms before the audience.

The scene is this: in Medina the Prophet is no longer only a religious leader but the administrator of a community. Everyone wants to reach him. And reaching him costs nothing. The consequence of this is that a portion of the time goes to matters that are not really important — and, more importantly, that speaking alone with the Prophet becomes a **mark of standing.**

How many times someone has spoken alone with the Prophet easily turns into a sign of status within a community. And this produces a value independent of the subject of the conversation: even if the matter is not important, having had the audience means something.

### فَقَدِّمُوا۟ بَيْنَ يَدَىْ نَجْوَىٰكُمْ

**قَدِّمُوا۟ — Root: ق-د-م.** The foot; to go ahead. In Form II (*tafʿīl*): to put forward, to send on ahead.

**بَيْنَ يَدَىْ** — word for word "between the two hands"; as an idiom, **"before, in advance of"**. In Arabic it is common to express what is in front of a thing as the direction in which the hands reach.

The same idiom occurs in another place in the Qurʾān, in a note of decorum concerning the Prophet: *"Do not **put yourselves forward** before God and His Messenger"* (al-Ḥujurāt 49/1). There it is asked that one not put oneself forward; here it is asked that something be put forward. The two verses together establish a balance: do not put yourself forward, but put your property forward.

### What the ruling achieved

The function of the alms condition can be read from several angles, and all of them fit the text:

**One. A filter.** When you load a cost onto something, whether that thing is really necessary is put to the test. The person who has a genuinely important matter pays the price; the one who comes only to be seen does not. That is, the condition measures the seriousness of the request.

**This is a mechanism known in economics**: a resource without a price is exhausted not in the order of need but in the order of demand. When a price is set, demand approaches need. I write this not as a claim that "the Qurʾān knew economics" but to show that the mechanism itself is old and familiar.

**Two. Putting a cost on standing.** If the audience has become a mark of status, the price of that mark must be paid. And the price goes to someone else.

**Three. The protection of time.** An administrator's time is the community's shared resource. This condition is an indirect way of protecting that resource.

**Four — and most interesting of all: the directing of the resource.** The price does not go to the Prophet. **It is given as alms** — that is, to those in need. That is, the price of access flows not to the person accessed but to the lowest layer of the community.

This last point separates the regulation from an "access fee". An access fee enriches the one who receives it and turns access into a purchasable good. Here there is no party receiving the fee; the payment is transferred to someone else.

### ذَٰلِكَ خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ وَأَطْهَرُ

Two elatives (*ism al-tafḍīl*, the form conveying superiority): *khayr* (better) and *aṭhar* (purer).

**"Better" by comparison with what?** The term of comparison is not stated. It is understood from the context: by comparison with an audience without giving alms.

**أَطْهَر — Root: ط-ه-ر.** Purity. What is being spoken of as purified?

The classical explanations go in two directions: (a) the purification of property — alms refining the property; (b) **the purification of the heart** — the refining away of the display and the seeking of standing within the request for an audience.

The second appears more suited to the context of the verse: the verse is already regulating a matter of intention. I record this as a preference; it is not binding.

### فَإِن لَّمْ تَجِدُوا۟ — the exemption, together with the ruling

The exemption is placed inside the ruling as well: whoever cannot find the means is exempt.

This is a consistent structure in the Qurʾān's financial obligations — we saw the same sequence in the ẓihār expiation (*fa-man lam yajid*, *fa-man lam yastaṭiʿ*). The obligation does not rise above the means.

And the names that come after the exemption are striking: **غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ.** For the one who has not the means it is not said "he is exempt", it is said "God is forgiving". That is, the exemption is framed not like the exercise of a right but like a favour. This preserves the seriousness of the obligation: the one who is exempt is also aware that he has missed something.

## 58/13 — ءَأَشْفَقْتُمْ أَن تُقَدِّمُوا۟ بَيْنَ يَدَىْ نَجْوَىٰكُمْ صَدَقَٰتٍ فَإِذْ لَمْ تَفْعَلُوا۟ وَتَابَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ فَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَأَطِيعُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥ وَٱللَّهُ خَبِيرٌۢ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ

*A-ashfaqtum an tuqaddimū bayna yaday najwākum ṣadaqāt, fa-idh lam tafʿalū wa tāba'llāhu ʿalaykum fa-aqīmu'ṣ-ṣalāta wa ātu'z-zakāta wa aṭīʿu'llāha wa rasūlah, wa'llāhu khabīrun bimā taʿmalūn*

"Did you shrink from giving alms before your private talk? Since you did not do it, and God has accepted your repentance, then hold up the prayer, give the zakāt, and obey God and His Messenger. God is aware of what you do."

### ءَأَشْفَقْتُمْ — to shrink from

**Root: ش-ف-ق.** The original sense of this root is **thinness**: a thing's being thin, the thin boundary between two things.

From the same root comes **شَفَق (*shafaq*)**: that thin, mixed intermediate zone between day and night. Neither wholly light nor wholly dark.

The analysis of this root was made in the sections on al-Inshiqāq (84/16) and al-Maʿārij (70/27-28). The finding established there was this: *ishfāq* is an intermediate feeling that is neither pure fear nor complete security — a hesitancy mixed with fear, a wariness. And that the expression *ghayru maʾmūn* (that of which one cannot be secure) and the word *muʾmin* come from the same root showed that faith is built in this intermediate zone.

**Here the same fine feeling appears in the face of something very everyday: money.**

The verse does not say "were you miserly", "did you not give", "did you hold back". It says **"did you shrink"**. The word describes not a bare miserliness but the hesitation that stands in front of that decision: the pause before the hand goes to the pocket.

This is a very accurate diagnosis. Because no one thinks "I do not want to give". What is thought are intermediate sentences such as "is it really necessary", "could it be another time", "is now quite the moment". *Ishfāq* is exactly the feeling of those intermediate sentences.

**The interrogative hamza (ءَ)** conveys rebuke (*tawbīkh*). That is, the question is not asking for information; it sets a behaviour in front of the addressee and shows it to him.

### The lifting of the ruling

**فَإِذْ لَمْ تَفْعَلُوا۟** — "since you did not do it". The verse openly records that the ruling was not carried out.

This is noteworthy in respect of the way the Qurʾān speaks about itself: the text does not conceal that one of its commands was not fulfilled; it puts it on record.

**وَتَابَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْكُمْ** — "and God has accepted your repentance". The non-fulfilment is named as a fault; but in the same sentence it is forgiven.

**And then three general obligations are set in place of the ruling:** the prayer, zakāt, obedience. That is, a particular financial condition is lifted, and in its place the obligations that already exist are recalled.

### The question of abrogation

This verse is counted, in the tradition of legal theory, the clearest and least disputed instance of the debate over **abrogation** (*naskh*, the lifting of a ruling by a ruling that comes later). Al-Baqara 2/106, the basic verse on abrogation, and the frame treated there, find a concrete instance here.

I do not re-open the discussion of the concept of abrogation itself here; I rely on the treatment in al-Baqara. But attention must be drawn to something this instance shows:

**The ruling did its work even without being carried out.**

Let us unfold this. The alms condition was laid down. According to the reports very few people — in one report only a single person — fulfilled it, and then the ruling was lifted. To all appearances a failed regulation.

But the real function of the regulation was to **make an attitude visible**. Before the condition was laid down, who genuinely had an important matter and who merely wanted to be seen could not be told apart. When the condition was laid down, demand fell. That is, a measurement was made and a result obtained.

The question of the thirteenth verse — "did you shrink?" — is exactly the result of that measurement. The ruling that was lifted left a diagnosis behind it.

This reading is an inference and I do not present it as a transmitted report. But the ordering of the verses supports it: the ruling is laid down, it is not carried out, and as it is lifted a question is asked. The question suggests that the real purpose of the ruling was to gather information.

### فَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ

The place of these two commands here is meaningful.

The analysis of the word **iqāma** was made at al-Baqara 2/3: from the root *q-w-m*, "to raise upright, to set up, to hold standing". The prayer is not merely performed; it is **held upright.**

**زَكَوٰة — Root: ز-ك-و.** This root has two senses, and both stand within the word: **to increase, to grow, to be blessed with abundance** and **to be cleansed, to be purified.**

How the two senses join is the finest side of the word: for a plant to be able to grow, the excess that harms it must be pruned away. Pruning is a diminishing, but its result is increase. Zakāt is what is subtracted from property but increases property.

**And now look at the sequence.** The ruling that was lifted was alms — that is, a voluntary, particular payment given for access to a particular person. What is put in its place is **zakāt**: obligatory, general, valid for everyone.

That is, the verse does this: it lifts a particular price of access and recalls the general obligation that everyone pays. The payment is not lifted; it **ceases to be personalised.**

This can also be read as a preparation for the second half of the sūra: the distinction of "two sides" that will be established in the next section will be built not upon personal privileges but upon shared obligations.

## 58/14 — أَلَمْ تَرَ إِلَى ٱلَّذِينَ تَوَلَّوْا۟ قَوْمًا غَضِبَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِم مَّا هُم مِّنكُمْ وَلَا مِنْهُمْ وَيَحْلِفُونَ عَلَى ٱلْكَذِبِ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

*A-lam tara ila'lladhīna tawallaw qawman ghaḍiba'llāhu ʿalayhim, mā hum minkum wa lā minhum, wa yaḥlifūna ʿala'l-kadhibi wa hum yaʿlamūn*

"Have you not seen those who take as allies a people with whom God is angry? They are neither of you nor of them. And they swear to a lie knowingly."

### تَوَلَّوْا۟ — to take as an ally

**Root: و-ل-ي.** The original sense of the root is **nearness and adjacency**. *Walī*: the one who is right beside you, with nothing else between. From the same root *walāya* (protection, authority), *mawlā* (master/freedman — running both ways), *tawallī* (to turn towards, to take someone near), *muwālāt* (alliance).

The concrete core of the root must be attended to: the matter is **not an emotional closeness but a positional adjacency.** The *walī* is the person who stands at your side, between whom and you no one comes.

This clarifies what is being discussed in this section of the sūra. The subject is not whether one loves someone or not; it is **choosing whose side to stand on.** This is the matter of taking one's place in the ranks.

This distinction must be stressed, because it is the most important safeguard against a misreading of the verse. In another place the Qurʾān says openly that a relation of **goodness and justice** with those who do not fight and do not drive out is not forbidden:

> *"God does not forbid you from doing good to those who have not fought you over religion and have not driven you from your homes, or from being just towards them. God loves the just."* (al-Mumtaḥana 60/8)

That verse draws, within the Qurʾān itself, the difference between *walāya* (taking one's place in the ranks) and *birr* and *qisṭ* (goodness and justice). Al-Mujādala 58/14 takes the first as its subject; the second is a separate field and is expressly preserved.

### قَوْمًا غَضِبَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهِم

Here, per the method (`STYLE.md`), an open note must be entered.

**The verse gives no group's name.** *"Qawman"* — indefinite: "a people". In the classical commentaries there are views on whom this expression points to, and these rest on the political picture of a particular period in Medina.

But the expression itself is a sentence of **attribute**: "a people with whom God is angry". An attribute depends not on identity but on situation.

The note of `STYLE.md` holds here exactly: no wholesale ruling is issued about an ethnic or religious group. What the verse describes is a community displaying a particular behaviour at a particular historical moment; and its real object is to speak not of that community but of **the hypocrites who make an alliance with it.** The subject of the sentence is *tawallaw* — those who take as allies.

Indeed the continuation of the verse shows this as well: the people criticised are not even of that community.

### مَّا هُم مِّنكُمْ وَلَا مِنْهُمْ — to be caught in between

One of the sharpest diagnoses of the sūra.

This sentence describes the hypocrite's position in a single stroke: **of neither side.** And this is the consequence of his own choices — in wanting to keep his relation with both sides open, he is left with a place in neither.

The same diagnosis is made in more detail in Sūrat al-Nisāʾ:

> *"They waver in between, belonging neither to these nor to those."* — **مُّذَبْذَبِينَ بَيْنَ ذَٰلِكَ لَآ إِلَىٰ هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ وَلَآ إِلَىٰ هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ** (al-Nisāʾ 4/143)

The word **مُذَبْذَب** completes the picture: its root *ذ-ب-ذ-ب* describes something hung on a rope and swinging both ways in the wind. It has no fixed place; with every gust it goes to the other side.

**And this conveys the impossibility of being on both sides at once.** A person who tries to hold a place on both sides can gain a full place in neither — because both sides know of his relation with the other.

This is as much a social observation as a moral ruling, and it holds at every scale. Trust requires taking a side; the person who gets on well with every side wins the trust of no side.

### وَيَحْلِفُونَ عَلَى ٱلْكَذِبِ وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ

**وَهُمْ يَعْلَمُونَ** — "and they know". This note separates the behaviour from a mistake. To swear falsely is not an error; it is a deliberate operation.

**حَلِف — Root: ح-ل-ف.** To swear. From the same root *ḥilf*: alliance, treaty (in the Jāhiliyya agreements between tribes were called *ḥilf*; as in *Ḥilf al-fuḍūl*).

An interesting intersection: the same root means both "oath" and "alliance". Because alliances were established by oath. And in this verse exactly an alliance (*tawallaw*) and an oath (*yaḥlifūn*) occur side by side. The kinship of the words comes from the phenomenon itself.

### The oath's becoming an instrument

This verse and the next two are built upon the oath. And the matter is this: the oath is a society's most basic instrument of trust.

The working of the oath rests on this: the one who gives his word accepts that something will befall him if he does not keep it. That is, the oath backs the word **with a price.**

The one who swears falsely uses up this mechanism itself. If oaths are not kept in a society, the oath becomes useless — and then no instrument for establishing trust remains.

For this reason the Qurʾān records the false oath as a grave offence. The harm is done not to the person lied to but **to the instrument itself.**

## 58/15-16 — أَعَدَّ ٱللَّهُ لَهُمْ عَذَابًا شَدِيدًا إِنَّهُمْ سَآءَ مَا كَانُوا۟ يَعْمَلُونَ • ٱتَّخَذُوٓا۟ أَيْمَٰنَهُمْ جُنَّةً فَصَدُّوا۟ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ فَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ مُّهِينٌ

*Aʿadda'llāhu lahum ʿadhāban shadīdā, innahum sāʾa mā kānū yaʿmalūn • Ittakhadhū aymānahum junnatan fa-ṣaddū ʿan sabīli'llāh, fa-lahum ʿadhābun muhīn*

"God has prepared for them a severe punishment. How evil is what they do! • They have taken their oaths as a shield and have turned people away from the path of God. For them there is a humiliating punishment."

### أَيْمَٰنَهُمْ — a word with two senses

**Root: ي-م-ن.** This root covers several things at once in Arabic, and all of them are connected to one another:

- **يَمِين (*yamīn*)** — the right hand.
- **يَمِين (*yamīn*)** — an oath, a sworn word.
- **يُمْن (*yumn*)** — blessing, good fortune (from the right side's being counted fortunate).
- **يَمَن** — Yemen; the region lying to the right with respect to the Kaʿba.

**What is the link between the oath and the right hand?** The classical explanation is this: in swearing, the right hand was extended and hands were clasped. An oath is a word sealed with a movement of the hand.

That is, at the origin of the word there is a **bodily movement**. This says what an oath is: the word alone was not seen as sufficient; a bodily sign was added to it. Words fly away; what has been shaken on remains.

**أَيْمَٰن** as a plural means "oaths" here; but the word's sense of the right hand stands in the background too, and gives the sentence an interesting flavour: they have made their oaths — that is, the hands that seal their words — into a shield.

### جُنَّة — a shield

**Root: ج-ن-ن.** The wealth of derivatives of this root in Arabic is striking, and all of them meet in a single idea: **to cover, to hide, to make invisible.**

| Word | Its meaning | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **جِنّ (*jinn*)** | An unseen being | Itself, from sight |
| **جَنَّة (*janna*)** | A garden | The trees cover the ground |
| **جَنِين (*janīn*)** | The child in the womb | The womb covers it |
| **مَجْنُون (*majnūn*)** | A person whose reason is covered | The reason is covered |
| **جُنَّة (*junna*)** | A shield | It covers the one who carries it |
| **جَنَّ ٱلَّيْلُ** | Night fell | The dark covers everything |

This root was touched on in Sūrat al-Jinn. What is to be added here is the place of **junna** in this family: a shield does not make its bearer invisible but makes him **unreachable**. It comes in between.

**And the metaphor sits exactly right:** an oath is normally something that strengthens a word. Here it has become an obstacle set in between. The man swears, and the other side cannot question him — because an oath closes off questioning. The social function of the oath (to confirm a word) has been inverted and turned into an instrument of defence.

This image **is repeated almost word for word in Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn**: *"They have taken their oaths as a shield and have turned people away from the path of God"* (al-Munāfiqūn 63/2). The two sūras use the same sentence. This shows that the behaviour described is not a single event but a recognised pattern.

### فَصَدُّوا۟ عَن سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ

**صَدَّ — Root: ص-د-د.** To turn, to hold back, to turn away. The verb can be used both intransitively (he himself turned away) and transitively (he turned another away); here the context indicates the second.

**The letter فَ (*fāʾ*)** conveys consequence: they took their oaths as a shield, and **as a result of this** they turned people from the path.

That is, the obstruction is the direct consequence of the shield. How? Because the person who has become untouchable stays within the community and works from the inside. Without the shield his position would be known and his effect limited.

### عَذَابٌ مُّهِينٌ — a repeated adjective

This adjective occurs for the second time in the sūra (the first at 58/5). And the two occurrences bind the sūra's two groups to one another: those who stand openly on the opposite side (5) and those who stand secretly on the opposite side (16). Punishment is promised to both with the same adjective.

This is the preparation for the two-sides table that will be established in the second half of the sūra: the sūra places open opposition and hidden opposition in the same category.

## 58/17 — لَّن تُغْنِىَ عَنْهُمْ أَمْوَٰلُهُمْ وَلَآ أَوْلَٰدُهُم مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ شَيْـًٔا أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ أَصْحَٰبُ ٱلنَّارِ هُمْ فِيهَا خَٰلِدُونَ

*Lan tughniya ʿanhum amwāluhum wa lā awlāduhum mina'llāhi shayʾā, ulāʾika aṣḥābu'n-nāri hum fīhā khālidūn*

"Neither their property nor their children will avail them anything against God. They are the companions of the fire; they will remain in it."

### أَمْوَٰل and أَوْلَاد — a repeated pair

These two words frequently occur side by side in the Qurʾān, and almost every time they do the same work: they name the two supports that make a person feel secure.

**Why these two?** Because a person's power in this world comes largely from two things:

- **Property** — power in the present. The means to buy, to influence, to protect.
- **Children** — power and continuity in the future. The continuation of his name, a support in old age, weight within the tribe.

That is, one provides a horizontal security (present influence), the other a vertical one (continuation through time). When the two come together, everything a person needs in order to count himself sufficient is complete.

**تُغْنِىَ — Root: غ-ن-ي.** *Ghinā*: wealth, freedom from need. *Ighnāʾ* (Form IV, *ifʿāl*): to make someone free from need, to suffice, to avail. *Ghaniyy*: the one who has need of nothing.

That the verb is chosen from this root is meaningful: property and children claim to make their owner *ghanī* (free from need). The verse rejects this claim — using the word's own root.

**لَّن** — a definite negation with regard to the future: "never".

### The link with al-Humaza

This verse must be read together with Sūrat al-Humaza, because the psychology of the same supposition was analysed there.

Al-Humaza 104/3: **يَحْسَبُ أَنَّ مَالَهُۥٓ أَخْلَدَهُۥ** — "he supposes that his property has made him everlasting."

The finding established there was this: beneath accumulation lies a search for immortality; property is supposed to be a buffer against death.

Al-Mujādala 58/17 rejects the same supposition by carrying it over to what comes after death. And note: **the verb used in al-Humaza and the verb used in the next verse of this sūra are the same** — *yaḥsabu* / *yaḥsabūna*. The two sūras name the same mental operation: supposing.

And the word *khālidūn* completes the irony here. The man in al-Humaza supposed that his property had made him *akhlada* (everlasting) — the same root **خ-ل-د**. In this verse everlastingness is genuinely granted, but in the fire: **هُمْ فِيهَا خَٰلِدُونَ**.

That is, the thing sought is found; with its place changed. This is a structure the Qurʾān often uses: not the rejection of the demand but **the granting of its counterpart in another place.**

There is a third link as well: at al-Muddaththir 74/12-13 it is said *"I gave him extended property and sons before his eyes"* — the same pair, counted this time as a favour. The three verses together: property and children are given (al-Muddaththir), immortality is supposed (al-Humaza), it avails nothing at the reckoning (al-Mujādala).

## 58/18 — يَوْمَ يَبْعَثُهُمُ ٱللَّهُ جَمِيعًا فَيَحْلِفُونَ لَهُۥ كَمَا يَحْلِفُونَ لَكُمْ وَيَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّهُمْ عَلَىٰ شَىْءٍ أَلَآ إِنَّهُمْ هُمُ ٱلْكَٰذِبُونَ

*Yawma yabʿathuhumu'llāhu jamīʿan fa-yaḥlifūna lahu kamā yaḥlifūna lakum wa yaḥsabūna annahum ʿalā shayʾ, alā innahum humu'l-kādhibūn*

"On the day when God raises them all up, they will swear to Him as they swear to you, and will suppose that they are on something. Know well: it is they who are the liars."

### Habit outliving death

This verse builds one of the most disturbing scenes in the Qurʾān, and its force comes from the **strangeness** of the scene.

The moment of resurrection. Everything is open. And at that moment what are they doing? **They are swearing.**

The illogic of this is plain: the function of an oath is to confirm something not seen. In a place where everything is seen an oath has no meaning at all. And yet they swear.

**كَمَا يَحْلِفُونَ لَكُمْ** — "as they swear to you". The same verb, the same pattern, the same reflex.

What the verse says is this: if a behaviour is repeated enough, it ceases to be the result of a calculation and becomes a **reflex**. A reflex continues even when the setting changes — because it does not read the setting.

This is an extremely accurate observation from the point of view of moral psychology. A person who has told a lie often enough no longer chooses the lie; the lie has become his default response. For this reason he goes on lying even in places where it is obvious that it will bring no benefit.

The Qurʾān builds this scene in other places too: *"Then they will have no excuse left but to say 'By God, our Lord, we were not mushrikūn'"* (al-Anʿām 6/23). The same reflex.

### وَيَحْسَبُونَ أَنَّهُمْ عَلَىٰ شَىْءٍ

**"They suppose that they are on something."**

The expression is strangely indefinite. "Something" — what it is is not said. And this indefiniteness looks deliberate.

**عَلَىٰ شَىْءٍ** — "on something". The particle *ʿalā* here conveys resting: standing upon a ground. That is, they suppose they have a support.

That what it is is not said suggests that the support is **undefined for them too**. They feel that they have a position, but they cannot say what it rests on. This is the exact counterpart of the state described at the beginning of the sūra, "neither of you nor of them": the person who has a relation with both sides supposes that he is standing somewhere, but cannot say where he stands.

The Qurʾān uses this expression in a positive context elsewhere, and the contrast is instructive: *"O People of the Book! You are **on nothing** until you apply the Torah, the Gospel and what has been sent down to you from your Lord"* (al-Māʾida 5/68). There the same pattern: *lastum ʿalā shayʾ*. Having a ground is tied to putting into practice.

### أَلَآ إِنَّهُمْ هُمُ ٱلْكَٰذِبُونَ

**أَلَآ** — the particle of drawing attention: "know well, take note!". In the Qurʾān it is used where a ruling is to be made definitive.

**هُمُ ٱلْكَٰذِبُونَ** — a threefold emphasis: *inna* + the separate pronoun *hum* + *al-kādhibūn* with the definite article.

In Arabic one could have said *inna hum kādhibūn* ("they are liars"). When *humu'l-kādhibūn* is said, restriction (*ḥaṣr*) arises: **"it is they who are the liars"** — as though the category of lying were assigned to them.

And this pattern will recur three times in the sūra, always in the same structure:

| Verse | Expression |
|---|---|
| **58/18** | *alā innahum humu'l-**kādhibūn*** |
| **58/19** | *alā inna ḥizba'sh-shayṭāni humu'l-**khāsirūn*** |
| **58/22** | *alā inna ḥizba'llāhi humu'l-**mufliḥūn*** |

Three verses give three rulings with the same pattern: the liars, those who suffer loss, those who attain deliverance. This threefold repetition is spread over verses 18, 19 and 22, and the sūra closes with it; the structure sets up a closing rhythm.

## 58/19 — ٱسْتَحْوَذَ عَلَيْهِمُ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنُ فَأَنسَىٰهُمْ ذِكْرَ ٱللَّهِ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ حِزْبُ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ أَلَآ إِنَّ حِزْبَ ٱلشَّيْطَٰنِ هُمُ ٱلْخَٰسِرُونَ

*Istaḥwadha ʿalayhimu'sh-shayṭānu fa-ansāhum dhikra'llāh, ulāʾika ḥizbu'sh-shayṭān, alā inna ḥizba'sh-shayṭāni humu'l-khāsirūn*

"Satan has taken hold of them and made them forget the remembrance of God. They are the party of Satan. Know well: the party of Satan are the losers."

### ٱسْتَحْوَذَ — to take hold of

**Root: ح-و-ذ.** The concrete sense of the root comes from herding: *al-ḥāʾidh* is the shepherd who **drives the flock from behind**. *Ḥāza'l-ibila*: he gathered the camels, put them before him and drove them.

The image is very clear: a shepherd gathers scattered animals, brings them together and drives them in the direction he wants. The animals may suppose that they are choosing their own direction; but the one who determines the direction is the man behind.

**Form X (*istifʿāl*)** here conveys completion and total seizure: not merely driving, but **taking hold completely and directing.**

**A point of morphology.** This verb is a known example in the Arabic grammar books. The normal rule is this: in Form X the weak middle letter (*wāw*/*yāʾ*) turns into an *alif* — *istaqwama* → *istaqāma*, *istaṣwaba* → *istaṣāba*. In this verb the transformation has not occurred: *istaḥwadha* has stayed as it is.

The lexicographers record this as **anomalous** (*shādhdh*) and mention it together with its few analogues. That the word has remained outside the rule leaves a roughness in the ear — it catches where it ought to run smooth. I do not turn this into a claim about meaning; I only record that the word stands in a rare and striking form in the language.

### فَأَنسَىٰهُمْ ذِكْرَ ٱللَّهِ — the making to forget

**فَ (*fāʾ*)** conveys consequence: the **result** of the taking hold is a making to forget.

That is, the work Satan does is not to make someone do something but to make him **forget** something. Not an active command to evil, but the erasure of a remembering.

This is a consistent part of the sūra's attitude towards Satan. In the tenth verse the function of *najwā* was "to grieve"; here it is "to make forget". Neither is a direct harm; both are a mental intervention.

**ذِكْر — Root: ذ-ك-ر.** To mention, to remember, to utter. At the centre of the root there is both holding in the mind and saying with the tongue — the two in a single word.

**And now look at the next sūra.** Al-Ḥashr 59/19:

> **وَلَا تَكُونُوا۟ كَٱلَّذِينَ نَسُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ فَأَنسَىٰهُمْ أَنفُسَهُمْ**
> "Do not be like those who forgot God, so that He made them forget themselves."

**Two neighbouring sūras give two links of the same chain of verbs:**

| | **al-Mujādala 58/19** | **al-Ḥashr 59/19** |
|---|---|---|
| Who makes forget | Satan | God |
| What is forgotten | The remembrance of God | The person himself |
| The structure | *fa-ansāhum* | *fa-ansāhum* |

The chain is completed: **Satan makes one forget God → the one who forgets God forgets himself.** The same verb (*ansā*), with two different agents and two different objects, in two neighbouring sūras.

We shall discuss the logic of the second link in detail in the section on al-Ḥashr. Here this much is enough: a person defines himself only by a reference; when the reference is erased the definition is erased too.

### حِزْب — the root itself

**Root: ح-ز-ب.** The word's use in Arabic goes in two directions:

1. **حِزْب (*ḥizb*)** — a community, a company, a side; a group gathered around a purpose. Also a portion of a thing (the division of the Qurʾān into "ḥizb"s is this sense).
2. **حَزَبَهُ ٱلْأَمْرُ** — the affair pressed him, hemmed him in, became a trouble to him. *Ḥāzib*: a distress, a heavy matter that befalls one.

**How do the two senses join?** The classical explanation is this: people gather when a distress comes. *Ḥizb* is originally **a community come together around an affair**. That is, what makes a group a group is a shared matter.

This explains well what the word is. A *ḥizb* is not a blood tie, not a tribe, not a lineage. **Those gathered around a shared affair.** The word names not an inherited belonging but a chosen one.

And this connects directly with the 22nd verse of the sūra: there it will be said that the tie of lineage does not determine which side one is on. The choice of the word *ḥizb* is apt for this reason — the word itself already conveys a togetherness outside lineage.

**Other uses in the Qurʾān:**

- **ٱلْأَحْزَاب** — the groups that combined at the Battle of the Trench; and the name of Sūrat al-Aḥzāb.
- *"Every group (*ḥizb*) rejoices in what it has"* — **كُلُّ حِزْبٍۭ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ** (al-Muʾminūn 23/53; the same sentence occurs at al-Rūm 30/32 as well). This verse conveys the nature of factionalism: every group counts what it has as sufficient and is happy with it. That is, factionalism is a state of **satisfaction** more than a claim to truth.

In Sūrat al-Bayyina the subject of division (*tafriqa*) was treated by way of al-Rūm 30/30-32; the finding there joins with this place: division comes **after** the proof has arrived, and in spite of the proof.

### ٱلْخَٰسِرُونَ — loss

**Root: خ-س-ر.** It is a commercial word: the diminishing of capital, to make a loss. *Khusrān* is not the absence of profit — it is **the going of the principal.**

This word was treated in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr: *"Man is surely in loss"* (al-ʿAṣr 103/2). The finding established there was this: loss is not a failure to gain; it is the losing of what one has. The capital in a person's hand is his lifetime, and that capital diminishes at every moment.

Here the same word becomes the attribute of a group. And opposite it, in the 22nd verse, *mufliḥūn* will be set.

## 58/20-21 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يُحَآدُّونَ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥٓ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ فِى ٱلْأَذَلِّينَ • كَتَبَ ٱللَّهُ لَأَغْلِبَنَّ أَنَا۠ وَرُسُلِىٓ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَوِىٌّ عَزِيزٌ

*Inna'lladhīna yuḥāddūna'llāha wa rasūlahu ulāʾika fi'l-adhallīn • Kataba'llāhu la-aghlibanna anā wa rusulī, inna'llāha qawiyyun ʿazīz*

"Those who draw a boundary against God and His Messenger are among the most abased. • God has written: 'I shall surely prevail, I and My messengers.' Surely God is strong, mighty."

### فِى ٱلْأَذَلِّينَ — "among the most abased"

**Root: ذ-ل-ل.** And this root has two faces; they must be distinguished.

- **ذِلَّة (*dhilla*)** — humiliation, being held in contempt. An abasement **imposed** from outside.
- **ذَلُول (*dhalūl*)** — docile, submissive, fit for use. An accepted **pliancy**.

The uses of the second sense in the Qurʾān are instructive:

- At al-Baqara 2/71, in the description of the cow to be slaughtered: *"Neither **broken to the yoke** (*dhalūl*) to plough the earth, nor a cow that waters the tilth."*
- At al-Naḥl 16/69, of the bee: *"Enter the ways of your Lord **submissively** (*dhululan*)."*
- At al-Mulk 67/15: *"It is He who made the earth **subservient** (*dhalūlan*) to you."*

That is, the root is neutral: the going of a thing's hardness, its becoming fit for use. **The difference lies in whether the submission is chosen or imposed.**

And this builds the irony of the verse. The person who committed *muḥādda* had refused to submit — he had drawn his own boundary. Its result is an abasement he did not choose. He who flees from a chosen submission falls into an imposed humiliation.

**ٱلْأَذَلِّين — the plural of the elative:** "the most abased ones". And the particle **فِى**: "**among** them". That is, the person is not abased alone; he is included in the class of the abased. This accords with the idea of *ḥizb* in the 19th verse: throughout the sūra everyone has a set.

### كَتَبَ ٱللَّهُ — "He has written"

**Root: ك-ت-ب.** To write. But in Arabic this verb is also used in the sense of "to decree, to make obligatory, to make definitive" (*kutiba ʿalaykumu'ṣ-ṣiyām* — "fasting has been written for you", al-Baqara 2/183).

**How do the two senses join?** What is written has been fixed. A spoken word can change; a written one does not. A record makes something irreversible.

In this verse *kataba* has the sense of "He made the ruling definitive".

### لَأَغْلِبَنَّ أَنَا۠ وَرُسُلِىٓ

**The heaviest form of emphasis:** at the head the **lām of the oath**, at the end the **heavy nūn** of emphasis. In Arabic this is the strongest way of emphasising a verb.

**أَنَا۠ وَرُسُلِىٓ** — "I and My messengers". The verb is already first person singular; the pronoun *anā* is grammatically unnecessary. That it has been added does two things: emphasis, and making it possible for *rusulī* to be coordinated with it (in Arabic, to coordinate with an attached pronoun it is preferred that the separate pronoun be mentioned).

**The order is striking:** first "I", then "My messengers". The prevailing is ascribed first to its source, then to its carriers.

### What does "to prevail" mean? — a place that requires care

This verse is one of those most open to political readings, and one must be careful here.

**The problem is this:** does the verse say that every prophet will win a worldly victory? The text does not permit this, because the Qurʾān itself says the contrary:

- *"They used to kill the prophets without right"* (al-Baqara 2/61).
- *"Some you denied, and some you killed"* (al-Baqara 2/87).

That is, some of the messengers were, by worldly measures, defeated. Then "prevailing" must be something else.

**The explanations given by the classical exegetes:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Prevailing by proof** | The victory is at the level of proof; the word the messenger brings cannot be refuted. The other side cannot silence it. |
| **Prevailing in the final reckoning** | The verse announces the ruling of the hereafter, not the outcome in this world. |
| **The prevailing of the cause** | Not the person of the messenger, but the message he brings, spreads and remains in the end. |
| **A general tendency** | The verse announces a rule, not a statement about every single event; it states the general direction of history. |

These views do not exclude one another and are often mentioned together in the classical commentaries.

**And this note must be entered:** this verse cannot be used by any group, movement or state to guarantee its own victory in advance. The verse writes the prevailing for God and His messengers; not for any structure that puts itself in their place. Per the method (`STYLE.md`) I do not enter into current political readings; but to say how this verse cannot be used is the business of exegesis.

Indeed the same sūra, one verse later, when it defines *ḥizb Allāh*, will give it as a **list of attributes** — not as the name of an institution, an organisation or a party.

### إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَوِىٌّ عَزِيزٌ

**قَوِىّ (*Qawiyy*) — Root: ق-و-ي.** Strength. From the same root *quwwa*, and *quwā* (the twists of a rope — what makes a rope strong is that its fibres are twisted together).

**عَزِيز (*ʿAzīz*) — Root: ع-ز-ز.** The concrete sense of this root is **hardness and scarcity**. *Arḍun ʿizāz*: hard ground that cannot be dug. *ʿIzza*: unconquerability, and rarity as well.

The two names together: to possess strength (*qawiyy*) and for that strength to be unconquerable (*ʿazīz*). One conveys quantity, the other quality.

And *ʿazīz* will occur in both the first and the last verse of the next sūra (al-Ḥashr). The sharing of a name between the two sūras goes together with the sharing of a subject: both take the question of power and prevailing as their subject.

## 58/22 — لَّا تَجِدُ قَوْمًا يُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْـَٔاخِرِ يُوَآدُّونَ مَنْ حَآدَّ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥ وَلَوْ كَانُوٓا۟ ءَابَآءَهُمْ أَوْ أَبْنَآءَهُمْ أَوْ إِخْوَٰنَهُمْ أَوْ عَشِيرَتَهُمْ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ كَتَبَ فِى قُلُوبِهِمُ ٱلْإِيمَٰنَ وَأَيَّدَهُم بِرُوحٍ مِّنْهُ وَيُدْخِلُهُمْ جَنَّٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَا رَضِىَ ٱللَّهُ عَنْهُمْ وَرَضُوا۟ عَنْهُ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ حِزْبُ ٱللَّهِ أَلَآ إِنَّ حِزْبَ ٱللَّهِ هُمُ ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ

*Lā tajidu qawman yuʾminūna bi'llāhi wa'l-yawmi'l-ākhiri yuwāddūna man ḥādda'llāha wa rasūlahu wa law kānū ābāʾahum aw abnāʾahum aw ikhwānahum aw ʿashīratahum, ulāʾika kataba fī qulūbihimu'l-īmāna wa ayyadahum bi-rūḥin minh, wa yudkhiluhum jannātin tajrī min taḥtiha'l-anhāru khālidīna fīhā, raḍiya'llāhu ʿanhum wa raḍū ʿanh, ulāʾika ḥizbu'llāh, alā inna ḥizba'llāhi humu'l-mufliḥūn*

"You will not find a people who believe in God and the last day forming a bond of affection with those who draw a boundary against God and His Messenger — even were they their fathers, their sons, their brothers or their clan. It is they in whose hearts God has written faith and whom He has supported with a spirit from Himself. He will admit them into gardens beneath which rivers flow, to remain in them forever. God is well pleased with them, and they are well pleased with Him. It is they who are the party of God. Know well: those who attain deliverance are the party of God."

### لَّا تَجِدُ — not a prohibition but a statement of fact

The side of the verse most easily passed over is the **mood** of the sentence.

The verse does not say *"do not form a bond of affection"*. It says **"you will not find"**. That is, not a command but a **declaration of impossibility** is being built.

The difference matters:

- Had a **command** been built, it could have been violated. Prohibitions can be broken; a broken prohibition is still a prohibition.
- When a **statement of fact** is built, a fact is announced: these two do not stand together.

That is, the verse says that faith and that bond **are incompatible with one another**. If a person is doing both at once, one of the two is not real.

**The address is singular:** *lā tajidu* — "you will not find". The one addressed is first the Prophet, but also everyone who reads. It is as though the text were giving the reader a task of observation: look, you will not see it.

### يُوَآدُّونَ — the difference between *wudd* and *ḥubb*

**Root: و-د-د.** *Wudd*: love. But which love?

The distinction the lexicographers make is this: *ḥubb* is love itself — what is in the heart. *Wudd* is **love made outward, turned into a relationship**. *Wudd* is loving + doing what love requires.

From the same root **ٱلْوَدُود (al-Wadūd)** is one of the names of God (Hūd 11/90; al-Burūj 85/14). This name was treated in Sūrat al-Burūj; there too the stress was on love's establishing a mutual relationship.

**Form III (*mufāʿala*):** *yuwāddūna* — a reciprocal *wudd*. That is, not a one-sided feeling but **a two-sided bond.**

**What this does in the verse is critical.** The verse does not say "they do not love". It does not forbid the love a person feels for his father, his son, his brother — such a prohibition would in any case be meaningless, because feelings do not change by command.

What is forbidden is **muwālāt/muwādda**: to establish a bond of mutual alliance, to take one's place in that rank. That is, the matter is not sentiment but **position.**

This distinction is what makes the verse applicable. To ask a person not to love his father is inhuman; to ask him not to stand in his father's rank is possible.

### مَنْ حَآدَّ ٱللَّهَ — not persons, but a position

Per the method (`STYLE.md`) an open note is needed here.

The verse does not enumerate a people, a religion, a lineage. What it enumerates is an **act**: *ḥādda* — to draw a boundary, to position oneself on the opposite side. And this act was defined in the fifth verse.

That is, the measure is not identity but **behaviour**. Whoever performs that act, the verse is speaking of him; whatever his identity, his affiliation, his name. Indeed the verse itself says this in the plainest way: the persons enumerated are **their own fathers, sons, brothers.** That is, not even the closest ring of identity serves as a measure.

And the note of al-Mumtaḥana 60/8 mentioned above holds here as well: to establish a relation of goodness and justice with those who do not fight and do not drive out is not forbidden. The two verses regulate different things.

### Four steps: father, son, brother, clan

**وَلَوْ كَانُوٓا۟ ءَابَآءَهُمْ أَوْ أَبْنَآءَهُمْ أَوْ إِخْوَٰنَهُمْ أَوْ عَشِيرَتَهُمْ**

The list is not random. It advances along two axes at once:

| Order | Bond | Axis |
|---|---|---|
| **Fathers** | Upwards | Vertical |
| **Sons** | Downwards | Vertical |
| **Brothers** | Sideways | Horizontal |
| **Clan** | Outwards | The wide ring |

That is, the list sweeps all the directions of kinship: up, down, sideways, and the wide circle. No direction is left open.

**And that it begins from the father is meaningful.** In tribal society the strongest bond is the father: identity comes from the father, the name comes from the father, protection comes from the father. That the list begins from the hardest case puts the force of the argument at the outset.

**عَشِيرَة — Root: ع-ش-ر.** *ʿAshara*: ten. And *muʿāshara*: to live together, to be intertwined. The *ʿashīra* is the wide kin-group within which a person lives and with which he shares his daily life.

The root's link with the number "ten" comes from the number's expressing a wholeness (the count of the fingers of the hands). *ʿAshīra* means the whole of a person's surroundings.

### That lineage is not a measure — a threefold link with al-Baqara

This principle was established in two separate places in Sūrat al-Baqara, and this verse completes the triad.

**Al-Baqara 2/124** — Abraham asks that the leadership given to him should continue in his descendants as well. The answer: **"My covenant does not reach the wrongdoers."** The finding established there: **lineage does not generate authority.** And this rule is not bent even for a prophet who asks.

**Al-Baqara 2/134 (and 2/141)** — *"That was a community that has passed away. Theirs is what they earned, and yours is what you earn."* The same sentence occurs twice in the sūra, each time immediately after the enumeration of a chain of ancestry. The finding established there: **lineage does not generate a record.** Neither for nor against.

**Al-Mujādala 58/22** — the third link: **lineage does not generate a rank.**

The three together build a system:

| Verse | What lineage does not generate |
|---|---|
| **al-Baqara 2/124** | Authority / office |
| **al-Baqara 2/134** | Record / account |
| **al-Mujādala 58/22** | Rank / belonging |

That is, the tie of lineage neither gives an office, nor transfers a merit or a sin, nor determines whose side one stands on. When all three are cut, one measure remains: the person's own choice.

**Other links in the Qurʾān:**

- **Hūd 11/45-46** — Noah says of his son, "he is of my family". The answer: *"He is not of your family; what he did was not righteous."* The place in the Qurʾān where the tie of lineage is cut most plainly.
- **Al-Tawba 9/23-24** — *"If your fathers, your sons, your brothers, your spouses, your clan… are dearer to you than God, His Messenger and struggle in His way, then wait."* The same list, in almost the same order.

When the list at al-Tawba 9/24 is compared with the list in this verse an interesting difference is seen: there the measure is **the quantity of love** ("if they are dearer"), here the measure is **taking one's place in the ranks**. Al-Tawba regulates priority, al-Mujādala position.

### كَتَبَ فِى قُلُوبِهِمُ ٱلْإِيمَٰنَ — what is written on the heart

And now return to the preceding verse: **كَتَبَ ٱللَّهُ لَأَغْلِبَنَّ** — "God has written: I shall prevail."

**The same verb, one verse apart, writes in two different places:**

| Verse | What is written | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **58/21** | The ruling of prevailing | (Not specified — the record of destiny) |
| **58/22** | Faith | On hearts |

The two writings are part of a single logic. The ruling written into history and the faith written on the heart are named with the same verb. I record this as a reading, but the repetition of the verb stands in the text.

**What does "to write on the heart" mean?** Writing conveys permanence and unchangeability. What is spoken is forgotten; what is written stays. That faith has been **written** on the heart says that it is not a temporary acceptance but a state made fixed.

And note the sequence: this sentence comes **as a consequence** of going beyond the tie of lineage. That is, the order is: the person chooses his rank → God writes faith on his heart. After the hard choice comes a strengthening.

### وَأَيَّدَهُم بِرُوحٍ مِّنْهُ — "He supported them with a spirit from Himself"

**أَيَّدَ — Root: أ-ي-د.** Power, strength. *Ayd*: strength. *Taʾyīd*: to strengthen, to support.

The word has a resemblance in sound to *yad* (hand), and the lexicographers sometimes connect them; but the roots are different (أ-ي-د / ي-د-ي). I record this as a possibility; I do not establish a firm link.

**بِرُوحٍ مِّنْهُ — "with a spirit from Himself".** This expression is disputed among the exegetes:

| View | Its ground |
|---|---|
| **Gabriel (Jibrīl)** | In the Qurʾān *rūḥ al-qudus* is used for Gabriel (al-Baqara 2/87, 2/253) and there too the same verb occurs: *ayyadnāhu bi-rūḥi'l-qudus* |
| **The Qurʾān / revelation** | *"We have revealed to you a spirit from Our command"* (al-Shūrā 42/52) |
| **The light of faith, the brightness arising in the heart** | The context: it has just been said that faith was written on the heart |
| **Help and victory** | From the sense of *rūḥ* as "liveliness, vitality" |

**There is a strong indication in favour of the first view:** the expression used of Jesus (ʿĪsā) at al-Baqara 2/87 is almost identical — *wa ayyadnāhu bi-rūḥi'l-qudus*. The same verb, the same particle, the same structure. This parallel is striking.

But here I adopt no preference. The word *rūḥ* is a word used in more than one sense in the Qurʾān, and there is not enough data to speak with certainty. I content myself with conveying the views.

**And its coming indefinite:** *bi-rūḥin* — "a spirit". Indefinite. This indefiniteness suggests that what exactly the word points to has been deliberately left open.

### رَضِىَ ٱللَّهُ عَنْهُمْ وَرَضُوا۟ عَنْهُ — mutual good pleasure

**Root: ر-ض-و.** Contentment, being well pleased. At the centre of the root lie "to accept, to find sufficient, not to object".

**A two-directional construction:** God is well pleased with them, they are well pleased with God. The same verb, with two agents.

This expression occurs in several places in the Qurʾān and each time comes as a concluding sentence. And in Sūrat al-Fajr the same pair met us in a different pattern:

> *"O soul at rest! Return to your Lord — **rāḍiya** (well pleased) and **marḍiyya** (well pleasing)."* (al-Fajr 89/27-28)

There we had an active participle and a passive participle; here two verbs. The same pair, in two different forms.

**The weight of the second direction.** The sentence "God is well pleased with them" is what one expects. The sentence "and they are well pleased with Him" is less spoken of and is harder.

Because this means the person's being at peace with what has befallen him — and in the context of this verse what has befallen is concrete: **separating from his father, his son, his brother, his clan.** For a person to be well pleased after doing that is not easy.

The setting of the two contentments side by side says that the relationship is not one-directional. The servant is not merely the one with whom there is good pleasure; he too is well pleased.

### ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ — the last word of the sūra

**Root: ف-ل-ح.** This root was treated at al-Baqara 2/5, and the finding established there sits directly on the close of the sūra:

> The root *f-l-ḥ*: **to split, to crack open, to divide.** From the same root comes **fallāḥ** — the farmer. The farmer is given this name because **he splits the soil**. The work of the plough is to break the crust of the earth. So *falāḥ* — deliverance — within the word's own logic: **to split an obstacle and come out, to break the shell, to come into a state of bearing fruit.**

**And now look at the content of the verse.**

The last verse of the sūra describes man's thickest shell: **the tie of lineage.** The ring that surrounds a person, that gives him his identity, and that is hardest to step out of. Fathers, sons, brothers, clan — these are the sturdiest walls around a person.

And those who split that wall and come out are called **mufliḥūn**: **those who split through.**

This overlap between the root of the word and the content of the verse can also be read as an accident; but given the consistency of the Qurʾān's word choices, it is at least worth recording. At al-Baqara 2/5 *mufliḥūn* was said, and seventy verses later the opposite picture came: the heart that does not split (2/74). Here a third position is added: the tie of lineage that is split.

And the next sūra, al-Ḥashr 59/9, will close another section with the same word: *"Those who are guarded against the *shuḥḥ* of their own souls — it is they who are the **mufliḥūn**."* Two neighbouring sūras name, with the same word, those who split two different shells: one the tie of lineage, the other the closedness of the soul.

### حِزْبُ ٱللَّهِ — a construct that must be read with care

The last sentence of the sūra is one of the most abused Qurʾānic expressions throughout history. For this reason what it says and what it does not say must be written out plainly.

**Note the structure of the verse:** *"It is **they** who are the ḥizb of God."* The demonstrative pronoun (*ulāʾika*) refers back to the **attributes** just enumerated:

1. To believe in God and the last day.
2. Not to form an alliance with those who stand on the opposite side — even were they kin.
3. To have faith written on their hearts.
4. To have been supported with a spirit.
5. To be well pleased and to be one with whom there is good pleasure.

That is, *ḥizb Allāh* is not a **name** but the **result** of these attributes. The attributes are enumerated first, and the construct is given afterwards.

**The meaning of this is:** no community, movement, institution or structure can take this construct as a title for itself. Because the granting of the construct has been tied to a list, and the last item of the list — God's good pleasure — is not a ruling any human being can issue about himself.

And the root of the word *ḥizb* supports this too: as we saw above, a *ḥizb* is those gathered around a shared affair. Not an inherited or a declared belonging; a togetherness formed around a burden that is carried.

Per the method (`STYLE.md`) I do not enter into current political disputes. But to say how this construct cannot be used lies within the bounds of exegesis: **whoever names himself with this construct has closed the verse's list in his own favour.** Whereas the list is open, and the ruling on its last item lies with no one.

## The sūra as a whole

### The architecture of the sūra

The sūra opens with a woman's complaint and closes with the description of a group. In between there are four sections, and the transitions between the sections are built with verbal links:

| Section | Subject | How it connects to the next |
|---|---|---|
| **1-4** | Ẓihār | *ḥudūd Allāh* (4) → *yuḥāddūna* (5): the same root |
| **5-6** | Those who stand on the opposite side | *yabʿathuhumu'llāhu jamīʿā* (6) → the same sentence at 18 |
| **7-13** | Najwā, the assembly, the private audience | *tuḥsharūn* (9) → the name of Sūrat al-Ḥashr |
| **14-22** | The two ḥizbs | *yuḥāddūna* (20): a repetition of the fifth verse, the frame closes |

**The central idea of the sūra** can be summarised thus: **position cannot be determined by word or by lineage.**

- A word (ẓihār) cannot make a woman a mother (1-4).
- A word (an oath) cannot make a person trustworthy (14-18).
- A word (a twisted greeting) cannot conceal an enmity (8).
- A tie of lineage cannot place a person in a rank (22).

In all four cases there is the same structure: **something established by tongue or by birth does not determine reality.** What determines it is what the person actually does.

### Roots that circulate through the sūra

Several roots occur in more than one place in the sūra, and their circulation establishes a pattern:

**ح-د-د (boundary)** — *ḥudūd Allāh* (4), *yuḥāddūna* (5, 20, 22). The boundary God draws and the boundary man draws against it. The backbone of the sūra.

**ن-ج-و (to draw aside)** — 7, 8 (twice), 9 (three times), 10, 12, 13. The word occurs nine times; the densest root of the sūra.

**ح-ل-ف (oath)** — 14, 18; and *aymān* (16). The oath's turning into a shield, and the attempt to use that shield on the day of resurrection too.

**ك-ت-ب (to write)** — 21, 22. The ruling written into history and the faith written on the heart.

**ح-ز-ب (company)** — 19 (twice), 22 (twice); four times. The closing concept of the sūra.

**س-م-ع (to hear)** — 1 (three times). Only in the first verse; but it lays the ground of the whole sūra.

### The two faces of hearing

The most elegant structure of the sūra is the use of the idea of hearing in two opposite directions:

**In the first verse:** a woman speaks, as though no one were listening. And she is heard. Here to be heard is a **consolation**.

**In the seventh verse:** three people draw aside and whisper, so that no one may hear. And they are heard. Here to be heard is a **warning**.

The same attribute (*Samīʿ*), the same verb (*samiʿa/yasmaʿu*), two opposite feelings. What separates them is who is speaking and why.

This is one of the most powerful structures the sūra builds, and it carries a principle: **invisibility takes its value from whose interest it serves.** For the weak, invisibility is a deprivation; for the powerful, an opportunity. The sūra removes both.

### What this says to the present

**One. The recording of a voice.** The opening of the sūra says where the quality of a legal order is to be measured: in what happens to the objection of the person in the weakest position. This measure has not aged at all and is applicable at every scale — in a family, in a workplace, in an institution.

**Two. The price of the word.** The logic of the ẓihār regulation was this: a spoken word binds the one who speaks it, but cannot lock another person's life. This distinction works today too: our words generate our own obligations; they do not grant the authority to determine other people's situations.

**Three. The pain of exclusion.** The tenth verse named the function of secret talk as "to grieve". This is different from a real harm but no less real. A group of people drawing aside and talking leaves a concrete effect on the one left outside — and the verse records that effect. At the same time it sets its limit: what is felt and what comes about are not the same thing.

**Four. The difference between privacy and hiding.** The ninth verse does not forbid secret talk; it regulates its content. This shows the way to defend privacy without legitimising hiding: that a conversation is secret is not the problem; whom it will harm is the problem.

**Five. Making room.** The command of the eleventh verse is directly applicable in every community today: the place one sits in, the turn to speak, the share of attention. These are limited resources, and once seized they are not easily given up.

**Six. The converting of knowledge into rank.** The passive structure in the expression "those who have been given knowledge" positions knowledge not as an acquisition but as a trust. The practical consequence of this: to establish superiority by knowledge contradicts the manner in which knowledge is given.

**Seven. The cost of access.** The regulation of the twelfth verse saw the problem created by access to an administrator being free of charge. Today its counterparts are many: attention, availability, the power to set the agenda. And the subtlety in the verse's solution is worth preserving: the price flowed not to the person accessed but to a third place.

**Eight. Not being on both sides at once.** The diagnosis of the fourteenth verse, *"neither of you nor of them"*, names a situation recognisable at every scale. And it says that this is a loss: the person who keeps his relation with both sides open can gain a place in neither.

**Nine. Behaviour that has become a reflex.** The scene of the eighteenth verse — continuing to swear even on the day of resurrection — conveys a behaviour's breaking loose from calculation and becoming automatic. This is an observation more than a moral warning: an attitude repeated enough is no longer being chosen.

**Ten. That lineage is not a measure.** The twenty-second verse takes identity's strongest form — the tie of blood — out of being a measure. The side of this that faces the present is wider than the verse's historical context: what determines where a person stands is not which family, which milieu, which group he comes from, but his own choice. And this choice holds even against the closest of ties.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

Per the method (`STYLE.md`), I list openly the points in this sūra on which no certainty is claimed:

- **The names in the occasion of revelation** (Khawla/Huwayla bint Thaʿlaba, Aws b. al-Ṣāmit) come from the reports, and there is variation among the transmissions. The Qurʾān gives no name.
- **Why the word "back" was chosen in ẓihār** — none of the explanations given is certain.
- **What the expression "going back on what they said" (58/3) means** is disputed among the schools of law; the views were conveyed, no preference was adopted and no legal ruling was issued.
- **The details of applying the expiation rulings** (who is exempt, when it becomes obligatory, the case of the fast being broken) are the subject of jurisprudence; they were not entered into here.
- **The theological interpretation of the expression "being-with" at 58/7** was conveyed; the debate was not entered into.
- **The wording of the twisted greeting** comes from the reports; the report is widespread in the sources, but no full chain and wording were transmitted here.
- **Whether "those given knowledge" at 58/11 are a separate group** is disputed; both readings were given.
- **The reports on the occasion of revelation of 58/11** differ; none was preferred.
- **Who the "people" at 58/14 are** — there are views; no group's name was given and the reading by attribute was preserved.
- **The expression "a spirit from Himself" at 58/22** is disputed; four views were given, no preference was adopted.
- **The scope of the expression "I shall prevail" (58/21)** — the classical explanations were conveyed; no political reading was pursued, and how the construct cannot be used was stated separately.
- **The construct "ḥizb Allāh"** was read as the result of a list of attributes; it was ascribed to no structure.
- **Some of the links established between roots** (such as *ayd*–*yad*, *falāḥ*–the splitting of the tie of lineage) were marked as my own reading; they were not presented as an agreement among the lexicographers.
