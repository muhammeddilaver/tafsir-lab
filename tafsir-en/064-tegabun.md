# 64. Sūrat al-Taghābun

Eighteen verses. It takes its name from a construct in the ninth verse: **yawm al-taghābun** — "the day of mutual loss in the bargain". This construct occurs only here in the Qurʾān.

The sūra is the last of the group known as the *musabbiḥāt* — that is, the sūras that open with a sentence of glorification. Its opening follows the shared pattern of that group; but where it arrives is another matter. A text that begins with a cosmic glorification comes down, eighteen verses later, into the inside of a house, to wife and child, to a piece of property lent out.

## What the sūra does

It is possible to say what the sūra does in a single sentence: **it sets up a balance for reckoning, and then asks that this balance be applied to the nearest objects in a human life.**

It proceeds in three steps.

**First step (1-4):** Who keeps the account? The answer is given across four verses: whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies Him; the dominion is His; He created you; He gave you your form; He knows what you conceal and what you make public. That is, the owner of the balance is introduced — and in the introduction the attributes of **seeing** and **knowing** are particularly brought forward. This is no accident: for a reckoning to be valid, the one who keeps it must see both sides.

**Second step (5-10):** When will the reckoning be settled? First an example is given from the past (5-6), then the ground of denial is recorded (7), then the call is made (8), then the day is named (9-10). And the name of that day is not a term from the hereafter; it is a **commercial** term: *taghābun*.

**Third step (11-18):** Then what is to be done today? Here the sūra goes somewhere unexpected. Having named the resurrection, it does not enter a vast scene but a person's **house**: affliction, obedience, reliance, wife, child, property, spending, a loan. For eight verses the subject is the things within reach of a person's hand.

**And the real move of the sūra is in this transition.** What is called "the day of mutual loss in the bargain" is not described as something that will take place far off, on a cosmic stage; immediately afterwards, the **material** of that loss is enumerated. If what you sold cheap is to become clear on that day, then it is necessary to look at what is in your hands right now. That is exactly what the sūra makes the reader do.

## The name of the sūra

**ٱلتَّغَابُن** (*al-taghābun*) is taken from the expression *dhālika yawmu'l-taghābun* in the ninth verse of the sūra. I shall analyse the word at the ninth verse; but there is something that must be known before beginning to read the sūra:

**This is not a word from the hereafter. It is a word from the marketplace.**

The proper sense of the root is to come off worse in a transaction, to give a thing away below its worth or to take it above its worth. In the names it gives the Day of Resurrection the Qurʾān generally chooses words that describe the event itself: *al-qāriʿa* (the striker), *al-ṭāmma* (the overwhelming), *al-ṣākhkha* (the deafening), *al-ghāshiya* (the enveloping), *yawm al-ḥisāb*, *yawm al-dīn*. The word chosen here, by contrast, describes not the event but **the result that will come to light on that day**: the recognition that a transaction was made wrongly.

I have not seen in the sources any record that the sūra is called by another name; this is its common name.

## Meccan or Medinan?

Here I do not speak with certainty. The sources differ, and the ground of the difference is inference from the text itself rather than transmitted report.

| View | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **Wholly Medinan** | In most of the lists of the order of revelation it is counted Medinan. The pattern *"Obey God, obey the Messenger"* in the twelfth verse is the language of the Medinan sūras. The arrangements about spending, obedience and family in verses 14-16 presuppose a social structure. The expression *"the light We sent down"* in the eighth verse requires the existence of a text that is being spoken about. | The first half of the sūra (1-13) is very close to the Meccan sūras in theme, in verse length and in the ordering of the verse-endings: divine unity, creation, past peoples, denial of the resurrection. There is not a single sentence that lays down a ruling. |
| **Wholly Meccan** | The content of the first thirteen verses is entirely doctrinal; the body of those addressed is unspecified; the address "O you who believe" comes only in the fourteenth verse. | The language of verses 14-18 presupposes a community with institutions. Most of the lists of revelation do not support this. |
| **Its first part Meccan, the rest Medinan** | The text changes both its address and its subject between the thirteenth and fourteenth verses: from cosmic and doctrinal language it moves to the address *"yā ayyuhā'lladhīna āmanū"* and to matters of the household. The break is in fact visible. | This is an **inference** made from the content of the text; it does not rest on a sound report. The claim that a sūra came down in two pieces requires evidence of its own besides. |

It can be said that the majority call it Medinan; but this is not beyond dispute. In my judgement the distinction does not change the understanding of the sūra — because what the sūra sets up is not a ruling for a period but a **model of accounting**. This preference is not binding.

## The occasion of revelation

I know of no widely accepted report of an occasion of revelation for the sūra as a whole. There is a group of reports transmitted for the fourteenth verse, and I shall relate it in its place, together with the reservations attaching to it.

## The structure of the sūra

| Section | Verses | Subject | Key word |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | 1-4 | Introduction of the one who keeps the account | *al-mulk*, *baṣīr*, *ʿalīm* |
| **II** | 5-7 | The record of the past and the ground of denial | *nabaʾ*, *zaʿm* |
| **III** | 8 | The call | *al-nūr* |
| **IV** | 9-10 | The name of the day and the two outcomes | *al-taghābun* |
| **V** | 11-13 | Affliction, obedience, reliance | *idhn*, *tawakkul* |
| **VI** | 14-17 | Inside the house: wife, child, property, spending | *fitna*, *qarḍ* |
| **VII** | 18 | The seal | *al-ghayb wa'l-shahāda* |

The striking side of the structure is that **the second half applies the first**. The attributes introduced in the first four verses (the one who sees, the one who knows, the one who knows the hidden and the open) are repeated exactly in the last verse; all the commands in between have been set beneath those attributes. The sūra builds a frame, fills the inside of the frame, then closes the frame.

## The basmala

The word-by-word analysis of the basmala was done in `001-fatiha.md`; I do not repeat it here.

The one note particular to this sūra is this: the text that opens with the names al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm will say in the fourteenth verse *"among your wives and your children there are enemies to you"*, and that same verse will end with *ghafūrun raḥīm*. That is, the warning is sealed with the second name of the basmala. I shall treat this link there.

## 64/1 — يُسَبِّحُ لِلَّهِ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ لَهُ الْمُلْكُ وَلَهُ الْحَمْدُ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

*Yusabbiḥu lillāhi mā fi'l-samāwāti wa mā fi'l-arḍ, lahu'l-mulku wa lahu'l-ḥamd, wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr*

"Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies God. The dominion is His, the praise is His. And He is powerful over everything."

### سَبَّحَ — glorification

**Root: س ب ح.** The concrete sense of the root is **to swim in water, to move through a medium without obstruction**. *Sabḥ* is the swimmer's going by cleaving the water; *sābiḥ* is the one who swims.

This root is used in the Qurʾān in its concrete sense as well:

- *"Each one swims in an orbit"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/33; Yāsīn 36/40) — *kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn*. This is the verb used of the heavenly bodies.
- In the oaths of Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt *al-sābiḥāti sabḥā* occurs; the analysis of this root was done there (`079-naziat.md`), and I do not repeat it here.

**How, then, does one pass from "swimming" to "glorification"?** The connection the lexicographers give is this: *tasbīḥ* is to **hold God away** from everything unworthy of Him, to **cut past and away** from those things. The swimmer cleaves the water and moves on; the one who glorifies likewise cleaves and passes the false conceptions about the divine Essence. Inside the word there is a movement of **purifying and holding at a distance**.

This is something other than what the English "to tell one's beads" suggests. Glorification is not an operation of counting; it is an operation of **placing**. It means "You are far from this."

**The pattern: Form II (*sabbaḥa*).** This form conveys intensity and repetition. Not a single utterance once made, but a continuous and widespread state.

### Who is glorifying: مَا, not مَنْ

This is the most easily skipped place in the verse.

In Arabic **مَنْ** (*man* — who) is used for beings that possess reason, and **مَا** (*mā* — what) for those without reason and for existents in general. The verse does not say *man fi'l-samāwāt*; it says **مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ**.

That is, the glorifying subject is not confined to the angels and to human beings. It says "whatever there is" — stone, tree, water, air, star, all of them.

This shows that the Qurʾān sets up glorification not as an **act of speech** but as a **state of being**. And the Qurʾān defends this choice openly:

> **وَإِن مِّن شَىْءٍ إِلَّا يُسَبِّحُ بِحَمْدِهِۦ وَلَٰكِن لَّا تَفْقَهُونَ تَسْبِيحَهُمْ**
> *"There is nothing that does not glorify Him with praise; but you do not understand their glorification."* (al-Isrāʾ 17/44)

This verse says two things: glorification is universal, and it is **imperceptible**. Without the second half the first half would be an assumption; the second half accepts imperceptibility from the outset.

I do not turn this into a claim about natural science — the verse speaks of no vibration and no frequency, and such a reading would be something put onto the text from outside. What the verse says is plainer: **existence itself is a declaration.** That a thing exists points to the one who brought it into existence; and this pointing is independent of whether the thing is aware of it or not.

### The form of the verb: يُسَبِّحُ — imperfect

And it would not be right to pass on here without making an observation about this group of sūras.

The sūras in the Qurʾān that open with a sentence of glorification are called the *musabbiḥāt*. Some of these sūras open with the **perfect** pattern (*sabbaḥa*), some with the **imperfect** (*yusabbiḥu*), and one with the imperative (*sabbiḥi'sma rabbika'l-aʿlā* — al-Aʿlā 87/1).

Al-Taghābun opens with the imperfect: *yusabbiḥu.*

**What is the meaning of the difference?** In Arabic the perfect conveys completedness, the imperfect ongoingness and renewal. From this the following reading can be made: the perfect pattern stresses that the glorification is **ancient and fixed**, the imperfect pattern that it **continues and is repeated**.

**But here I must enter a reservation.** I cannot claim that this difference is tied to a consistent rule in every one of the *musabbiḥāt* sūras; the exegetes have proposed various explanations on this point and none of them is certain. The difference of pattern is a fact standing in the text; the meaning drawn from it is interpretation. Here I only record the difference, and content myself with saying that for this sūra the imperfect suits the flow: the sūra will describe an order **working at this moment**, and will ask at the end for the things **that must be done at this moment**.

### لَهُ ٱلْمُلْكُ وَلَهُ ٱلْحَمْدُ — two frontings

The sentence builds the same structure twice: **lahu** first, **al-mulk** and **al-ḥamd** after.

In Arabic the predicate's coming before the subject (*taqdīm*) conveys **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*). Not "the dominion is His" but **"the dominion is His alone"**. In English we do this with stress; Arabic does it with word order.

And the choice of the two words is meaningful:

- **الْمُلْك** — ownership and disposal. Root **م ل ك**: to hold powerful disposal over something. The analysis of this root was done in `067-mulk.md`; I do not repeat it here.
- **الْحَمْد** — praise. Root **ح م د**; its analysis was done in `001-fatiha.md`.

**The coming of the two side by side sets up an equation.** Dominion is power; praise is an assessment of worth. A being may be powerful and yet not deserve praise; it may deserve praise and yet be powerless. The verse gives both to the same address.

And this is the precondition of the framework of exchange that will be set up at the end of the sūra: in a transaction one looks for both the **capacity to pay** and the **trustworthiness** of the other party. The verse records both at once. I write this as my own reading; I do not present it as a classical transmitted report.

### وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

**قَدِير** — root **ق د ر**. The three branches of meaning in this root (measure/determining, worth/honour, straitness/squeezing) were analysed in detail in `097-kadr.md`; I do not repeat that analysis. The use here belongs to the first branch: **the power to do a thing to its measure.**

And this sense of the root separates the word "power" a little from what the English "almighty" suggests. *Qadīr* is not the one who can do everything; He is **the one who can do everything to its measure**. Power and measure stand in the same root.

This root will not return again in the sūra; but it will come back in Sūrat al-Ṭalāq (65/3) with the sentence *"God has set a qadr — a measure — for everything"*. Two neighbouring sūras use the same root in two different directions: power here, measure there.

## 64/2 — هُوَ الَّذِي خَلَقَكُمْ فَمِنكُمْ كَافِرٌ وَمِنكُم مُّؤْمِنٌ وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

*Huwa'lladhī khalaqakum fa-minkum kāfirun wa minkum muʾmin, wa'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna baṣīr*

"He is the one who created you. Then some of you are deniers, some of you are believers. And God sees what you do."

### The problem the verse sets up

At first sight this verse looks like a plain statement of fact. It is not. It is the most compressed expression of one of the most debated matters in the Qurʾān — the relation between a person's act and his creation.

The sentence is built thus: **an act of creation**, then the letter **fāʾ**, then **two groups**.

The difficulty is in the *fāʾ*. In Arabic the *fāʾ* can convey succession and cause. The sentence "He created, **then** some of you are deniers, some of you believers" says that the two states come after the creation. But after it in what sense? Merely in respect of time, or in respect of cause?

In the classical commentaries this is the direct field of the debate in *kalām*. I am not in a position here to relate all the parties in full, nor am I going to settle the dispute. What I can do is to show **what the wording of the verse says and what it does not say**.

**What the verse says:** He is the one who created. And the created stand in two states.

**What the verse does not say:** the cause of the two states. In the sentence there is no *jaʿalakum* (He made you …); there is *khalaqakum* (He created you). And the two states are given not as the **object** of the creation but as a **distribution among** those created: *minkum … wa minkum* — "some of you … some of you".

This distinction is not small. The sentence does not say "He created you as deniers and believers". It says "He created you; among you there are deniers and believers too." The wording itself preserves the difference between these two sentences — and exegesis must not efface a difference that the wording preserves.

### The link with `076-insan.md`: two forks, in two different places

The third verse of Sūrat al-Insān set up the same duality:

> **إِنَّا هَدَيْنَٰهُ ٱلسَّبِيلَ إِمَّا شَاكِرًا وَإِمَّا كَفُورًا**
> *"We showed him the way: he is either grateful or ungrateful."* (al-Insān 76/3)

In that section the following were established, and I do not repeat them, I only recall them: the structure *immā … wa immā* conveys **division** (*taqsīm*), sets out two possibilities of equal weight and exhaustively, and leaves no third option; *shākiran* and *kafūran* are grammatically **circumstantial accusatives**. And a point recorded there holds here too: **in the Qurʾān what is most often set opposite denial is not faith but gratitude**; denial is at bottom a notion of ingratitude.

**Now let us set the two verses side by side. The difference lies in where the fork is placed.**

| | **al-Insān 76/3** | **al-Taghābun 64/2** |
|---|---|---|
| Before the fork | *hadaynāhu'l-sabīl* — **the way was shown** | *khalaqakum* — **created** |
| The words of the fork | *immā … wa immā* — possibility | *minkum … wa minkum* — distribution |
| Type of sentence | A circumstantial clause: a state of possibility | A nominal sentence: **a state already realised** |
| The pair of opposites | *shākir* / *kafūr* — the axis of gratitude | *kāfir* / *muʾmin* — the axis of faith |
| Time | Looks forward | Looks at the situation |

**Al-Insān describes a possibility: the way was shown, you will be one of the two.** Al-Taghābun, by contrast, records an **actual picture**: humankind was created, and at this moment there are two groups within it.

That is, one of them stands before the decision, the other looks at the result of the decision. The same duality, seen from two different times.

I write this as my own reading. I do not claim that there is a classical exegetical tradition binding the two verses together; the link has been drawn from the structure of the two texts.

### Why does كَافِر come first?

In the verse the order is this: **first the denier, then the believer.**

This is striking, because the Qurʾān's habit is generally the reverse — the good is mentioned first. Indeed in al-Insān 76/3 too *shākiran* comes first.

In the classical commentaries several explanations are proposed for this order. I relate them, but I take none of them as certainly correct:

| Explanation | Its ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **It reports the majority** | On that day most of the addressed body were in denial; the order is built according to the actual situation. | Reasonable, but drawn from history rather than from the text. |
| **The verse-ending and the ordering of sound** | There is a difference of phonetic weight between *kāfirun* and *muʾmin*; the arrangement may have been built for rhythm. | Weak: the verse-ending of the sentence is neither of the two, but *baṣīr*. |
| **The flow of the sūra** | The sūra will speak of the deniers in verses 5-7; the order announces what is to follow. | It has a ground within the text; this looks the most defensible explanation. |
| **The heavier is mentioned first** | In texts of warning what is dangerous is brought forward. | It can be said as a general tendency, not as a rule. |

**And here I prefer to stop.** Drawing meaning from word order is a legitimate method of Qurʾānic exegesis; but to draw a wisdom from every order makes the method unfalsifiable. Here we have four possibilities and no evidence that would make one of them certainly preferable to another. I record the order; I leave the interpretation to the reader.

### وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ — the work of the verse-ending

The verse does not end with a classification; it ends with a sentence about **seeing**.

**بَصِير** — root **ب ص ر**: to see, and beyond seeing, **to distinguish, to grasp**. The analysis of this root was done in `067-mulk.md` (in connection with the chain 67/3-4, 19, 23); I do not repeat it here.

The function of the verse-ending here is this: the verse named two groups — *kāfir* and *muʾmin*. These two names are names that people stick on one another all too easily. And immediately after the naming, the verse says **to whom the authority of seeing belongs**: *wa'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna baṣīr.*

That is, the classification was made, but the authority to apply the classification was not given to the one addressed.

And note: the sentence does not say "God sees what you are"; it says **"He sees what you do"** — *bimā taʿmalūn*. The measure has been tied to **deed**. This is another face of the point treated in `107-maun.md` as the whole backbone of that sūra: the evidence of belief is laid down as deed.

## 64/3 — خَلَقَ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ بِالْحَقِّ وَصَوَّرَكُمْ فَأَحْسَنَ صُوَرَكُمْ وَإِلَيْهِ الْمَصِيرُ

*Khalaqa'l-samāwāti wa'l-arḍa bi'l-ḥaqq, wa ṣawwarakum fa-aḥsana ṣuwarakum, wa ilayhi'l-maṣīr*

"He created the heavens and the earth with truth; He gave you form and made your forms good. And to Him is the coming home."

### The verse's play of scale

The verse consists of three sentences, and among the three there is a great change of scale:

1. **The heavens and the earth** — the whole universe.
2. **Your forms** — an area as small as a human face.
3. **To Him is the coming home** — the whole of time.

From the cosmos to the face, from the face to the final outcome. And this is the continuation of the movement that began in the preceding verse: the sūra goes back and forth continuously between the widest and the nearest. This oscillation will settle entirely on the "near" at the end of the sūra (in verses 14-17).

### بِالْحَقِّ — "with truth"

**Root: ح ق ق.** The concrete sense of the root is **a thing's being in its proper place and being fixed**; *ḥaqqa'l-shayʾ* — a thing came to pass, became fixed. The broad analysis of this root was done in `069-hakka.md`; I do not repeat it here.

The **bāʾ** here conveys *muṣāḥaba* (accompaniment) or *mulābasa* (adherence to a state): the creating took place as something adhering to "truth".

**What does that mean?** At least two things:

- **Not purposeless.** Creation is not a game or a waste. The Qurʾān rejects this openly in many places: *"We did not create the heaven and the earth and what is between them as a game"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/16).
- **Not arbitrary, but measured.** *Ḥaqq* is what is fixed and in place. Creation was done with an order, and that order is not an alterable preference.

And this reservation sets up a precondition for the whole sūra: **a reckoning is meaningful only in a purposeful creation.** In a purposeless universe there could be no such thing as *taghābun* — coming off worse in a bargain — because coming off worse requires the existence of a value. The verse lays here the ground of the claim made in the ninth verse.

### وَصَوَّرَكُمْ فَأَحْسَنَ صُوَرَكُمْ — form, and the goodness of form

**Root: ص و ر.** And this root must be carefully distinguished from the root treated in `095-tin.md`.

*Ṣūra* is a thing's **outward appearance, form, shape**. *Taṣwīr* is the work of giving form. From the same root come *muṣawwir* (the one who gives form; it occurs in the Qurʾān as one of the names of God, at al-Ḥashr 59/24), *taṣawwur* (setting up the form of a thing in the mind), and the words Turkish has borrowed from this root: *tasvir* (depiction), *suret* (image), *tasavvur* (conception).

**The pattern: Form II (*ṣawwara*).** This form, as was recorded in `098-beyyine.md` in connection with *muṭahhara*, conveys intensity and comprehensiveness: to form from beginning to end, in detail.

### The link with `095-tin.md`: *taqwīm* and *taṣwīr*

In Sūrat al-Tīn the following sentence was treated:

> **لَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ فِىٓ أَحْسَنِ تَقْوِيمٍ**
> *"We have surely created the human being in the finest constitution."* (al-Tīn 95/4)

I do not repeat the analysis made there; I recall it: **تَقْوِيم** is a Form II verbal noun from the root **ق و م** (*qāma* → *qawwama* → *taqwīm*: straightening, setting right, giving form, appraising value); and *aḥsan* answers both to aesthetic beauty and to **fitness for the work** — the reading "the most serviceable order" is also possible. It was also recorded there that the second element of the construct coming indefinite (*aḥsani taqwīmin*) conveys **magnification**.

**Now let us compare the two expressions. The roots are different, and the difference is meaningful.**

| | **al-Tīn 95/4** — *aḥsani taqwīm* | **al-Taghābun 64/3** — *aḥsana ṣuwarakum* |
|---|---|---|
| Root | **ق و م** — to stand upright, to straighten | **ص و ر** — to give form, to shape |
| What it looks at | **Constitution** — structure, build, working order | **Form** — the appearance that is seen |
| Scope | *al-insān* — the human being as a species | *ṣuwarakum* — **your forms**, plural and belonging to you |
| Definiteness | *taqwīmin* — indefinite, magnifying | *ṣuwarakum* — in construct, **owned** |
| Its opposite within the sūra | *asfala sāfilīn* — the fall | *al-maṣīr* — the coming home |

Two differences need dwelling on.

**The first difference: structure and appearance.** *Taqwīm* has to do with **how a thing is built** — backbone, balance, upright stance, working order. *Taṣwīr*, by contrast, with **how it looks**. Sūrat al-Tīn takes the human being as a structure and says that this structure can be spoiled (*thumma radadnāhu asfala sāfilīn*). Al-Taghābun takes the human being as an appearance and says that this appearance has been **given**.

**The second difference, and to my mind the more important: plural and possession.** The verse does not say *al-ṣūra* or *ṣūratan*; it says **صُوَرَكُمْ** — "your forms". Two things are said at once:

- **Plural:** not a single mould. Forms differ; the verse does not make the difference singular.
- **Possession:** the form has been made **yours**. What is given is at the same time what is your own.

And now look at the sentence structure of the verse: *wa ṣawwarakum* **fa** *aḥsana ṣuwarakum*. The verb comes twice. First "He gave form", then "He made your forms good". The second sentence is not a repetition of the first; it adds a **qualification** on top of the first.

That is, the verse says: form was given — and the form given was given well.

**The practical consequence of this must be stated while staying within the verse's own limits.** The verse lays down no standard of beauty; it says nothing about which face is beautiful and which ugly. What it says is more basic: **form is given not as a defect but as a piece of work well done.** And along with the possessive suffix: each one's own, for himself.

It is possible to turn this into a point bearing on the present, but without exaggeration. In an environment where appearance has turned into a measure of worth and this measure is worked constantly by comparison, what the verse does is to take the measure out of comparison: *ṣuwarakum* is plural and each one of them is inside the qualification *aḥsan*. No room has been left for comparison. This makes a quiet link with the idea of "mutual loss in the bargain" that will be set up in the ninth verse of the sūra — because coming off worse begins with measuring value in the wrong place.

### وَإِلَيْهِ ٱلْمَصِيرُ — the coming home

**مَصِير** — root **ص ي ر**: to **turn from one state into another, to arrive, to end up being that**. The verb *ṣāra* in Arabic does not mean "became" but **"came to be in that state"**; it contains a process and an arrival.

*Maṣīr* is on the pattern of a noun of place: **the place arrived at.** And its being chosen as the verse-ending is meaningful — because the word conveys not a place but a **result**.

This word will occur **twice** in the sūra: here (3) and in the tenth verse (*wa biʾsa'l-maṣīr* — "what an evil arrival"). The difference between the two sums up the whole structure of the sūra:

- **In verse 3** *al-maṣīr* is neutral: the coming home is to Him. The same for everyone.
- **In verse 10** *biʾsa'l-maṣīr*: the arrival that is evil. For some.

That is, the coming home is one; the place arrived at is not. Two groups that set out on the same road do not go to the same place.

## 64/4 — يَعْلَمُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَيَعْلَمُ مَا تُسِرُّونَ وَمَا تُعْلِنُونَ وَاللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ بِذَاتِ الصُّدُورِ

*Yaʿlamu mā fi'l-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ, wa yaʿlamu mā tusirrūna wa mā tuʿlinūn, wa'llāhu ʿalīmun bi-dhāti'l-ṣudūr*

"He knows what is in the heavens and on the earth; He knows what you conceal and what you make public. And God knows the very substance of the breasts."

### Knowing in three layers

The verse narrows knowing into three rings, and each ring is further inside than the one before it:

| Ring | Expression | Field |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | *mā fi'l-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* | The outside — the whole universe |
| **2** | *mā tusirrūna wa mā tuʿlinūn* | A person's acts — hidden and open |
| **3** | *dhāti'l-ṣudūr* | The **substance** of the breasts — what has not yet turned into act |

And note: in the first verse *al-mulk* (ownership), in the second *baṣīr* (seeing), here *ʿalīm* (knowing). The sūra counts off in order the powers of the one who keeps the account — owner, seer, knower. Without all three together no reckoning can be set up.

### تُسِرُّونَ / تُعْلِنُونَ

**أَسَرَّ** — root **س ر ر**: to conceal. From the same root comes *sirr* (what is hidden).

The root has an interesting side: from the same root come also **سُرُور** (*surūr* — joy) and **سَرِير** (*sarīr* — throne, couch). The lexicographers explain these derivations along different lines and there is no agreement among them; for that reason I do not build a link of meaning from here. I only record the word's relatives.

The verb *asarra* is in Form IV and is, strikingly, one of the *aḍdād* — that is, it can be used both for "he concealed" and for "he disclosed, he whispered". Here, because of *tuʿlinūn* facing it, the meaning is beyond dispute: to conceal.

**أَعْلَنَ** — root **ع ل ن**: to bring out into the open, to make public. The Turkish words *alenî* (open, overt), *ilan* (announcement) and *ilanat* (announcements) come from here.

**The mentioning of the two together is an operation of addition.** In Arabic, setting two contrary notions side by side (*ṭibāq*) is the way of covering the whole — to say "hidden and open" is to say "all of it". It is what English does with "sooner or later", "more or less", "high and low".

But there is an addition here: the ordering. **First the hidden, then the open.** This order is widespread in the Qurʾān and carries an emphasis: the hard case is stated first. Knowing the open is expected anyway; the real claim is knowing the hidden.

### بِذَاتِ ٱلصُّدُورِ — "the substance of the breasts"

One of the densest constructs in the Qurʾān, and hard to translate.

**ذَات** — root **ذ و**: the feminine of *dhū*, which conveys possession. But when used inside a construct, *dhāt* leaves behind the sense of "possessor of" and comes to mean **"the substance, the very self, the reality"** of a thing. The philosophical term "essence" and the Turkish usage *zatında* ("in itself") come from here.

**صُدُور** — the plural of *ṣadr*: the breast, the cavity of the chest, the front part of a thing. From the same root come *ṣadāra* (precedence), *ṣudūr* (a thing's issuing forth), *maṣdar* (source). At the centre of the root stand **the front part and the place of issue**.

**The meaning of the construct:** not "the things inside the breasts" — that would be *mā fi'l-ṣudūr*, and the Qurʾān uses that expression too. The word chosen here is **dhāt**: the **very self** of the breasts, their **substance**, the thing they have as their own.

That is, what is known is not the passing thoughts that circulate in the breast; it is **what the breast is**. Not the intention that has turned into act, but the structure that produces intentions.

**And this sets up a balance with the verse-ending of the second verse.** There it said *bimā taʿmalūna baṣīr* — "He sees what you do". Here *bi-dhāti'l-ṣudūri ʿalīm* — "He knows the substance of the breasts". The difference between the two is the difference between two verbs: **seeing** looks outward, **knowing** looks inward.

Together the two verses set this up: in the reckoning there is both an outside and an inside. And this is the technical precondition of the idea of "coming off worse" in the ninth verse of the sūra — because in a transaction, establishing that one party came off worse requires that the **real** value of the goods be known. The sūra says here who it is that knows the real value.

## 64/5 — أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ نَبَأُ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا مِن قَبْلُ فَذَاقُوا وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

*A lam yaʾtikum nabaʾu'lladhīna kafarū min qabl, fa-dhāqū wabāla amrihim wa lahum ʿadhābun alīm*

"Has the report of those who denied before not come to you? They tasted the ill consequence of their own affair; and for them there is a painful punishment."

### أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ — a question, or a reminder?

The sentence opens with a question: *a lam yaʾtikum* — "has it not come to you?"

In Arabic the interrogative hamza coming at the head of a negative sentence generally conveys **taqrīr**: making the one addressed affirm something he already knows. This technique was treated in `094-insirah.md` (*a lam nashraḥ laka ṣadrak*) and its reverse was recorded in `073-muzzemmil.md` (the question that makes one realise he can give no answer).

The use here is close to the one in al-Inshirāḥ: the one addressed is obliged to say "yes, it has come." For the report really has come — narratives about ʿĀd, Thamūd and the people of Lot were in circulation in the Arabian peninsula; their ruins lay along the trade routes.

**And the real function of the question comes out here.** The question is not asking for information. It is making one grant that the information **has arrived**. And at the next step the matter is opened of why information that has arrived produced no result.

### نَبَأ — *nabaʾ*

The analysis of this root was done in detail in `078-nebe.md`. I do not repeat the findings there; I only recall three items, because in this verse all three are at work:

1. Under the root **ن ب أ** lies the idea of "coming from one place to another, being carried"; a report is what is **carried**.
2. According to the definition recorded in al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī's *al-Mufradāt* and widely transmitted in the exegetical tradition, a *nabaʾ* is a report that **carries great benefit, by which knowledge or a strong conviction is produced**; whereas *khabar* requires none of these.
3. But the Qurʾān itself also uses the word *nabaʾ* for a report that is commanded to be verified (al-Ḥujurāt 49/6) — so the definition makes it a condition not that the report be **true** but that it be **of the kind that carries weight**. The sentence recorded in the section on al-Nabaʾ was this: *"A nabaʾ is a report on hearing which something ought to change."*

**And this verse is the clearest example of that sentence.**

The verse asks "has the report not come to you" and names the report a **nabaʾ** — that is, a report on hearing which something ought to change. Then it says what happened. And the continuation of the sūra (verse 6) will say what those who received the report did: nothing changed.

That is, the verse brings the definition of the word face to face with the fact. **The nabaʾ came, and did not work as a nabaʾ.**

This root will return once more in the sūra, in the seventh verse: *thumma la-tunabbaʾunna bimā ʿamiltum* — "then you will surely be informed of what you did". I shall treat the relation between the two verses there.

**A note on grammar.** The construct *nabaʾu'lladhīna kafarū* can be read in two ways: "the report **of** those who denied" (belonging to them, the report about them) or "the report coming **from** those who denied". The context requires the first; I record the second possibility only to show the breadth of the Arabic construct.

### فَذَاقُوا — "they tasted"

One of the verbs the Qurʾān uses most in its accounts of punishment, and its choice is no accident.

**Root: ذ و ق.** *Dhawq* is tasting — the tongue's taking the taste of a thing.

**Why "they tasted"?** Because tasting is the most immediate form of knowing. You can see a thing, hear it, have it described to you — in all of these a distance comes in between. In tasting there is no distance: what is tasted is inside the mouth.

And the logic of the verse works exactly from here. At the head of the sentence there is a **report** (*nabaʾ*), at the end there is a **tasting** (*dhāqū*). The difference between the two is the whole claim of the verse:

> **A report comes from far off; a taste is taken on the spot.**

That is, the verse sets two forms of knowing side by side and says this to the one addressed: you have received the report. Whoever will not be content with the report, tastes.

I write this as my own reading. This contrast of the words stands in the text; I am not transmitting it from a classical commentary.

**The voice of the verb is meaningful too:** *dhāqū* — active. Not "they were made to taste", but "they tasted". The verb is ascribed to them. And what they tasted is stated: the ill consequence of their own affair.

### وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ — "the ill consequence of their affair"

**Root: و ب ل.** The concrete sense of the root is **heavy rain in large drops**: *wābil*. This concrete sense occurs openly in the Qurʾān — *"When a *wābil* (a heavy rain) strikes it…"* (al-Baqara 2/264-265).

Two branches come out from here:

- **وَبَال (*wabāl*)** — the **heavy and harmful consequence** of an affair. Turkish has taken the word over in exactly this sense: *vebali boynuna*, "let its *wabāl* be on his neck."
- **وَبِيل (*wabīl*)** — heavy, severe, hard to digest. In the Qurʾān it is used of Pharaoh's being seized: *akhadhnāhu akhdhan wabīlā* (al-Muzzammil 73/16) — that verse was treated in `073-muzzemmil.md`.

**The root's passage from "heavy rain" to "heavy consequence"**, so far as the lexicographers record it, goes by way of the idea of weight and of pressing down: rain in large drops strikes the soil, covers it over, presses it down with its weight. There is also the sense of "what is hard to digest" — a pasture is called *wabīl* when it is heavy and harmful, when it makes one ill on being eaten. This second branch, joined with the verb *dhāqū* (they tasted), sets up a consistency in the verse: **what is tasted is something that cannot be digested.**

I record this link as an observation; I do not claim that the choice of words was deliberate, but it is a fact that the two words meet in the same field of meaning.

**أَمْرِهِمْ — "of their own affair".** And this possession sets up the verse's logic of justice: what is tasted is not a disaster coming from outside but the consequence **of their own affair**. The ill consequence has come out of the affair itself.

This construct will find an echo within the sūra: in Sūrat al-Ṭalāq almost the same sentence occurs — *fa-dhāqat wabāla amrihā* (al-Ṭalāq 65/9). That two neighbouring sūras use the same expression is striking; I shall make a note of it in the relevant place.

### وَلَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ — two times

The sentence sets two different times side by side, and this is the finest point of the verse:

- *fa-dhāqū* — **perfect**: it happened, it is over. A consequence realised in this world.
- *wa lahum ʿadhābun alīm* — a **nominal sentence**, future: for them there is a punishment besides.

That is, the verse does not count the consequence in this world as the **whole** of the reckoning. A collapse seen within history is not a closure; it is a sign.

This is the determining feature of the Qurʾān's reading of history, and it requires a reservation: **it does not follow from this that every society that collapses has been punished, or that every society that stands is approved.** The sūra itself will block this in the fifteenth verse — there it will be said that abundance is a *fitna*. Standing is not a certificate.

## 64/6 — ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُ كَانَت تَّأْتِيهِمْ رُسُلُهُم بِالْبَيِّنَاتِ فَقَالُوا أَبَشَرٌ يَهْدُونَنَا فَكَفَرُوا وَتَوَلَّوا وَّاسْتَغْنَى اللَّهُ وَاللَّهُ غَنِيٌّ حَمِيدٌ

*Dhālika bi-annahu kānat taʾtīhim rusuluhum bi'l-bayyināti fa-qālū a-basharun yahdūnanā, fa-kafarū wa tawallaw, wa'staghna'llāh, wa'llāhu ghaniyyun ḥamīd*

"That is because their messengers kept coming to them with clear proofs and they said, 'Shall a mortal guide us?' So they denied and turned away. And God showed that He needed nothing. God is without need, worthy of praise."

### The work of the verse: recording the cause

The preceding verse stated the result (they tasted). This verse states the **cause**: *dhālika bi-anna* — "this is because."

And the cause given is striking. The expected cause might have been an immorality or an injustice. Instead of that the verse records a **sentence**: *a-basharun yahdūnanā.* That is, what is shown as the ground of destruction is not an act but an **objection**.

### كَانَتْ تَأْتِيهِمْ — continuousness

Note the structure of the verb: *kānat taʾtīhim* — the perfect *kāna* + the imperfect *taʾtī*. In Arabic this compound conveys an action that **went on and was repeated in the past**.

That is, not "their messengers came to them" but **"their messengers used to keep coming to them."** Not a single event but a repeated state. And this sets up the side of the verse that has to do with justice: the warning was not given once and passed over.

**بِٱلْبَيِّنَٰت** — the plural of *bayyina*. The root of this word (**ب ي ن**) and the definition "the proof that distinguishes, and is clear because it distinguishes" were analysed in detail in `098-beyyine.md`; I do not repeat that analysis.

Only one point needs carrying over here: there the coming of *bayyina* in the **singular** and **definite** (*al-bayyina*) was dwelt on, and it was established that that single bayyina was not a book but **a person**. Here the word is **plural** and **definite**: *al-bayyināt*. The difference is one of scope: there a messenger himself was the proof; here the proofs the messengers brought are being counted.

### أَبَشَرٌ يَهْدُونَنَا — "shall a mortal guide us?"

The densest sentence of the sūra, and the direct counterpart of the main thesis of `098-beyyine.md`.

**بَشَر** — root **ب ش ر**. At the centre of this root stands **skin**: *bashara* is a person's outer skin. The analysis of the root was done in `074-muddessir.md` (in connection with 74/25 and 74/29: *qawlu'l-bashar* and *lawwāḥatun li'l-bashar*); what was recorded there was this — both good tidings (*bushrā*) and scorching show themselves in the skin.

The word *bashar* names the human being in the Qurʾān in his **biological, flesh-and-bone** aspect. The word *insān*, by contrast, more in his relational and moral aspect. This distinction is not absolute — the Qurʾān uses both flexibly — but the tendency runs this way.

**And that the word chosen in the objection is exactly this one shows the logic of the objection.** Those who object do not say "shall a human being"; they say **"shall a mortal"**. That is, they reduce the one before them to his flesh and his skin: a body that eats, drinks, tires and dies.

**Its coming indefinite is meaningful too:** *a-basharun*, "a mortal" — unspecified. The person's name, his qualities, his past are not mentioned. Only his kind is stated. The belittling is done by leaving him unspecified.

**The verb's being plural:** *yahdūnanā* — "shall they guide us". The subject is singular (*basharun*), the verb plural. Classical grammar explains this thus: *bashar* here is a generic noun, and in the singular form the plural is intended — "the tribe of mortals". That is, the objection is directed not at a person but at a **category**: what is refused is that a being of the human kind should do this work.

### The link with `098-beyyine.md`: the target of the objection

When the second verse of Sūrat al-Bayyina was treated, the following was established, and I do not repeat it, I recall it: **a bayyina is not a text but a person** — *rasūlun mina'llāhi yatlū ṣuḥufan muṭahhara*. And four consequences of that choice were counted there; the fourth was this: *"a proof is fragile. A human being grows tired, falls ill, dies. To choose a human being as the bayyina is to place the proof inside fragility."*

And this too was recorded there: the Qurʾān records this objection itself — the mushrikūn say *"what sort of messenger is this, he eats food, he walks about in the markets"* (al-Furqān 25/7); and the Qurʾān does not reject the objection, it turns the proposition round: *"Say: I am only a mortal like you; it is revealed to me"* (al-Kahf 18/110).

**Al-Taghābun 64/6 is the barest form of the same objection.**

| | **al-Furqān 25/7** | **al-Taghābun 64/6** |
|---|---|---|
| The form of the objection | Detailed: he eats, he walks in the market | A single word: *bashar* |
| Against whom | Against a particular messenger | Against **all the messengers**, throughout all history |
| Its time | Now | The past — *kānat taʾtīhim* |
| Its outcome | It is recorded | It is recorded as **the ground of destruction** |

The meaning of the difference is this: al-Taghābun presents this objection not as a local reaction but as a **repeated pattern**. All the peoples framed the same sentence.

**And an observation on why the objection is so durable — this is my own reading.** The objection in fact looks reasonable: one expects a source of knowledge to be greater than the one who carries the knowledge. The disproportion between source and carrier disturbs the mind. People think an extraordinary message requires an extraordinary carrier.

The Qurʾān's answer is not to meet this expectation but to question the expectation itself — and, as was shown in `098-beyyine.md`, the carrier's being human is counted not a defect but a **condition**: because what is delivered is something livable, and the proof of livability can only be someone living it.

### وَٱسْتَغْنَى ٱللَّهُ — the coldest sentence in the sūra

A sentence of three words. And one of the most distancing expressions in the Qurʾān.

**Root: غ ن ي.** *Ghinā* is **needlessness, self-sufficiency**. The English rendering "wealth" catches only one side of the root; in Arabic *ghaniyy* is not one whose property is much but **one who is not in need**. The two notions overlap but are not the same: wealth is a quantity, *ghinā* a position.

**The pattern: Form X (*istaghnā*).** This form generally conveys **seeking** (*istaghfara* — he sought forgiveness). But it has a second function as well, and that is what is at work here: to **show that an attribute is present in oneself**, to be in that state. *Istaghnā* — He made plain that He was in no need, He showed His needlessness.

This is hard to put into English. Expressions such as "God showed Himself above need" or "it became plain that God had need of nothing" can be used. What the sentence does is this:

**They turned away; and from the other side no answering move came.**

This is a heavier sentence than a threat. A threat shows interest; indifference does not. What the deniers do is, to their own minds, a great move — to refuse an invitation, to turn a messenger away. The verse records that that move produced no deficiency at all on the other side.

**And this is a principle the Qurʾān repeats:**

> *"Moses said: If you and all who are on the earth were ungrateful, know that God is indeed without need, worthy of praise."* (Ibrāhīm 14/8)

The same two names, in the same order, in the same context. And the verse-ending of al-Taghābun is exactly this: *wa'llāhu ghaniyyun ḥamīd.*

**What does the coming of the two names together do?** *Ghaniyy* conveys needlessness, *ḥamīd* worthiness of praise. These work in two different directions:

- Had there been only *ghaniyy*, a cold indifference would have remained in the picture: He has no need, therefore He takes no interest.
- The addition of *ḥamīd* cuts that off. To deserve praise requires doing good. The one without need gives not because He is without need but **because He is generous**.

That is, the end of the verse balances the coldness in its middle: a giving that expects no return. And this joins with the *lahu'l-mulku wa lahu'l-ḥamd* of the first verse — there dominion and praise were set side by side; here *ghinā* and praise.

## 64/7 — زَعَمَ الَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا أَن لَّن يُبْعَثُوا قُلْ بَلَىٰ وَرَبِّي لَتُبْعَثُنَّ ثُمَّ لَتُنَبَّؤُنَّ بِمَا عَمِلْتُمْ وَذَٰلِكَ عَلَى اللَّهِ يَسِيرٌ

*Zaʿama'lladhīna kafarū an lan yubʿathū, qul balā wa rabbī la-tubʿathunna thumma la-tunabbaʾunna bimā ʿamiltum, wa dhālika ʿala'llāhi yasīr*

"Those who denied claimed that they would never be raised. Say: Yes indeed, by my Lord, you will surely be raised; then you will surely be informed of what you did. And that is easy for God."

### زَعَمَ — a claim without ground

The first word of the verse settles by itself the epistemological position of the opposition.

**Root: ز ع م.** The word's use in Arabic is particular: *zaʿm* is **a claim that has no proof, for which its maker takes no responsibility**. When *zaʿama* is placed before an utterance, a doubt about the truth of that utterance is automatically added.

This is preserved in the English rendering too: not "he supposed" but **"he claimed", "allegedly", "so-called"**. The Turkish *zaîm* ("guarantor, leader") belongs to another related branch — the root also has the sense of "to take upon oneself, to stand surety", and the lexicographers connect the two senses: *zaʿm* is to **take an utterance upon oneself**, but without the power to pay what it costs.

A saying the lexicographers transmit widely states that *zaʿm* is "the by-name of falsehood" — that is, if an utterance begins with *zaʿama*, a falsehood is expected to follow. I relate this saying without stating whose it is, as **an expression widely transmitted by the lexicographers**; I do not ascribe it to a source.

**The Qurʾān uses this word consistently.** Almost everywhere in the Qurʾān the word marks utterances that come from the mouths of opponents and are to be rejected. There is also a striking usage: in answer to those who make claims without ground the Qurʾān says *"bring your proof"* (this pattern occurs at al-Baqara 2/111 as *hātū burhānakum*; it was treated in `002-bakara.md`).

**So the first word of the verse settles the frame of the dispute:** the other side has no proof in hand. Denial of the resurrection is recorded not as a piece of knowledge but as a **claim**.

And there is evidence for this within the text: the Qurʾān relates in many places the ground given by those who deny the resurrection, and almost every time the ground is **not an argument but an expression of astonishment**: "rotted bones?", "after we have become dust?" These are not proofs but a difficulty of conception.

### أَن لَّن يُبْعَثُوا — a double reinforcement

**لَنْ** is the particle in Arabic that negates the future, and it is stronger than *lā*: "it will never be."

There is also *an* in the sentence: *an lan yubʿathū*. That is, the claim itself has been framed as a verbal-noun clause and the strongest particle of negation has been placed inside it.

**The certainty of the opposition is recorded.** Those who deny do not say "perhaps it will not be"; they say "it will never be." And behind this certainty there is no ground at all — because the verb at the head of the sentence is *zaʿama*.

**The asymmetry here is the centre of the verse:** on one side a claim without proof yet certain, on the other an answer supported by an oath.

### قُلْ بَلَىٰ — the difference between *balā* and *naʿam*

The answer begins with **بَلَىٰ**, and this is one of the subtlest particles in Arabic.

In Arabic there are two words for affirmation, and the two do not do the same work:

| Particle | When it is used | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **نَعَمْ (naʿam)** | In answer to a positive question | It confirms whatever was said in the question |
| **بَلَىٰ (balā)** | In answer to a **negative** sentence or a negative question | It **rejects** the negation, that is, it affirms |

The difference is easy to see with an example. If someone asks "aren't you coming?":

- If *naʿam* is said: "yes — I am not coming." The negation has been confirmed.
- If *balā* is said: "no — I am coming." The negation has been rejected.

English has no such distinction, and for that reason something is lost in translation. In English "yes" and "no" remain ambiguous against a negative question; in Arabic there is no ambiguity.

**The use here is exactly in place:** the claim is negative (*lan yubʿathū*), the answer is *balā* — that is, the negation is decisively rejected.

**And this particle occurs several more times in the Qurʾān in the context of the resurrection, doing the same work.** One of the best-known examples is in Sūrat al-Qiyāma: *"Does the human being suppose that We cannot gather his bones? **Balā** — We are able to set right even his fingertips"* (al-Qiyāma 75/3-4); those verses were treated in `075-kiyame.md`.

### وَرَبِّي — the oath

**The second part of the answer is an oath.** The *wāw* here is the particle of oath: *wa rabbī* — "by my Lord."

And this is a point to be noted: **the Prophet is commanded to swear.** There are many places in the Qurʾān where God swears (such as the openings of the sūras al-Shams, al-Ḍuḥā, al-Tīn, al-ʿAṣr, al-Fajr); but for a messenger to be told "swear" is a rare situation.

There are other places in the Qurʾān where the Prophet is commanded to swear on the subject of the resurrection; this is a recurring pattern. **But I do not write the sūra and verse numbers of those places here, because I am not certain of them.** What can be recorded is this: this pattern occurs several times in the Qurʾān, and every time the subject is the **resurrection**.

**Why is an oath necessary?** As an observation — this is my own reading: an oath is an instrument that comes into play where proof does not work. If a thing can be proved there is no need for an oath; if it cannot be proved, the oath is the speaker's putting forward his own trustworthiness.

And the resurrection is by definition a report that cannot be proved — because it has not yet happened and is not a repeatable event. For that reason an utterance about it is only as strong as the trustworthiness of the one who utters it. The oath puts exactly that on the table.

**The content of the oath is meaningful too:** not "by God" but **"by my Lord"** — *wa rabbī*. The word means the one who nurtures and brings up, the one who owns (root **ر ب ب**; its analysis was done in `001-fatiha.md`). That is, the name sworn by is the name that conveys a **relationship**. The speaker swears by a name with which he himself has a relation.

### لَتُبْعَثُنَّ — the arrangement of the reinforcements

Look at the structure of the answer: **قُلْ بَلَىٰ وَرَبِّي لَتُبْعَثُنَّ**

Let us count how many reinforcements there are in one sentence:

1. **بَلَىٰ** — the particle that rejects the negation.
2. **وَرَبِّي** — the oath.
3. **لَ** — the *lām* that prepares the answer to the oath.
4. **نَّ** — the heavy *nūn* of emphasis.

Four layers. And in the claim on the other side too there was a strong negation (*lan*). The sentence meets the other side's certainty with its own certainty — and puts down more.

**The voice of the verb: passive.** *Tubʿathūn* — "you will be raised". This is an attitude maintained throughout the sūra. The verbs ascribed to the human being are active (*taʿmalūn*, *tusirrūn*, *tuʿlinūn*, *dhāqū*), while those belonging to God are either ascribed to Him directly or framed in the passive. The boundary is engraved into the grammar.

### ثُمَّ لَتُنَبَّؤُنَّ بِمَا عَمِلْتُمْ — the return of *nabaʾ*

**And here is the finest internal link of the sūra.**

In the fifth verse there was a **نَبَأ**: the report of those who came before, a report that reached the ones addressed and produced no result.

Here the same root returns — but this time **as a verb, in the passive voice, and about the one addressed himself**: *la-tunabbaʾunna bimā ʿamiltum* — "you will be informed of what you did."

| | **64/5** | **64/7** |
|---|---|---|
| Form | A noun: *nabaʾ* | A verb: *tunabbaʾūn* (Form II, passive) |
| Subject of the report | **Others** — those who came before | **You** — your own deeds |
| Who receives the report | You | You |
| Its time | It came (perfect: *a lam yaʾtikum*) | It will come (*la-tunabbaʾunna*) |
| Its outcome | Can be rejected | **Cannot be rejected** |

What the structure says is this: **the report comes twice.** The first is about others and comes so that a lesson may be taken; it can be rejected. The second is about the person himself and comes to give information; it cannot be rejected, because its object is the person's own act.

And recall the definition in `078-nebe.md`: a *nabaʾ* is a report on hearing which something ought to change. When the first *nabaʾ* came there was still the possibility of changing. When the second comes there will be nothing left to change.

**I record this as my own reading.** That the two verses use the same root is a datum of the text; to set up this relation between them is my own inference and is not binding.

**A note on grammar.** *Tunabbaʾūn* is the passive of *nabbaʾa* (Form II). Recall that Form II conveys intensity: the information will be given fully and in detail. The Qurʾān states this detail openly elsewhere — the expression that the record of deeds *"leaves out nothing small or great"* occurs in Sūrat al-Kahf (18/49).

### وَذَٰلِكَ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ يَسِيرٌ

**يَسِير** — root **ي س ر**: ease, being doable without strain. This root was treated in `094-insirah.md` in connection with *yusr*; I do not repeat it here.

**The function of the verse-ending:** the objection ran that the resurrection was **impossible**. This answer does not reject the impossibility — it declares the category of impossibility **beside the point**. It does not say "it is not hard"; it says "it is easy".

And the qualification *ʿala'llāh* is important: the ease lies not **in the act itself** but **relative to the agent**. The difficulty of a task is not an absolute quality; it depends on who does it. The verse shifts the measure from the act to the agent.

This word will return in Sūrat al-Ṭalāq as well (not *yasīran* but *yusrā*, 65/4 and 65/7); I shall gather the shared vocabulary between the two neighbouring sūras at the end of the sūra.

## 64/8 — فَآمِنُوا بِاللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِ وَالنُّورِ الَّذِي أَنزَلْنَا وَاللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

*Fa-āminū bi'llāhi wa rasūlihi wa'l-nūri'lladhī anzalnā, wa'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna khabīr*

"So believe in God, in His Messenger and in the light We sent down. And God is aware of what you do."

### The sūra's only direct call to faith

This verse ties the picture built up over eight verses to a command: *fa-āminū.* The **fāʾ** at the head conveys succession and consequence — "since it is so".

And the sūra gives this command **once**. What follows will always be other verbs: guard yourselves, hear, obey, spend, lend. That is, the command to believe is a door; the room behind it is furnished with deeds.

### ٱلنُّورِ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنزَلْنَا — "the light We sent down"

Here the Qurʾān speaks of itself as **light**. And the choice of the word needs dwelling on, because there were alternatives: *kitāb*, *dhikr*, *furqān*, *hudā*, *āyāt*.

**Root: ن و ر.** *Nūr* is light itself. In Arabic two words are used for light, and the following distinction is widely made among the lexicographers:

| Word | Its definition | Example of use in the Qurʾān |
|---|---|---|
| **ضِيَاء (*ḍiyāʾ*)** | Light whose source is in itself | Of the sun: *jaʿala'l-shamsa ḍiyāʾan* (Yūnus 10/5) |
| **نُور (*nūr*)** | Reflected, transmitted light | Of the moon: *wa'l-qamara nūrā* (the same verse) |

This distinction is widely transmitted among the lexicographers; but I cannot claim that it works this sharply in every use in the Qurʾān — because the Qurʾān uses the word *nūr* of God as well (al-Nūr 24/35). I record the distinction not as a fixed rule but as **a widespread definition**.

**Three observations on what the word "light" does in this verse:**

**One. Light is not seen; it makes seeing possible.** When light enters a room your eye does not see the light, it sees the objects lit by the light. To name revelation *nūr* is to count it not an object looked at but **an instrument by which one looks**.

**Two. Light distinguishes — and this joins with the other words of the sūra.** In the sixth verse there was *bayyināt*; in `098-beyyine.md` it was shown that at the centre of that root stands **distinguishing**. Light does exactly this: it separates what it illuminates from its surroundings. The two words say the same work with two different images.

**Three. Light does not compel.** Light is not a coercion; it has no effect on one who shuts his eyes. This is another expression of the point recorded in `098-beyyine.md` — *"a prophet is not a force that compels people but a proof submitted to the court."* And the twelfth verse of the sūra will say this openly: *"Our Messenger's duty is only clear conveying."*

**What the light points to is disputed.** In the classical commentaries two main views are transmitted:

| View | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **The Qurʾān** | The verb *anzalnā* (We sent down) is used of revelation. The Qurʾān describes itself as *nūr* in other places too. | — |
| **The Prophet** | That a third thing should be counted after "God and His Messenger" has been said requires that thing to be separate; but *nūr* is used of the Prophet too in the Qurʾān (at al-Māʾida 5/15 *nūr* and *kitābun mubīn* are mentioned together, and it has been disputed who the *nūr* in that verse is). | If the verb *anzalnā* is used of a person, a figurative reading becomes necessary; besides, the Messenger has already been mentioned in the sentence. |

The first view seems more suited to the text: the verb *anzalnā* and the third place in the sentence point to a text that was sent down. I state this as a preference; it is not binding.

### وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ — the verse-ending: *khabīr*

The second verse ended with *baṣīr* (the one who sees), the fourth with *ʿalīm* (the one who knows), this verse ends with **خَبِير** (the one who is aware).

**Root: خ ب ر.** And this root is directly related to the analysis of *nabaʾ* in the fifth verse of the sūra. As was recorded in `078-nebe.md`, in Arabic there are two words for a report: *khabar* and *nabaʾ*; and *khabar* is the general one, *nabaʾ* the weighty one.

**خَبِير** is the *faʿīl* pattern of this root and means "possessor of report" — but in Arabic it has a special further sense: **one who knows the inner face, the hidden side of a thing.** *Khubr* is to know a thing by trying it and by entering into it. The work of the farmer who ploughs the soil and sees what is under it is called *khabr* from the same root; the lexicographers record this link.

So the three verse-endings give three different forms of knowing:

| Verse | Verse-ending | The kind of knowing |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | *baṣīr* | By seeing — from outside |
| 4 | *ʿalīm* (+ *bi-dhāti'l-ṣudūr*) | By knowing — from inside |
| 8 | *khabīr* | **By being aware** — acquainted with the inner face |

And this triad will be gathered into a single construct in the last verse of the sūra: *ʿālimu'l-ghaybi wa'l-shahāda.*

## 64/9 — يَوْمَ يَجْمَعُكُمْ لِيَوْمِ الْجَمْعِ ذَٰلِكَ يَوْمُ التَّغَابُنِ وَمَن يُؤْمِن بِاللَّهِ وَيَعْمَلْ صَالِحًا يُكَفِّرْ عَنْهُ سَيِّئَاتِهِ وَيُدْخِلْهُ جَنَّاتٍ تَجْرِي مِن تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا أَبَدًا ذَٰلِكَ الْفَوْزُ الْعَظِيمُ

*Yawma yajmaʿukum li-yawmi'l-jamʿ, dhālika yawmu'l-taghābun, wa man yuʾmin bi'llāhi wa yaʿmal ṣāliḥan yukaffir ʿanhu sayyiʾātihi wa yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtiha'l-anhāru khālidīna fīhā abadā, dhālika'l-fawzu'l-ʿaẓīm*

"On the day He gathers you for the day of gathering. That is the day of mutual loss in the bargain. Whoever believes in God and does a righteous deed, He will cover over his evil deeds and admit him into gardens beneath which rivers flow, to remain there for ever. That is the great winning."

The heart of the sūra. And the phrase that carries the sūra's name is here, in the second word of the verse.

### يَوْمَ يَجْمَعُكُمْ لِيَوْمِ ٱلْجَمْعِ — the doubling of the gathering

The verse opens by using the same root twice in succession: *yajmaʿukum* (He will gather you) and *yawmi'l-jamʿ* (the day of gathering). Root **ج م ع**: to bring together, to gather.

This repetition is a manner of speech known in Arabic as **ishtiqāq** (the use together of words from one root), and it conveys emphasis. It is not a repetition like the English "they gathered at a gathering"; it is the placing of the act of gathering both as an act and as the name of a day.

**And the function of the repetition is this:** that day is by definition a day of **gathering**. The Qurʾān uses this name too among the names it gives that day. Gathering is the precondition of the reckoning — those who are scattered cannot settle accounts.

**The letter لِ (*lām*).** *Li-yawmi'l-jamʿ* — "**for** the day of gathering". The *lām* here conveys purpose or time. Both have been defended: "for the day of gathering" (purpose) or "on the day of gathering" (time). The difference of meaning is small; preferring the first adds a purposiveness to the sentence, which agrees with *bi'l-ḥaqq* in the third verse.

### And then: ذَٰلِكَ يَوْمُ ٱلتَّغَابُنِ

**Here is the move of the sūra.**

The day of gathering has been announced. The reader now expects a description of that day — its terror, its sound, its scale. This is what the Qurʾān does in other sūras: the sky is split, the stars scatter, the mountains are set moving.

This sūra does none of that. It gives the day a second name. And the name it gives is not cosmic but **commercial**.

That is, the sentence does this: the day of *gathering together* is at the same time the day of *the comparing of ledgers*. And the name of what comes to light when the comparison is made: **taghābun.**

### ٱلتَّغَابُن — root and pattern

**Root: غ ب ن (gh-b-n).**

The concrete sense of the root is **to come off worse in a transaction**. To put it more exactly: a party's suffering a loss **because he does not know the value**. *Ghabana fi'l-bayʿ* — he came off worse in the sale. *Ghabn* is the buyer's paying too much or the seller's letting a thing go too cheap.

The word has another use as well, and it completes the picture: **غَبْن** also means **weakness in judgement, in understanding** — *ghabīna'l-raʾy*, one whose judgement is weak. That is, the root covers both the transaction itself and the **wrong appraisal** that led to the transaction.

And another branch the lexicographers record: *ghabana'l-thawb* — to **fold** a garment, to turn it over and hide part of it. Seen from here, the picture of coming off worse is this: part of a thing has been folded away and hidden, and the buyer has priced it looking only at the visible part. I relate this derivation as a **point about the language**; I do not claim that this is the root's principal sense.

**The pattern: تَفَاعُل (*tafāʿul*).** And this is the pattern treated in detail in `102-tekasur.md`.

I do not repeat what was recorded there; I recall it: **Form VI in Arabic conveys reciprocity (*mushāraka*)** — it shows that the action goes back and forth between two or more parties (*qātala* → *taqātala*; *ʿarafa* → *taʿārafa*; *fākhara* → *tafākhara*).

Accordingly:

- *Ghabana* — he cheated, he brought someone to loss in a transaction.
- **تَغَابَنَ** — **they came off worse at one another's hands / they cheated one another.**

And *taghābun* is the verbal noun of this verb: mutual loss in a bargain.

### What reciprocity means — a comparison of four words

In this commentary words conveying reciprocity and contest have been analysed in four separate places. Setting the four side by side clarifies what the word here does.

| Word | Root | The object of the contest or transaction | Ruling | Where it was treated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **تَكَاثُر** (*takāthur*) | ك ث ر | To outdo in quantity | **Blamed** — "it distracted you" | `102-tekasur.md` |
| **تَنَافُس** (*tanāfus*) | ن ف س | To compete in what is valuable | **Commanded** — "let those who compete compete" | `083-mutaffifin.md` |
| **اِسْتِبَاق** (*istibāq*) | س ب ق | To get ahead in good works | **Commanded** | `002-bakara.md` (2/148) |
| **تَغَابُن** (*taghābun*) | غ ب ن | **Mutual loss in a bargain** | **A statement of fact** — not a ruling but the name of an outcome | Here |

*Istibāq* is in Form X, not Form VI; I have taken it into this table to complete the notion of "contest", not for shared pattern. If I did not point this out the table would be misleading.

**And the distinction set up in `002-bakara.md` in connection with 2/148 is decisive here:**

> *"The difference between the two contests lies in whether the object is divisible. Property, rank, number — these are limited; one person's gaining means another's losing, and so the contest produces conflict. Good, by contrast, is not limited; one person's doing much good does not diminish another's share."*

**Taghābun is the counterpart of that distinction on the Day of Resurrection.**

A transaction is by definition **zero-sum**: if one party bought cheap, the other party sold cheap. Gain and loss are two faces of the same operation. Form VI says exactly this: *taghābun* is not a one-sided coming off worse; it is the name of an operation in which **one party's gain is the other party's loss**.

### Then who takes what from whom?

The verse does not say. In the classical commentaries several explanations are proposed; I have tried to gather them. I present none of them as certainly correct.

| # | Between whom the loss is | Its explanation | Its strong side | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Between the people of the Garden and the people of the Fire** | Those who enter the Garden take over the places in the Garden that belonged to the people of the Fire; loser and winner change places. Thus the operation really is reciprocal. | It meets the reciprocity requirement of the pattern exactly. | This explanation is grounded in the classical sources on a transmitted report; I cannot speak with certainty about the soundness of that report. |
| 2 | **With one's own self** | A person has come off worse by giving his hereafter for this world; the taker and the giver are both himself. | It overlaps exactly with the metaphor of trade in `002-bakara.md`: giving guidance and taking error. | It does not fully meet Form VI's requirement of "two parties". |
| 3 | **Among human beings** | The accounts of those who cheated one another in this world are settled on that day; who cheated whom comes to light. | The reading closest to the commercial sense of the root. | The context of the verse is not a reckoning of transactions but a general picture of final outcomes. |
| 4 | **Everyone's learning the true price** | On that day it becomes clear what everything was worth. The loss is mutual because everyone looks at his own transaction and sees that he set the wrong price. | It overlaps with the root's sense of "not knowing the value"; general and comprehensive. | The least determinate reading. |

**My judgement and its ground.** Rather than counting the four readings as alternatives to one another, it seems to me more consistent to separate the **two layers** of the word:

At the root of *ghabn* there were two things: **wrong appraisal** and **the outcome of the transaction**. The word carries both layers at once. In that case what the verse says may be this: on that day both the outcome of the transactions (who gained what) and the correctness of the appraisals (who reckoned what at what price) come to light.

And the reciprocity, in its plainest form, lies here: **in a transaction the price is one; but the parties' opinions about that price are two.** When the transaction is over, one of the two has been wrong. The Day of Resurrection is the day on which opinions are compared with the price.

I present this as a preference, not as a transmitted report. It is not binding, and the four readings above are defensible as well.

### The sūra's vocabulary of trade

A word does not enter a text by itself. If *taghābun* was chosen, there must be other words from the same field around it. Let us look:

| Word | Verse | Its counterpart in the field of trade |
|---|---|---|
| **ٱلْمُلْك** | 1 | Ownership, possession |
| **ٱلتَّغَابُن** | 9 | Coming off worse in a transaction |
| **ٱلْفَوْز** | 9 | Winning, deliverance — used also of winning at gambling and in a race |
| **أَجْرٌ عَظِيم** | 15 | **Wage** — the return for work done |
| **قَرْضًا حَسَنًا** | 17 | **Loan, debt** |
| **يُضَٰعِفْهُ** | 17 | **Multiplying** — the increasing of the principal |
| **شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ** | 16 | Miserliness, holding on |
| **أَنفِقُوا** | 16 | To spend, to let go out of one's hand |

Eight words. And there is a striking absence: **the words بيع (selling) and شراء (buying) do not occur in the sūra.** The two verbs the Qurʾān uses most in building its metaphor of trade are missing here.

I record this as an observation and add an interpretation — this is my own reading: the sūra describes not the **act** of the transaction but its **outcome**. Not the moment of sale but the bill. Indeed the word too is not a verb but the name of a day.

**And the discussion of the metaphor of trade in `002-bakara.md` completes this place.** There, in connection with al-Baqara 2/16, the following was recorded, and I do not repeat it, I recall it: the Qurʾān takes this language from Mecca's own language; Mecca was a merchant city, and measure and the reckoning of profit were part of daily speech. And the point really stressed there was this: *"The description of the transaction must be read carefully: they gave guidance and took error. That is, they had something in their hands and they let it go. This shows that going astray is positioned not as 'never having had' but as 'exchanging what one has'."*

**Al-Taghābun turns that finding into the name of a day.** In al-Baqara a transaction was described; here the **day on which the accounts of that transaction are settled** is named. And the chain recorded in `002-bakara.md` — at 2/16 guidance is sold, at 2/86 the hereafter, at 2/102 the person himself — comes out at this day.

### Why did the sūra choose this word? An observation

The three items below are my own reading; they are not transmitted reports.

**One. Coming off worse is different from being robbed.** When a person is robbed he is a victim; when he comes off worse in a bargain he entered the transaction **by his own consent**. The word *ghabn* carries this difference: the one who came off worse was not forced, he was persuaded.

This changes the sūra's conception of the hereafter. What is described on that day is not a victimhood; it is **the coming to light of a contract the person signed himself.**

**Two. Coming off worse is understood afterwards.** A person who comes off worse in a transaction does not know at the time of the transaction that he is coming off worse — had he known, he would not have done it. Coming off worse appears only when the real value is learned. That is, there is a **delay** inside the word.

And the sūra had already set up this delay: the fourth verse said *bi-dhāti'l-ṣudūr* (the one who knows the real value), the eighteenth verse will say *ʿālimu'l-ghaybi wa'l-shahāda*. Between these two ends there is a life in which a person transacts at a **price he does not see**.

**Three. The word conveys not a ruling but an outcome.** *Takāthur* was a blamed act; *tanāfus* a commanded one. *Taghābun* is neither commanded nor forbidden — because it is not an act but a **state**. Here the sūra is not laying down a rule of conduct; it is naming a result of accounting.

And precisely for that reason the verse does not end here.

### The second half of the verse: those who do not come off worse

Had *taghābun* been said and left there, the verse would have been a bleak statement of fact. The verse goes on and describes **the party that does not come off worse**:

> *wa man yuʾmin bi'llāhi wa yaʿmal ṣāliḥan…*

**The conditional structure.** *Man* (whoever) is a conditional particle; two verbs form the condition, three the answer. And the condition consists of two items: **faith** and **righteous deed**. The eighth verse of the sūra had commanded faith; here a second condition is added beside it.

This pair is one of the most frequently repeated formulas in the Qurʾān, and it sets up the general frame of the finding treated in `107-maun.md`: belief and conduct are given not as two separate headings but as **two items of a single list**.

**And note: *ṣāliḥan* is indefinite and singular.** Not "righteous deeds" but "a righteous deed". In Arabic this conveys the genus — whatever it may be, a deed that is righteous. The threshold has not been raised.

### يُكَفِّرْ عَنْهُ سَيِّئَاتِهِ — covering over

The first item of the answer: *yukaffir ʿanhu sayyiʾātih.*

**Root: ك ف ر.** And this is the very root that occurred in the second verse of the sūra as *kāfir*.

As was recorded in `002-bakara.md` and repeated in `076-insan.md`: the proper sense of the root is **to cover, to close over** — a farmer is called *kāfir* because he covers the seed with earth. I do not repeat that analysis.

**But something extraordinary is happening here and it must be recorded: the same root occurs in this sūra as the act of two opposite subjects.**

| Verse | Word | Who covers | What is covered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | *kāfirun* | **The human being** | The truth he has seen |
| 9 | *yukaffir* | **God** | The servant's evil deeds |

The same act, in two directions. When a human being covers the truth it is called *kufr* and is blamed; when God covers the servant's fault it is called *takfīr* and is a favour.

The difference lies in the object: **what is covered.** The act is the same, the ruling opposite.

I record this link as an observation. That the two words are from the same root is a datum of the text; I do not claim that a deliberate contrast has been set up between them, but where they stand within the sūra is worth noting.

**The pattern: Form II (*kaffara*).** Intensity: to cover thoroughly, to close over completely.

**The particle عَنْ.** *Yukaffir ʿanhu* — "He covers it away from him, He covers it by removing it from him". The particle *ʿan* conveys moving away. That is, the evil is not merely hidden; it is **separated** from the person.

**سَيِّئَات — evil deeds.** Root **س و أ**: to be bad, to be ugly, to grieve someone. Inside the word lies the idea of "what brings a person into a bad state".

And note: what is covered in the answer clause is *sayyiʾāt*, not *dhunūb* or *āthām*. The Qurʾān uses these words with different weights; *sayyiʾa* generally conveys the lighter kind. I do not present this distinction as a fixed rule — the Qurʾān's use of words is not that rigid — but I record the choice of the word.

### جَنَّٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ

One of the most often repeated descriptions of the Garden in the Qurʾān.

**جَنَّة** — root **ج ن ن**: **to cover, to hide.** From the same root come *jinn* (the unseen being), *janīn* (the one in the womb, the covered one), *majnūn* (whose mind is covered), *junna* (shield). The analysis of this root was done in `072-cin.md`; I do not repeat it here.

**And this must be noticed:** a *janna* is a garden that **covers** what is beneath it because its trees are dense. That is, the word "garden" is itself a word of covering.

This sets up a third covering within the verse: the servant covers the truth (*kāfir*), God covers the fault (*yukaffir*), and the servant is taken beneath a cover (*janna*). Not three separate roots — two are **ك ف ر**, one **ج ن ن** — but all three come out at the same image. I record this as an observation, not as a claim about roots.

**تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ** — "beneath which rivers flow". This construct occurs dozens of times in the Qurʾān and every time in the same form.

**نَهَر** — root **ن ه ر**: to flow, to flow broadly. And as was recorded in `098-beyyine.md`, some of the lexicographers set up a link of derivation between *nahār* (daytime) and *nahr* (river) — it is said that in both lies the idea of "advancing by cleaving and widening"; but as was noted there too, this is not beyond dispute.

**Why "beneath"?** In the classical commentaries two explanations are given: that the rivers flow beneath the pavilions and the trees (a description of place), or that the water flows not visibly but underneath, so as to feed the roots (a description of function). Both are defensible; I adopt no preference.

### خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَآ أَبَدًا — and an asymmetry

**خَالِدِين** — root **خ ل د**: to remain long, to be continuous. In Arabic *khuld* does not necessarily mean "eternity"; it can convey a long and unbroken continuance as well.

And precisely for that reason the verse adds a second word: **أَبَدًا** — for ever. This is the word that makes the duration decisively endless.

**Now look at the tenth verse:** there too there is *khālidīna fīhā* — but *abadā* is **absent**.

| | **64/9** — the Garden | **64/10** — the Fire |
|---|---|---|
| Wording | *khālidīna fīhā **abadā*** | *khālidīna fīhā* |
| Reinforcement | Present | **Absent** |

This asymmetry was noted in `098-beyyine.md` too, and the reservation entered there holds here as well; I repeat it exactly: **the difference within the sūra is real and must be shown; but no conclusion about the duration of Hell can be drawn from it, because the Qurʾān uses the word *abadan* of Hell too in other places** (al-Nisāʾ 4/169; al-Jinn 72/23). This is a debate in *kalām* and is not the subject of this commentary.

The only thing that can be recorded is this: **in this sūra** the reinforcement has been placed on one side only.

### ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ — the winning

**Root: ف و ز.** *Fawz* means **deliverance and winning**; both at once. In Arabic both escaping a danger and obtaining a prize are *fawz*. The same root is used of the winning arrow in games of chance (*fāza*).

And this two-sidedness of the word sits exactly in the context of the verse: *taghābun* was a word of loss; *fawz* is a word of gain. The two in the same verse, from the same field.

**And once more the choice of word is commercial.** The verse does not say "that is the great reward" (*al-ajru'l-ʿaẓīm*) — that word will come in the fifteenth verse. Here it says **winning**.

The difference between them is this: *ajr* is the return for work, and is calculable. *Fawz* is the outcome of a transaction, and it is **out of all proportion** — the winner takes more than he put in.

**The demonstrative: ذَٰلِكَ.** The verse uses *dhālika* twice: *dhālika yawmu'l-taghābun* and *dhālika'l-fawzu'l-ʿaẓīm*. The demonstrative of distance.

In `107-maun.md` two functions of this pronoun were recorded (belittling/distancing, and visibility). Here the second function holds: distinct enough to be pointed at from far off. And the two occurrences of *dhālika* bind the two ends of the verse together — one points to the loss, the other to the gain.

## 64/10 — وَالَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا وَكَذَّبُوا بِآيَاتِنَا أُولَٰئِكَ أَصْحَابُ النَّارِ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا وَبِئْسَ الْمَصِيرُ

*Wa'lladhīna kafarū wa kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā ulāʾika aṣḥābu'l-nāri khālidīna fīhā, wa biʾsa'l-maṣīr*

"And those who denied and cried lies to Our signs are the people of the Fire; they remain there. What an evil arrival!"

### Two verbs: كَفَرُوا and كَذَّبُوا

The verse sets two verbs side by side and the two are not the same thing.

- **كَفَرُوا** — they covered. An inward operation: closing over what one has seen.
- **كَذَّبُوا** — they cried lies. An outward operation: saying "this is a lie".

This distinction was treated in `107-maun.md` and I do not repeat it: *kadhaba* (Form I) means "he told a lie" — the agent is a liar; *kadhdhaba* (Form II) means "he called another's true utterance a lie" — what he does is to reject what is true. And what was recorded there is this: *takdhīb* is not a silent denial but **an active stance, a taking up of position**.

And this connects with the second verse of the sūra: there only *kāfirun* had been said — a state. Here an act is added to the state.

### أَصْحَابُ ٱلنَّارِ — "the people of the Fire"

**Root: ص ح ب.** *Ṣaḥb* is to be together, to keep company, **to be found alongside**. *Ṣāḥib* is a travelling companion; *ṣuḥba* is being together; *aṣḥāb* is its plural.

The Qurʾān uses this construct often: *aṣḥābu'l-nār*, *aṣḥābu'l-janna*, *aṣḥābu'l-yamīn*, *aṣḥābu'l-shimāl*, *aṣḥābu'l-kahf*, *aṣḥābu'l-ukhdūd* (treated in `085-buruc.md`).

**The choice of the word is striking.** It does not say "those who enter the Fire" or "those who are in the Fire"; it says **"the companions of the Fire"**. Inside the word *ṣuḥba* there is a **continuity and a belonging**: not a place one owns but a place one belongs to.

And a point recorded in `070-mearic.md` bears on this place: in the root of the word *ʿizīn* too there is belonging — everyone has a group. Here too a group is being named: the person is not present in the Fire, he **belongs** to the Fire.

### وَبِئْسَ ٱلْمَصِيرُ — a return to the third verse

**بِئْسَ** in Arabic is a *fiʿl al-dhamm* — a verb of dispraise. Its counterpart is *niʿma* (a verb of praise). Both are frozen verbs; they do not inflect.

And **ٱلْمَصِير** was the verse-ending of the third verse: *wa ilayhi'l-maṣīr* — "to Him is the coming home."

The sūra brings the same word back seven verses later, and this time places a verb of dispraise before it.

| | **64/3** | **64/10** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *wa ilayhi'l-maṣīr* | *wa biʾsa'l-maṣīr* |
| What it says | **To whom** the coming home is | **What** the arrival is like |
| Scope | Everyone | Some |
| Tone | Neutral | Dispraise |

The meaning of this is: **the address of the coming home and the character of the arrival are separate things.** Everyone comes home to the same place; but not everyone meets the same thing.

This agrees with the picture the sūra has been building from the start. In the second verse people came out of a single creation and were divided into two groups; here they come out of a single homecoming and are divided into two outcomes. The structure is the same: **a common beginning, a diverging outcome.**

## 64/11 — مَا أَصَابَ مِن مُّصِيبَةٍ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِ اللَّهِ وَمَن يُؤْمِن بِاللَّهِ يَهْدِ قَلْبَهُ وَاللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

*Mā aṣāba min muṣībatin illā bi-idhni'llāh, wa man yuʾmin bi'llāhi yahdi qalbah, wa'llāhu bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm*

"No affliction strikes without God's leave. Whoever believes in God, He sets his heart straight. And God knows everything."

### Why did the subject change?

The tenth verse closed the two outcomes. The eleventh verse enters the subject of **affliction** without any sentence of transition. This abrupt shift calls for an explanation.

In my judgement the connection is this, and I write it as my own reading: up to now the sūra has described the **great reckoning**. Now it moves to **how that reckoning is lived today**. And the first test a person meets today is what befalls him.

That is, the sūra moves from theory to practice — and as the first item of practice it chooses affliction. The following verses will carry this on: obedience (12), reliance (13), family (14), property (15), guarding oneself and spending (16), the loan (17). For seven verses the subject is always **the person's present life**.

### مُصِيبَة — affliction

**Root: ص و ب.** The proper sense of the root is not "calamity"; it is **to hit the mark, to find the target, to fall in the right place**.

This gives a picture quite different from the one the English "calamity" suggests:

- **أَصَابَ (*aṣāba*)** — he hit the mark, he struck the target, he reached it.
- **صَوَاب (*ṣawāb*)** — the right, the on-target. Not the Turkish *sevap* ("religious merit"), which is *thawāb* and comes from the root **ث و ب**; the two must not be confused.
- **صَيِّب (*ṣayyib*)** — rain; what comes down from the sky and hits the earth. It occurs at al-Baqara 2/19.
- **مُصِيبَة (*muṣība*)** — that which hits.

**So at the centre of the word there is no pain; there is hitting the mark.** A *muṣība* is "what befalls" — it may be good as well as bad. In the Qurʾān's usage it is used predominantly for what is bad, but the word itself does not require this.

And this changes the work the verse does. The verse does not say "bad things happen by God's leave"; it says **"nothing that strikes strikes without leave"**. Randomness is being rejected.

### مِن مُّصِيبَةٍ — the pleonastic *min*

A point of grammar: the sentence could have said *mā aṣāba muṣībatun*. It says: *mā aṣāba **min** muṣībatin.*

The *min* here is counted in grammar as **pleonastic** (*zāʾid*) — it adds no new element to the meaning of the sentence, but it **makes the scope absolute**. It is reflected in English by "no … at all".

Compare:
- *mā aṣāba muṣībatun* — "an affliction did not strike."
- *mā aṣāba min muṣībatin* — **"no affliction at all struck."**

The difference is the closing off of exceptions. When *min* comes, the whole genus is covered; no instance is left outside.

And the sentence is built with the structure **مَا … إِلَّا**: negation + exception. That this structure conveys **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*) was treated in `098-beyyine.md` in connection with 98/4. So in the sentence two separate instruments of absoluteness come one on top of the other: *min* closes off the scope, *illā* closes off the condition.

### بِإِذْنِ ٱللَّهِ — "leave"

**Root: أ ذ ن.** And the concrete sense of the root is **the ear**: *udhun*, ear. This link was recorded in `071-nuh.md`: according to the lexicographers, the connection between *idhn* (leave) and *udhun* (ear) is that giving leave is **lending an ear**.

That is, *leave* is hearing a request and giving an answer to it. Inside the word there is a movement of **hearing and approving**.

**And the choice of the word is decisive here.** The verse does not say *bi-amri'llāh* (by God's command); it says *bi-idhni'llāh*.

| Word | What it conveys |
|---|---|
| **أَمْر (command)** | Direct ordering; the source of the act is the one who commands |
| **إِذْن (leave)** | Giving leave; **not preventing** the act from taking place |

The difference is not small. The sentence "it happens by command" says that everything that happens is directly ordered. The sentence "it happens by leave" says something narrower: **nothing takes place outside His knowledge and His permitting.**

I do not present this distinction as a choice in *kalām* and I do not open that debate here. What I record is only the choice of the word — and the choice makes the sentence's claim more guarded.

### وَمَن يُؤْمِن بِٱللَّهِ يَهْدِ قَلْبَهُ — the lock of the sentence

**The most disputed place in the verse is here, and the dispute comes out of a pronoun.**

The sentence runs: *wa man yuʾmin bi'llāhi yahdi qalbah.*

The subject of the verb *yahdi* is not written. And the pronoun in *qalbah* is unspecified too. Several readings come out of this.

| # | Its subject | Its meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **God** | "Whoever believes in God, God sets his heart straight." | The context is enumerating God's acts; the subject of the preceding sentence is God too. The most widespread reading. |
| 2 | **The believer himself** | "Whoever believes in God has set his own heart straight." | The pronoun of the conditional particle *man* is the nearest subject; grammatically possible. |
| 3 | **Faith** | "Faith sets his heart straight." | The verbal noun understood from the verb *yuʾmin* is taken as the subject. A rare analysis. |

**All three have been defended in the classical sources and all three are grammatically possible.** I do not impose a preference.

This much I can say: the three readings come out in practice at the same place — a link is set up between faith and the heart's being set straight. The difference lies in the direction of the link: in the first the straightening comes from outside, in the second from inside, in the third from the act itself.

### What does "the heart's being set straight" mean?

The verse does something here and it needs seeing: **it does not remove the affliction.**

Look at the structure of the sentence. The first sentence said that the affliction comes by leave. The second sentence says what will be given to the one who believes — and what is given is not the lifting of the affliction but **the setting straight of the heart**.

That is, the promise is not that the event will change; it is that the person standing in the event will change.

**This distinction is decisive for the sūra's understanding of morality, and it joins with the finding recorded in `094-insirah.md`.** There, when the difference between *maʿa* (with) and *baʿda* (after) was treated, the following was said, and I do not repeat it, I recall it: *"To know that a hardship 'will pass' teaches a person to wait… To know that there is 'something inside' a hardship teaches him to look."*

Al-Taghābun 64/11 sets up the same structure: it is not said that the affliction will be lifted, it is said that the heart inside the affliction will be set straight. What changes is not the outside.

**قَلْب** — root **ق ل ب**: **to turn, to turn upside down, to revolve.** *Inqilāb* (overturning), *taqlīb* (turning over), *mutaqallib* (that which turns) are from the same root. The very name of the thing conveys instability: the heart is **what turns**.

And this makes the verse's request meaningful. What is asked for a turning thing is that it be **set straight** — *yahdi*, that is, that it be given a direction. Not that it be fixed in place; that it be directed.

**The Qurʾān explained by the Qurʾān.** Another verse directly connected with this one gives the words to be said in the face of affliction:

> **ٱلَّذِينَ إِذَآ أَصَٰبَتْهُم مُّصِيبَةٌ قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لِلَّهِ وَإِنَّآ إِلَيْهِ رَٰجِعُونَ**
> *"Those who, when an affliction strikes them, say: 'We belong to God, and to Him we return.'"* (al-Baqara 2/156)

That verse falls inside the section treated in `002-bakara.md`. And note: there too the verb is **أَصَابَ** and the noun **مُصِيبَة**. The same root, the same structure.

The division of labour between the two verses is this: al-Taghābun states the **source** of the affliction (leave), al-Baqara **what is to be said** in the face of affliction. One is an explanation, the other a response.

## 64/12-13 — وَأَطِيعُوا اللَّهَ وَأَطِيعُوا الرَّسُولَ فَإِن تَوَلَّيْتُمْ فَإِنَّمَا عَلَىٰ رَسُولِنَا الْبَلَاغُ الْمُبِينُ • اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ وَعَلَى اللَّهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ

*Wa aṭīʿu'llāha wa aṭīʿu'l-rasūl, fa-in tawallaytum fa-innamā ʿalā rasūlina'l-balāghu'l-mubīn • Allāhu lā ilāha illā hū, wa ʿala'llāhi fa'l-yatawakkali'l-muʾminūn*

"Obey God, obey the Messenger. If you turn away, our Messenger's duty is only clear conveying. • God — there is no god but He. And let the believers rely on God alone."

I take the two verses together, because the two complete a single movement: the limiting of responsibility and the showing of what one leans on.

### Two *aṭīʿū* — the repetition of the verb

The verse does not say "obey God and His Messenger". It builds the verb **twice**: *aṭīʿu'llāha* **wa** *aṭīʿu'l-rasūl.*

In Arabic two objects could have been joined to a single verb. The repetition of the verb is counted in classical grammar a structure showing that each of the two obediences is wanted **in its own right**.

Only here a reservation must be entered: the Qurʾān frames the same call in some places with a single verb as well. That is, the repetition is not a rule but an emphasis. I draw no firm conclusion from this about the relation between the two obediences — that is a separate debate in the literature of legal theory and is not the subject of this commentary.

**أَطَاعَ** — root **ط و ع**: to do a thing **willingly and with ease**. And the root's opposite is interesting: *karh* (by force, unwillingly). The Qurʾān uses the two side by side: *ṭawʿan aw karhan* — willingly or unwillingly.

That is, inside the word *obedience* there is **consent**. Not submitting the neck; complying voluntarily. The English word "obedience" has largely lost this side.

From the same root come *taṭawwuʿ* (work done voluntarily and over and above) and *istiṭāʿa* (being able) — and *istiṭāʿa* will be the key word of the sixteenth verse: *ma'staṭaʿtum*. The root works four times in the sūra and in two different directions: here **complying**, there **being able**.

### فَإِنَّمَا عَلَىٰ رَسُولِنَا ٱلْبَلَٰغُ ٱلْمُبِينُ — the limit of responsibility

**إِنَّمَا** — a particle of restriction: "only, nothing but". It locks the ruling of the sentence onto a single thing.

**ٱلْبَلَٰغ** — root **ب ل غ**: **to reach a place, to arrive, to attain.** *Bulūgh* (reaching maturity), *balīgh* (one who brings his speech to its target), *balāgha* are from the same root. And *bālighu amrih* (al-Ṭalāq 65/3), which will be treated in `065-talak.md`, is from the same root too.

*Balāgh* is the **delivering** of a message. And there is an end point inside the word: delivering ends when it has arrived.

**ٱلْمُبِين** — root **ب ي ن**; analysed in `098-beyyine.md`. *Mubīn* can mean both "manifest" and "making manifest" (the verb *abāna* is both intransitive and transitive). Both hold here: the conveying is both clear and clarifying.

**And what the sentence does is this: the job description of the messenger is given, and the description is kept narrow.**

What falls to the messenger: delivering.
What does not fall to the messenger: securing the result.

This is the same as the finding recorded in `071-nuh.md`, where it was shown to be engraved into the grammar of that sūra: *"the sentence of judgement does not come out of Noah's mouth. Noah tells, asks, prays; but it is not he who frames the sentence 'they were drowned'. The boundary between messenger and judgement is engraved into the grammar of the sūra."*

Al-Taghābun states the same boundary in one sentence.

**And the side of this boundary that bears on the present is direct** — I write it as my own reading: does the responsibility of a person who tells something end with the telling, or does it continue until the other side is convinced? The verse says the first. The practical consequence of this is both a relief and a reservation: a relief, because you are not responsible for the outcome; a reservation, because there is the condition *mubīn* — the delivering must be **clear**. A conveying that is not understood does not count as a conveying.

### ٱللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ — what one leans on

The thirteenth verse opens with the sentence of divine unity. And this is the **only** explicit statement of unity in the sūra.

The structure: *lā ilāha illā hū.* Negation (*lā ilāha* — there is no god) and affirmation (*illā hū* — but He). The analysis of this structure was taken up in the sections `112-ihlas.md` and `109-kafirun.md`; I do not repeat it here.

**There is no conjunction in the verse.** The twelfth verse ends, the thirteenth verse begins directly with *Allāhu*. The transition between them is without a conjunction — in grammar this is called *faṣl*, and it is used when the link between two sentences is so tight that a conjunction becomes unnecessary.

The link is this: the preceding verse said "if you turn away, the messenger's duty is only conveying." That is a sentence which relieves the messenger but leaves the one addressed hanging in the air. The thirteenth verse fills the gap: **even if you turn away, the reality does not change.**

### وَعَلَى ٱللَّهِ فَلْيَتَوَكَّلِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ — reliance

**Root: و ك ل.** The concrete sense of the root is **to commit a task to another, to appoint an agent**. A *wakīl* is the person on whose behalf business is done; *wakāla* is the giving of that authority.

**The pattern: Form V (*tawakkala*).** This form conveys reflexivity and the bringing about of a state in oneself: to commit oneself to an agent.

**And the legal origin of the word protects the notion from being misunderstood.** Agency in Arabic is a term of law and has this structure:

- The principal transfers **a particular task** to the agent — not everything, a specified task.
- Agency does not mean the principal's **abandoning his own affair**; it is his transferring the **disposal** over that affair.
- And agency is meaningful only in the measure of the agent's power and trustworthiness.

That is, reliance is not giving up work; it is **accepting that the outcome is not in one's own hand**. A person does his work; he transfers the disposal over the outcome of the work.

And this sits exactly in place in the flow of the sūra. The preceding verse had limited the messenger's responsibility to conveying — that is, it had taken the outcome away from him. This verse does the same operation for the believers.

**Word order: fronting.** *Wa ʿala'llāhi fa'l-yatawakkal* — the prepositional phrase (*ʿala'llāh*) has been brought before the verb. In Arabic this conveys **restriction**: "on God alone". What we would say in English with stress, Arabic says with word order.

**The imperative form: فَلْيَتَوَكَّلْ** — a command to the third person with the *lām* of command. "Let them rely." It does not say directly "rely"; a rule is laid down by way of the third person. This turns the sentence from a form of address into a **principle**: this is what one who is a believer does.

## 64/14 — يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِنَّ مِنْ أَزْوَاجِكُمْ وَأَوْلَادِكُمْ عَدُوًّا لَّكُمْ فَاحْذَرُوهُمْ وَإِن تَعْفُوا وَتَصْفَحُوا وَتَغْفِرُوا فَإِنَّ اللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū inna min azwājikum wa awlādikum ʿaduwwan lakum fa'ḥdharūhum, wa in taʿfū wa taṣfaḥū wa taghfirū fa-inna'llāha ghafūrun raḥīm*

"O you who believe! Among your wives and your children there are enemies to you; so be on your guard against them. But if you pardon and overlook and forgive — God is forgiving, merciful."

The hardest verse of the sūra, and the one most open to abuse. And precisely for that reason it is obligatory to see **both halves** of the sentence at once. To read the verse cut off at its first half is to read not the verse but a corrupted copy of it.

### First the address: يَا أَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟

For the first time in the sūra a community is directly addressed. For thirteen verses the address has been either general ("He created you") or singular ("say"). Here the one addressed is named: **those who believe.**

And the naming is meaningful: the warning is made to those who have believed. That is, the danger described is not a danger **outside** faith; it is a situation a person of faith may meet in his own house.

### مِنْ — the most critical letter of the verse

**The verse does not say "your wives and your children are enemies to you".**

The sentence runs: *inna **min** azwājikum wa awlādikum ʿaduwwan lakum.*

The **مِنْ (*min*)** here is the letter treated in detail in `098-beyyine.md` in connection with 98/1. What was recorded there deserves repeating, because its function here is the same: ***min* in Arabic conveys the partitive: a part of a whole.** In "I ate of the bread", *min* points not to the whole loaf but to a piece of it.

That is, what the sentence reports is not "wives and children" but **"some of your wives and your children"**.

And the rule of method recorded in `098-beyyine.md` at the place where this letter occurs holds here exactly:

> *"The verse does not lay down a wholesale ruling about a group; it describes an attribute. Whoever carries that attribute is the one the verse is speaking of."*

**And one further detail of grammar reinforces this: عَدُوًّا is indefinite and singular.** Not "enemies" but "an enemy". In Arabic this conveys the genus and can imply **fewness**. The verse gives no number, but it certainly frames no generalisation either.

Without entering this reservation it is not possible to read the verse. And what the readings that fail to enter it have produced through history is plain to see.

### عَدُوّ — what does "enemy" mean?

**Root: ع د و.** And the proper sense of the root is not enmity: **to overstep the limit, to cross the boundary, to leap over a thing.**

- **عَدَا (*ʿadā*)** — he ran, he passed, he overstepped. *ʿAdw* is running — the name of Sūrat al-ʿĀdiyāt is from this root (treated in `100-adiyat.md`).
- **اِعْتَدَى (*iʿtadā*)** — he overstepped the limit, he attacked. *Iʿtidāʾ* is transgression.
- **عُدْوَان (*ʿudwān*)** — enmity, overstepping the limit.
- **عَدُوّ (*ʿaduww*)** — enemy: **the one who crosses the boundary, who comes in between and does harm.**

**This sense of the root determines how the verse is read.** *ʿAduww* does not mean "one who hates you"; it means **"one who oversteps your boundary, who comes in between in a way that harms you"**. Hatred is not required. A person may overstep your boundary while loving you very much.

And in the context of the family this is exactly meaningful. For a wife or a child to do a person harm does not require harbouring enmity towards him. Most often the opposite is the case: **the bond itself makes crossing the boundary easier.** He asks because he loves, he blocks because he asks, he harms because he blocks.

I write this as my own reading. The sense of the root is a datum of the text; the conclusion I draw from it is interpretation.

### The report of the occasion of revelation

For this verse a group of reports of the following content is transmitted in the classical sources: in Mecca certain people became Muslims and wished to emigrate; but their wives and their children held them back, wept and pleaded, and for this reason the emigration was delayed or did not take place. Later, when they did emigrate, or when they saw what the others had learned, they wished to punish those they had left behind; the second half of the verse came to prevent this.

**I relate this report with three reservations:**

1. **It is transmitted** — I give it as a transmitted report; I am not in a position to assess its chain or its degree of soundness.
2. **The details do not agree with one another.** In different versions of the report the names of the persons and the details of the event change. This calls up the criterion recorded in `107-maun.md`: that more than one name is given for the same verse suggests that these names are answers, given after the event, to the question "who could have been meant?" For that reason **I write no names.**
3. **The rule of method holds:** *al-ʿibratu bi-ʿumūmi'l-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣi'l-sabab* — the ruling goes by the generality of the wording, not by the particularity of the occasion. The verse does not say "those who prevented the emigration"; it says "those who are enemies to you".

**But the report has one use and it should be recorded:** if this context is correct, the "enmity" the verse describes is not a wickedness but **a form of love**. A weeping child is not ill-intentioned in trying to stop a departing father. And it is precisely for this reason that the verse adds its second half: **the description "enemy" is not an accusation but a description.**

### فَٱحْذَرُوهُمْ — "be on your guard"

The imperative verb has been chosen with great care and this needs seeing.

**Root: ح ذ ر.** *Ḥadhar* is **being alert and taking precautions** against a danger. Inside the word there is neither an attack nor a rupture; there is only **attentiveness**.

The verse does not say any of these:

- *Fāriqūhum* — separate from them.
- *Ukhrujū ʿanhum* — leave them.
- *Qātilūhum* — fight them.
- *Lā tuḥibbūhum* — do not love them.

**It says: احْذَرُوا — be on your guard.**

The difference is great. *Ḥadhar* does not end the relationship; it asks for an **alertness** within the relationship. The closest English is "to be on one's guard" — to be prepared against something without letting go of it.

And the Qurʾān uses the same root elsewhere in this sense. Another derivative of the word, *ḥidhr* (precaution), occurs in Sūrat al-Nisāʾ in the context of war — and even there it does not say "flee" but "take your precautions".

**So the verse gives not a command to break off but a command to pay attention.** And the object of the command is determined too: *fa'ḥdharūhum* — the pronoun goes back to the *ʿaduwwan* at the head of the sentence, that is, to that part; not to all wives and children.

### And now the second half: وَإِن تَعْفُوا۟ وَتَصْفَحُوا۟ وَتَغْفِرُوا۟

**The balance of the verse is set up here, and this is the sūra's most delicate move.**

A warning was given. And no sooner is the warning given than, inside the same verse, with no pause at all, **three verbs of pardon** are lined up.

The easiest way to see this is by a comparison: had the warning been in one verse and the pardon in another, the two verses could have been torn apart from each other. People could have taken the first and left the second. **The sūra has made this impossible: the two are inside the same verse.**

And the verse ends not with names of threat but with **names of mercy**: *ghafūrun raḥīm*.

### Three verbs: عفو، صفح، غفر

In `002-bakara.md`, in connection with 2/109, the first two were treated. I do not repeat what was recorded there, I recall it:

> **عفو** — root *ʿ-f-w*: **to erase, to wipe out the trace.** It is used also of the wind's erasing the tracks on the sand. That is, pardon is not "I accept but I do not forget"; it is **erasing the trace**.
>
> **صفح** — root *ṣ-f-ḥ*: **page, surface.** The verb means "to turn the page", or "to turn one's face away". That is, to close the matter over and pass on.

To these a **third** is added here:

**غفر** — root **غ ف ر**: **to cover, to close over, to wrap with something protective.** And the concrete product of the root shows the word exactly: **مِغْفَر (*mighfar*)** — the armoured headpiece worn in battle. The Turkish *miğfer* (helmet) comes directly from this root. There is also *ghifāra*, a cover drawn over something.

That is, *maghfira* is the **protective** form of covering: to close a thing over so that no harm may come to it.

**And this is the third root of covering within the sūra.** In the ninth verse there was *yukaffir* (**ك ف ر**), there was *jannāt* (**ج ن ن**); here *taghfirū* (**غ ف ر**). Three separate roots, the same image: **closing over.**

The difference can be gathered in this table:

| Root | Its concrete sense | The form of covering |
|---|---|---|
| **ك ف ر** | Covering the seed with earth | Covering by burying — making invisible |
| **ج ن ن** | The trees' shading what is beneath them | Covering by enveloping — taking inside |
| **غ ف ر** | Helmet, cover | **Covering by protecting** — keeping from harm |

All three do the same act, all three with a different purpose.

### The order of the three verbs: an ascent?

The three verbs come in this order: *taʿfū*, *taṣfaḥū*, *taghfirū*.

In the classical commentaries it is said that this order conveys a climb. It is possible to read the sequence thus:

| Verb | What is done | What it looks at |
|---|---|---|
| **عفو** | Giving up punishment — erasing the trace | It looks at the **act**: the account is closed |
| **صفح** | Turning the page, turning one's face away — closing the subject | It looks at the **relationship**: the matter is not reopened |
| **غفر** | Covering over protectively | It looks at the **person**: he is protected |

On this reading the order works thus: first the punishment is lifted, then the matter is closed, and finally the person is protected. That is, pardon does not end with the lifting of the punishment; it goes on to the repair of the relationship and at last to the protection of the person.

**But I do not present this climb as a fixed rule.** In Arabic the use together of near-synonyms may be for emphasis (the style of *tarāduf*), and in that case drawing meaning from the order would be forced. The table above is a **possible reading**, not a finding.

What can be said with certainty is plainer: **all three verbs look in the same direction and none of them says "forget".** All three are things done knowingly. Pardon is not not-knowing; it is not acting although one knows.

### The balance of the verse — and the impossibility of a one-sided reading

This verse can be misused in both directions and both must be explicitly excluded.

**The first wrong reading: "the family is the enemy."** The text does not give this:

1. There is the letter **مِنْ** and it conveys the partitive: some of them.
2. *ʿAduwwan* is indefinite and singular: an instance, not a category.
3. The command is *fa'ḥdharū* — attention; not rupture, abandonment or enmity.
4. The second half of the verse lines up three verbs of pardon and ends with two names of mercy.
5. And the Qurʾān names the same relationship elsewhere as a **sign**: *"And of His signs is that He created for you mates from among yourselves, that you may find rest in them, and put love and mercy between you"* (al-Rūm 30/21).

**The second wrong reading: "then the warning can be disregarded."** This too runs against the text. The warning is real, it is explicit and it is supported by a command. The verse does not say "sometimes it happens like this"; it reports the matter reinforced with *inna*.

**The balance the verse sets up is this:** seeing and acting are two separate tasks.

And this is exactly the same as the finding recorded in `002-bakara.md` for 2/109:

> *"This distance between the severity of the diagnosis and the mildness of the command balances the general tone of the section: **seeing** what the other side does and how one is to **act** towards him are two separate tasks. Seeing does not require responding."*

**The parallel between the two verses is structural:**

| | **al-Baqara 2/109** | **al-Taghābun 64/14** |
|---|---|---|
| The diagnosis | Envy — after the truth has become clear | Enmity — overstepping the boundary |
| About whom | **Many** of the People of the Book | **Some** of the wives and children |
| The restricting expression | *kathīrun min* | *min* |
| The command | *fa'ʿfū wa'ṣfaḥū* | *fa'ḥdharūhum* + *taʿfū, taṣfaḥū, taghfirū* |
| The closing | *ḥattā yaʾtiya'llāhu bi-amrih* | *ghafūrun raḥīm* |

The two verses use the same method: **a diagnosis is made, then the response is limited.** The difference is that al-Taghābun first gives a command of precaution (*iḥdharū*) and then presents pardon as **conditional**: *wa in taʿfū* — "if you pardon". That is, pardon is not commanded; it is **encouraged**, and its return is shown.

### The framing of the return: فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

Note the logic of the sentence. The condition: if you pardon. The answer: **God is forgiving.**

The expected answer would have been "God will pardon you". The verse does not say this; it states God's **attribute**.

This is a widespread pattern in the Qurʾān and it does something stronger: it lays down the return not as a promise but as a **character**. That is, pardon is not bought in return for a transaction; it is said that the one who pardons is facing in the same direction as a Being who pardons.

**And the verb and the attribute are from the same root:** if you do *taghfirū*, He is *ghafūr*. The servant does, God **is**. The servant's act is temporary and conditional; the attribute is permanent and absolute.

This agrees with a finding recorded in `074-muddessir.md`: there, of the construct *ahlu'l-taqwā wa ahlu'l-maghfira*, the following was said — *"taqwā is the servant's work; God is worthy of it."* The same asymmetry is here too.

**And a return to the basmala.** In the introduction to the sūra I entered a note: this verse ends with *raḥīm* — with the second name of the basmala. That is, the sentence carrying the sūra's harshest warning is sealed with the name at the sūra's opening. Warning and mercy stay inside the same frame.

## 64/15 — إِنَّمَا أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَأَوْلَادُكُمْ فِتْنَةٌ وَاللَّهُ عِندَهُ أَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ

*Innamā amwālukum wa awlādukum fitna, wa'llāhu ʿindahu ajrun ʿaẓīm*

"Your properties and your children are only a trial. And with God is a great reward."

### Its difference from the preceding verse

The two verses come one after the other but they do not say the same thing. The difference needs seeing:

| | **64/14** | **64/15** |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Wives and children | **Properties** and children |
| Scope | *min* — some of them | **All** — no restriction |
| Qualification | *ʿaduww* — enemy (some of them) | *fitna* — trial (all of them) |
| Command | *fa'ḥdharū* — be on your guard | No command — **a statement of fact** |

**This is the most important difference: in the fourteenth verse there was a restricting letter, here there is none.**

And this shows that the verses say different things. The fourteenth verse reported a **possibility**: some of them may be enemies. The fifteenth verse reports a **state**: property and offspring, all of them, are a trial.

The two do not contradict each other. "Enemy" is a qualification of behaviour and does not apply to everyone; "trial" is a qualification of **position** and admits no exception. That a thing is a test does not require that thing to be bad.

### إِنَّمَا — the particle of restriction

The sentence begins with *innamā*: "only, nothing but". This particle locks the ruling onto a single thing.

But note: what does it lock? Two readings are possible:

- **"Your properties and your children are *nothing but* a trial"** — that is, they are nothing else. This reading looks severe.
- **"Your properties and your children *are indeed a trial*"** — here *innamā* is for emphasis, and the force of restriction is weak.

In classical grammar it is recorded that *innamā* does not everywhere convey full restriction and that it sometimes only emphasises. Which it is here I cannot say with certainty. The second reading seems better suited to the context — because the Qurʾān names property and offspring elsewhere as **adornment** and as **blessing** as well, so that the reading "they are nothing else" would come into tension with the Qurʾān's own usage.

This is a preference and it is not binding.

### فِتْنَة — trial

**Root: ف ت ن.** This root was fully analysed in `085-buruc.md`; I do not repeat that analysis. I recall only two items, because the verse can be understood only with them:

1. **The proper sense of the root: to put gold into the fire and melt it, thereby separating out the alloy inside it and bringing its purity to light.** The one who does this work is called *fattān*. At the centre of the root stand three elements: fire, testing, separating out.
2. **The usual English renderings of *fitna* — "sedition", "strife" — hold only one end of this range.** The centre of the field the Qurʾān uses is **testing**.

And in the table of usages in `085-buruc.md` there was this item: **being tested by blessing** — *"Your properties and your children are only a trial"* (al-Anfāl 8/28).

**Al-Anfāl 8/28 and al-Taghābun 64/15 are almost the same sentence.** The only difference is that al-Anfāl, after saying *amwālukum wa awlādukum*, ends with a different verse-ending. That the same sentence occurs in two places shows that the expression is a settled formula.

### The joining of three texts: al-Burūj, al-Jinn, al-Fajr

This verse completes two links already set up in the corpus. The three must be seen together.

**The first link — `085-buruc.md`: what the root is.** *Fitna* is the operation of testing by fire and bringing purity to light. Testing stands at the centre of the word.

**The second link — `072-cin.md` 72/17: the explicit statement that abundance is a trial.** There, the sentence *li-naftinahum fīh*, which comes immediately after the promise of abundant water, was treated and the following was recorded: *"In the preceding verse a promise was given — abundant water, blessing, ease. And immediately afterwards what that promise is is stated: an instrument of testing."*

And of the letter *fī* the following was established there: *"The testing is not something added to the abundance; it is something inside the abundance. Blessing and testing are not two separate operations but two names of a single one."*

**The third link — `089-fecr.md` 89/15-16: taking abundance for honouring.** There two inferences of the human being were recorded — when given abundance, *"My Lord has honoured me"*, when given straitness, *"My Lord has humiliated me"* — and **both** were cut off with *kallā*. In `072-cin.md` these two texts were joined thus:

| | **al-Fajr 89/15-16** | **al-Jinn 72/17** |
|---|---|---|
| Who is speaking | The human being | The revelation |
| What is said | "Abundance is an honouring" (and this is rejected) | "Abundance is a testing" (and this is established) |
| The method | Recording the human being's inference and rejecting it | Stating the correct thing directly |

**Al-Taghābun 64/15 is the third of these links — and it specifies the object.**

In Sūrat al-Jinn the instrument of testing was **water**; a general abundance. In al-Fajr it was **provision**; a general ease. Here the object is named: **property and offspring.** That is, the descent has been made from the most abstract to the most concrete.

| | **al-Burūj 85** | **al-Jinn 72/17** | **al-Fajr 89/15-16** | **al-Taghābun 64/15** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What it does | Analyses the root | States the purpose of abundance | Cuts off the human being's wrong inference | **Names the object** |
| The instrument of testing | (general) | Abundant water | Breadth or straitness of provision | **Property and offspring** |

And the four together frame this sentence: **abundance is neither an honouring nor a humiliation — it is a testing; and among the things tested are the ones a person loves most.**

**And that obligatory reservation entered in `089-fecr.md` holds here too, and I repeat it:** *"it is not being said that abundance is not an honouring."* A blessing is a blessing; the verse does not reject the blessing. What it says is **what the blessing is for.**

In the same way: **this verse does not blame having children or having property.** The Qurʾān counts both as blessings; it records prayers being made for a child (the prayers of Abraham and of Zechariah), and it says that property is a good. The only thing the verse says is the **position** of these two blessings: not a possession held in the hand but an instrument by which a measurement is taken.

### The difference between trial and enmity

Reading the two verses together brings out a distinction, and this distinction clarifies the sūra's attitude on the subject of the family. The table below is my own reading:

| | **Enemy (14)** | **Trial (15)** |
|---|---|---|
| Its source | The other party's behaviour | **The relationship itself** |
| On whom it depends | On them | **On you** |
| Its scope | Some of them | All of them |
| What is asked | Attention (*ḥadhar*) | (No command — the next verse will give the command) |

**The meaning of the difference is this:** the fourteenth verse describes something the other party may do. The fifteenth verse describes a situation in which **the other party need do nothing at all**. A child, doing nothing, merely by existing, can be a test for his father — because the testing is set up not in the child's act but in the place the father gives him.

This is the most delicate side of the verse: **a trial is not the fault of the one loved; it is the measure of the one who loves.**

### وَٱللَّهُ عِندَهُۥٓ أَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ — a comparison

The verse does not end with a statement of fact; it puts down an **alternative**.

And this belongs inside the sūra's vocabulary of trade: **أَجْر** — wage. Root **أ ج ر**: the return for a piece of work, rent, wage. *Ijāra*, *ajīr*, *mustaʾjir* are from the same root.

*Ajr*, as was recorded in `098-beyyine.md`, is a calculable return: the price of the work done. This is its difference from *fawz* (winning) in the ninth verse — *fawz* is out of all proportion, *ajr* is reckoned.

**The word order of the sentence sets up a comparison:**

- What is in your hands: *amwālukum wa awlādukum* — **yours**, beside you, visible.
- What is with Him: *ajrun ʿaẓīm* — **with Him**, far off, unseen.

**عِندَهُ** — "with Him". This adverbial conveys not so much place as **ownership and safekeeping**: standing somewhere, being kept.

And its link with the sūra's central notion is direct. *Taghābun* was coming off worse; coming off worse comes from valuing what is in hand too high and what is not in hand too low. This verse sets two things side by side and leaves the comparison of prices to the reader.

**The verse does not say which is the more valuable.** It only calls one of them *ʿaẓīm* (great). Completing the comparison is left to the reader — and according to the ninth verse, the day of those who make that comparison wrongly will be *taghābun*.

## 64/16 — فَاتَّقُوا اللَّهَ مَا اسْتَطَعْتُمْ وَاسْمَعُوا وَأَطِيعُوا وَأَنفِقُوا خَيْرًا لِّأَنفُسِكُمْ وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْمُفْلِحُونَ

*Fa'ttaqu'llāha ma'staṭaʿtum wa'smaʿū wa aṭīʿū wa anfiqū khayran li-anfusikum, wa man yūqa shuḥḥa nafsihi fa-ulāʾika humu'l-mufliḥūn*

"So guard yourselves against God as far as you are able; and hear, and obey, and spend — that is better for yourselves. And whoever is guarded against the grasping of his own soul, those are the ones who attain deliverance."

### Four commands, one chain

The fifteenth verse made a statement of fact. This verse gives what that statement requires, in four commands:

1. **ٱتَّقُوا۟** — guard yourselves
2. **ٱسْمَعُوا۟** — hear
3. **أَطِيعُوا۟** — obey
4. **أَنفِقُوا۟** — spend

The order does not look accidental: an inner attitude (guarding), a receiving (hearing), a complying (obedience), a giving (spending). From inside outwards, from attitude to property.

And the fourth binds into the sūra's framework of trade: the last command is **to spend property**. The fifteenth verse had counted property a trial; the sixteenth verse says what is to be done with property.

### مَا ٱسْتَطَعْتُمْ — "as far as you are able"

The most discussed reservation in the sūra, and perhaps in all the verses of the Qurʾān on this subject.

**Root: ط و ع.** This is the very root of the verb *aṭīʿū* in the twelfth verse — and, as was recorded at the twelfth verse, at the centre of the root stands **doing willingly and with ease**.

**The pattern: Form X (*istaṭāʿa*).** That this form conveys seeking we recorded at the sixth verse (*istaghnā*); here another of its functions is at work: to **find** a thing, to be capable of it. *Istiṭāʿa* is the state of being able.

**And the choice of the root sets up the logic of the reservation:** *ṭāʿa* (doing willingly) and *istiṭāʿa* (being able) are from the same root. That is, the verse ties a thing that is to be done willingly to the limit of what can be done. The language itself has set up the link: work done voluntarily can be voluntary only as far as one's capacity reaches.

**The *mā* in the sentence is *maṣdariyya-ẓarfiyya*:** "to the measure of what you are able / for as long as you are able". Both are defended; the difference of meaning is small.

### The link with `073-muzzemmil.md`: the ruling stretched to the measure

This reservation is the short expression of a mechanism already analysed in the corpus.

When the twentieth verse of Sūrat al-Muzzammil was treated the following was established, and I do not repeat it, I recall it:

> *"This shows how the lightening works: **the act is preserved, the measure is stretched.**"*

There the command was *wa rattili'l-qurʾān* (recite in measure); its lightened form became *fa'qraʾū mā tayassara mina'l-qurʾān* (recite what comes easily to you). The act stayed the same — reciting. What changed was how much was to be recited.

And there was one more finding there: *"The ruling changes not by a request from the human being but by the lawgiver's own movement. The human being did not bargain, did not ask leave, did not offer an excuse."*

**Al-Taghābun 64/16 performs the same operation — but with a difference.**

| | **al-Muzzammil 73/20** | **al-Taghābun 64/16** |
|---|---|---|
| Method | The full command is given first, then lightened | **The reservation is given together with the command** |
| The word of measure | *mā tayassara* — what is easy | *ma'staṭaʿtum* — what one is able for |
| Time | In two stages (the beginning / the end of the sūra) | In a single sentence |
| What it is tied to | To the situation (illness, travel, war) | To **capacity** |

The difference is this: in al-Muzzammil the lightening is lived as a **process**; here it is laid down as a **principle**. Al-Muzzammil showed the measure by applying it; al-Taghābun declares it.

And this sentence recorded in `073-muzzemmil.md` binds the two texts together: *"the adjustment of the demand to a person's condition is not declared as a rule, it is applied."* Al-Taghābun 64/16 is the place where that rule **is declared**.

### And the tension with Āl ʿImrān 3/102

There is another command in the Qurʾān concerning *taqwā*, and it appears to be in tension with this verse:

> **يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ حَقَّ تُقَاتِهِۦ**
> *"O you who believe! Guard yourselves against God as He should rightfully be guarded against."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/102)

In one place "as is His right", in another "as far as you are able". The two measures are not the same.

**This is a matter openly debated in the classical commentaries** and three main views are transmitted:

| # | View | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Al-Taghābun 64/16 has abrogated Āl ʿImrān 3/102** | A later ruling lifts an earlier one. This is the view ascribed to some of the Companions and the Successors. | The order of revelation of the two verses is not certainly known; a claim of abrogation requires it. Besides, whether there is a real contradiction between them is disputed. |
| 2 | **There is no abrogation; the second explains the first** | "Guarding oneself as is His right" already is "guarding oneself as far as one is able"; because nothing beyond his capacity is asked of anyone. The two verses say the same thing. | In that case the expression *ḥaqqa tuqātih* in 3/102 seems to lose its emphasis. |
| 3 | **The two verses look in two different directions** | 3/102 sets the **goal** (where one is to look), 64/16 determines the **obligation** (how much one is answerable for). The goal is absolute, the responsibility limited. | This distinction is not written explicitly in the text; it is an inference. |

**I do not impose a preference and I issue no ruling on the question of abrogation.** This much I can say: the third view agrees with the general principle the Qurʾān lays down elsewhere — *"God does not burden anyone beyond his capacity"* (al-Baqara 2/286). And that verse was treated in `002-bakara.md`.

This preference is not binding.

### ٱتَّقُوا۟ — a reminder

**Root: و ق ي.** This root was treated in detail in `002-bakara.md` in connection with 2/2 (*al-muttaqīn*); I do not repeat it here.

A one-sentence reminder: **taqwā is not fearing but guarding oneself**; inside the word there is a **shield** (*wiqāya* — protection). And this measure recorded there holds here too: *"The muttaqī is not the fearful one; he is the attentive one."*

This will be needed for the second half of the verse.

### وَٱسْمَعُوا۟ وَأَطِيعُوا۟ — hearing and complying

Two verbs one after the other, and their order is meaningful: **first hearing, then obedience.**

In Arabic *samiʿa* is not merely the ear's taking in a sound; it means also **to hear and accept, to lend an ear**. Indeed in the expression *samiʿa'llāhu li-man ḥamidah*, which occurs in the context of al-Fātiḥa as well, *samiʿa* means not "He heard" but "He accepted".

And this pair is mentioned in the Qurʾān together with its opposite: *samiʿnā wa aṭaʿnā* (we heard and we obeyed) and *samiʿnā wa ʿaṣaynā* (we heard and we disobeyed). The difference between the two is after the hearing — the hearing is common, the outcome different.

**In `067-mulk.md` there was an important finding about this root, and it connects here:** there the expression *law kunnā nasmaʿu **aw** naʿqilu* in al-Mulk 67/10 was treated and the following was recorded — *"not 'and' but 'or': two independent channels of deliverance, both of them closed."* That is, hearing is counted one of the channels of deliverance.

### وَأَنفِقُوا۟ خَيْرًا لِّأَنفُسِكُمْ

**أَنفِقُوا۟** — root **ن ف ق**. The concrete sense of the root is interesting: *nafaqa* is a thing's **being used up, coming to an end, going out of one's hand**. From the same root come **نَفَق** (*nafaq*) — an underground tunnel, a burrow (*nāfiqāʾ* is the field-mouse's escape hole); and **نِفَاق** (*nifāq*) — hypocrisy, that is, entering from one side and going out at the other.

That is, *infāq* is **letting property go out of one's hand**. Inside the word there is a diminishing — and the seventeenth verse will turn exactly this diminishing round.

**خَيْرًا لِّأَنفُسِكُمْ — a point of grammar.** *Khayran* is in the accusative and there are several explanations for this in grammar: the object of a deleted verb (understood as *iʾtalū khayran*), or the predicate of an omitted subject (understood as *yakun khayran*). The detail does not change the meaning here.

What does change the meaning is the expression **لِّأَنفُسِكُمْ**: "for yourselves".

This is a reservation that turns the direction of spending round. Spending is to all appearances done for another; the verse says that it belongs to **the one who does it**. And the choice of word reinforces this: *li-anfusikum* — "for your souls".

### وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ — the finest place in the verse

**And here there is a move built at the level of the word. It needs seeing.**

The verse had begun with **ٱتَّقُوا۟** — from the root **و ق ي**, imperative, active: *guard yourselves.*

The verse ends with **يُوقَ** — **the same root**, imperfect, **passive**: *if he is guarded.*

| | **ٱتَّقُوا۟** (the beginning of the verse) | **يُوقَ** (the end of the verse) |
|---|---|---|
| Root | و ق ي | و ق ي |
| Form | Imperative | Imperfect |
| Voice | **Active** — you do it | **Passive** — it is done to you |
| Against what | Against God (responsibility) | Against **the grasping of one's own soul** |
| Reservation | *ma'staṭaʿtum* — as far as you are able | (no reservation) |

**What the verse says is this:** you were commanded to guard yourselves, and you were asked to guard yourselves as far as you are able. But to be guarded against the grasping of the soul — that is something **done to you**.

This is not a detail of grammar; it is the verse's centre of meaning. A sentence that begins with a command ends with a passive verb. The human being strives; but the crossing of the hardest obstacle is given to him.

And this asymmetry appears elsewhere in the Qurʾān too. The finding recorded in `002-bakara.md` in connection with 2/37 has the same structure: *"Adam does not find the words of repentance himself; he receives them from his Lord… The door of repentance is not opened by the effort of the one who repents; it has already been left open."*

I record this as an observation. That the two verbs are from the same root and in opposite voices is a datum of the text; the conclusion I draw from it is interpretation and is not binding.

### شُحّ — miserliness?

**Root: ش ح ح.** It is usually rendered into English as "miserliness", but the word differs from *bukhl* (stinginess) and the difference is important.

The distinction the lexicographers record is this:

| Word | What it conveys |
|---|---|
| **بُخْل (*bukhl*)** | Not giving what one has — a **behaviour** |
| **شُحّ (*shuḥḥ*)** | Not giving **and** setting one's eye on what another has — a **character** |

That is, *shuḥḥ* is miserliness **joined with greed**: closing the hand and opening the eye at once. For this reason the word is more comprehensive than *bukhl* — *bukhl* looks at the act, *shuḥḥ* at the structure that produces the act.

**And the construct confirms this: شُحَّ نَفْسِهِ — "the shuḥḥ of his soul".** The miserliness is attributed not to the person but to the **soul**. That is, the verse counts this not a defect coming from outside but a tendency present in the human being's own structure.

This has a link with the analyses of the soul treated in `091-sems.md` and `092-leyl.md`; I do not repeat the findings there.

### ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ — those who attain deliverance

**Root: ف ل ح.** And the concrete sense of the root sets the word in a quite different place: **to split, to cleave open.**

*Falaḥa'l-arḍa* — he split the soil, he ploughed it. **فَلَّاح (*fallāḥ*)** — a farmer; the English word *fellah* comes from here. The farmer splits the soil, because a seed can find room only in soil that has been split.

**The pattern: Form IV, active participle (*mufliḥ*).** *Aflaḥa* — he attained deliverance, he reached what he sought.

**The root's passage from "splitting" to "deliverance"** is explained by the lexicographers thus: whoever **splits through** an obstacle reaches his goal. Deliverance is the opening of the blockage in front of one.

And this root belongs to the same family of images as the roots **ف ك ك** (untying a bond) and **ب ي ن** (opening a gap) treated in `098-beyyine.md`: in all of them there is an **opening**.

**Look at where it stands in the verse:** deliverance is given to the one guarded against the *shuḥḥ* of his soul. That is, what is split — the blockage that is opened — is **the closedness of the hand**. The concrete sense of the word and the subject of the verse fit each other exactly: the tightly closed hand is the hard soil that must be split.

I record this as an observation; I am not transmitting it from a classical commentary.

**Word order: فَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُفْلِحُونَ.** Three reinforcements one on top of another: the demonstrative (*ulāʾika*), the pronoun of separation (*hum*), and the definite article (*al-mufliḥūn*). In Arabic this triad sets up **restriction**: "those who attain deliverance are they and they alone."

## 64/17 — إِن تُقْرِضُوا اللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا يُضَاعِفْهُ لَكُمْ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ وَاللَّهُ شَكُورٌ حَلِيمٌ

*In tuqriḍu'llāha qarḍan ḥasanan yuḍāʿifhu lakum wa yaghfir lakum, wa'llāhu shakūrun ḥalīm*

"If you lend God a good loan, He will multiply it for you and forgive you. God is one who returns thanks, forbearing."

The sūra's language of trade takes here its last and boldest form.

### قَرْض — the loan, and the proper sense of the root

**Root: ق ر ض.** And the concrete sense of the root carries the whole weight of the word: **to cut, to sever, to gnaw off with the teeth.**

The concrete usages give the picture clearly:

- **مِقْرَاض (*miqrāḍ*)** — shears. A cutting instrument. It has no connection with the English "shears", but the function is the same.
- **قَارِض / قَوَارِض (*qāriḍ*)** — a rodent. An animal that severs with its teeth. In modern Arabic this word is still used for the class of rodents.
- **قَرَضَ الثَّوْبَ** — he cut the cloth.
- And the concrete sense of the root occurs openly in one place in the Qurʾān: in the narrative of the Companions of the Cave, the sun's *taqriḍu* the cave — "cutting past" it, that is, turning aside from it and moving away (al-Kahf 18/17).

**Where, then, does the sense "loan" come from?** Directly: **what is lent is a piece cut and severed from a person's property.**

That is, a *qarḍ* is a slice separated from the whole of one's property. The word pictures the act of giving as a **diminishing** — just like *infāq* (using up, going out of the hand) in the preceding verse.

And this binds the two verses together: the sixteenth verse said *anfiqū* (let it out of your hand), the seventeenth verse says *tuqriḍū* (cut and give). Both words carry the sense of diminishing. Then a third word comes and turns the diminishing round: *yuḍāʿifhu* — He multiplies it.

### The link with `002-bakara.md`: partiality

And here a finding already set up in the corpus comes into play.

When al-Baqara 2/3 was treated the following was recorded, and I do not repeat it, I recall it:

> *"The verse does not say 'they spend what We have given them', it says 'of what We have given them'. In Arabic *min* here conveys partiality: a portion of it. That is, one is not asked to give away everything."*

And the verse that sets up the balance was mentioned there too: *"Do not keep your hand chained to your neck, nor open it out altogether"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/29).

**The word *qarḍ* carries this partiality in its root.** To cut a thing is possible only if there is a whole — and what is cut is a part of the whole. Had the whole been given, it would not have been called *qarḍ*.

That is, the limit set up in `002-bakara.md` by a **letter** (*min*) is set up here by a **root**. The two verses say the same balance with two different grammatical instruments.

I write this as my own reading; the sense of the root is a datum of the text, this link between the two verses is my own inference.

### حَسَنًا — what does a "good" loan mean?

The adjective determines the quality of the loan. In the classical commentaries several contents are given to this adjective, and they do not exclude one another:

| Explanation | Its account |
|---|---|
| **Without expecting a return** | No favour claimed and no injury in the giving. The reservation *lā yutbiʿūna mā anfaqū mannan wa lā adhā* at al-Baqara 2/262-264 runs this way. |
| **From lawful property** | The source of what is given is clean. |
| **With a willing heart** | Not by force or unwillingly. |
| **From what is good** | Not the worst of what is to be let go; the reservation *wa lā tayammamu'l-khabītha minhu tunfiqūn* at al-Baqara 2/267 runs this way. |

All four are defensible and all four come out at the same place: **what is qualified is not the amount of what is given but the manner of giving.** The verse does not say "if you give much"; it says "if you give well".

### God's taking a loan — how is this expression to be understood?

This is the side of the verse most dwelt on, and it requires speaking carefully.

The sentence runs: *in tuqriḍu'llāha* — "if you lend God". That is, God is shown as the borrowing party.

**At first sight this is strange.** The sixth verse of the sūra had just said *wa'llāhu ghaniyyun ḥamīd* — "God is without need", that is, He needs nothing at all. Why should one who has no need borrow?

**This question must be answered by saying that the expression is a figure of rhetoric** — and this must be done without exaggeration.

The relation of lending has three properties, and the expression loads all three at once:

**One. In a loan ownership is not transferred.** The property lent remains the lender's; it will be taken back. In a gift it is not so. That is, the expression says that what is given **is not lost**.

**Two. In a loan repayment is a debt.** A gift is without return; a loan creates an obligation. The expression positions the return not as a promise but as a **debt**.

**Three. A loan raises the position of the giver.** The lending party stands in a superior position relative to the borrowing party — the borrower is the one in need. This is the most striking thing the expression does: **it puts the human being in the position of creditor.**

And this third is the source of the whole effect of the verse. The Qurʾān presents the alms to be given not as a favour or a tax but as **a debt of which one is the creditor**.

**But here one must stop and enter a reservation.** No divine need can be inferred from this expression; the sūra's own sixth verse blocks it. The expression is a **manner of speech** describing the position of the giver. How such expressions in the Qurʾān — hand, face, coming, sitting — are to be understood is a separate debate in the literature of *kalām* and I do not carry it here.

What I shall record is narrower and tied to the text: **the expression is itself the method of encouragement.** And the encouragement is done not by frightening but **by raising position**. This agrees with a finding recorded in `071-nuh.md`: there it was shown that Noah's proposal was framed *"not by frightening but by gain"*.

### يُضَٰعِفْهُ — multiplying

**Root: ض ع ف.** And the root has an interesting side: the same root means both **weakness** (*ḍaʿf*) and **doubling** (*ḍiʿf*).

The lexicographers explain this thus: *ḍiʿf* is a thing's **like**, that is, another as much as itself. When two things are put one on top of the other they "double" each other. Weakness, on the other hand, is power's being divided and broken up. The common ground is the idea of **splitting in two / doubling**. I relate this link of derivation as the lexicographers record it; I do not claim it is certain.

**The pattern: Form III (*ḍāʿafa*).** This form normally conveys reciprocity; here it is in the sense of intensity and repetition — it is used close in meaning to *ḍaʿʿafa* in Form II.

**And the amount is not stated.** The verse does not say "He doubles it"; it says "He multiplies it". The amount has been left open.

Elsewhere the Qurʾān gives a number for this multiplying — the likeness of a single grain giving seven ears and each ear holding a hundred grains occurs at al-Baqara 2/261, and there too the number is left open by saying *wa'llāhu yuḍāʿifu li-man yashāʾ*. That is, even where a number is given, the upper end is left open.

**The meaning of this within the framework of trade is this:** this is not a fair exchange. In a fair exchange the return equals the value of what is given. Here the return is **out of all proportion**.

And this completes the idea of *taghābun* in the ninth verse: coming off worse is a transaction in which what is given and what is taken are not equal. Here the verse sets up **a deliberate inequality** — but in the opposite direction. That is, at the end of the sūra a transaction is shown that is the contrary of coming off worse: an exchange in which the giver gains.

I write this as my own reading.

### وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ — the second return

The return for the loan has two items: **multiplying** and **forgiveness**.

The second is unexpected. In a relation of debt the debtor pays the principal and any increase there may be; he does not **forgive the creditor's sins**. Here the verse goes beyond the framework of the relation.

The root **غفر** was treated at the fourteenth verse (helmet, protective cover); I do not repeat it here. Only one link should be made: in the fourteenth verse it was the **servant** who forgave (*taghfirū*) and in return it was said that God is *ghafūr*. Here the servant **gives** and in return is forgiven.

The two verses tie the same root to two different acts: forgiving and giving. Both are met with forgiveness.

### وَٱللَّهُ شَكُورٌ حَلِيمٌ — two names

The most striking of the sūra's verse-endings.

**شَكُور** — root **ش ك ر**. And the word's being used of God requires an explanation, because *thanks* is normally the attitude shown **by the lower to the higher**.

In `076-insan.md`, when this root was treated, it was recorded that it occurs within that sūra in three forms: *shākiran* (3), *shakūrā* (9), *mashkūrā* (22) — and this was established: *"It turns the direction of thanks about: asked of the human being → not asked of the human being → given to the human being."*

The pattern **شَكُور** conveys intensity. Used of God, the lexicographers give it this sense: **the one who accepts little as much, who gives a great return for a small good.** That is, *shakūr* is not the one who is grateful; He is **the one who gives back more than was given**.

And the verse-ending overlaps exactly with the content of the verse: the verse spoke of multiplying, the verse-ending gives the name of the one who multiplies.

**حَلِيم** — root **ح ل م**: not to hurry, not to grow angry, not to answer at once. *Ḥilm* is being able to defer the punishment while having the power. The word is counted as of the same root as *ḥulm* (dream, puberty), another branch of **ح ل م**; the lexicographers do not establish the link firmly, and neither do I.

**The coming of the two names together sets up a balance:**

- *Shakūr* — He returns good for good, and more than was given.
- *Ḥalīm* — He is **not in haste** to return evil for evil.

That is, the same attitude in both directions: to good at once and abundantly, to evil late and in measure. The verse-ending closes the sūra's whole language of reckoning with a description of temperament.

And the placing of the name *ḥalīm* here seems also to bear on the matter of the loan: for those who do not lend, no threat comes. The sūra encourages; it does not punish.

## 64/18 — عَالِمُ الْغَيْبِ وَالشَّهَادَةِ الْعَزِيزُ الْحَكِيمُ

*ʿĀlimu'l-ghaybi wa'l-shahādati'l-ʿazīzu'l-ḥakīm*

"The knower of the unseen and the seen; the mighty, the wise."

### A seal of three words

The sūra ends not with a command but with a **list of names**. And the list gathers up and seals the attributes introduced in the first four verses of the sūra.

### عَالِمُ ٱلْغَيْبِ وَٱلشَّهَٰدَةِ

**ٱلْغَيْب** — root **غ ي ب**: **to disappear from sight, to be covered, not to be present.** *Ghāba* — it set, it disappeared; *ghaybūba*, absence. This root was treated in `002-bakara.md` in connection with 2/3 (*yuʾminūna bi'l-ghayb*); I do not repeat it here.

**ٱلشَّهَٰدَة** — root **ش ه د**: **to be present, to see, to bear witness.** *Shāhid* is the one present; *mashhad* the place seen; *shahāda* the reporting of what one has seen.

**The two words are exact opposites:** one is what is not present, the other what is present. In Arabic setting two opposites side by side (*ṭibāq*) covers the whole — to say "hidden and open" is to say "all of it".

And this construct matches the fourth verse of the sūra exactly:

| **64/4** | **64/18** |
|---|---|
| *mā tusirrūna* — what you conceal | *al-ghayb* — what is unseen |
| *mā tuʿlinūn* — what you make public | *al-shahāda* — what is seen |
| *ʿalīmun bi-dhāti'l-ṣudūr* | *ʿālimu'l-ghaybi wa'l-shahāda* |

The difference is one of scale. In the fourth verse the hidden and the open were **a person's acts**; here they are the whole of existence. The sūra applies the same duality first to the human being, then to the universe.

### And this seal is what makes the ninth verse possible

**The tightest internal link of the sūra is here.**

*Taghābun* — coming off worse — can be established only on one condition: **that the true price be known.** You realise that you came off worse in a transaction only when you learn the real value of the goods. One who does not know the price does not know that he came off worse either.

And the ninth verse had declared that day "the day on which the loss is understood". For this to be possible, there must be, on that day, someone who knows the true price.

**The eighteenth verse names that one:** the knower of both the unseen and the seen.

That is, the last verse of the sūra is the **technical precondition** of the claim that carries the sūra's name. The reckoning of a loss in a bargain can be kept only by one who sees both sides.

I write this as my own reading. This relation between the two verses has been drawn from the structure of the text; I am not transmitting it from a classical commentary.

**And thought of within the metaphor of exchange, this detail falls into place too:** in a transaction a person sees only the *shahāda* — the visible price, what can be handled, what can be counted. The *ghayb* side is closed to him. This is exactly the source of the loss described in the ninth verse: **setting a price with incomplete information.**

### ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

**ٱلْعَزِيز** — root **ع ز ز**. The concrete sense of the root is **hardness, firmness, not wearing away**. *Arḍ ʿazāz* — hard, barren ground. From this two senses come out: **to prevail** (*ʿizza*) and **to be rare, hard to come by** (*ʿazīz* — a thing's being seldom found).

That is, *ʿazīz* is not the exact equivalent of the word "powerful". Inside it there are **unbreakability and rarity**: the one not overcome, not worn away, and whose like is seldom found.

**ٱلْحَكِيم** — root **ح ك م**: the concrete sense of the root is **to restrain, to bridle**; *ḥakama* is a horse's bridle — the instrument that keeps the animal on the right road. From this come *ḥukm* (a binding decision), *ḥikma* (putting a thing in its proper place), *ḥākim*.

*Ḥakīm* can mean both "the one who judges" and "the possessor of wisdom"; the *faʿīl* pattern is open to both.

**The coming of the two names together sets up a balance for the sūra:**

- *ʿAzīz* by itself: irresistible power. This is a picture open to arbitrariness.
- With *ḥakīm* added: power works together with a measure that puts things in their proper place.

And this pair is the counterpart at the sūra's end of the pair *lahu'l-mulku wa lahu'l-ḥamd* in the first verse: there too power (dominion) and worth (praise) were set side by side. The sūra sets up the same balance twice, at its opening and at its close.

**And one last detail: the sūra does not end with a command.** The seventeenth verse was a conditional sentence; the eighteenth verse is names only. That is, the sūra's last word is not a demand but an **introduction**. Everything that is asked has been said; what remains is before whom it is done.

## The weave of the sūra

The words that look scattered through the sūra form, when one looks back, several families. I can distinguish four.

### The first family: the roots of COVERING

Three separate roots in the sūra carry the same image, and all three attach to different subjects.

| Root | Word | Verse | Who covers | What | Ruling |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ك ف ر** | *kāfirun* | 2 | **The human being** | The truth he has seen | Blamed |
| **ك ف ر** | *yukaffir* | 9 | **God** | The servant's evil deeds | A favour |
| **ج ن ن** | *jannāt* | 9 | (A place) | Those who enter | A reward |
| **غ ف ر** | *taghfirū* | 14 | **The servant** | His kinsman's fault | Encouraged |
| **غ ف ر** | *ghafūr* | 14 | **God** | (An attribute) | — |
| **غ ف ر** | *yaghfir* | 17 | **God** | The giver's fault | A return |

**What the weave says:** the same act — closing something over — takes entirely opposite rulings according to subject and object. To cover the truth over is denial; to cover a fault over is forgiveness.

And the sūra builds a bridge between these two ends: in the fourteenth verse it is **the servant** who is asked to cover. That is, the servant is positioned as a being capable of the second kind of covering.

I record this as my own reading; the roots are a datum of the text, this classification among them is my own inference.

### The second family: the words of TRADE

The word carrying the sūra's name was a term of exchange. And it is not alone:

| Word | Verse | Its counterpart in trade | Its direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ٱلْمُلْك** | 1 | Ownership | Whose it is |
| **ٱلتَّغَابُن** | 9 | Coming off worse | The name of the loss |
| **ٱلْفَوْز** | 9 | Winning | The name of the gain |
| **أَجْر** | 15 | Wage | The return for labour |
| **أَنفِقُوا۟** | 16 | Spending | Letting go out of hand |
| **شُحّ** | 16 | Holding on | Not giving |
| **قَرْض** | 17 | Loan | Cutting and giving |
| **يُضَٰعِفْ** | 17 | Multiplying | The increase of the principal |

Eight words, in five separate verses. And note the ordering: the words cluster in the **second half** of the sūra. In the first eight verses there is only *al-mulk*.

That is, the sūra opens its language of trade **after** it has named the Day of Resurrection. First the day is named, then how that day's ledger is kept is described.

### The third family: the verse-endings of KNOWING

The verse-endings of the sūra set up a series of knowing:

| Verse | Verse-ending | The form of knowing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *qadīr* | (Power — not knowing) |
| 2 | *baṣīr* | By seeing — from outside |
| 4 | *ʿalīm bi-dhāti'l-ṣudūr* | By looking inward |
| 8 | *khabīr* | Aware of the inner face |
| 11 | *ʿalīm* (bi-kulli shayʾ) | By encompassing |
| 18 | *ʿālimu'l-ghaybi wa'l-shahāda* | **Both fields at once** |

Five of the six verse-endings are about knowing. And the last gathers up the others.

Its link with the sūra's central notion is direct: coming off worse arises from an **asymmetry of information**. If in a transaction one of the parties knows more than the other, the other comes off worse. The attributes of knowing repeated throughout the sūra say that on that day the asymmetry will be gone.

### The fourth family: FAMILY and PROPERTY

The last four verses of the sūra stay within a single field:

- **14** — wives and children: the possibility of enmity, and pardon
- **15** — properties and children: trial
- **16** — guarding oneself, hearing, obeying, spending; and *shuḥḥ*
- **17** — the loan and the multiplying

And these four verses are the **field of application** of the great frame set up in the sūra's first thirteen verses.

**The move the sūra makes is this:** having named the Day of Resurrection "coming off worse", it says **with what material** the coming off worse is done. And the material is not far away: wife, child, property.

That is, the sūra ties the great reckoning to small objects. This is the same as a method recorded in `067-mulk.md`: there too the sūra came down from a cosmic claim to a bucket of water.

### And a sequence of one word: و ل د

The word **أَوْلَاد** occurs twice in the sūra: at 14 and 15. And in both verses it is paired with another word:

- **14:** *azwāj* + *awlād* → *ʿaduww* (some of them)
- **15:** *amwāl* + *awlād* → *fitna* (all of them)

Children are in both lists; their partners change. Once they are paired with the spouse, once with property.

This joins with the verse al-Ḥadīd 57/20 mentioned in `102-tekasur.md` — there, within the description of the life of this world, *takāthurun fi'l-amwāli wa'l-awlād* occurred, and in `102-tekasur.md` the following was recorded: *"the verse says something Sūrat al-Takāthur does not say: the abundance of what. 'In properties and in children.'"*

**Al-Taghābun 64/15 uses the same pair** — *amwālukum wa awlādukum* — but this time it speaks not of a contest in abundance but of a testing. The same two objects, in two different sūras, the subject of two different operations.

## What this says to the present

**One. Coming off worse is different from being robbed.**

The name of the sūra is not a word of victimhood. *Ghabn* is the state of a person who entered the transaction of his own free will and calculated the price wrongly.

The side of this that bears on the present produces a direct question: **what are we exchanging for what, and who set the price?**

What a person gives his time, his attention, his health, the years he would have spent with those close to him — this is mostly not an explicit decision. Nobody says "I am selling ten years of my life for this". The exchange is made through single small choices, and the sum of them becomes visible only afterwards.

The **delay** the word *ghabn* carries is exactly here: the one who comes off worse does not know at the time of the transaction that he is coming off worse. Had he known, he would not have done it.

What the verse does is not an accusation but **an invitation to take stock**: ask the price now.

**Two. Testing and punishment are not the same thing.**

The fifteenth verse counts property and offspring a *fitna*. And, as was shown in `085-buruc.md`, this word comes from the operation of purifying in fire — a testing is not an evil.

Its practical counterpart is this: **whether a thing one possesses is good or bad is determined not by what that thing is but by where that thing takes the person.**

And that two-directional cut recorded in `089-fecr.md` holds here too: abundance is neither an honouring nor a humiliation. To read one's own condition as a divine approval or a divine refusal is to put the measure in the wrong place — and whoever accepts that measure will be obliged to collapse when the reverse comes.

**Three. The balance of the verse cuts off both extreme readings about the family.**

The fourteenth verse can easily be misused today in two directions.

In one direction: the sacralising of the relationship. The thought that the family bond justifies everything, that it cannot be questioned even when it does harm. The verse cuts this off — there is a warning, and the warning is reinforced with *inna*.

In the other direction: the instrumentalising of the relationship. Counting as "obstacles" and abandoning those close to one who do not clear a person's path. The verse cuts this off too — the command is *fa'ḥdharū*, attention and not separation; and the second half of the verse lines up three verbs of pardon.

**What the verse sets up is not a middle way; it is two separate tasks.** Seeing a situation correctly and how one is to act towards it are separate questions. Today these two questions are often confused with each other: people behave as though seeing something as a problem automatically required moving away from it. The verse separates the two questions and answers the second on the side of pardon.

**Four. "As far as you are able" — and the two ways this can be used.**

The reservation of the sixteenth verse can be worked in both directions and both need seeing.

Its right direction: the obligation has been tied to capacity. A person held responsible for something he cannot do either collapses or gives up. As was shown in `073-muzzemmil.md`, the Qurʾān makes this adjustment repeatedly within its own text.

But the reservation has a second direction too, and the verse closes it: *ma'staṭaʿtum* means "as far as you are able", not "as far as you feel like". The measure is **capacity**, not preference. And where his capacity ends a person knows himself — which makes the verse both impossible to loosen and impossible to audit from outside.

In my judgement this is the real side of the verse that bears on the present: **the measure has been left to the person himself, and precisely for that reason nobody can issue a ruling about another's measure.**

**Five. The passivity of being guarded against the soul's grasping.**

The finest place in the verse was the use of the same root in two different voices: *ittaqū* (active imperative) and *yūqa* (passive).

The side of this that bears on the present has to do with a person's relation to his own character. A person can change his behaviour; but he cannot directly change the tendency that produces the behaviour. *Shuḥḥ* is not a behaviour but a structure — not giving **and** setting one's eye on what another has.

The verse accepts this and does not apply the imperative there. What is commanded is what lies outside: guard yourselves, hear, obey, spend. **Change in the structure, by contrast, is laid down as something given.**

The practical consequence of this changes the direction of the effort: a person calls himself not to an operation on his character but to repeated acts. And the name of what comes after the acts is, in the verse, *falāḥ* — the splitting of the soil.

**Six. The metaphor of the loan and the feeling of loss in giving.**

The root of the word *qarḍ* was "to cut and sever". The verse does not hide what giving makes a person feel: something is being diminished.

The side of this that bears on the present has to do with the psychology of the act of giving. That giving is hard is not a defect; the word itself accepts it. The hardness comes from what is given really having been given.

And the verse's answer is not to deny the hardness — it is **to change the name of the transaction**: what is cut off and given is not a loss but a receivable.

This must be read not as a formula of consolation but as a change of frame. The verse does not say "do not grieve, it will come back"; **it enters the accounting record in a different account.**

**Seven. The sūra does not end with a command.**

The last verse consists of three names: the knower, the mighty, the wise.

This sums up the whole method of the sūra. Over seventeen verses findings were made, warnings were given, commands were listed — and in the last word none of them was repeated. Only **before whom** one stands was stated.

And this is like the attitude recorded in `080-abese.md`: there the criticism was said and left, no apology was demanded. Here too the commands are said and the one addressed is left free. The text does not run after what it has said.
