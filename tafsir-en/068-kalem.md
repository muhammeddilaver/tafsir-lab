# 68. Sūrat al-Qalam

Fifty-two verses. Meccan. It takes its name from the word **ٱلْقَلَم** in the first verse; because of that word's first letter it is also called **Nūn**.

The sūra opens with a letter, then swears by a pen, then tells the Prophet "you are not possessed". These three steps show from the outset what the sūra is doing: **an accusation is being answered, and the answer is given by writing itself.**

In the middle of the sūra there is a narrative: a garden and its owners. Seen from outside, the narrative looks as though it cuts across the movement of the sūra — but it does not. The first section of the sūra draws the portrait of a man; the narrative tells one instance of that portrait; after the narrative the text returns to the question of the reckoning. What stands in the middle is not an interruption but an **example**.

**Let me say at the outset what the sūra is really doing, as my own reading: Sūrat al-Qalam is a dispute about reputation.** The other side takes aim at the Prophet's mind; the sūra enumerates the other side's character. And it proposes a ground on which the two may be measured: what has been written is what lasts. The sūra opens with a pen and closes with *"it is a reminder for all beings"* — that is, with the question of where the disputed word is written down.

## The skeleton of the sūra

| Section | Verses | Function |
|---|---|---|
| The oath and the first answer | 1-4 | The pen; "you are not possessed"; a reward that is not cut off; a great character |
| It will become plain who is stricken | 5-7 | Time will give the verdict |
| The offer of compromise | 8-9 | "If you would smear on the oil, they would smear too" |
| The portrait | 10-13 | Nine attributes |
| Cause and consequence | 14-16 | Wealth and sons; "tales of the ancients"; a brand on the snout |
| The narrative of the garden | 17-33 | A garden gone in a single night; "they made no exception" |
| The comparison | 34-41 | Shall We treat the Muslims like the criminals? Where are your proofs? |
| That day | 42-43 | The day the matter turns serious; the moment when prostration is impossible |
| Leave Me | 44-47 | Being drawn on by degrees; respite; "My plan is firm" |
| Patience and Jonah | 48-50 | Do not be like the man of the fish |
| The close | 51-52 | Felling with a look; "this is a reminder for all beings" |

## An observation about sound

**All fifty-two of the sūra's fifty-two verses end on a sound made through the nose: *nūn* or *mīm*.**

*Yasṭurūn, majnūn, mamnūn, ʿaẓīm, yubṣirūn, maftūn, muhtadīn, mukadhdhibīn, yudhinūn, mahīn, namīm, athīm, zanīm, banīn, awwalīn, khurṭūm, muṣbiḥīn, yastathnūn, nāʾimūn, ṣarīm, muṣbiḥīn, ṣārimīn, yatakhāfatūn, miskīn, qādirīn, ḍāllūn, maḥrūmūn, tusabbiḥūn, ẓālimīn, yatalāwamūn, ṭāghīn, rāghibūn, yaʿlamūn, naʿīm, mujrimīn, taḥkumūn, tadrusūn, takhayyarūn, taḥkumūn, zaʿīm, ṣādiqīn, yastaṭīʿūn, sālimūn, yaʿlamūn, matīn, muthqalūn, yaktubūn, makẓūm, madhmūm, ṣāliḥīn, majnūn, li'l-ʿālamīn.*

**Every one of them, without exception.** And the sūra opens with the letter **ن**.

**In the discussion of al-Baqara 2/1 the disconnected letters were treated, and this finding was set down:** the letters may stand there to display the **raw material** of the Qurʾān; a challenge is only meaningful if the material is held in common.

**This observation about sound is consistent with that finding and carries it one step further:** the opening letter of the sūra returns at every one of the sūra's verse-endings. The material is shown at the outset, then repeated fifty-two times.

**I record this as an observation; I am not building a numerical claim or any kind of letter-reckoning on it.** What I record is a countable fact: the sound structure of the verse-endings belongs to the same family of sounds as the opening letter.

## نٓ and the pattern of the disconnected letters

**The pattern established in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/1 needs recalling here,** because Sūrat al-Qalam was one of the instances of that pattern and was already named there:

> *"Looking at the verses that follow these letters, a regularity appears: most of the letter-groups are followed immediately by an expression that speaks of the Book. […] نٓ → 'By the pen and what they write in lines' (al-Qalam 68/1)."*

**So this sūra was one of the proofs of that pattern. Now, in its own discussion, it confirms it.**

And the manner of the confirmation is worth noting: in the other sūras it is the **Book** that is named after the letter (*al-kitāb*, *tanzīl al-kitāb*, *āyāt al-kitāb*). Here it is not the Book but **the instrument that makes the Book**: the pen and what is written.

**This is the pattern in its barest form, and I record it as my own reading:** in the other sūras the move is from the letter to the product (the Book); here the move is from the letter to the **tool**. Letter — pen — writing: the order is the order in which writing comes to be.

## Meccan, and the order of revelation

On the sūra's being Meccan there is something close to unanimity.

**In the widely circulated lists of the order of revelation this sūra is placed immediately after al-ʿAlaq, in second position.** The precision of these lists is disputed, and in this commentary the order of revelation is not used as a proof; but the finding recorded in the discussion of al-ʿAlaq is worth repeating here:

> *"In the widely circulated lists of the order of revelation this sūra comes immediately after al-ʿAlaq. That is, both of the first two texts of revelation speak of the pen. That is too narrow an interval to be counted a coincidence."*

**One further note is needed:** there are views that a part of the sūra (in particular the garden narrative of 17-33 and the section after it) came down later. I do not transmit claims of this kind of division, since I cannot vouch for them; I treat the sūra as a whole.

**I transmit no particular report of an occasion of revelation.** Reports that the portrait in verses 10-13 means a particular person are widespread in the books of exegesis, and several names are mentioned. **I do not write those names,** because I cannot vouch for the soundness of the reports and — more importantly — because the principle written into the method applies here directly: **what the verse describes are attributes; whoever bears the attribute is included in it.** The same principle was applied in the discussion of al-Humaza.

The basmala was treated in earlier sections; the verses are numbered without it.

## 68/1 — نٓ ۚ وَٱلْقَلَمِ وَمَا يَسْطُرُونَ

*Nūn, wa'l-qalami wa mā yasṭurūn*
"Nūn. By the pen and by what they write, line upon line."

### نٓ — the letter

**In the discussion of al-Baqara 2/1 the disconnected letters (*al-ḥurūf al-muqaṭṭaʿa*) were treated at length; I do not repeat those findings here.** In summary: the meaning of these letters is **not known with certainty**; one body of scholars refers the meaning to God, while among the views that seek an explanation the one that finds most support from within the text is the "material/challenge" account. And as was written there plainly: **no numerical reckoning is done** on these letters.

**I add here a few notes particular to this sūra.**

**One. Sūras that open with a single letter.** Three sūras in the Qurʾān open with a single letter: **Ṣād, Qāf, Nūn.** What the three have in common is that the letter is followed by an **oath** or a **description**. In al-Qalam an oath follows.

**Two. The name of the letter and the way it is written.** In the codex this verse is written with a single letter (ن), but it is read **"nūn"** — that is, the name of the letter is read. This is the general rule for the disconnected letters, and it was recorded in the discussion of al-Baqara: not *alam* but *alif-lām-mīm.*

**Three. The senses the word "nūn" carries.** In Arabic the word *nūn*, besides being the name of a letter, expresses two further things: **fish** and — according to some lexicographers — **an inkwell.** Explanations built on both senses are transmitted in the books of exegesis.

**Care is required with both:**

| Explanation | Ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Fish** | *Nūn* in Arabic means fish; and the Qurʾān calls Jonah (Yūnus) *Dhū'l-Nūn* (the man of the fish) (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/87). **And Jonah is named at the end of this sūra (68/48).** | A link within the sūra can be made; but this does not show that the meaning of the letter is "fish" |
| **Inkwell** | Being named alongside the pen makes this explanation attractive: inkwell and pen | Some lexicographers record this sense; but the sense "inkwell" for *nūn* is neither widespread nor well founded |

**I record both explanations and adopt neither.** The first has an attraction from within the sūra — the sūra opens with *nūn* and closes with *ṣāḥib al-ḥūt* (the man of the fish) — and it is possible to note that overlap as an observation. **But I do not present it as exegesis;** the note in al-Baqara, that the meaning of the letter is unknown, stands, and overlaps of this kind slide easily into forcing.

### وَٱلْقَلَمِ — the oath by the pen

**Root: ق-ل-م.** This root was treated in the discussion of al-ʿAlaq (96/4); I do not repeat it here. In summary of the finding there: the concrete sense of the root is **to cut, to whittle, to trim the ends** (*qulāma* — a paring; *taqlīm* — the cutting of nails). And the conclusion drawn from it was this: *"the pen is not 'the thing that writes'; it is 'the thing that has been whittled'. It takes its name not from its function but from its making. […] The pen is not found in nature; it is made."*

**Now I add what belongs to Sūrat al-Qalam in particular: the Qurʾān here swears by a tool.**

This is worth dwelling on. Oaths in the Qurʾān are generally sworn by elements of **nature**: the sun, the moon, the night, the morning, the fig, the olive, the star, the dawn, the horse, the mountain. All of these are **given** things; none is man-made.

**The pen, by contrast, is man-made.** A whittled reed.

**That the Qurʾān should swear by a man-made object is worth noting, and I record it as an observation.** But the observation has a limit: as was stated in the discussion of al-ʿAlaq, the verse there says that the one who **taught** this technology is likewise God. So what is sworn by is not a human invention; it is a capacity that was taught to the human being.

**And the context of the oath is meaningful in its own right.** The answer that comes in the second verse is: *"You are not possessed."*

**The link between the pen and the charge of possession is one of the finest points in the sūra.** The possessed man is one whose speech does not come from himself — he is the mouth of a jinn. The soothsayer's word is volatile: it cannot be measured, it is not recorded; **the product of the pen, by contrast, stays put, is compared, is checked.**

So the sūra answers an oral accusation on a **written** ground. **I record this as my own reading,** but that the two verses stand one after the other is a datum of the text.

### وَمَا يَسْطُرُونَ — "and what they write"

**Root: س-ط-ر.** This root was treated in the discussion of al-Muṭaffifīn (83/13) by way of *asāṭīr*; I do not repeat it here. In summary of the finding there: *saṭr* — **a line, a row, a series**; *saṭara* — he drew a line, he wrote line upon line; *misṭar* — a ruler; *musaṭṭar* — set out in lines.

**At the centre of the root stands the idea of an ordered sequence, and this explains the choice of the word.** The verse does not say *"wa mā yaktubūn"* (what they write); it says *"wa mā **yasṭurūn**"*. That is, not merely writing, but **setting into lines** — putting in order, arranging in sequence.

**The difference is this:** *kataba* is writing itself; *saṭara* is the **shaping** of writing. Scattering letters onto a page anyhow is *kitāba*; writing line upon line is *tasṭīr*.

**And this distinction sets up a contrast with the fifteenth verse of the sūra.** There the denier will call the revelation *asāṭīr al-awwalīn* — "the things the ancients **set out in lines**". The same root, in two different mouths:

| Verse | Word | Whose | What it says |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *yasṭurūn* | The owner of the text | **Swears by** what is written |
| 15 | *asāṭīr* | The denier | Belittles the revelation as **old writings** |

**The sūra exalts, in its first verse, the very root of the accusation that will be levelled against it.** In the fifteenth verse it will be said *"these are what the ancients wrote"*; the first verse says *"by what they write"*.

**I offer this as my own reading,** but that the two words share a root and stand in the same sūra is a datum of the text. A similar finding was made in the discussion of al-Muṭaffifīn, where it was recorded that three roots for writing (*kataba*, *raqama*, *saṭara*) occur together.

### Who is the subject of the verb? — a disagreement

*Yasṭurūn* is plural, third person: "they write". **But who?**

| View | Who | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The angels** | The angels who record deeds | The Qurʾān names this recording plainly elsewhere (*kirāman kātibīn* at al-Infiṭār 82/10-12) |
| **People in general** | Everyone who writes | The verb is left indefinite; and the pen is a human tool |
| **Those who hold pens, the scribes** | Those who recorded the revelation, and the people of writing generally | Its being named together with the pen |
| **Left indefinite** | The subject is deliberately not stated; the oath is by the act of writing itself | There is no subject in the sentence |

**I incline to the fourth view, and the ground is this:** the verse does not state the subject, and its not stating it does not look like an omission. What is sworn by is not *"they"* but *"mā yasṭurūn"* — **what they write.** The weight falls not on the writer but on the written.

**This is a preference, and it is not binding;** the first view too holds a strong place in the classical sources and accords with the record of the angels in al-Infiṭār.

### What this says to the present

**In the discussion of al-ʿAlaq what the pen says to the present was treated;** I do not repeat those findings here. In summary: *"Writing makes both truth and falsehood permanent. The record carries justice and slander alike."*

**Here I add a layer particular to this sūra: permanence is a ground for reckoning.**

The first verse of the sūra is an oath, the second a defence. And the ground on which the defence is made is the ground of the pen: what is said is recorded, and what is recorded is compared.

**The present-day counterpart of this is direct.** When a claim is oral, it depends on the standing of the one who makes it; when it is written, it is bound to its own content. In disputes that run on reputation, who said it matters; in disputes that run on the record, what was said matters.

The sūra opens with a pen a dispute that is being conducted over mental health — that is, a dispute aimed wholly at a person. And where it ends, in the fifty-second verse, is not whose the word is but whom it addresses: *"it is a reminder for all beings."*

## 68/2-3 — مَآ أَنتَ بِنِعْمَةِ رَبِّكَ بِمَجْنُونٍ ۝ وَإِنَّ لَكَ لَأَجْرًا غَيْرَ مَمْنُونٍ

*Mā anta bi-niʿmati rabbika bi-majnūn — wa inna laka la-ajran ghayra mamnūn*
"You, by the favour of your Lord, are not possessed. And there is indeed for you a reward that is not cut off."

### مَجْنُون — one whose mind is covered over

**Root: ج-ن-ن.** This root was treated in the discussions of al-Baqara 2/25 and al-Takwīr 81/22; I do not repeat it here. In summary: the sense of the root is **to cover, to conceal**, and all its derivatives turn about invisibility — *janīn* (covered in the womb), *jinn* (the unseen being), *majnūn* (one whose mind is covered over), *mijann* (a shield), *janna* (a garden that covers the ground with its trees).

**And the finding recorded in the discussion of al-Takwīr holds here as well:**

> *"Majnūn does not map exactly onto today's 'mentally ill'. The form of the word is a passive participle: 'made jinn-struck' — that is, one who has come under the influence of a jinn, one whose speech comes not from himself but from it."*

**This is the character of the accusation: not an insult but a claim about a source.** Not "this man is talking nonsense"; rather, "this man's speech comes to him from outside, but not from where we suppose — from the jinn."

**And this is what makes the sūra's opening with the pen meaningful** — as was recorded in the first verse.

**One more observation, as my own reading:** from the same root comes *janna*, and the subject of the narrative in the middle of the sūra is a **garden** (*al-janna*, 68/17). So the *majnūn* of the sūra's second verse and the *janna* of its seventeenth are from one root. The shared idea of the root is **covering**: the one covers the mind, the other covers the soil.

This is not a claim about wordplay; it is a consequence of the breadth of the root in Arabic. I content myself with recording it.

### بِنِعْمَةِ رَبِّكَ — the note in the middle of the sentence

**The point of word order is here.** The sentence could have been built thus: *"Mā anta bi-majnūn"* — "you are not possessed". That would have sufficed.

But a note has been inserted into the middle: **بِنِعْمَةِ رَبِّكَ** — "by the favour of your Lord".

**And this note changes the sense of the sentence.** Soundness of mind ceases to be a personal characteristic and is named a **favour**.

The verse therefore does two things at once:

1. **It rejects the accusation** — you are not possessed.
2. **It does not turn the rejection into a boast** — this is not your accomplishment; it was given to you.

**The second is the finest point in the verse.** When a person is told "you are not mad", the natural response is a defence and a certain pride. The note placed in the middle of the sentence prevents this.

**And this is consistent with the sūra as a whole, as my own reading:** the sūra is a dispute about reputation, and it will go on to enumerate the other side's faults (10-13). Immediately before such a section, the side being defended is kept from **laying claim to its own merit**.

**نِعْمَة — root ن-ع-م.** And the concrete sense of the root is **softness**. *Nāʿim* — soft, fine; *nuʿūma* — softness. From here it passed to "ease, abundance, favour". From the same root comes *naʿam* (camels, livestock) — wealth itself.

So a favour, at the root of the word, is **a lessening of life's hardness**.

### أَجْرًا غَيْرَ مَمْنُونٍ — an attribute with two senses

**Root: م-ن-ن.** And this root carries two distinct senses; both are mentioned in the classical sources for this verse and both can be defended.

**First sense — to cut, to diminish:**
*Manna'l-ḥabl* — he cut the rope, he snapped it. *Manūn* — time, *dahr* (that which cuts and consumes). On this reading *ghayra mamnūn* = **not cut off, not exhausted, not diminished.**

**Second sense — to hold a favour over someone:**
*Manna ʿalayhi* — he made his kindness felt, he threw the good he had done in the other's face. *Minna* — making the other feel indebted by recalling a kindness. On this reading *ghayra mamnūn* = **not held over one's head.**

**The two readings say two different things:**

| Reading | Sense | What it stresses |
|---|---|---|
| **Not cut off** | The reward is continuous, inexhaustible | The **amount and duration** of the reward |
| **Not held over one's head** | The reward is not given in a way that leaves one indebted | The **manner of giving** of the reward |

**I adopt no preference between the two, and the ground is this: both are genuine senses of the root, and the context sustains both.**

**But I want to add this, as my own reading:** the second sense accords especially well with the context of the opening verses. In the second verse what was given to the Prophet (the soundness of his mind) was named a **favour**. In the third verse what will be given (the reward) is named as something that **will not be held over his head**.

So two consecutive verses say two things: what you have is not from you, but you will not have it recalled to you again and again.

**The second sense has support from within the Qurʾān:** the Qurʾān plainly forbids making a charity felt as a favour — *"Do not annul your alms by holding them over people's heads and by injury"* (al-Baqara 2/264). The same root, in the same negative sense.

**And that the name *al-Mannān* comes from this root produces a tension** — the word can mean both "the one who gives much" and "the one who holds a favour over another". In the Qurʾān God's recalling of what He has given is set up not as a *minna* but as a **proof**. The difference between the two needs recording separately: holding a favour over someone puts him in debt; recalling informs him.

### What this says to the present

**The structure of the accusation "madman"** is worth dwelling on, because it has not changed.

The easiest way not to agree with a view is to argue about **the one who holds it** rather than about the view. When the person is targeted, the claim is left undefended — because the one who would defend it is busy defending himself.

And the accusation of insanity is the harshest form of this technique: the more the accused speaks, the more his condition is taken as confirmed. His defence of himself can be used as proof that he needed defending. It is a closed structure.

**The answer the sūra gives is worth noting: it rejects the accusation and leaves the dispute to time** (verses 5-7). That is, the way out of a closed structure is not to argue inside it but to wait for the outcome.

**A second aspect: the idea of a reward not held over one's head.**

The hardest part of help between people is what comes after it. What has been given goes on standing as a debt even after it is given; and the recalling of the debt takes away the value of what was given.

Al-Baqara 2/264 says this outright: holding a favour over someone **annuls** the charity. The giving loses its validity.

The measure the verse draws is this: what is given must not remain in the giver's hand after the moment of giving. This is a principle easy to measure in daily life — it is enough to count how many times you have mentioned what you gave.

## 68/4 — وَإِنَّكَ لَعَلَىٰ خُلُقٍ عَظِيمٍ

*Wa innaka la-ʿalā khuluqin ʿaẓīm*
"And you are indeed upon a great character."

One of the most concentrated sentences the Qurʾān speaks about the Prophet. And it rests on a single word.

### خُلُق and خَلْق — one root

**Root: خ-ل-ق.** And this root has two vocalisations and two senses in Arabic:

- **خَلْق (khalq)** — to create, creation, the form of the thing created. Outward appearance.
- **خُلُق (khuluq)** — disposition, character, moral make-up. Inward structure.

**The two are from one root, and this is among the most telling kinships in Arabic.**

The concrete sense of the root is **to give form according to a measure**. The lexicographers say *khalaqa'l-adīm*: **he cut the leather to shape** — a cobbler cutting leather to a pattern. So *khalq* is not bringing into being at random; it is **cutting and shaping to a measure.**

**And now it becomes clear what the word *khuluq* means: character is a person's inward shape.**

| | **خَلْق** | **خُلُق** |
|---|---|---|
| Belongs to | The body | The self |
| Its visibility | Seen with the eye | Seen in behaviour |
| What they share | **Both are form; both have been cut to shape** | |

**The idea this kinship yields is the following, and it is an explanation the lexicographers transmit:** Arabic counts a person's character a **structure**, as his body is. As the face has a definite form, so does the disposition. Both have been "cut to shape".

**And two conclusions follow, both of which I write as my own inference:**

**One. Character is not a list of behaviours but a form.** A person's *khulq* is not the sum of the good deeds he does; it is the mould they came out of. This is why the verse does not say "you do a great deal of good"; it says **"you are upon a great character"**.

**Two. There is a continuity between creation and character.** The human body is a given form; the disposition is a form both given and acquired. The shared root does not tear the two apart.

**A caution is needed:** I do not draw from this kinship a conclusion of the kind "character is innate and unchangeable". The Qurʾān speaks of moral change, and the whole of its commanding presupposes change. What the root says is that character is **a structure**; not that the structure is fixed.

### عَلَىٰ — "upon"

**A point of word order, and easy to miss.**

The verse does not say *"innaka la-dhū khuluqin ʿaẓīm"* (you are the possessor of a great character). It uses the preposition **عَلَىٰ**: **"you are upon a great character."**

*ʿAlā* in Arabic conveys **being on top of something, resting on it, standing upon it**.

**The difference is this:** "you possess" conveys ownership — character is a thing in the person's hand. "You are upon" conveys **position** — the person stands upon his character.

**And this attaches to a finding recorded in the discussion of al-Fātiḥa:** there the expression *"ʿalā hudan min rabbihim"* (**upon** a guidance from their Lord) was compared with *"fī ḍalālin"* (**in** error) — the one a ground stood upon, the other a place fallen into.

**The same preposition does the same work here:** character is set up as a ground stood upon. The ground is underneath, and it bears.

**One more observation:** the twenty-second verse of Sūrat al-Mulk had *"ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm"* (**upon** a straight path). The same structure: a person walking upon a ground. Here the ground is named **khulq**.

### عَظِيم — great

**Root: ع-ظ-م.** And the concrete sense of the root is **bone**: *ʿaẓm* — bone. *ʿAẓīm* originally means "big-boned"; from there it passed to "great, exalted".

**This concrete root of the word says something:** greatness here is not bulk but **skeleton**. That is, the soundness of the load-bearing structure.

Joined to the sense of *khulq* as "form", this gives a coherent picture: a form, and a sound skeleton bearing that form.

**And note to whom the attribute is ascribed:** the Qurʾān mostly uses *ʿaẓīm* of such things as its own verses, the Throne, the punishment, the reward. Its use of the word for a human being's character shows the weight of the attribute.

### The function of the verse in its context

**Two verses earlier an accusation was rejected: "you are not possessed." Here the move is from the negative to the positive.**

And the logic of the move is this, as my own reading: rejecting an accusation is not enough; something must be put in its place. To say "I am not mad" is a defence; to say **"his character is sound"** is a statement of position.

**And the measure chosen is worth noting: character.**

The other side was speaking in terms of the mind. The verse does not answer in terms of the mind — it does not say "he is very intelligent". **It answers in terms of character.**

**The ground for this choice may be measurability:** whether a person's mind is sound is disputable; his character, on the other hand, can be observed. This finding was made in the discussion of al-Takwīr: *"you know him. He has been among you for forty years. […] Understanding whether a man is possessed requires no knowledge; nearness is enough."*

Sūrat al-Qalam uses the same ground but with a different word: it says he is not possessed, then says **what kind of man he is**.

**And the rest of the sūra confirms the choice:** in verses 10-13 the other side too will be described in terms of character. Nine attributes will be counted and none of them will have to do with the mind.

| | **The Prophet (68/4)** | **The other side (68/10-13)** |
|---|---|---|
| Measure | Character | Character |
| Number | **One** quality: *khuluqun ʿaẓīm* | **Nine** qualities |
| Structure | Whole — a form | In pieces — a list |

**I record this contrast as my own reading and add the following:** that one side is described in a single word and the other in nine does not look like coincidence. A sound form can be stated in one word; the defects of a corrupted form have to be counted one by one.

### The Qurʾān explained by the Qurʾān

The closest explanation of this verse occurs in Āl ʿImrān:

> *"By a mercy from God you were gentle with them. Had you been harsh and hard-hearted, they would have scattered from around you."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/159)

**Setting the two verses side by side is instructive:**

- Al-Qalam 68/4 **describes** the character: great.
- Āl ʿImrān 3/159 states the **consequence** of the character: people did not scatter.

And the verse in Āl ʿImrān repeats the note of al-Qalam 68/2 as well: *"by a mercy from God"* — that is, again it is not presented as the man's own accomplishment.

**One further link:** in the next section of Sūrat al-Qalam it will be said *"do not obey those who deny"* (8) and *"they want you to smear on the oil"* (9). The "gentleness" of Āl ʿImrān 3/159 and the "smoothing over" of al-Qalam 68/9 are different things and must not be confused — I shall take that up when I come to it.

### What this says to the present

**Setting character up as a "form" stands against an understanding of morality built on lists of behaviours.**

The attraction of a list morality is plain: it can be measured, taught, audited. Do this, do not do that. And it is necessary — there is no morality without rules.

But the list has a weakness: **it falls silent in situations outside the list.** And most of life is outside the list.

What the word *khulq* points to is the structure that produces the list. What a person will do in a situation he has not met before is known not from his list but from his form.

**This has a practical consequence:** the best way to learn about a person's character is to look at how he behaves where there is no rule. Where there is a rule, everyone can behave alike; where there is none, the form comes out.

**A second aspect: the humility the root produces.**

Since *khulq* and *khalq* are from one root, character too is a kind of creation — that is, part of it is given. A person's patience is partly a matter of temperament; so is his quickness to anger.

This does not remove responsibility — but it **makes comparison difficult.** To set two people's characters side by side and declare one superior requires knowing where each of them started. And that is not known.

And the measure of *aḥsan* treated in the discussion of al-Mulk fits exactly here: the measure is what you did with what you had.

## 68/5-7 — فَسَتُبْصِرُ وَيُبْصِرُونَ ۝ بِأَييِّكُمُ ٱلْمَفْتُونُ ۝ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ هُوَ أَعْلَمُ بِمَن ضَلَّ عَن سَبِيلِهِۦ وَهُوَ أَعْلَمُ بِٱلْمُهْتَدِينَ

*Fa-sa-tubṣiru wa yubṣirūn — bi-ayyikumu'l-maftūn — inna rabbaka huwa aʿlamu bi-man ḍalla ʿan sabīlihī wa huwa aʿlamu bi'l-muhtadīn*
"Soon you will see, and they will see: which of you it was that was stricken. Your Lord surely knows best who has strayed from His path; and He knows best those who are rightly guided."

### فَسَتُبْصِرُ وَيُبْصِرُونَ — both sides will see

**Root: ب-ص-ر.** In the discussion of al-Mulk it was treated how this root forms a backbone running through that sūra. Here the root is in another function: **the deferral of the verdict.**

**The structure of the sentence is worth noting: two verbs, two subjects, one root.**

- *fa-sa-tubṣiru* — "you will see" (singular, addressee).
- *wa yubṣirūn* — "and they will see" (plural, absent third person).

**And the verb is the same.** The act of seeing is distributed equally to both sides. There is nothing that one side will see and the other will not.

**This says how the dispute will close: no one will persuade anyone; the event will appear to both at once.**

**And the prefix س (sa-) conveys the near future.** In Arabic *sa-* is used for the near and *sawfa* for the distant future (the distinction is not absolute, but this is the general tendency). Its use here says that the waiting will not be long.

**It is worth comparing the sentence at al-Mulk 67/29:**

> *"**Soon you will know** who is in manifest error"* (al-Mulk 67/29 — *fa-sa-taʿlamūn*).

| | **al-Mulk 67/29** | **al-Qalam 68/5** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *taʿlamūn* — you will know | *tubṣiru / yubṣirūn* — you will see |
| Who | Only the addressee (plural) | **Both sides** |
| Subject | Who is in error | Who is stricken |

**The two verses do the same work: they leave the verdict to time.** The difference is that al-Qalam makes both sides partners in the act of seeing.

### بِأَييِّكُمُ ٱلْمَفْتُونُ — a construction that has to be resolved

**This expression is the most disputed place in the sūra grammatically.** And the dispute turns on a single letter: **بِ.**

**ٱلْمَفْتُون — root ف-ت-ن.** And the concrete sense of the root is very clear: *fatana'l-dhahab* — **he put the gold into the fire.** Melting a metal in fire to learn its purity. *Fitna* is first the name of that operation; then it widened to "trial", then to "disorder, collapse".

*Maftūn* is a passive participle: **put into the fire, tested** — and from there **turned in the head, stricken, driven mad.**

**Now the problem: how is *bi-ayyikum* to be resolved?**

| View | The function of the *bāʾ* | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Redundant** | Adds no meaning; it reinforces | "Which of you is the stricken one" |
| **Conveys place** | "in, among" | "**In which of you** the strickenness lies" |
| **مفتون taken as a verbal noun** | *Maftūn* here is a verbal noun meaning "madness" (like *maʿqūl*, *maysūr*) | "In which of you that madness lies" |
| **The author of the disorder is being sought** | "Because of which of you" | "Through which of you did this disorder arise" |

**All four views exist in the classical sources. The second and third are close to one another, and it is these that fit a translation best.**

**My preference is toward the second/third view,** and the ground is the context: the charge of *majnūn* occurred in the second verse; here the same charge is handed back. **It is not binding;** the fourth view too has support from the context — the sūra really is describing an atmosphere of *fitna* (division, disorder).

**Whatever the case, what the sentence does is plain: the accusation is returned, but not with an accusation — with a question.**

The verse does not say *"you are the ones who are mad"*. It says **"it will be seen in which of you it lies"**. That is, no verdict is given; it is announced that a verdict will be given.

**This is an instance of the principle recorded in the discussions of al-Ghāshiya and al-Mulk:** the authority to call to account has not been given to the messenger. And here a further step is taken — the messenger does not even accuse the other side.

### إِنَّ رَبَّكَ هُوَ أَعْلَمُ — the one to whom the verdict belongs

The seventh verse closes the question of the sixth: **He is the one who knows.**

**And the structure of the sentence is symmetrical:**

- *huwa aʿlamu bi-man ḍalla ʿan sabīlih* — He knows best the one who has strayed.
- *wa huwa aʿlamu bi'l-muhtadīn* — He knows best those who are rightly guided.

**The same expression for both sides.** No partisan sentence of the kind "He knows the one who strays, and He will save you" is built; both groups are objects of the same knowledge.

**A point of word order:** in the first half there is a **verbal clause** (*man ḍalla* — the one who strays), in the second half a **noun** (*al-muhtadīn* — those in guidance).

In classical grammar a verbal clause conveys **occurrence and renewal**, a noun **fixity**. This distinction was used in the discussions of al-Ghāshiya and al-Mulk as well.

**The reading that follows, as my own reading:** straying is named as an **act**, guidance as a **state**. Straying is something done; being in guidance is a position occupied.

This reading should not be pressed — in the Qurʾān these two words occur in other constructions too. But the choice in this verse stands in the text.

**سَبِيل — path.** In the discussion of al-Fātiḥa the words for way were distinguished: *sabīl* a particular way of going, a route; *ṣirāṭ* a broad main road. Here we have *sabīlihī* — "His way" — a construct, so a particular route.

### What this says to the present

**I think the sentence "soon you will see" contains an ethic of disputation, and I record that as my own reading.**

In a dispute, when you cannot persuade the other side there are three options: apply pressure, keep arguing, or wait for the outcome.

The sūra chooses the third. And the condition of that choice is this: **believing that the outcome will come.** The one who can wait is the one who has something to wait for.

**And the converse also holds:** if no outcome is coming, the argument must be won today. So it is not strange that a disputant who does not believe in an afterlife should be in a hurry.

**A second aspect: not returning the accusation.**

The sixth verse avoids the easy course of saying "you are the mad ones". And this is a rare stance today — the commonest answer to an accusation is to send the same accusation back.

What the sūra does is to turn the accusation into a **question**. The difference between a question and an accusation is whether or not it puts the other on the defensive.

## 68/8-9 — فَلَا تُطِعِ ٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ ۝ وَدُّوا۟ لَوْ تُدْهِنُ فَيُدْهِنُونَ

*Fa-lā tuṭiʿi'l-mukadhdhibīn — waddū law tudhinu fa-yudhinūn*
"So do not yield to those who deny. They would like you to smear on the oil, so that they might smear too."

The loveliest word in the sūra is here. And it is the word the Qurʾān chooses to describe an offer of compromise.

### فَلَا تُطِعِ — "do not yield"

**Root: ط-و-ع.** And beneath the root lies **willing compliance**. *Ṭāʿa* — he obeyed willingly; *ṭawʿan* — willingly (its opposite is *karhan* — under compulsion). The Qurʾān uses this pairing: *ṭawʿan aw karhan* — willingly or unwillingly.

**This side of the word matters: *iṭāʿa* is not something done under compulsion.** For what is done under compulsion Arabic has another name.

**And this makes the verse's warning meaningful.** The Prophet is not told "do not obey them under compulsion" — no one could compel him in any case. He is told **"do not comply willingly"**. The danger, then, is not pressure but **persuasion**.

**Note the mood of the verb: prohibition.** And prohibitions of this kind addressed to the Prophet in the Qurʾān are not an accusation; in Arabic this is a common idiom for closing off a possibility. Moreover the address covers those who follow him as well.

**ٱلْمُكَذِّبِين — those who deny.** Root *k-dh-b*. In Form II (*kadhdhaba*), intensity and continuity: not denying once but **making denial one's occupation.**

**And the description is worth noting: the verse does not say "do not obey the unbelievers", it says "do not obey those who deny".** The difference is this: *kufr* is a state of belief, *takdhīb* an **act**. What is not to be obeyed is not a body of believers but a way of behaving.

### وَدُّوا۟ لَوْ — "they would like"

**Root: و-د-د.** To love, to desire, to want. From the same root come *wudd* (affection) and the name *al-Wadūd* (the loving).

**And the fine point of the word is here: *wadda* conveys not a demand but a wish.** The verse does not say "they demanded"; it says **"they desire"**. That is, there is not even an open offer on the table; there is an expectation.

**The particle لَوْ** here adds the tone of "if only": *waddū law* — "they wish that it might be so."

**This catches the real form of offers of compromise, as my own reading:** such offers are generally not made openly. No one says "give up that clause"; an atmosphere of expectation is created, and the other side is expected to soften of its own accord.

### تُدْهِنُ — to smear on oil

**Here is the word. Root: د-ه-ن.**

**The concrete sense of the root: oil.** *Duhn* — oil, especially oil rubbed onto hair and skin. *Dihān* — oil; it occurs in the Qurʾān (the expression at al-Raḥmān 55/37 that the sky will be "like oil" is built with a word from this root). *Mudhin* — one who oils.

**And *idhān* (Form IV): to smear on oil, to grease — and figuratively: to go soft, to compromise, to slacken, to look the other way.**

**How this figure is built has to be seen, because the whole force of the word is there.**

**What does oil do?**

1. **It reduces friction.** Two oiled surfaces do not catch on each other; they slide.
2. **It hides roughness.** An oiled surface shines; scratches and pores become invisible.
3. **It stays on the surface.** Oil does not penetrate; it sits on top.
4. **It is temporary.** It wipes off, it runs, it goes.

**All four work exactly for "accommodation":**

| Property of oil | Its counterpart in accommodation |
|---|---|
| Reduces friction | The disagreement is smoothed; the conflict lifts |
| Hides roughness | **The difference does not disappear; it becomes invisible** |
| Stays on the surface | The agreement concerns the relationship, not the content |
| Is temporary | Once the oil is wiped away the difference reappears |

**The second row is the heart of the word.** *Idhān* is not the abandoning of a view. The man who smears on oil does not remove the scratch beneath; he **makes it invisible.**

**So the verse describes not a demand for a change of mind but a demand for an *appearance*.** What is wanted is not that the Prophet give up his belief; it is that **the difference cease to be visible.**

**This finding is confirmed by the second half of the verse:** *tudhinu **fa**-yudhinūn* — "if you smear on the oil, they will smear too."

**That is, the other side will do the same thing.** No one changes belief; both sides oil.

### فَيُدْهِنُونَ — oiling on both sides

**The conjunction فَ conveys cause and effect:** if you do it, they will do it too.

And this is where the offer looks fair: **it is mutual.** No one-sided concession is asked; both sides will soften.

**And it is precisely for this reason that it is refused.** The verse says that mutuality does not make the offer legitimate.

**Why? — I write this as my own reading:**

In mutual oiling neither side appears to lose anything. But something is lost: **the difference itself.**

If the difference between two sides is a difference of opinion, hiding it harms neither. But if the difference is a **claim to truth**, hiding it annuls the claim to truth. For the meaning of a claim to truth is precisely the maintaining of the difference.

**This matter was treated in its sharpest form in Sūrat al-Kāfirūn,** and the finding there holds here: that sūra rejects an offer of compromise, and in rejecting it leaves the other side a space of its own — *"to you your religion, and to me mine."*

**The difference between the two sūras is worth recording:**

| | **al-Kāfirūn 109** | **al-Qalam 68/9** |
|---|---|---|
| What is refused | An offer of shared worship | Mutual softening |
| The form of the refusal | The boundary drawn openly | "Do not obey them" |
| The result | The two domains stay separate | The difference stays visible |

**Al-Kāfirūn declares the distinction; al-Qalam prevents the distinction from being rubbed out.** The two are two faces of one work.

### The difference between gentleness and smoothing over

**There is a possible misunderstanding here and it needs closing off.**

The verse is not criticising **gentleness of temper**. Four verses earlier the Prophet's character was praised; and in Āl ʿImrān 3/159 one component of that character was plainly gentleness: *"By a mercy from God you were gentle with them."*

**So where does the difference lie?**

| | **لِنْت (Āl ʿImrān 3/159)** | **إدهان (al-Qalam 68/9)** |
|---|---|---|
| Softening towards what | **Persons** | **The claim** |
| What changes | Manner | Content |
| Its aim | To preserve the relationship | To avoid conflict |
| Its result | The message arrives | **The message is blurred** |

**The distinction is this, and I write it as my own reading: gentleness belongs to manner, oiling to content.**

Treating a person courteously and not telling him what he needs to be told are two different things. The first is character; the second is *idhān*.

And the two are easily confused — because they look the same: the voice drops, the tension eases, the atmosphere relaxes.

**The difference shows up afterwards:** what was said gently has been said; what was slid past with oil has not.

### What this says to the present

**This word is, for today, the busiest place in the sūra.**

*Idhān* is the name of one of modern life's most widespread social skills: **managing disagreement.** And in most places this skill counts as a virtue — accommodating, flexible, constructive, diplomatic.

**The verse is not against the skill itself; it is against where it is used.**

Softening an unnecessary argument in a meeting is useful. Reducing friction in a family is good. These are not *idhān*; they are good sense.

***Idhān* is the oiling of a claim to truth.** That is, knowing that something is wrong and, so as not to spoil the atmosphere, leaving the impression that it is not wrong.

**Its concrete forms:**

**At work.** Seeing an error and, to protect relationships, saying "that too is an approach". The sentence is not a lie; but the difference has been rubbed out.

**In public debate.** Where one of two views is plainly baseless, saying "there are valid points on both sides". This sentence, which looks like balance, conceals an imbalance.

**In religious life.** Coming to the point of not stating a ruling so as not to hurt the person addressed — and taking this for courtesy.

**And the finest point of the verse is that the offer is mutual.** *Fa-yudhinūn* — they will oil too. So the offer is not presented as a loss; it is presented as a **gain**. Both sides will be comfortable.

**Comfort is not itself a proof.** This is what the sūra says.

**One further boundary must be drawn:** no conclusion of the kind "there is to be no compromise on anything" follows from this verse. The Qurʾān commands agreement, peace and forbearance many times over. What is refused is not compromise but **the concealment of the difference.** Agreeing on a matter and pretending that a disagreement on a matter does not exist are two different things.

## 68/10-13 — وَلَا تُطِعْ كُلَّ حَلَّافٍ مَّهِينٍ ۝ هَمَّازٍ مَّشَّآءٍۭ بِنَمِيمٍ ۝ مَّنَّاعٍ لِّلْخَيْرِ مُعْتَدٍ أَثِيمٍ ۝ عُتُلٍّۭ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ زَنِيمٍ

*Wa lā tuṭiʿ kulla ḥallāfin mahīn — hammāzin mashshāʾin bi-namīm — mannāʿin li'l-khayri muʿtadin athīm — ʿutullin baʿda dhālika zanīm*
"And do not yield to any of these: the great swearer, the contemptible; the constant faultfinder, the one who goes about carrying tales; the withholder of good, the aggressor, the sinner; the coarse, and beyond that the base-born."

**The most concentrated portrait of a person in the Qurʾān.** Nine attributes, four verses, a single breath.

### The structure of the portrait

**First the construction of the sentence has to be looked at.** In the eighth verse it was said *"do not obey those who deny"* — plural. In the tenth verse the text moves to the singular: **كُلَّ حَلَّافٍ** — "any great swearer".

**The word كُلّ** was treated in the discussion of al-Humaza; the note there was this: *kull* is singular in form and plural in sense; and it indicates **not a person but a type**.

**And the principle stated in the discussion of al-Humaza holds here too and needs repeating:** *"to take up the text and fit other people into the description, and to pass judgement on them, is to commit the very act the sūra describes."*

**This caution is especially necessary for this portrait,** because a list of nine attributes invites a person to fit his acquaintances into it. The sūra does not want this; **it is describing a type.**

**And all nine attributes are indefinite:** *ḥallāfin, mahīnin, hammāzin…* Not one of them carries the definite article. So it is not "that man" but "someone of this kind".

### The pattern of the attributes

**Five of the nine attributes are in the intensive form, and this determines the character of the portrait:**

| Attribute | Pattern | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| **حَلَّاف** | *faʿʿāl* | One who does much, who has made it a trade |
| **هَمَّاز** | *faʿʿāl* | One who does much |
| **مَشَّاء** | *faʿʿāl* | One who does much |
| **مَنَّاع** | *faʿʿāl* | One who does much |
| **مَهِين** | *faʿīl* | A settled quality |
| **أَثِيم** | *faʿīl* | A settled quality |
| **زَنِيم** | *faʿīl* | A settled quality |
| **مُعْتَد** | Active participle (Form VIII) | One who does the act |
| **عُتُلّ** | *fuʿull* | An adjective |

**The *faʿʿāl* pattern in Arabic conveys a trade:** *ḥaddād* (blacksmith), *najjār* (carpenter), *khabbāz* (baker). So an attribute on this pattern points to the man who does the thing **regularly**.

**The finding recorded in the discussion of al-Humaza for the *fuʿala* pattern holds here as well:** *"the pattern conveys that the behaviour has settled into the personality, has become a way of looking. Not the one who does the act, but the one who has become the act."*

**The *faʿʿāl* pattern does the same work by another route: it turns the behaviour into a trade.**

### The order of the attributes and its logic

**The most striking thing about this portrait is that the attributes are not strung together at random. The order follows a movement, and I offer this as my own reading:**

| Order | Attribute | Where it operates | Domain |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *ḥallāf* | In the mouth — oaths | **Speech** |
| 2 | *mahīn* | In the person himself | **Worth** |
| 3 | *hammāz* | Face to face / behind the back | **Speech** |
| 4 | *mashshāʾ bi-namīm* | Between two people | **Speech** |
| 5 | *mannāʿ li'l-khayr* | In what he holds in his hand | **Hand** |
| 6 | *muʿtad* | At another's boundary | **Hand** |
| 7 | *athīm* | In his own account | **Inward** |
| 8 | *ʿutull* | In his body, in his nature | **Make-up** |
| 9 | *zanīm* | In his lineage | **Origin** |

**The movement is this: it begins in speech, passes to the hand, then descends inward, and finally arrives at origin.**

That is, the portrait is built **from the outside inwards**: first what is seen (his speech), then what is done (his behaviour), then what is (his nature), and at the end where he came from (his lineage).

**A similar structure was identified in the discussion of al-Fajr** — there it was recorded that four verbs "descend from outside inwards" and this conclusion was reached: *"The Qurʾān generally builds its diagnosis in this way: it starts from the visible act and descends to the source."*

**The portrait in al-Qalam follows the same method but with a longer ladder.**

### حَلَّاف — the great swearer

**Root: ح-ل-ف.** To swear, to take an oath. *Ḥalf* — an oath; *ḥilf* — a pact, an alliance (a binding by oath). Pacts such as the *ḥilf al-fuḍūl* of the age before Islam came from this root.

**And why is this the first attribute?**

**There is a reason for swearing a great deal: one's word is not trusted.**

A man whose word is accepted has no need of oaths. The oath is a support summoned to carry a load the word cannot carry. And the more often the support is summoned, the weaker the word is understood to be.

**So the first attribute says the man's trustworthiness without saying it directly.** It does not say "liar"; it says "great swearer". The result is the same, the route finer.

**The Qurʾān makes this observation elsewhere too:** using an oath as a shield is named among the qualities of the hypocrites.

**And that this should be the portrait's first attribute connects it with the preceding verse (9):** there was a desire for compromise there. Offers of compromise generally come in words, and a word requires trustworthiness. With its first attribute the portrait says what the word of the type who brings the offer is worth. **I record this link as my own reading.**

### مَهِين — contemptible

**Root: م-ه-ن.** And the sense of the root moves back and forth between **service and lowliness**: *mihna* — trade, craft, service (the Turkish word *mihnet* is from this root); *mahīn* — lowly, worthless, feeble.

**The Qurʾān uses this word in one place very strikingly:** of the fluid from which the human being is created it says *māʾin **mahīn*** (a fluid held of no account) (al-Sajda 32/8; a similar expression at al-Mursalāt 77/20). So the word means "counted as worthless".

**And its placement here is meaningful:** the second attribute of the man who tries to gain weight by swearing is **worthlessness**. That is, the reason for the effort is stated.

**One further reading is possible, and both occur in the classical sources:**

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| **He himself is worthless** | Weak in judgement, without standing, taken seriously by no one |
| **He makes others worthless** | Forced as regards the pattern; less defensible |

**The first reading is the widespread one and suits the pattern** (*mahīn* is an adjective carrying a passive-participle sense). I prefer it; it is not binding.

### هَمَّاز — the constant faultfinder

**Root: ه-م-ز.** This root was treated in the discussion of al-Humaza (104/1); I do not repeat it here. In summary of the finding there: *"Its original sense in the lexicon is to press, to squeeze, to break, to prod. To bear down on something and crush it. The name of the letter hamza in the Arabic alphabet also comes from here."*

**And this verse was already named in the discussion of al-Humaza:** *"'The great faultfinder, the carrier of tales' (al-Qalam 68/11). Hammāz — again in the intensive pattern, from the same root, and again within the portrait of a person."*

**Now I make good on that note.** Two sūras name the same act in two different patterns:

| | **al-Humaza 104/1** | **al-Qalam 68/11** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | *humaza* | *hammāz* |
| Pattern | *fuʿala* | *faʿʿāl* |
| What the pattern conveys | Become a disposition | Become a trade |
| What stands beside it | *lumaza* (the fault-finder) | *mashshāʾ bi-namīm* (the tale-carrier) |

**Both are intensive patterns and both say the same thing.** The difference lies in the second attribute set beside each: in al-Humaza another form of belittling (*lamz*), in al-Qalam the **carrying** of belittlement (*namīma*).

**So al-Humaza widens the belittling, while al-Qalam sets it in motion.**

And the conclusion recorded in the discussion of al-Humaza holds here too: *"The common ground of the two words is the act itself — belittling someone — and the difference lies in the instruments of that act."*

### مَشَّآءٍۭ بِنَمِيمٍ — the carrier of tales

**The construction has to be resolved word by word.**

**مَشَّاء — root م-ش-ي.** To walk. In the *faʿʿāl* pattern: **one who walks a great deal.**

**نَمِيم — root ن-م-م.** And the concrete sense of the root is interesting: *namma* — to spread the scent or the trace of a thing; to **bring out and spread** a piece of news. *Namīma* — carrying a word one has heard from one person to another in order to damage a relationship. In English it is rendered "talebearing" or "backbiting".

**From the same root come *namnam* (fine traces in sand) and — as the lexicographers record — *namla* (ant):** the ant is said to have been given this name because it moves constantly and leaves a trail. **I transmit this derivation as the lexicographers transmit it; it carries no claim to certainty.**

**And now the picture of the construction:** *mashshāʾin bi-namīm* — **one who walks a great deal, carrying tales.**

**Note the verb the expression chooses: to walk.**

The verse does not say "carrier of words"; it says **"one who walks with words"**. At the centre of the act, then, is not speaking but **movement**.

**This catches the mechanics of talebearing, and I write it as my own reading:** talebearing is not a conversation but a **haulage**. A word is taken from one place and carried to another. And carrying takes labour — one goes, one waits, one watches for the right moment.

The *faʿʿāl* pattern turns that labour into a trade: the man does this for a living.

**A link within the sūra, as my own reading:** in the discussion of al-Mulk it was seen that the verb *mashy* distinguishes two ways of walking (67/22 — face down and upright). Here there is a third: **the walk that carries tales.** The same verb, three different moral positions.

### مَّنَّاعٍ لِّلْخَيْرِ — the withholder of good

**مَنَّاع — root م-ن-ع.** Treated in the discussion of al-Māʿūn (107/7); I do not repeat it here. In summary of the finding there: *"manʿ is the withholding of something that has been asked for. That is, there is a request. If no one asked, there is no 'withholding'."*

**This finding operates directly here: withholding requires a request.** So the man in the portrait stands facing someone who is asking him for something.

**And the pattern is *faʿʿāl*: he has made withholding his trade.**

**خَيْر — good.** The word's use in the Qurʾān is broad and includes the sense of **property**. This was recorded in the discussion of al-Fajr: *"The Qurʾān also uses the word khayr for property (al-Baqara 2/180: 'if he leaves behind some good')."* The *ḥubb al-khayr* of al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/8 is likewise love of property.

**So *mannāʿ li'l-khayr* can be understood in two ways, and both occur in the classical sources:**

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| **Withholder of property** | Miserly; not giving what is in his hand |
| **Withholder of good** | One who not only does not give himself but prevents others from giving |

**The second reading is broader and fits the sense of *manʿ* better** — because *manʿ* is not "not to give" but "to prevent". For miserliness Arabic has the word *bukhl*, and the Qurʾān uses it.

**My preference is toward the second reading; it is not binding.** The two do not exclude one another: the man who does not give is discomfited by the man who does.

**And this builds a bridge to the garden narrative.** In the narrative the intention of the garden's owners is exactly this: *"Let no poor man enter it today upon you"* (68/24). So the counterpart of the verb *manʿ* in the narrative is an instance of the attribute in the portrait.

**I record this link as my own reading;** but that the two sections stand in the same sūra and describe the same behaviour is a datum of the text.

**The link with al-Māʿūn and al-Fajr.** In the discussion of al-Māʿūn it was treated how the threshold is lowered step by step: not feeding but urging, then merely a loan. In the discussion of al-Fajr the same two groups (the orphan and the poor) were named.

**What the three sūras have in common is this: all of them set withholding up as a matter of belief.** In al-Māʿūn the man who denies the religion repels the orphan; in al-Fajr the love of wealth is named together with denial of the day of reckoning; in al-Qalam the man who denies the revelation withholds the good.

### مُعْتَدٍ أَثِيمٍ — aggressor, sinner

**These two words were treated in the discussion of al-Muṭaffifīn (83/12), and the link with Sūrat al-Qalam was already made there.** The note there was this:

> *"مَنَّاعٍ لِّلْخَيْرِ مُعْتَدٍ أَثِيمٍ (al-Qalam 68/12) — 'the withholder of good, the aggressor, the sinner.' That is, the phrase muʿtadin athīm occurs in two places in the Qurʾān and both are inside the portrait of a person."*

**I record their roots briefly and pass on:**

**مُعْتَد — root ع-د-و.** To go beyond the limit, to cross the boundary, to act with enmity. *ʿAduww* — enemy; *ʿudwān* — aggression. In Form VIII (*iʿtadā*): **to cross the boundary knowingly.**

**أَثِيم — root أ-ث-م.** Sin. And the concrete sense of the root is **heaviness and slowness**: the lexicographers explain the verb *athima* as "to lag behind, to grow heavy". So sin, at the root of the word, is what **weighs a person down and leaves him behind.**

The *faʿīl* pattern: settled, become a disposition.

**The order of the two words is meaningful:** first the act (*muʿtad* — one who crosses the boundary), then the state (*athīm* — sinner). What is done, and what the doing leaves in the person.

### عُتُلّ — coarse

**Root: ع-ت-ل.** And on the concrete sense of the word the lexicographers give several closely related descriptions:

- **Coarse, harsh, hard-natured.** One who does not soften.
- **Heavily built, strong.** A bully.
- **Gluttonous.** One who eats a great deal. (This sense is recorded in some sources.)
- **One who drags off.** The verb *ʿatala* means "to seize a man by the collar and drag him"; the Qurʾān uses it: *"Seize him and **drag** him into the midst of the Fire"* (al-Dukhān 44/47 — *faʿtilūhu*).

**The last sense is the most concrete use of the root and explains the others:** *ʿutull* is a man with brute force enough to drag another, and with no scruple about using it.

**And it must not be confused with the root *ʿutuww* (ع-ت-و) treated in the discussion of al-Mulk** — the final letters differ; they are separate roots. But the family of meaning is close: both carry **hardness and unbendingness**. I record this as an association, not as a claim of derivation.

**Its pattern, *fuʿull*, is a heavy pattern little used in Arabic,** and the sound itself suits the sense of the word: two *ḍamma*s and a doubled *lām* — a heavy, blocked sound in the mouth.

### بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ زَنِيمٍ — "and beyond that"

**The ninth and last attribute. And before it there is a note: بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ — "after all this", "and beyond that".**

**The function of this note has been debated:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Sequence** | Eight attributes have been counted; the ninth is being added |
| **Aggravation** | "As if all this were not enough, on top of it" — the last attribute is the heaviest |
| **A separate category** | The preceding eight attributes are the man's **own acts**; the ninth is his **situation** — hence the separation |

**I incline to the third view, and the ground is this:** all eight attributes are things the man does or is. *Zanīm*, on the other hand — whatever its sense — concerns the man's **position**. The note *baʿda dhālika* may be signalling that the category has changed.

**This is a preference, and it is not binding.**

### زَنِيم — the most disputed word

**Root: ز-ن-م.** And on the sense of the word the lexicographers give several descriptions; all of them converge at one point.

**Concrete senses:**

- **زَنَمَة (zanama)** — the piece of flesh hanging from a sheep's or goat's ear; or the **mark/notch** made in an animal's ear. A brand made so that the animal may be known in the flock.
- **زَنِيم (zanīm)** — one who has joined a group later, who is not of that lineage by origin; one who has been **attached**.
- **دَعِيّ (daʿiyy)** is counted a synonym: one whose parentage is uncertain, who is ascribed to a lineage but is not of it.

**The common idea is this: to be attached to a place one does not belong to.** So too is the piece of flesh on the ear — it is part of the body but has no function; it dangles.

**Now great care is needed here, and the rule written into the method comes into play.**

**There are explanations that give the word the sense "born illegitimate", and these are recorded in the classical sources. I record this explanation as a possibility, but I do not dwell on it, and I write the ground plainly:**

**The Qurʾān condemns no one for his birth.** How a person was born is not his act, and the Qurʾān does not call to account for what is not an act. This is the foundation of the Qurʾān's understanding of responsibility.

**For this reason I understand the function of the word in its context as follows, as my own reading:** *zanīm* describes the man's **position in society** — one who seems to belong somewhere but in fact does not, one who is attached, who has fastened on.

**And this reading is consistent with the portrait as a whole:**

All the attributes of the man in the portrait gather around an **anxiety about position**:

- He swears a great deal — so that his word will be accepted.
- He carries tales — to gain a place by carrying information.
- He finds fault — to rise by lowering someone else.

**These are behaviours a man whose place is secure would not need.** And the ninth attribute states the reason: the man is **attached**.

**I offer this reading as my own inference, not as a classical transmission.** The concrete sense of the word (the marked animal joined later to the flock) is a datum; the link I make with the portrait is interpretation.

**One further note:** the word's sense of mark or brand sets up an echo with *sa-nasimuhu* (we shall brand him) in the sixteenth verse. **The branded animal, the nose to be branded.** Two words in the same sūra, three verses apart. I record this as an observation.

## 68/14-16 — أَن كَانَ ذَا مَالٍ وَبَنِينَ ۝ إِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ءَايَٰتُنَا قَالَ أَسَٰطِيرُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ ۝ سَنَسِمُهُۥ عَلَى ٱلْخُرْطُومِ

*An kāna dhā mālin wa banīn — idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā qāla asāṭīru'l-awwalīn — sa-nasimuhu ʿalā'l-khurṭūm*
"Because he is a man of wealth and sons. When Our verses are recited to him he says, 'tales of the ancients'. Soon We shall brand him upon the snout."

### أَن كَانَ — the two heaviest words in the sūra

**Nine faults were counted in the portrait. Now the question is: how are these faults tolerated?**

The verse gives the answer: **wealth and sons.**

**And note the grammar of the answer.** The function of the particle **أَنْ** at the head has been debated, and there are two readings:

| Reading | The function of *an* | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Causal** | "because" | He behaves like this — or is received like this — **because** he has wealth and sons |
| **Interrogative — with a variant reading** | "because…?" | Is it **because** he has wealth and sons that he calls the verses tales? |

**This expression was treated in the discussion of al-Muṭaffifīn and this note was made:** *"The classical grammarians explain the an at the head of this expression as causal: that is, he says this 'because he has wealth and sons'."*

**And the conclusion reached there was this:** *"This is the plainest instance of what al-Muṭaffifīn 83/12 says: the cause of denial is not a piece of reasoning but a position."*

**For the second reading a variant reading is recorded** (reading the hamza lengthened, *āan*, which gives an interrogative sense). **I record it, but since I cannot state with certainty which reading belongs to which authority, I write no name.** Both readings establish the same connection; the difference is whether the connection is a question or a statement.

### What the cause is attached to — a disagreement

**Where the sentence attaches is also disputed:**

| View | Attachment | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **To the nine attributes** | *"Do not yield to him… because he has wealth and sons"* | The reason for not obeying: he is taken seriously merely because of his wealth |
| **To the fifteenth verse** | *"Because he has wealth and sons he calls the verses tales"* | The reason for his denial: his position |
| **To an implied verb** | *"Is it because he has wealth and sons that he swaggers?"* | The reason for the censure |

**The second and the first views do not exclude one another, and in my judgement the sūra says both at once:** wealth and sons are the reason both for the **toleration** of the faults and for their owner's **regarding himself as self-sufficient**.

**This is a preference, and it is not binding.**

### مَال وَبَنِين — wealth and sons

**This pair is the Qurʾān's standard phrase for worldly power** and is repeated in many places.

**Why these two?**

| | **مال (wealth)** | **بنون (sons)** |
|---|---|---|
| What it provides | Purchasing power | Manpower |
| How it protects | A resource | Defence, continuity |
| What it promises | Present security | **Continuation into the future** |

**In seventh-century Arab society these two were the whole of a man's power.** In the tribal order the number of male children meant political weight directly: the number of people who would stand beside him in a quarrel and seek his blood when he died.

**And what the two have in common is this, as my own reading:** both promise **duration**. Wealth meets future needs; sons continue after a man's death.

**This finding was made in the discussion of al-Humaza and fits exactly here:**

> *"The value of an instrument lies in its use; the value of an end lies in its existence."* and *"yaḥsabu anna mālahu akhladahu — 'my wealth makes me last'."*

**And in the discussion of al-Fajr a second layer was added:** *"rabbī akramani — 'my wealth shows my worth'."*

**Sūrat al-Qalam adds a third layer: wealth and sons give a man the *nerve* to reject the verses.**

| Sūra | The function of wealth | The false belief |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Humaza 104** | A certificate of immortality | "Wealth makes me last" |
| **al-Fajr 89** | A certificate of worth | "Wealth shows my worth" |
| **al-Qalam 68** | **A certificate of sufficiency** | "With my wealth, what need have I of any of this" |

**The third arrives at the same place as the concept of *istighnāʾ* treated in the discussion of al-Layl.** The finding there was: *"'I have no need of anything' is, in the language of the sūra, a moral position — and not a good one."*

**And there is a fourth layer, particular to this sūra, as my own reading:** here wealth and sons affect not only their owner but **those around him**. The reason a man with nine faults is deferred to is that man's wealth. That is, wealth makes its owner's character **invisible.**

**This makes the sūra's nine-attribute portrait meaningful anew:** the portrait is long because ordinarily these faults are not counted. The faults of a man with wealth are not counted. By counting them one by one, the sūra lifts the veil of wealth.

### إِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ءَايَٰتُنَا قَالَ أَسَٰطِيرُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ

**This sentence is word for word the same as al-Muṭaffifīn 83/13 and was treated there; I do not repeat it here.**

**Summary of the findings in the discussion of al-Muṭaffifīn:**

- **تُتْلَىٰ** — root *t-l-w*: to follow, to come after. *"Reciting is the letters following one another. At the centre of the word is not sound but sequence."* The verb is passive and, together with *idhā*, conveys continuity: the same thing happens every time.
- **ءَايَٰتُنَا** — *"The construct is made to the first person plural… the verse states to whom the thing rejected belongs. The man is not rejecting 'a text'; he is rejecting a word whose owner is known."*
- **أَسَٰطِير** — root *s-ṭ-r*: *"saṭr — a line, a row, a series… Its sense: things written and compiled, narratives handed down from the ancients."* And the disagreement about its singular was recorded there.

**And in the discussion of al-Muṭaffifīn the overlap between the two sūras was set out in a table and this note was made:** *"I record this overlap between the two sūras as a datum. That the same phrase and the same sentence occur in two places is a fact of the text; I make no claim that there is a deliberate reference from one sūra to the other."*

**That note holds here as well.**

**The only thing I need to add as particular to Sūrat al-Qalam is this, and it was recorded earlier:** in the first verse it was said *wa mā **yasṭurūn*** (by what they write). In the fifteenth verse, from the same root, *asāṭīr*. **The sūra hears, in its fifteenth verse, as an insult the root it exalted in its first.**

**And the character of the objection is worth noting:** to say *"tales of the ancients"* is not to **deny** the text; it is to **refer it to history**.

The man does not say "this is false". He says "this is old". That is, he never enters the content at all; he classifies the text as a **genre** and passes on.

**I record that this is a technique of disputation and that it is widespread today as well, as my own inference:** refuting a claim is hard; placing it in a category is easy. Once the category is applied, there is no need to look at the content of the claim.

### سَنَسِمُهُۥ عَلَى ٱلْخُرْطُومِ — a brand on the nose

**The harshest sentence in the sūra.**

**وَسَمَ — root و-س-م.** And the concrete sense of the root is **to brand, to mark**: *wasama'l-baʿīr* — he seared the camel with hot iron, he branded it.

**Its derivatives:**

- **سِمَة (sima)** — a brand, a mark.
- **سِيمَا (sīmā)** — the mark on the face, appearance. It survives in Turkish as *sima*.
- **مَوْسِم (mawsim)** — a time known by particular marks; the season of pilgrimage, the season of rain. The Turkish word *mevsim* comes from this root.

**In the discussion of al-Fātiḥa this root was named while the root of the word *ism* was being debated.** The note there was this: *"There are two old views on its root: s-m-w (height) or w-s-m (mark, trace, brand). Both in fact arrive at the same place: a name is the mark that distinguishes a thing from others and makes it visible."*

**Now that note bears fruit here, as my own reading:** if a name is a mark that identifies a thing, so is a brand. The difference is that one is **given** and the other **struck**. And the brand in the verse writes the man's real name on his face.

**Brand and mark in the Qurʾān:**

- *"You will know them by their **mark**"* (al-Baqara 2/273 — of the poor).
- *"**Their mark** is on their faces from the trace of prostration"* (al-Fatḥ 48/29).

**And now see the contrast — I record this as my own reading:**

| | **al-Fatḥ 48/29** | **al-Qalam 68/16** |
|---|---|---|
| Where the mark is | On the face | **On the nose** |
| The trace of what | Prostration | A brand |
| How it came about | **By the person's own act** | **Struck from outside** |
| What it shows | That he has bowed | That he has not bowed |

**Two faces, two traces.** One that he placed himself, one that was placed upon him.

### ٱلْخُرْطُوم — snout

**Root: خ-ر-ط-م.** A four-letter root. **Its sense: the nose of an animal — in particular the trunk of an elephant and the snout of a beast of prey.** Used of a human being in Arabic it is plainly degrading.

**And there are several layers in the choice of the word:**

**One. Degradation.** For a human nose the word is *anf*. To say *khurṭūm* is to put a man down among the animals.

**Two. The nose and pride.** In Arabic the nose is the organ of pride — and so it is in English: "his nose in the air", "to look down one's nose". In Arabic *shamakha bi-anfihī* (he raised his nose) conveys arrogance.

**So the punishment is struck on the organ of the offence.** The man had raised his nose; the brand goes there.

**Three. Visibility.** The nose is the most protruding and most visible part of the face. Striking the brand there makes it impossible to hide.

**Four. Permanence.** A brand is not wiped away. And the sūra opened with a pen — the most distinctive property of writing is likewise permanence.

**I record this fourth link as my own reading:** the sūra opens with the idea of a permanent record and closes this section with the idea of a permanent brand. What is written stays; the brand that is struck stays too.

**And the link with *zanīm* was recorded earlier:** *zanama* is the mark on an animal's ear. In the thirteenth verse the man was described as a "marked animal"; in the sixteenth it is announced that the mark will be struck.

**That the two words stand in the same sūra and that both concern the branding of animals is a datum of the text; the link I make between them is interpretation.**

### The time of the punishment — a disagreement

**سَنَسِمُهُ** — the prefix *sa-* conveys the future. **But when?**

| View | When | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **In the hereafter** | On the day of resurrection there will be a mark on his face | The Qurʾān has descriptions of faces darkening, turning black |
| **In this world** | A brand of humiliation and disgrace was struck in this world; his standing was gone | *Sa-* conveys the near future |
| **Figurative** | The brand is a lasting ill repute; this is how his name will be spoken | The figurative use of the word is widespread in Arabic |

**All three views exist in the classical sources and they do not exclude one another.** I state no preference; all three have support from the text and the verse does not explain the matter.

**But one observation is worth recording:** the verse says not **what** the punishment is but **where** it will be struck. The stress falls on the place. And this brings forward the punishment's **visibility** rather than its nature.

### What this says to the present

**These three verses complete the sūra's social diagnosis: nine faults were counted, then the reason they are overlooked was stated.**

**And this is one of the most everyday observations there is: wealth suspends moral assessment.**

A person's faults are talked about in inverse proportion to that person's power. A minor rudeness in someone powerless is recounted at length; a systematic fault in someone powerful is waved away as "his style".

**What the verse does is to read the list anyway.** Nine attributes are counted in spite of the veil of wealth.

**A second aspect: the "tales of the ancients" technique.**

Placing a view in a category without looking at its content and moving on is still today the commonest shortcut in argument. Once the category is applied the argument is over — because what is now being argued about is not the claim but the label.

**And note the order of the verse:** first the man's position is stated (wealth and sons), then what he says. That is, the sūra explains the word by the position of the one who speaks it.

**It must be recorded that this can also work in the opposite direction,** because it is dangerous: explaining a person's word by his position may be justified, as the sūra does here; but made into a method it becomes an easy way of refuting anything. The sūra does this for one man, after nine faults have been counted — not as a general rule.

## 68/17-20 — إِنَّا بَلَوْنَٰهُمْ كَمَا بَلَوْنَآ أَصْحَٰبَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ إِذْ أَقْسَمُوا۟ لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا مُصْبِحِينَ ۝ وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ ۝ فَطَافَ عَلَيْهَا طَآئِفٌ مِّن رَّبِّكَ وَهُمْ نَآئِمُونَ ۝ فَأَصْبَحَتْ كَٱلصَّرِيمِ

*Innā balawnāhum kamā balawnā aṣḥāba'l-jannati idh aqsamū la-yaṣrimunnahā muṣbiḥīn — wa lā yastathnūn — fa-ṭāfa ʿalayhā ṭāʾifun min rabbika wa hum nāʾimūn — fa-aṣbaḥat ka'l-ṣarīm*
"We have tried them as We tried the owners of the garden, when they swore that they would harvest it in the morning and made no exception. While they slept, something from your Lord went round it, and by morning it was as though it had been reaped black."

### The place of the narrative in the sūra

**First this must be established: the narrative is not an interruption but an example.**

The connective says so: **إِنَّا بَلَوْنَاهُمْ كَمَا بَلَوْنَا** — "We tried **them** … **as** We tried…"

To whom does the pronoun *hum* refer? To the deniers of the preceding section — and in particular to the man who calls the verses tales because he owns wealth.

**So the narrative enters as a comparison: "your situation resembles this."**

**بَلَوْنَا — root ب-ل-و.** Treated in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/49, with a layer added in the discussion of al-Mulk 67/2: *"the original sense of the root is to wear out, to use up… The Arabs tested a thing's durability by using it."*

**And the past tense of the verb is worth noting:** *balawnāhum* — we tried. The trial, then, is **over and done with.** The rich of Mecca have already been tried; the narrative shows the result of the trial.

### أَصْحَٰبَ ٱلْجَنَّةِ — the owners of the garden

**جَنَّة — root ج-ن-ن.** The same root as the *majnūn* of the second verse; recorded earlier. The finding in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/25: *"janna — a garden that covers the ground with its trees."*

**Here the word has its lexical sense: a garden.** Not the garden of the hereafter; a real, wooded, productive garden.

**أَصْحَاب — root ص-ح-ب.** Treated in the discussion of al-Takwīr: to be together, to keep company. *Aṣḥāb* means those **named together with** something.

**And the fine point of the construct is here, as my own reading:** the men are named not "the owners of the garden" but "the **companions** of the garden". In Arabic *aṣḥāb al-janna* does convey ownership as well; but the idea of togetherness at the root of the word suggests that the men have become identified with the garden. Their identity is named by what they own.

**And the question of what is left when the garden is gone will be answered at the end of the narrative.**

**On the historical setting of the narrative:** there are reports about where and when the narrative took place; a particular place and a particular community are named. **I do not write these names,** because I cannot vouch for them and because they are not needed for the narrative's function. The Qurʾān too gives no place and no name — this may be taken as a sign that the narrative is built as an **example**.

### إِذْ أَقْسَمُوا۟ لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا مُصْبِحِينَ — the oath

**أَقْسَمُوا۟ — root ق-س-م.** To swear. And the original sense of the root is **to divide, to apportion** (*qisma*, *taqsīm* are from the same root). That an oath should be named by this root has to do, as the lexicographers transmit, with the swearer's **staking a share** or with the matter's being definitively **separated out**. **I transmit this explanation without any claim to certainty.**

**And now a link within the sūra:** in the tenth verse the portrait's first attribute was **حَلَّاف** — the great swearer.

**The men in the narrative swear too.** The same act, a different word, the same behaviour.

| | **68/10** | **68/17** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | The man in the portrait | The owners of the garden |
| What | *ḥallāf* — one who swears a great deal | *aqsamū* — they swore |
| Result | His word is not accepted | **The oath does not hold** |

**The narrative dramatises the portrait's first attribute, as my own reading.** What the oaths of a man who swears a great deal come to is seen in the narrative: in a single night they come to nothing.

**صَرَمَ — root ص-ر-م.** To cut, to sever. And the word's principal use is for **harvest**: cutting fruit from the branch and gathering it. *Ṣirām* — the time of picking. From the same root come *ṣarīm* (cut off, severed) and *ṣarrām*.

**The root's second sense: to cut off a relationship.** *Ṣarama raḥimahu* — he severed the tie of kinship. In Turkish the root should not be confused with *sarım / sarmak*; the resemblance is accidental and the words are unrelated.

**That the two senses live in one root will acquire meaning at the end of the narrative.**

**Note that the verb is emphatic: لَيَصْرِمُنَّهَا** — with the *lām* and the heavy *nūn*. That is, not "we shall gather it" but **"we shall most certainly and without fail gather it."** The resolve is built into the grammar.

**مُصْبِحِين — having entered upon the morning.** Root *ṣ-b-ḥ*; *aṣbaḥa* was treated in the discussion of al-Mulk 67/30.

**And this word occurs three times in the narrative:** 17 (*muṣbiḥīn*), 21 (*muṣbiḥīn*), 22 (*ighdū* — go out early). The narrative, then, turns about the word **morning**.

**The reason is the construction of the narrative, as my own reading:** the disaster happens by night and is discovered in the morning. And the men had planned for the morning. **The plan belongs to the morning, the disaster to the night.**

### وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ — "they made no exception"

**The most discussed place in the narrative, and a verse standing on its own.**

**Root: ث-ن-ي.** To bend, to fold in two, to turn. *Ithnā* — two; *tathniya* — the dual. *Istithnāʾ* — to set something apart from the general, to leave it outside.

**But what did they not except?**

**The view of the overwhelming majority of classical exegesis: they did not say *in shāʾ Allāh*.** That is, in saying "we shall certainly harvest it in the morning" they did not add the note "if God wills".

**And this reading has very strong support from within the Qurʾān:**

> *"Do not say of anything, 'I shall certainly do this tomorrow' — unless you say 'if God wills'."* (al-Kahf 18/23-24)

**The overlap between the two texts is striking:**

| | **al-Kahf 18/23-24** | **al-Qalam 68/17-18** |
|---|---|---|
| What is said | *"I shall do this tomorrow"* | *"We shall certainly harvest in the morning"* |
| What is missing | *"Illā an yashāʾa'llāh"* | *"Wa lā yastathnūn"* |
| Time | Tomorrow | Morning |
| Mood | Certainty | **Reinforced certainty** |

**The verse in al-Kahf gives the command; the narrative in al-Qalam shows the outcome for one who breaks it.** I record this link; I make no claim that the two texts refer deliberately to one another.

**A second view is also recorded:** the expression "they made no exception" may be saying that **they did not set aside the poor man's share.** That is, they abandoned the custom of setting aside a portion of the harvest.

| View | Ground | Objection |
|---|---|---|
| **They did not say "if God wills"** | The overlap with al-Kahf 18/23-24; the lexical sense of the word | — |
| **They did not set aside the poor man's share** | The continuation of the narrative describes this (verse 24); and *istithnāʾ* means "to set a portion apart" | When the word is used absolutely in this sense, what is set apart is generally stated |

**Both are defensible and they do not exclude one another.** **I prefer the first view** — the overlap with the verse in al-Kahf is a strong ground. **It is not binding;** the second view makes a tighter link with the continuation of the narrative and is not weak.

### What "if God wills" actually means

**This note has to be made, because the phrase has worn thin in everyday use.**

*In shāʾ Allāh* mostly does two jobs in ordinary speech: it expresses a wish ("*in shāʾ Allāh* it will pass off well") and it provides an escape ("*in shāʾ Allāh* I'll come" = probably I won't).

**What was missing in the narrative is neither of these.**

**What was missing is a note of fact:** the future is not in my hands.

And this is not a matter of prayer but of **framing a true sentence**. The sentence "tomorrow I shall do this" assumes that tomorrow is in one's own hands. The assumption is false. The note makes the sentence true.

**I add this as my own reading:** what is punished in the narrative is not a missing prayer but an **error about reality**. The men made plans relying on an authority they did not have.

And the continuation of the narrative confirms it: the garden really was not in their hands.

**A caution:** no conclusion of the kind "planning is wrong" follows from this. The men's error was not that they planned; it was that they built the plan in the mood of **certainty**. *La-yaṣrimunnahā* — two separate instruments of emphasis have been used.

### فَطَافَ عَلَيْهَا طَآئِفٌ — something going round

**Root: ط-و-ف.** To turn about, to circle, to encompass. *Ṭawāf* — circling the Kaʿba; *ṭāʾifa* — a group (those who go about together).

**And the word-choice of the verse is extremely careful:**

- What it was is **not said**: *ṭāʾifun* — "something that went round". Indefinite, unnamed.
- What it did is **not said**: only "it went round".
- Where it came from **is said**: *min rabbika* — "from your Lord".

**So the verse hides the agent and the manner of the event and gives only its source.**

**This is a choice of style and its effect is as follows, as my own reading:** what the disaster was (fire? flood? wind? insects?) is made unimportant. The only thing that matters is where it came from.

**The classical exegetes offer conjectures as to what the *ṭāʾif* was** — a fire, a calamity, a wind. **I do not transmit these conjectures,** because the verse does not say, and its not saying looks deliberate.

**And the worship-related sense of the word *ṭawāf* produces an echo here:** the same verb is used for circling the Kaʿba. Here something circles the garden. **I record this as an observation about the word;** I make no claim that the verse intends the association.

**وَهُمْ نَآئِمُونَ — "while they slept".**

**This note is the narrative's quietest emphasis.** The men made their plan and went to sleep. And the plan collapsed while they slept.

**Sleep is a person's most defenceless state,** and the Qurʾān uses this elsewhere too. But its function here is different, as my own reading: **sleep is the moment when control leaves one's hands.** In saying "certainly, in the morning", the men had not reckoned with the night that lay between.

And they could not see what happened inside that night — because they were asleep.

### فَأَصْبَحَتْ كَٱلصَّرِيمِ — "it turned black"

**And now a play on words — but the Qurʾān's kind of play on words.**

The men had sworn to **صرم** the garden (to harvest it).

The garden became **صريم**.

**The same root. The act and the outcome from one word.**

**On the sense of the word صَرِيم there is disagreement, and the views must be recorded:**

| View | Sense | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Harvested** | Like a garden whose fruit has been picked, emptied | The original sense of the root |
| **The black night** | *Ṣarīm* is used in Arabic for the night as well (that which is cut off from the day) | The garden's blackening, its looking burnt |
| **Cut down** | Cut off at the root, destroyed | The root's sense of "cutting" |
| **A sand dune / barren ground** | Some lexicographers record *ṣarīm* also as black earth on which nothing grows | A lexical entry |

**The views differ but the outcome is the same: nothing was left in the garden.**

**And the real point does not change, whichever sense is taken:**

**The men swore to cut the garden; the garden was cut. The oath was fulfilled — but not by them.**

**I record this as the sharpest irony of the narrative.** What they wanted happened; the work they wanted done was done. Only the doer changed.

And this was exactly what was missing in the eighteenth verse: they did not say *"if God wills"*. God willed, and the garden was cut — but not as they had willed it.

## 68/21-27 — فَتَنَادَوْا۟ مُصْبِحِينَ ۝ أَنِ ٱغْدُوا۟ عَلَىٰ حَرْثِكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰرِمِينَ ۝ فَٱنطَلَقُوا۟ وَهُمْ يَتَخَٰفَتُونَ ۝ أَن لَّا يَدْخُلَنَّهَا ٱلْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكُم مِّسْكِينٌ ۝ وَغَدَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ حَرْدٍ قَٰدِرِينَ ۝ فَلَمَّا رَأَوْهَا قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لَضَآلُّونَ ۝ بَلْ نَحْنُ مَحْرُومُونَ

*Fa-tanādaw muṣbiḥīn — ani'ghdū ʿalā ḥarthikum in kuntum ṣārimīn — fa'nṭalaqū wa hum yatakhāfatūn — an lā yadkhulannahā'l-yawma ʿalaykum miskīn — wa ghadaw ʿalā ḥardin qādirīn — fa-lammā raʾawhā qālū innā la-ḍāllūn — bal naḥnu maḥrūmūn*
"In the morning they called out to one another: 'Go out early to your field, if you mean to harvest!' They set off, whispering together: 'Let no poor man enter it today upon you!' They went out early, judging themselves able to withhold. But when they saw it they said: 'We must have lost our way.' Then: 'No — we have been deprived.'"

The middle section of the narrative. And the step-by-step account of one morning.

### فَتَنَادَوْا۟ — they called out to one another

**Root: ن-د-و / ن-د-ي.** To call out, to summon. *Nidāʾ* — a call; *nādī* — an assembly, a gathering place.

**In Form VI (*tanādaw*): calling to one another.**

**And the word sets up a contrast with the next verb:**

| Verse | Verb | The state of the voice |
|---|---|---|
| 21 | *tanādaw* — they **called out** to one another | **Loud** |
| 23 | *yatakhāfatūn* — they are **whispering** | **Low** |

**Between the two verses the voice drops. And the moment it drops is the moment the intention is spoken.**

**This is one of the finest details in the narrative and I offer it as my own reading:** the call to go to the harvest is made by shouting — there is nothing to be ashamed of. The decision to keep the poor man out is spoken in a whisper.

**The dropping of the voice shows that the intention is conscious.** The men know that what they are doing is wrong; otherwise they would not whisper.

### أَنِ ٱغْدُوا۟ — "go out early"

**Root: غ-د-و.** And the sense of the word is **to set out on a task at the first hour of the day**. *Ghadāt* — the morning hour; *ghuduww* — the going out in the morning. Its opposite is *rawāḥ* — the return in the evening. Arabic names these two movements with separate words.

**And the verb occurs twice in the narrative:** 22 (*ighdū* — imperative) and 25 (*ghadaw* — they went out). The command was given and it was carried out.

**حَرْث — root ح-ر-ث.** And the sense of the root is **to plough the soil, to sow**. *Ḥarth* — crop, field, what is sown.

**The fine point of the word is here:** in the seventeenth verse the garden was named *janna* (garden); here it is called *ḥarth* (tilled field).

*Janna* is what covers, what shades, what is beautiful. *Ḥarth* is what is **laboured over** — ploughed, sown, worked.

**The change of word shows the men's way of seeing, as my own reading:** the word out of their own mouths is *ḥarth* — "the product of our labour". The word the text uses is *janna* — "a garden given".

**And the word *ḥarth* is named in the Qurʾān together with a warning:** crop and produce become a matter of trial when they are counted a man's "own work". The question asked about the sown seed in Sūrat al-Wāqiʿa belongs to this frame.

**إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰرِمِينَ — "if you mean to harvest".**

Again the root **صرم**. The third time in the narrative. And in the conditional: "if you are harvesters."

**This conditional clause completes, with irony, what was missing in the eighteenth verse:** the men did not say "if God wills", but they did say "if you are harvesters". So they laid down a condition — but they attached the condition to **their own resolve**, not to God's willing.

**I record this as my own reading;** but that the two expressions stand in the same narrative, one a condition and the other an exception, is a datum of the text.

### فَٱنطَلَقُوا۟ — they set off

**Root: ط-ل-ق.** To release, to let loose. *Ṭalāq* — the dissolving of the marriage tie; *ṭilq* — free.

**In Form VII (*inṭalaqa*): to go as though released, to set off swiftly.**

**There is a freedom and a speed in the word** — a going that recognises no obstacle. And this gives the men's state of mind: sure of themselves, quick, resolved.

### يَتَخَٰفَتُونَ — to whisper

**Root: خ-ف-ت.** To lower the voice, to drop it. *Khafata* — his voice fell; and the lexicographers relate this root to death as well: *khāfit* — one whose voice has been cut off, who has died.

**In Form VI: whispering to one another.**

**The Qurʾān uses this word in another place in an interesting way:** the same root occurs where it is commanded that the voice in prayer be neither loud nor too hushed (in the frame of al-Isrāʾ 17/110). So the word describes a voice too low to be heard.

**And the description of whispering is this, as my own inference:** a whisper is the form given to a word that is not meant to be heard. But hidden from whom?

There is no one in the garden. The poor have not yet come. There is no one about to hear.

**The men are hiding it not from one another, and not from anyone at all — and still they whisper.**

**This connects directly with al-Mulk 67/13:** *"Whether you hide your word or speak it aloud, He knows what is in the breasts."*

The two texts say the same thing from two directions: the one announces that hiding will be of no use, the other shows people trying to hide.

### أَن لَّا يَدْخُلَنَّهَا ٱلْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكُم مِّسْكِينٌ — the heart of the narrative

**This is what they say in their whispering. And the real offence of the narrative is here.**

**Note the grammar of the sentence, for the violence is there:**

- **لَا يَدْخُلَنَّ** — prohibition + the heavy *nūn*. **A reinforced prohibition:** "let him on no account enter!"
- **ٱلْيَوْمَ** — "today". The stress falls on today.
- **عَلَيْكُم** — "upon you". The preposition *ʿalā* here carries a tone of **imposition and unwelcomeness**.
- **مِّسْكِين** — indefinite: "**a** poor man". Not even a single one.

**So the sentence is: let not even one poor man, today, come in upon you.**

**And see where the emphasis is used:** in the seventeenth verse, *la-yaṣrimunnahā* — "we shall certainly harvest it". Here, *lā yadkhulanna* — "let him on no account enter". **The same grammatical instrument, in two places: to guarantee the harvest and to keep the poor man out.**

**This shows that the two resolves in the narrative are of the same intensity.** The men are as determined to prevent as they are to gather.

**مِسْكِين — root س-ك-ن.** Stillness, immobility. *Miskīn*, at the root of the word, is one left so helpless that **he cannot move** — poverty has made him unable to stir from where he is.

**This word was treated in the discussion of al-Māʿūn** and its difference from *faqīr* was dwelt on. There too the phrase *"the food of the poor man"* was taken up and this finding was made: the food is named as belonging to the poor man — although it has not yet been given.

**And now three sūras come together:**

| Sūra | Verb | Who | To whom |
|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Māʿūn 107/2-3** | *yaduʿʿu* (repels), *lā yaḥuḍḍu* (does not urge) | The one who denies the religion | The orphan, the poor |
| **al-Fajr 89/17-18** | *lā tukrimūna*, *lā taḥāḍḍūna* | Those addressed | The orphan, the poor |
| **al-Qalam 68/24** | *lā yadkhulanna* (let him not enter) | The owners of the garden | **The poor man** |

**What the three have in common: the relation established with the poor is counted a sign of belief.**

**Their differences are meaningful too, as my own reading:**

- **In al-Māʿūn** the poor man comes and is **repelled.**
- **In al-Fajr** no **urging** is done on behalf of the poor — that is, inaction.
- **In al-Qalam** the poor man is prevented **before he even arrives.**

**The third is the furthest stage.** The man in al-Māʿūn turns down a request; the men in al-Qalam take measures to prevent the request from **arising**.

**And the finding recorded in the discussion of al-Māʿūn operates here once more:** *"manʿ is the withholding of something that has been asked for. That is, there is a request."* The men in al-Qalam fall outside even that definition: they prevent before a request has formed.

**And now the link with the portrait is made:** in the twelfth verse it was said **مَنَّاعٍ لِّلْخَيْرِ** (the withholder of good). The narrative is that attribute brought to life.

**A historical note:** the poor coming to the fields at harvest time to gather what was left was an established practice in the agricultural societies of that period. The Qurʾān commands that the due be given on the day of harvest (at al-Anʿām 6/141 it is commanded, *"give its due on the day of its harvest"*). What the men wish to prevent may be an established right. **I record this as a possibility;** the verse does not speak explicitly of a legal right.

### وَغَدَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ حَرْدٍ قَٰدِرِينَ — a disputed verse

**حَرْد — root ح-ر-د.** And on the sense of the word the lexicographers give several descriptions:

| Sense | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Prevention, withholding** | *Ḥarada* — he prevented, he held back |
| **Anger, rancour** | *Ḥarida* — he grew angry |
| **Purpose, resolve** | *Ḥarada ḥardan* — he set himself towards a thing, he intended it |
| **A direction, a side** | To go towards one side |

**The translation of the sentence changes with this choice:**

- *"They went out judging themselves able to withhold."*
- *"They went out in anger, resolved."*
- *"They went out set upon a purpose, in strength."*

**My preference is toward the first sense** and the ground is the preceding verse: the decision to withhold was taken there. The sense "withholding" for the word *ḥard* attaches the sentence to the verse before it.

**It is not binding;** the other senses too are defended in the classical sources.

**قَادِرِين — "as those with the power".** Root *q-d-r*; treated in the discussion of al-Mulk 67/1: at the centre of the root stands **measure**.

**And the place of the word here is the sharpest irony in the narrative:**

**The men go out "as those with the power" — and where they are going there is nothing at all.**

That is, they lay claim to power over a thing that is no longer in their hands. The garden is already gone; they are still planning how to portion it out.

**I record this picture as my own reading:** this is the comic side of the narrative — and its being comic increases its seriousness. A person can make plans about something that is not in his hands and never notice.

### فَلَمَّا رَأَوْهَا قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّا لَضَآلُّونَ — the first reaction

**And they see the garden.**

**Their first sentence: "We must have lost our way."**

**ضَلَّ — root ض-ل-ل.** And the original sense of the root is **to lose the way**; the moral sense of "going astray" comes from there. This root was treated in the discussion of al-Fātiḥa.

**So the first reaction is not to accept that the garden is gone but to think they have come to the wrong place.**

**This catches the way the human mind works, and I write it as my own reading:** when expectation and reality conflict, the first thing the mind does is to question not reality but **its own position** — because that is cheaper. To say "I have come to the wrong place" is easier than to say "I have lost everything".

**And the double sense of the word adds one more layer here:** *ḍāllūn* means both "those who have lost the way" and "those who have gone astray". The men mean it in the first sense; but the second sense stands inside the sentence.

**So without knowing it the men pass a true judgement on themselves.** I record this as an observation about the word; some of the classical exegetes too draw attention to the double sense.

### بَلْ نَحْنُ مَحْرُومُونَ — the second reaction

**The particle بَلْ** in Arabic conveys *iḍrāb*: turning back from what was just said and putting something else in its place. It was treated in the discussions of al-Infiṭār and al-Muṭaffifīn.

**So the men correct their own words:** "no, we have not lost our way — we have been deprived."

**مَحْرُوم — root ح-ر-م.** To forbid, to prevent, to deprive. *Ḥarām* — forbidden; *ḥaram* — a protected area forbidden to enter; *ḥurma* — inviolability.

**And now the sharpest verbal stroke of the narrative, as my own reading:**

| Verse | Who | Verb | To whom |
|---|---|---|---|
| 24 | The men | *lā yadkhulanna* — let him not enter | To the poor man |
| 27 | The men | *maḥrūmūn* — we have been deprived | **To themselves** |

**Those who prevent turn out to be the prevented.**

**And the choice of word says this openly:** *maḥrūm* is a passive participle — "one who has been deprived". So there is an agent; the men accept that they have been **prevented**.

**The same structure was identified in the discussion of al-Humaza:** *"The one who counts others worthless and throws them away is counted worthless and thrown away."* The symmetry built there for the verb *nabz* is built here for *ḥirmān*.

**One further note:** the Qurʾān uses the word *maḥrūm* in another place in a very different context — among those who have a right in wealth the expression *"the one who asks and the one who has been deprived"* (*al-sāʾili wa'l-maḥrūm*) occurs (al-Dhāriyāt 51/19; al-Maʿārij 70/25).

**So *maḥrūm* is normally, in the Qurʾān, a name for the poor man.**

**And in the narrative the owners of the garden give themselves that name.** The men who would not let the poor man in end up being called by the poor man's name.

**I record this overlap as my own reading;** but the word's use in the two places is a datum of the text.

## 68/28-33 — قَالَ أَوْسَطُهُمْ أَلَمْ أَقُل لَّكُمْ لَوْلَا تُسَبِّحُونَ ۝ قَالُوا۟ سُبْحَٰنَ رَبِّنَآ إِنَّا كُنَّا ظَٰلِمِينَ ۝ فَأَقْبَلَ بَعْضُهُمْ عَلَىٰ بَعْضٍ يَتَلَٰوَمُونَ ۝ قَالُوا۟ يَٰوَيْلَنَآ إِنَّا كُنَّا طَٰغِينَ ۝ عَسَىٰ رَبُّنَآ أَن يُبْدِلَنَا خَيْرًا مِّنْهَآ إِنَّآ إِلَىٰ رَبِّنَا رَٰغِبُونَ ۝ كَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْعَذَابُ ۖ وَلَعَذَابُ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ أَكْبَرُ ۚ لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ

*Qāla awsaṭuhum a-lam aqul lakum lawlā tusabbiḥūn — qālū subḥāna rabbinā innā kunnā ẓālimīn — fa-aqbala baʿḍuhum ʿalā baʿḍin yatalāwamūn — qālū yā waylanā innā kunnā ṭāghīn — ʿasā rabbunā an yubdilanā khayran minhā innā ilā rabbinā rāghibūn — kadhālika'l-ʿadhāb, wa la-ʿadhābu'l-ākhirati akbar, law kānū yaʿlamūn*
"The most balanced among them said: 'Did I not say to you, why do you not glorify God?' They said: 'Glory be to our Lord; we have indeed been wrongdoers.' Then they turned on one another, reproaching one another. They said: 'Woe to us! We have indeed been transgressors. It may be that our Lord will give us something better than it in exchange; to our Lord we now turn.' Such is the punishment. And the punishment of the hereafter is greater — if only they knew."

### قَالَ أَوْسَطُهُمْ — "the middlemost of them"

**Root: و-س-ط.** Middle, that which is in the middle. *Wasaṭ* — middle; *awsaṭ* — the most central.

**And on the sense of the word there are two readings:**

| Reading | Sense | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Middle in age** | The one between the eldest and the youngest | The lexical sense of the word |
| **The most balanced, the most equitable** | The one whose judgement is most measured | In Arabic *wasaṭ* conveys **excellence** |

**The second sense draws support from the Qurʾān's own usage:** *"We have made you a middle community"* (al-Baqara 2/143 — *ummatan **wasaṭan***). There *wasaṭ* is not an arithmetical middle; it means **balanced, choice, good**.

**And the Qurʾān uses the same root once more in the sense of "the best":** among the prayers, *al-ṣalāt al-**wusṭā*** (al-Baqara 2/238) — the middle, or the most excellent, prayer.

**I prefer the second reading** and the ground is the context: the man is the one in the group who says what is right; the information that he was "middle in age" would serve no purpose here. **It is not binding;** the two may also come together in the same man.

### أَلَمْ أَقُل لَّكُمْ — "did I not say to you?"

**The most human sentence in the narrative. And the most useless one.**

The man was right. He had warned them. And now he recalls his warning.

**But note: the man is there too.** He went to the garden with them. He shared in their whispering, or at least did not prevent it. And now he declares that he was right.

**This is a finding I write as my own reading:** the man who knew the right thing and said it but did not change the outcome is not presented in the narrative as one who has been saved. He too loses his garden. And he too is — in the thirty-first verse — inside the group that says "we have been transgressors".

**The narrative does not count having spoken the truth sufficient on its own.**

**And this connects with the eighth verse of the sūra:** *"do not yield to those who deny."* The man in the narrative had in a sense yielded too — he spoke, and then fell silent.

**I record this link as my own reading;** the verse does not say it, but the construction of the narrative is open to this reading.

### لَوْلَا تُسَبِّحُونَ — "why do you not glorify God?"

**The particle لَوْلَا** with an imperfect verb conveys in Arabic **urging and reproach**: "if only you had done it", "why do you not do it".

**سَبَّحَ — root س-ب-ح.** And the concrete sense of the root is **to swim**: *sabaḥa fi'l-māʾ* — he swam in the water. The Qurʾān uses this sense: *"each swimming in an orbit"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/33; in the frame of Yā-Sīn 36/40).

**How does one get from swimming to *tasbīḥ*?**

The explanation the lexicographers transmit is this: the swimmer moves through the water **swiftly and without obstruction**. *Tasbīḥ* is to **remove God far** from every kind of deficiency, to declare Him free of it — that is, to carry Him past and beyond every imputation of fault.

**I transmit this explanation as the lexicographers transmit it; it carries no claim to certainty.** But the root's sense of "swimming" is undisputed in the lexicons.

**So what exactly was the *tasbīḥ* the men did not perform?**

**There is disagreement, and both views must be recorded:**

| View | What it means | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Saying "if God wills"** | *Tasbīḥ* here is the counterpart of the *istithnāʾ* of the eighteenth verse | The link between the two verses; the man says "did I not say", so he had said something before — and that must be the omission of verse 18 |
| **Remembering God, giving thanks** | Naming the giver of the favour; acknowledging the poor man's due | The general sense of the word |

**I prefer the first view** and the ground is the construction of the narrative itself: the man's warning must correspond to something, and the only omission explicitly recorded in the narrative is in the eighteenth verse. **It is not binding.**

**And that the word *tasbīḥ* has been chosen is meaningful, as my own reading:** "saying 'if God wills'" looks like a formula. Saying *tasbīḥ* states the substance of the matter — to declare God free of deficiency, that is, **to acknowledge that nothing happens without His willing it.**

So the point is not to utter a word but to recognise a fact. This is what was recorded in the discussion of the eighteenth verse.

### سُبْحَٰنَ رَبِّنَآ إِنَّا كُنَّا ظَٰلِمِينَ — a glorification come too late

**And now they glorify God.**

*Subḥāna rabbinā* — this was exactly what was wanted. But after the time for it has passed.

**And immediately after, a confession: *innā kunnā ẓālimīn* — "we have indeed been wrongdoers".**

**ظَلَمَ — root ظ-ل-م.** And the original sense of the root is **to put a thing somewhere other than its place**; *ẓulma* (darkness) is from this root too — light not being where it should be.

**So wrongdoing is putting a thing somewhere other than where it belongs.**

**And this is exactly what had happened in the narrative:** the poor man's due had been taken from the poor man's place and put in the place of the garden's owners.

**Note the form of the verb: *kunnā* — "we were".** A finding that looks back to the past, then. The men do not say "we did wrong"; they say **"we have been wrongdoers"** — they acknowledge not an act but a **state**.

**This form shows the depth of the confession, as my own reading:** to say "I did wrong" is to acknowledge an act; to say "I have been a wrong sort of man" is to acknowledge oneself. The second is heavier.

### يَتَلَٰوَمُونَ — reproaching one another

**Root: ل-و-م.** To reproach, to blame. *Lawm* — reproach; *lawwāma* — one who reproaches much (*al-nafs al-lawwāma* at al-Qiyāma 75/2).

**In Form VI: mutual reproach.**

**And this scene comes immediately after the confession. The order is worth noting:**

1. The one who warned recalls his warning (28)
2. Glorification and confession (29)
3. **Blaming one another** (30)
4. Confession again (31)

**So they oscillate between confession and blame.**

**This is a finding I record as my own reading:** it is the most familiar shape of behaviour after a disaster. First acceptance, then a search for someone responsible, then acceptance again. Unable to carry the weight of the offence, the mind tries to distribute it outwards.

**And a note from the discussion of al-Baqara echoes here:** for the verb *fa'ddāraʾtum fīhā* (you cast it upon one another) at 2/72 this finding was made: *"In a single sentence the social scene of a murder whose author cannot be found is drawn — everyone pushes the crime onto someone else."*

**The difference in al-Qalam is this:** there the offence was being covered up; here the offence has been admitted but **cannot be apportioned.**

### يَٰوَيْلَنَآ إِنَّا كُنَّا طَٰغِينَ — the second confession

**وَيْل — wayl.** Treated in the discussions of al-Humaza and al-Māʿūn: destruction, punishment, and a cry of anguish.

**طَغَىٰ — root ط-غ-ي.** Treated in the discussion of al-Fajr; I do not repeat it here. In summary: the concrete sense of the root is **water overflowing its vessel**; *ṭughyān* is the crossing of a limit.

**And the second confession uses a different word from the first:**

| Verse | Word | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| 29 | *ẓālimīn* | **We put a thing out of its place** — a violation of right |
| 31 | *ṭāghīn* | **We crossed the limit** — a violation of measure |

**The two words name two distinct faults, as my own reading:** the one states the offence committed against the poor man (wrongdoing), the other the attitude taken towards God (transgression).

**And the order is meaningful: first what was against a human being, then what was against God.** So it had been throughout the narrative — first the decision to keep out the poor man, then the oath without exception.

**One further observation:** the word *ṭāghīn* is from the same root as the word used in Sūrat al-Fajr for the destruction of peoples. The men in the narrative name themselves in the language of peoples that were destroyed.

### عَسَىٰ رَبُّنَآ أَن يُبْدِلَنَا — hope

**And at the end of the narrative there is a sentence of hope. This sets the narrative apart from the other accounts of destruction.**

**عَسَىٰ** — "it may be that". A verb in Arabic conveying expectation.

**بَدَّلَ — root ب-د-ل.** To change, to put another in place of a thing. *Badal* — that which takes a thing's place.

**رَٰغِبُون — root ر-غ-ب.** And the concrete sense of the root is **breadth**: *raghīb* — wide. *Raghba* is to turn towards a thing and to want it.

**And the preposition matters:** *rāghibūna **ilā*** — "we turn towards…". In Arabic *raghiba fī* (he desired a thing) and *raghiba ʿan* (he turned away from a thing) are opposites. Here *ilā* is used: a turning towards.

**But was this repentance accepted?**

**The Qurʾān does not say. And its not saying is worth noting.**

**The classical exegetes differ on this:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **It was accepted** | The narrative ends with a sentence of hope; and the Qurʾān generally accepts sincere repentance |
| **It was left undetermined** | The verse gives no outcome; the function of the narrative is to warn, not to give good news |
| **This is only a wish** | *ʿAsā* conveys no certainty; the men are hoping, and it is not said that it will happen |

**I adopt no preference.** The text is silent, and its silence may have a function.

**But this must be recorded, as my own reading:** that the narrative ends with a hope leaves a door open for the sūra's addressees — the rich of Mecca. Had the narrative ended "and they were destroyed", the example would have been closed. Ending as it does, the example stays open.

### كَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْعَذَابُ — the verdict of the narrative

**And the narrative closes with a verdict: *kadhālika'l-ʿadhāb* — "such is the punishment."**

**The brevity of the sentence is striking.** A narrative running to sixteen verses is summed up in four words.

**And then a comparison: *wa la-ʿadhābu'l-ākhirati akbar* — "the punishment of the hereafter is greater."**

That is, the whole disaster related in the narrative — a garden gone in a single night, the shock of the morning, the mutual recriminations — is positioned as a **small example**.

**لَوْ كَانُوا۟ يَعْلَمُونَ — "if only they knew".**

**The sentence has no apodosis.** In Arabic the answer to a *law* condition is sometimes left unsaid, and this is left for the hearer to complete.

If they had known, what then? It is not said.

**This gap is the same technique recorded for *al-ghāshiya* in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya:** what is not said becomes limitless.

### The narrative's function in the sūra: a mirror for the rich of Mecca

**Now we can gather up why the narrative is in this sūra.**

**The seventeenth verse had already made the link:** *"We tried them… as We tried."* The narrative, then, is a comparison.

**And the elements of the comparison correspond one by one:**

| The man in the portrait (10-16) | The owners of the garden (17-33) |
|---|---|
| *ḥallāf* — the great swearer (10) | *aqsamū* — they swore (17) |
| *mannāʿ li'l-khayr* — the withholder of good (12) | *lā yadkhulanna… miskīn* — let no poor man enter (24) |
| *an kāna dhā mālin wa banīn* — because he has wealth (14) | *ḥarth*, *janna* — men of property (17, 22) |
| He calls the verses tales (15) | He counts the favour his own labour |
| *sa-nasimuhu* — he will be branded (16) | *ka'l-ṣarīm* — the garden was branded (20) |

**Five items, five correspondences.** The narrative is the portrait in the form of a story.

**And in the Meccan context this is a direct mirror, as my own reading:** Mecca was a city grown rich by trade; there was an order in which wealth was directly bound to social standing. The narrative takes aim at that order's most invisible assumption: **the assumption that what you own is yours.**

**And the scale of the narrative matters:** what is related is not the destruction of a people but a family's garden. Not, that is, a disaster on the scale of Noah's flood.

**This brings the narrative closer.** When a people is destroyed the reader does not see himself in that people. But the loss of a garden can happen to anyone.

## 68/34-41 — إِنَّ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ جَنَّٰتِ ٱلنَّعِيمِ … أَمْ لَهُمْ شُرَكَآءُ

*Inna li'l-muttaqīna ʿinda rabbihim jannāti'l-naʿīm — a-fa-najʿalu'l-muslimīna ka'l-mujrimīn — mā lakum kayfa taḥkumūn — am lakum kitābun fīhi tadrusūn — inna lakum fīhi la-mā takhayyarūn — am lakum aymānun ʿalaynā bālighatun ilā yawmi'l-qiyāmati inna lakum la-mā taḥkumūn — salhum ayyuhum bi-dhālika zaʿīm — am lahum shurakāʾu fa'l-yaʾtū bi-shurakāʾihim in kānū ṣādiqīn*
"For those who guard themselves there are, with their Lord, gardens of bliss. Shall We then treat the Muslims like the criminals? What is the matter with you? How do you judge? Or have you a book of your own, in which you study? Is it written in it that whatever you choose shall be yours? Or have you oaths binding upon Us, running to the day of resurrection, that you shall have whatever you decree? Ask them which of them will stand surety for that. Or have they partners? Then let them bring their partners, if they are truthful."

The sūra leaves the narrative and enters a legal examination. And the examination proceeds by questions set one after another.

### جَنَّٰتِ ٱلنَّعِيمِ — the garden comes back

**I record this as an observation about the word:** what was destroyed in the narrative was a **garden** (*janna*). What is promised to the other party in the thirty-fourth verse is **gardens** (*jannāt*).

| | **The narrative (17-33)** | **Verse 34** |
|---|---|---|
| What is owned | *janna* — a garden | *jannātu'l-naʿīm* — gardens of bliss |
| Where | On the earth | *ʿinda rabbihim* — with their Lord |
| Its end | *ka'l-ṣarīm* — it turned black | Lasting |

**By using the same word in two different places the sūra sets up a comparison:** the garden that was clutched at is gone; the garden that will be given is *ʿinda rabbihim*.

**And the note *ʿinda rabbihim* matters:** the garden is not with them but **with their Lord**. That is, it is not yet in their hands — and precisely for that reason it cannot be lost.

**نَعِيم — root ن-ع-م.** The same root as the *niʿma* of the second verse; treated there: the concrete sense of the root is **softness**.

### أَفَنَجْعَلُ ٱلْمُسْلِمِينَ كَٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ — the main question

**This sentence is the axis of the sūra's section of examination.**

**And the logic of the question is this:** the other side is doing well in this world. They have wealth, sons, standing. The conclusion they may draw from this is: **we shall do well in the hereafter too.**

The Qurʾān records this inference elsewhere as well — the deniers' claim that "nothing will happen to us".

**The verse turns this claim into a question of logic: is it reasonable that both should be treated the same?**

**مُسْلِم — root س-ل-م.** And the original sense of the root is **soundness, freedom from defect, completeness**: *salima* — he was sound, he was saved. *Salām* — well-being; *silm* — peace.

*Aslama* (Form IV): **to hand oneself over, to give a thing over whole.** *Muslim* — one who hands himself over.

**مُجْرِم — root ج-ر-م.** And the concrete sense of the root is **to cut, to pluck off**: *jarama'l-nakhla* — he cut and gathered the dates. From there it passed to the sense of "crime"; *jurm* — a crime.

**And now an interesting proximity, as my own reading:** the verb *ṣarama* (to cut, to harvest) used in the narrative and the root *jarama* belong to the same field of meaning — both have to do with plucking fruit from the branch.

**I record this not as a claim of derivation but as an observation about a family of meaning.** The two roots are distinct; but that Arabic should derive its word for "crime" from "plucking off" is worth noting: a crime is the tearing of a thing from where it belongs. And this overlaps with the definition of *ẓulm* treated at the thirty-first verse.

**Setting the two words side by side is meaningful too:**

| | **مسلم** | **مجرم** |
|---|---|---|
| Sense of the root | Soundness, wholeness | **Plucking off** |
| Direction of the act | Handing oneself over | Pulling a thing away |

**So the opposition stands in the roots of the words as well:** the one is whole and gives, the other tears away.

### مَا لَكُمْ كَيْفَ تَحْكُمُونَ — "what is the matter with you?"

**مَا لَكُمْ** — a formula in Arabic conveying astonishment and rebuke: "what is the matter with you?", "what is wrong with you?"

**كَيْفَ تَحْكُمُونَ** — "how do you judge?"

**حَكَمَ — root ح-ك-م.** And the original sense of the root is **to hold back, to rein in**: *ḥakama'l-dābba* — he bridled the beast. *Ḥakama* — the bit put in a horse's mouth.

**Two words are born from here:**

- **حُكْم (ḥukm)** — a decision; the cutting short of a matter and binding it.
- **حِكْمَة (ḥikma)** — the knowledge that holds a person back from error.

**So to give a judgement is, at the root of the word, an act of *reining in*:** not letting a matter run loose but tying it somewhere.

**And the question of the verse is: by what authority do you tie it?**

**The verb is plural and imperfect: *taḥkumūn* — you keep giving judgement.** Not a single act of judging, then, but a settled habit.

**And the word will come again in the thirty-ninth verse:** *inna lakum la-mā **taḥkumūn*** — "you shall have whatever you decree". By using the same verb twice the sūra names the claim: these people are **giving judgements** — in their own favour.

### Four times "or" — the demand for proof

**Between verses 37 and 41 the word أَمْ (or) comes four times, and each time it eliminates a source of proof:**

| Verse | Question | Which source of proof |
|---|---|---|
| 37-38 | *am lakum kitābun fīhi tadrusūn* | **A written source** — revelation, a book |
| 39 | *am lakum aymānun ʿalaynā* | **A covenant** — a pledge taken from God |
| 40 | *salhum ayyuhum bi-dhālika zaʿīm* | **A guarantor** — someone who takes responsibility |
| 41 | *am lahum shurakāʾ* | **A partner/intermediary** — some other power |

**All four are eliminated. And the four cover every ground on which a claim could rest in that period:** book, contract, surety, protection.

**This is the structure of a legal interrogation, and I record it as my own reading:** the verses take up the other side's claim with the logic of a court — "what have you got?"

### أَمْ لَكُمْ كِتَٰبٌ فِيهِ تَدْرُسُونَ — book and study

**دَرَسَ — root د-ر-س.** And the concrete sense of the root is interesting: **to pass over a thing again and again, to wear it away until its traces are gone.** *Darasati'l-rīḥu'l-āthār* — the wind effaced the traces. *Dirās* — threshing grain on the floor, going over it.

**From here the sense of "study" is born: going over a text again and again.**

**And the idea of wearing away at the root of the word is instructive:** to learn is to go back and forth over a text so often that the text is worn. The Turkish word *ders* — lesson — comes from this root and carries the same idea.

**And now a link within the sūra, as my own reading:** in the first verse **the pen** and **what is written** were named. In the thirty-seventh verse the other side is asked: **have you too a book that you study?**

**The sūra puts its question to the other side from its own ground.** The claim to a written source is the ground exalted at the opening of the sūra. The other side is asked, "have you one as well?"

**إِنَّ لَكُمْ فِيهِ لَمَا تَخَيَّرُونَ — "is whatever you choose in it?"**

**تَخَيَّرَ — root خ-ي-ر.** To choose, to choose the better. In Form V: **to choose for oneself, to take what one likes.**

**And the irony of the sentence is sharp:** it cannot be written in a book that "whatever you want shall be yours" — because such a book is not a book but a wish-list.

**The verse pictures the other side's belief as a wish-list.** And it does not say so directly; it does it with a question.

### أَمْ لَكُمْ أَيْمَٰنٌ عَلَيْنَا بَٰلِغَةٌ — a pledge bound by oath

**أَيْمَان — root ي-م-ن.** *Yamīn* — the right hand; and from there "oath". In Arabic right hands were struck together when an agreement was made; the name of the oath comes from there.

**بَٰلِغَة — root ب-ل-غ.** To reach, to arrive, to come to maturity. *Bāligha* — reaching, definitive, firm.

**The construction: "firm oaths, binding upon Us, valid until the day of resurrection."**

**And a legal language is being used here: a contract with a term, binding, reinforced by oath.** Such a contract is made between human beings; the verse asks: **did you make one with Us?**

**One further link within the sūra:** in the tenth verse the portrait's first attribute was *ḥallāf* (the great swearer); in the seventeenth the owners of the garden swore. Now the other side is asked: **have you an oath taken from God?**

**Oaths in three places: their own oaths, the oath in the narrative, and the alleged divine oath.** The sūra takes up the idea of the oath three times from different directions. **I record this as my own reading.**

### سَلْهُمْ أَيُّهُم بِذَٰلِكَ زَعِيمٌ — who is the guarantor?

**زَعِيم — root ز-ع-م.** And the root has two senses:

1. **To claim, to assert without ground.** *Zaʿama* — he claimed (usually with a negative tone). *Zaʿm* — a groundless claim.
2. **To stand surety, to take responsibility.** *Zaʿīm* — the spokesman, the leader of a group; and **the guarantor.**

**The Qurʾān uses the second sense plainly in one place:** in the narrative of Joseph (Yūsuf), of the missing measure it is said *"and I am surety for it"* (*wa anā bihī **zaʿīm***) (Yūsuf 12/72).

**The relation between these two senses of the word is instructive, as my own inference:** the one who makes a claim has to stand behind his claim. A guarantor is one who stands behind his word. So the difference between *zaʿm* and *zaʿāma* is whether responsibility is taken up or not.

**And the question of the verse aims exactly at this:** you make the claim — but **who stands surety?**

**This is one of the hardest questions that can be asked in a dispute, and it holds today too:** is there anyone standing behind a claim who will pay the price if it turns out false?

### أَمْ لَهُمْ شُرَكَآءُ — partners

**شَرِيك — root ش-ر-ك.** To be a partner. *Shirk* — associating a partner with God; *sharika* — a partnership.

**And the demand of the verse is concrete: *fa'l-yaʾtū bi-shurakāʾihim* — "let them bring their partners."**

**So it is not an abstract argument but a demand for production.** The Qurʾān uses this method elsewhere too: the alleged intermediaries are asked to be **brought forward**.

**And the demand is itself an answer:** asking for a thing that cannot be brought shows that the thing does not exist.

### In kānū ṣādiqīn — "if they are truthful"

**And the section connects with a formula that occurred at the beginning of the sūra.**

This conditional clause (*in kānū ṣādiqīn*) comes often in the Qurʾān at the end of demands for proof addressed to deniers.

**And there is a symmetry here, as my own reading:** at the beginning of the sūra the other side was interrogating the Prophet's **mind**. From the thirty-fourth verse on, the sūra interrogates the other side's **proofs**.

| | **The other side's method** | **The sūra's method** |
|---|---|---|
| What it targets | The person (*majnūn*) | **The claim** (where is your proof?) |
| How | Labelling | Asking questions |
| Its answer | The outcome is awaited (5-7) | Concrete production is demanded |

**The sūra does not do to others what was done to it.**

## 68/42-43 — يَوْمَ يُكْشَفُ عَن سَاقٍ وَيُدْعَوْنَ إِلَى ٱلسُّجُودِ فَلَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ ۝ خَٰشِعَةً أَبْصَٰرُهُمْ تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ ۖ وَقَدْ كَانُوا۟ يُدْعَوْنَ إِلَى ٱلسُّجُودِ وَهُمْ سَٰلِمُونَ

*Yawma yukshafu ʿan sāqin wa yudʿawna ilā'l-sujūdi fa-lā yastaṭīʿūn — khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum tarhaquhum dhillah, wa qad kānū yudʿawna ilā'l-sujūdi wa hum sālimūn*
"On the day when the matter is laid bare and they are called to prostrate but cannot; their eyes cast down, humiliation covering them. Yet they used to be called to prostrate while they were sound and whole."

The most disputed verse in the sūra. And it touches on one of the most delicate subjects in the history of theology.

### يُكْشَفُ عَن سَاقٍ — an ambiguous expression

**First it is necessary to establish exactly what the text says, because the debate is often conducted over something the text does not say.**

**The literal data of the verse:**

1. **The verb is passive:** *yukshafu* — "it is uncovered", "it is laid bare". **No agent is stated.**
2. **The noun is indefinite:** *sāqin* — "a shin". **There is no definite article.**
3. **There is no construct:** it does not say *sāqihī* ("His shin"). **There is no annexation.**

**These three data stand in the text and this is the ground of the debate.** The verse does not say, literally, that a shin is ascribed to God.

### The idiom in Arabic

**And the word has an established idiomatic use in Arabic:**

**كَشَفَ عَن سَاقٍ / قَامَتِ ٱلْحَرْبُ عَلَى سَاقٍ** — "the matter has turned serious", "the situation has grown hot", "the joking is over."

**The origin of the idiom, as the lexicographers transmit it, is this:** among the old Arabs, when a man set about a serious task, when he prepared to flee or to fight, the skirt of his garment was gathered up and **the shin was bared.** A long garment hinders running; tucking it up is the sign of setting to work.

**So the idiom is born not from a body part but from a *movement of preparation*.** The English expression "to roll up one's sleeves" carries almost exactly the same picture — and no English speaker understands it as a statement about anyone's arms.

**This is a linguistic datum and needs recording.** But it does not by itself end the debate, because the question is how a scriptural text is to be handled.

### Two stances

**In this commentary I take no side. I transmit both stances as they are:**

| | **Tafwīḍ** (referral) | **Taʾwīl** (interpretation) |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Accepts the wording as it stands; refers its modality to God | Understands the wording according to its idiomatic/figurative use in Arabic |
| In this verse | "*Sāq* occurs in the text; God knows its meaning. We do not liken, nor do we empty it out" | "*Kashf ʿan sāq* means in Arabic 'the matter turning serious'; the verse describes the severity of that day" |
| Its ground | Not to get ahead of the text; the line transmitted as the stance of the early generations | The literal structure of the verse (passive verb, indefinite noun, absence of annexation) and the established idiom in Arabic |
| Its anxiety | If the door of interpretation is opened, the text dissolves | Clinging to the wording opens a door to likening God to creatures |

**Both stances agree on the principle that God does not resemble created things.** Where they part is over how the wording is to be handled — this is a difference of method, not a difference of creed.

**One further note is needed:** various reports are transmitted about this verse in the exegetical and hadith literature. **I do not transmit these reports, because I cannot vouch for their soundness or their wording.** The rule written into the method operates here directly: I do not ascribe to a source a saying of which I am not certain.

**And this I must say plainly:** to state no definite preference on this matter is not an evasion but a choice of method. Taking sides in theological disputes is not the business of this commentary; transmitting the disagreement accurately and honestly is.

### The function of the verse in its context

**Theological dispute aside, the function of the verse in its context is beyond dispute, and it is this that needs recording.**

The verse sets a scene: **they are called to prostrate and they cannot prostrate.**

**And this is where the whole argument of the sūra arrives.**

### وَيُدْعَوْنَ إِلَى ٱلسُّجُودِ فَلَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ — being unable to prostrate

**سَجَدَ — root س-ج-د.** To bow, to fall to the ground. And in the concrete sense of the root lies **the touching of the forehead to the ground**.

**فَلَا يَسْتَطِيعُونَ — "they cannot".** Root *ṭ-w-ʿ*; the same root as the *tuṭiʿ* of the eighth verse: **to comply willingly.**

**And now a link within the sūra, as my own reading:**

| Verse | Word | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | *fa-lā tuṭiʿi'l-mukadhdhibīn* | **Do not comply with them** (you could comply; do not) |
| 42 | *fa-lā yastaṭīʿūn* | **They cannot comply** (they want to comply; they cannot) |

**The same root, in two places, in two opposite conditions.** In the one there is the possibility of complying and it is forbidden; in the other there is the wish to comply and no possibility.

**I record this contrast as a datum of the text;** the link I make is interpretation.

**And the idea the verse produces is this: a capacity, unused, is lost.**

This runs along the same line as the principle treated in the discussion of al-Layl. The finding there was: *"a behaviour grows easier the more it is repeated… The verse positions this melting away as a punishment."*

Al-Qalam 68/42-43 shows the same principle from the other side: **what is not done becomes, in the end, impossible to do.**

### خَٰشِعَةً أَبْصَٰرُهُمْ — eyes cast down

**خَشَعَ — root خ-ش-ع.** This root was treated at length in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya (88/2); I do not repeat it here. In summary of the finding there: *"khushūʿ is a lowering visible from outside: the voice dropping, the neck bending, the eye falling."*

**And the principal finding of the discussion of al-Ghāshiya operates here exactly:**

> *"The difference lies in whether it is voluntary or not. The khushūʿ of prayer is a lowering that is chosen. The khushūʿ of that day is compulsory. The person is not lowering himself; he finds himself lowered."*

**Al-Qalam 68/42-43 is the plainest statement of that finding:** the person is called to prostrate (choice), but cannot do it (compulsion). And his eyes fall (compulsory *khushūʿ*).

**So the principle recorded in al-Ghāshiya — "there is no option of not bowing; only when to bow can be chosen" — has here turned into a scene.**

### تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ — humiliation covering them

**رَهِقَ — root ر-ه-ق.** To come over a thing and cover it, to overtake and envelop it. *Rahaq* — an oppression that covers, excess.

**And the direction of the verb is worth noting: the humiliation finds them; they do not go to the humiliation.** *Tarhaquhum* — humiliation envelops them.

**ذِلَّة — root ذ-ل-ل.** Treated in the discussion of al-Mulk 67/15: the root has two neighbouring senses — *dhull* (abasement) and *dhill* (docility).

**And now a link is made between the two sūras, as my own reading:**

| | **al-Mulk 67/15** | **al-Qalam 68/43** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | *dhalūl* — docile, made submissive | *dhillah* — humiliation, abasement |
| To whom | To the earth | To the deniers |
| Its character | **A favour** — the earth was made submissive to man | **A punishment** — man was abased |

**The same root, a favour in the one and a punishment in the other.** The root's two branches of meaning (docility and lowliness) carry this difference.

### وَقَدْ كَانُوا۟ يُدْعَوْنَ إِلَى ٱلسُّجُودِ وَهُمْ سَٰلِمُونَ — the real sentence

**And the heaviest sentence in the verse is this.**

*"Yet they used to be called to prostrate **while they were sound and whole**."*

**سَالِم — root س-ل-م.** The same root as the *muslim* of the thirty-fifth verse; treated there: **soundness, freedom from defect, completeness.**

**And this is one of the sūra's tightest internal links, as my own reading:**

| Verse | Word | Who | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| 35 | *al-muslimīn* | Those who hand themselves over | Those who **give** themselves |
| 43 | *sālimūn* | The deniers (in this world) | Those who counted themselves **sound** |

**The same root: one has handed over his soundness, the other has trusted in his soundness.**

**And the principle the sentence produces is this:** the call was made while the person's strength was intact. That is, the choice was placed before them at a time when a choice could be made.

**This is a note that looks to the sūra as a whole:** the men in the narrative had been warned too (verse 28: *"did I not say to you?"*). The verses were recited to the man in the portrait too (verse 15). The warning was always there.

**And the picture recorded in the discussion of al-Mulk echoes here:** there those in the Fire are asked *"did no warner come to you?"* and the answer *"yes, one came"* is received. The two sūras say the same thing: the problem is not a lack of information.

### What this says to the present

**The most practical idea the verse produces is the passage from "cannot" to "does not".**

A person's not doing something is mostly a choice. But as the choice is repeated, it ceases to be a choice.

**This is observable in daily life:** a skill long unused is lost; a relationship long unmade becomes impossible to make; a word long unsaid becomes unsayable.

**The verse carries this mechanism into the hereafter:** the person who does not bow by choice in this world cannot bow on that day.

**And the note *sālimūn* fixes where the responsibility lies:** the call was made while the person was strong. That his strength went afterwards does not mean the call did not reach him.

**A boundary:** I do not draw from this verse a general ruling of the kind "a late repentance is never accepted". The verse describes the day of reckoning; the Qurʾān has other statements about the door of repentance in this world, and they must be taken up in their own places.

## 68/44-47 — فَذَرْنِى وَمَن يُكَذِّبُ بِهَٰذَا ٱلْحَدِيثِ ۖ سَنَسْتَدْرِجُهُم مِّنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ ۝ وَأُمْلِى لَهُمْ ۚ إِنَّ كَيْدِى مَتِينٌ ۝ أَمْ تَسْـَٔلُهُمْ أَجْرًا فَهُم مِّن مَّغْرَمٍ مُّثْقَلُونَ ۝ أَمْ عِندَهُمُ ٱلْغَيْبُ فَهُمْ يَكْتُبُونَ

*Fa-dharnī wa man yukadhdhibu bi-hādhā'l-ḥadīth, sa-nastadrijuhum min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūn — wa umlī lahum, inna kaydī matīn — am tasʾaluhum ajran fa-hum min maghramin muthqalūn — am ʿindahumu'l-ghaybu fa-hum yaktubūn*
"Leave Me and the one who denies this discourse. We shall draw them on by degrees, from where they do not know. I give them respite; My plan is firm. Or are you asking them for a fee, so that they are weighed down by a debt? Or is the unseen with them, so that they write from it?"

### فَذَرْنِى — "leave Me"

**Root: و-ذ-ر.** To leave, to abandon. And this verb has an interesting property: **its perfect is not used** — one does not say *wadhara* in Arabic; *taraka* is used instead. Only the imperative and imperfect forms survive.

**And the expression itself is an idiom in Arabic: *dharnī wa fulānan* — "leave me alone with him."**

**Its sense:** "do not get involved; this is between him and Me."

**And the picture the sentence produces, as my own reading:** the Prophet is under a burden — persuading those he addresses, saving them. The verse takes that burden away.

**This runs along the same line as the finding recorded in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya:** *"You are only a reminder; you are not one who compels them"* (88/21-22) and *"their reckoning is Ours"* (88/26). The finding there was: *"not being a controller is not a deficiency but an exemption."*

**Sūrat al-Qalam grants the same exemption by a command: leave it.**

**هَٰذَا ٱلْحَدِيث — "this discourse".**

**حَدِيث — root ح-د-ث.** This root was treated in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya (88/1); I do not repeat it here. In summary of the finding there: *"the original sense of the root is to be new, to come about afterwards… From here it passed to 'word, news, account' — because news is the transmitting of what is new."*

**And the second finding recorded there operates here in particular:** *"ḥadīth is not a heavy word like 'book' or 'revelation'. It belongs to the spoken language, an everyday word."*

**And now a contrast, as my own reading:** in the fifteenth verse the denier called the Qurʾān *asāṭīr al-awwalīn* — the writings of **"the ancients"**. In the forty-fourth verse the Qurʾān calls itself *hādhā'l-**ḥadīth*** — **"this new discourse"**.

| | **68/15** | **68/44** |
|---|---|---|
| Who says it | The denier | The owner of the text |
| Word | *asāṭīru'l-**awwalīn*** | *hādhā'l-**ḥadīth*** |
| Sense of the root | **Old**, of those who came before | **New**, come afterwards |

**The two words are exact opposites and they name the same thing.** The claim is "this is old"; the answer is "this is new."

**I record this as my own reading;** but that two words with opposite senses are used of the same object in the same sūra is a datum of the text.

### سَنَسْتَدْرِجُهُم — being drawn on by degrees

**The sūra's most technical concept. Root: د-ر-ج.**

**The concrete sense of the root: a step, a degree, a rung.** *Daraja* — a step; *daraja* (verb) — to advance step by step, to climb by stages. *Durj* — a small box (things put in it by stages). *Idrāj* — to insert one thing inside another.

**In Form X (*istadraja*): to draw someone towards oneself by degrees, to catch him by bringing him nearer step by step.**

**And the picture of the word is this:** the hunter brings the quarry closer without startling it. At every step the quarry feels safe, because no single step looks dangerous. The danger is in the **sum** of the steps.

**مِّنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ — "from where they do not know".**

**This note completes the definition of being drawn on:** the person does not know he is being drawn. If he knew, it would not be a drawing-on at all.

**And from here comes the most uncomfortable side of the concept:** being drawn on does not feel like a bad thing. Matters go well, opportunities increase, no obstacle appears.

### The link with al-Aʿrāf 7/182-183

**This link was already made in the discussion of al-Layl, and the note there was this:**

> *"There is a counterpart to this within the Qurʾān: 'Those who deny Our verses We shall draw on by degrees, from where they do not know' (al-Aʿrāf 7/182; similarly at al-Qalam 68/44). There too the punishment is described not as an obstruction but as a flow."*

**Now I make good on that note and set the two texts side by side:**

| | **al-Aʿrāf 7/182-183** | **al-Qalam 68/44-45** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | *alladhīna kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā* | *man yukadhdhibu bi-hādhā'l-ḥadīth* |
| Verb | *sa-nastadrijuhum min ḥaythu lā yaʿlamūn* | **The same expression** |
| What follows | *wa umlī lahum, inna kaydī matīn* | **The same expression** |

**The two texts are almost word for word the same.**

**I record this as a datum; I make no claim that there is a deliberate reference from one sūra to the other** — the note made for a similar overlap in the discussion of al-Muṭaffifīn holds here as well.

**And the principal finding in the discussion of al-Layl gives the heart of the concept:**

> *"The form of the punishment is not the closing of the road but the opening of it… The miser is not made to find miserliness harder — he is made to find it easier… And the fine point here is this: the person does not experience it as a punishment. On the contrary, he experiences it as relief."*

**Being drawn on by degrees is the name of that principle.**

**And the last sentence in the discussion of al-Layl is worth repeating here:** *"both directions grow easier with time — and growing easier is no proof that the direction taken is the right one."*

### وَأُمْلِى لَهُمْ — respite

**Root: م-ل-و / م-ل-ي.** To give a long span, to allow ample time. *Malā* — a long time; *malawān* — night and day.

**And from the same root comes *imlāʾ*** — to dictate a text. The Turkish word *imlâ* comes from here.

**The relation between the two senses is explained by the lexicographers thus:** to dictate is to speak to another **while allowing him time** — to speak slowly so that he can write.

**And this sets up an echo with the sūra's opening with the pen, as my own reading:** the same root means both "to give respite" and "to dictate". The sūra began with writing; here respite is named by a word from the same root as dictation.

**I record this as an observation about the word;** I make no claim that the verse intends the association.

**The function of the respite:** giving a person time does two things at once — it opens a possibility of repentance and it makes the outcome heavier. The Qurʾān sets up this doubleness elsewhere too.

### إِنَّ كَيْدِى مَتِينٌ — "My plan is firm"

**This sentence is uncomfortable, and the source of the discomfort is the translation.**

**كَيْد — root ك-ي-د.** And the sense of the root is **hidden planning, taking measures without letting them be sensed**. It is rendered into English as "plot, scheme, guile", and these renderings load the word with a negative moral charge.

**In Arabic *kayd* is not necessarily evil.** The Qurʾān shows this plainly: in the narrative of Joseph, the plan laid in order to keep his brother back is named *kayd*, and that plan is one **God taught**: *"Thus did We teach Joseph a plan"* (the expression *kidnā li-Yūsuf* occurs in the frame of Yūsuf 12/76).

**So *kayd* holds no "injustice" in the word itself; it holds *concealment*.**

**And the logic of its use in the verse is this, as my own reading:** the other side lays hidden plans (the sūra has related whisperings, desires for compromise, efforts at entrapment). The verse answers by using the same word: **you have your plan, and I have mine.**

The Qurʾān often uses this matching of language: the other side's act comes back in the same word. An instance of this was recorded in the discussion of al-Humaza (the verb *nabz*).

**مَتِين — root م-ت-ن.** And the concrete sense of the root is **the back, the firm and thick part**: *matn* — the back of a thing, its body; and from there "firmness". In Turkish the same root yields both *metin* ("firm") and *metin* ("the main body of a work"); English covers these two senses with two unconnected words.

**So *kaydī matīn* — "My plan is one whose back is sound", that is, it does not collapse, it does not break.**

### أَمْ تَسْـَٔلُهُمْ أَجْرًا — "are you asking for a fee?"

**And the sūra asks two more questions. These questions are the continuation of the *am* series that ran between verses thirty-seven and forty-one.**

**The logic of the question: there may be reasonable grounds for people to refuse an invitation. The verse eliminates them one by one.**

**The first reasonable ground: cost.** If a price is being asked in return for the invitation, refusing it is intelligible.

**مَغْرَم — root غ-ر-م.** A debt that must be paid, compensation, loss. *Gharīm* — a debtor; *ghurm* — loss. And from the same root comes *gharām* — a passion that will not let a person go, that clings; the Qurʾān uses this for the punishment as well.

**The common idea of the root: a burden that clings and will not let go.**

**مُّثْقَلُون — root ث-ق-ل.** Weight. *Thiql* — weight, a load. *Muthqal* — left under a heavy load.

**And the answer itself is not in the sentence — because everyone knows it.** The Prophet was asking no fee. The Qurʾān says this plainly in many places.

**So the question is rhetorical, and it says this:** you have no reasonable ground for refusing. This is free.

### أَمْ عِندَهُمُ ٱلْغَيْبُ فَهُمْ يَكْتُبُونَ — the second question

**The second reasonable ground: counter-knowledge.** If they have better information in hand, refusing is intelligible.

**غَيْب — root غ-ي-ب.** The unseen, what cannot be reached by the senses. Treated in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/3.

**فَهُمْ يَكْتُبُونَ — "so that they write from it?"**

**And here is the loveliest turn in the sūra, as my own reading.**

The first verse: *wa mā **yasṭurūn*** — by what they write.
The forty-seventh verse: *fa-hum **yaktubūn*** — do they write?

**The sūra opens with a pen and closes its examination by asking the other side, "have you a pen too?"**

**And the question takes aim at the source of the claim:** the one who rejects the revelation must, in rejecting it, be relying on some knowledge. Where does that knowledge come from? Does he see the unseen and write it down?

**So the verse says this: denial too is a claim to knowledge, and the source of that claim is asked for as well.**

**This is the sharpest logical stroke in the sūra and it holds for today as well:** to claim that something does not exist requires a ground just as much as claiming that it does. An absence of knowledge does not establish a negative judgement either.

**And the sūra's three words for writing are completed here:**

| Verse | Word | Who | What |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *yasṭurūn* | Indefinite | An oath by what is written |
| 15 | *asāṭīr* | The denier | Counting the revelation old writing |
| 47 | *yaktubūn* | The denier (as a question) | **Has he a record of his own?** |

**Exactly such a table was set up in the discussion of al-Muṭaffifīn and this note was made:** *"I offer this as my own reading. That the three roots occur in the sūra is a datum of the text; the contrast I make between them is an interpretation."* **The same note holds here.**

## 68/48-50 — فَٱصْبِرْ لِحُكْمِ رَبِّكَ وَلَا تَكُن كَصَاحِبِ ٱلْحُوتِ إِذْ نَادَىٰ وَهُوَ مَكْظُومٌ ۝ لَّوْلَآ أَن تَدَٰرَكَهُۥ نِعْمَةٌ مِّن رَّبِّهِۦ لَنُبِذَ بِٱلْعَرَآءِ وَهُوَ مَذْمُومٌ ۝ فَٱجْتَبَٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ فَجَعَلَهُۥ مِنَ ٱلصَّٰلِحِينَ

*Fa'ṣbir li-ḥukmi rabbika wa lā takun ka-ṣāḥibi'l-ḥūt, idh nādā wa huwa makẓūm — lawlā an tadārakahu niʿmatun min rabbihī la-nubidha bi'l-ʿarāʾi wa huwa madhmūm — fa'jtabāhu rabbuhū fa-jaʿalahu mina'l-ṣāliḥīn*
"So be patient for the judgement of your Lord; and be not like the man of the fish, when he called out while he was choked with grief. Had a favour from his Lord not reached him, he would have been cast onto the bare ground, blamed. But his Lord chose him and made him one of the righteous."

Towards the end of the sūra a prophet is suddenly named. And not as a positive example.

### فَٱصْبِرْ لِحُكْمِ رَبِّكَ — the command to be patient

**صَبَرَ — root ص-ب-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root is **to hold a thing in a place, to confine it**: *ṣabara nafsahu* — he held himself back. *Ṣabr* is also the name of the bitter plant called aloes.

**So patience is, at the root of the word, an act of *holding*** — not a passive enduring but an active restraining.

**لِحُكْمِ رَبِّكَ — "for the judgement of your Lord".**

**And the preposition is worth noting: *li-* (for, towards).** Not "be patient until your Lord's judgement" but **"be patient for your Lord's judgement."**

**So patience is not a waiting but a *turning towards*.** The person is not waiting for time to pass; he is standing facing a judgement.

**حُكْم — root ح-ك-م.** Treated at the thirty-sixth verse: the original sense of the root is **to bridle, to rein in**.

**And a link within the sūra, as my own reading:** in the thirty-sixth verse the other side was asked *"how do you judge?"* In the forty-eighth the Prophet is told *"be patient for the judgement of your Lord."*

| Verse | Word | Who | What he is doing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 36, 39 | *taḥkumūn* | The deniers | **They give judgement** |
| 48 | *ḥukmi rabbika* | The Prophet | **He awaits the judgement** |

**Two sides, two different positions before the same root.**

### وَلَا تَكُن كَصَاحِبِ ٱلْحُوتِ — "be not like the man of the fish"

**And now the most striking thing in the verse: a prophet is named as a negative example.**

**This is rare in the Qurʾān and must be read carefully.**

**صَاحِب — root ص-ح-ب.** Treated in the discussion of al-Takwīr: to be together, to keep company.

**And the construct is odd: *ṣāḥib al-ḥūt* — "the companion/owner of the fish".** The Qurʾān names the same man elsewhere as *Dhū'l-Nūn* (the man of the fish) (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/87) and also by his name (Jonah, Yūnus).

**Here the name is not given.** Only the attribute.

**I think this is a fine point, as my own reading:** when a negative example is given, no name is named. In the fifty-first verse it will be said to the Prophet "they would almost fell you with their looks" — and there too the accusers go unnamed. The sūra names situations, not persons.

**حُوت — fish.** And the sūra's first letter was **ن**. That the word *nūn* means "fish" in Arabic was recorded in the discussion of the first verse — and it was stated there that this explanation was not adopted.

**Still, that the sūra opens with *nūn* and comes close to closing with *ḥūt* is worth recording as an observation.** I do not present it as exegesis; I note the overlap between the words.

### إِذْ نَادَىٰ وَهُوَ مَكْظُومٌ — choked with grief

**نَادَىٰ — root ن-د-و/ن-د-ي.** The same root as the *tanādaw* of the twenty-first verse: to call out.

**And what he called is not said here.** The Qurʾān says it elsewhere:

> *"And remember Dhū'l-Nūn, when he went off in anger… He called out in the darknesses: 'There is no god but You; glory be to You; I have indeed been one of the wrongdoers.'"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/87)

**And the last part of this sentence coincides exactly with the narrative in Sūrat al-Qalam:**

| | **The owners of the garden (68/29)** | **Jonah (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/87)** |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence | *subḥāna rabbinā innā kunnā ẓālimīn* | *subḥānaka innī kuntu mina'l-ẓālimīn* |
| Structure | Glorification + confession of wrongdoing | Glorification + confession of wrongdoing |

**The two sentences are almost identical. And both belong to two different stories named in the same sūra.**

**I record this as an observation; I make no claim that there is a deliberate reference from one text to the other.** But the overlap is a datum of the text and it is meaningful for the sūra as a whole: the men in the narrative and Jonah frame the same sentence.

**مَكْظُوم — root ك-ظ-م.** **And this is one of the loveliest words in the sūra.**

**The concrete sense of the root: *kaẓama'l-qirba* — he filled the waterskin and tied its mouth.** A vessel, that is, filled up and stopped.

**Its derivatives:**

- **كَظِيم (kaẓīm)** — one who has buried his grief inside. The Qurʾān uses it of Jacob (Yaʿqūb): *"His eyes turned white from grief; and he was filled within (**kaẓīm**)"* (Yūsuf 12/84).
- **كَاظِم (kāẓim)** — one who swallows his anger. The Qurʾān names it as a quality of those who guard themselves: *al-**kāẓimīna**'l-ghayẓ* — "those who swallow their anger" (Āl ʿImrān 3/134).
- **كِظَامَة (kiẓāma)** — the tie that binds the mouth of a thing.

**And now the picture of the word is complete: a waterskin filled to the brim and tied shut.** There is pressure inside; but it cannot get out.

**In the discussion of al-Mulk the word *ghayẓ* was treated** and this was recorded there: *"Ghayẓ is swallowed anger. The Qurʾān records this plainly: one of the qualities of those who guard themselves is al-kāẓimīna'l-ghayẓ — 'those who swallow their anger'."*

**And now a tension appears and must be handled honestly:**

**In one place the Qurʾān praises "swallowing one's anger" (Āl ʿImrān 3/134); here a prophet who "called out while choked with anger" is named as a negative example.**

**This is not a contradiction, but it needs explaining.**

| | **Āl ʿImrān 3/134** | **al-Qalam 68/48** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Those who guard themselves | Jonah |
| Anger against what | Anger against **people** | Distress at **a situation / a task** |
| Result | To forgive, to pass over | **To leave the task and go** |

**The difference lies not in the swallowing of the anger but in what is done after the anger has been swallowed.**

**And I write this as my own reading:** *kaẓm* is to fill a waterskin and tie it. A waterskin holds out only so far. The man in Āl ʿImrān swallows his anger and **stays**; Jonah swallows his anger and **goes.**

So the verse takes aim not at the emotion itself but at **the act the emotion displaced.**

**And the command of the verse confirms this: *fa'ṣbir* — be patient.** Patience is not the tying of the waterskin; it is **staying in place** while it is tied.

### The other pieces of the story of Jonah in the Qurʾān

**Sūrat al-Qalam does not tell the story; it refers to it. The pieces are given in other places in the Qurʾān.**

**The passages I am sure of:**

- *"And remember Dhū'l-Nūn…"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/87-88) — his going, his prayer in the darknesses, his rescue.
- *"And Jonah too was one of the messengers… The fish swallowed him… We cast him onto the bare ground, and he was sick"* (in the frame of al-Ṣāffāt 37/139-145). **And there this expression occurs: *fa-nabadhnāhu bi'l-ʿarāʾi wa huwa saqīm*.**
- *"If only there had been a town that believed… except the people of Jonah"* (Yūnus 10/98).

**Yūnus 10/98 is worth noting:** the people of Jonah are named in the Qurʾān as the only community that believed before the punishment came. **That is, the people the prophet left behind did believe.**

**This changes the whole of the story, as my own reading:** Jonah's reason for leaving was his conviction that his people would not be set right. The conviction turned out to be wrong.

**And from here comes the lesson the sūra gives the Prophet:** your conviction about the state of the other side cannot be a ground for leaving. You are not the one who knows the outcome.

### لَنُبِذَ بِٱلْعَرَآءِ وَهُوَ مَذْمُومٌ — being cast out

**نَبَذَ — root ن-ب-ذ.** This root was treated in the discussion of al-Humaza (104/4); I do not repeat it here. In summary of the finding there: *"to throw a thing away because one counts it worthless, to fling it aside as of no account… The distinguishing feature of the word is that it places a value judgement inside the act of throwing. Not every throwing is nabz; nabz is the counting of the thing thrown as useless."*

**And al-Ṣāffāt 37/145 was in the list recorded in the discussion of al-Humaza:** *"We cast him onto the bare open ground."*

**Now the two verses have to be set side by side, because there is a difference of one word and that word changes everything:**

| | **al-Ṣāffāt 37/145** | **al-Qalam 68/49** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *fa-nabadhnāhu* — We cast him (**it happened**) | *la-nubidha* — he would have been cast (**it did not happen**) |
| Place | *bi'l-ʿarāʾ* | *bi'l-ʿarāʾ* |
| State | *wa huwa **saqīm*** — he was sick | *wa huwa **madhmūm*** — blamed |

**The two verses describe the same scene but the last word differs. And the moods of the verbs differ too.**

**In al-Ṣāffāt the event happened: he was cast out, he was sick.**
**In al-Qalam the event did not happen: he would have been cast out, blamed — but a favour from his Lord reached him.**

**So the two verses do not contradict; they describe two different scenarios:**

- **What happened:** he was cast onto the bare ground, **sick**. (al-Ṣāffāt)
- **What did not happen:** he would have been cast onto the bare ground, **blamed**. (al-Qalam)

**What makes the difference is the conditional clause of the forty-ninth verse: *lawlā an tadārakahu niʿmatun min rabbihī* — "had a favour from his Lord not reached him."**

**I offer this reading as my own reading;** but the difference of mood between the two verses (perfect / condition with *lawlā*) is a datum of the text, and some of the classical exegetes too draw this distinction.

**تَدَٰرَكَ — root د-ر-ك.** To reach, to arrive, to overtake. *Idrāk* — to grasp (the mind reaching a thing); *darak* — the bottom of a thing.

**In Form VI (*tadāraka*): to arrive one after another, to reach just in time.** There is a picture in the word of **arriving at the last moment**.

**And a nearness of words: the *istidrāj* (د-ر-ج) of the forty-fourth verse and the *tadāruk* (د-ر-ك) here are from different roots** — their last letters differ. They must not be confused. But both carry an idea of "approaching/reaching", and they stand five verses apart in the sūra. **I record this as an association, not as a claim of derivation.**

**عَرَاء — root ع-ر-ي.** Bareness. *ʿArāʾ* — ground with nothing on it, treeless, shadeless, empty.

**The picture of the word: an open space with nothing to shelter in.**

**مَذْمُوم — root ذ-م-م.** To blame, to censure. *Dhamm* — blame (the opposite of praise). *Dhimma* — responsibility, the obligation of protection — and this second sense, though it looks like the opposite of the first, is from the same root: the holder of a *dhimma* is one who will be **blamed** if he does not protect.

### فَٱجْتَبَٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ — he was chosen

**Root: ج-ب-ي.** To collect, to gather together, to bring in. *Jibāya* — the collection of taxes; *jābī* — the collector.

**In Form VIII (*ijtabā*): to gather for oneself, to pick out and set apart.**

**And the fine point of the word is here, as my own reading:** *ijtibāʾ* is **gathering by selection** from among what is scattered. In the preceding verse the man "would have been cast onto the bare ground" — that is, scattered. In this verse he is gathered in.

**And the sense "to gather in" of the root *jabā* echoes the verb *ṣarama* (to harvest) of the narrative.** The owners of the garden went to gather and found nothing; Jonah is gathered.

**I record this as an observation about the word;** the two roots are distinct and I claim no deliberate link.

**فَجَعَلَهُۥ مِنَ ٱلصَّٰلِحِينَ — "and made him one of the righteous".**

**صَلَحَ — root ص-ل-ح.** And the sense of the root is **a thing's being serviceable, sound, fit for use**. *Ṣalāḥ* — soundness; *iṣlāḥ* — putting right; *ṣulḥ* — peace (the mending of a broken relationship). Its opposite is *fasād*.

**And the preposition *min* is worth noting: *mina'l-ṣāliḥīn* — "one of the righteous".** He is being included in a group.

**The structure of the verse announces an outcome:** the episode is closed, the man has returned to his place. And this completes the example the sūra gives the Prophet: **Jonah was named as a negative example, but he was not named with a bad end.**

**This fine point matters, as my own reading:** the verse criticises a prophet and in the same breath exalts him. The criticism is made not to bring the man down but to separate out the **behaviour**.

**And this is consistent with the sūra as a whole:** from the beginning the sūra counsels looking at the act, not at the person. Even the portrait of the other side is drawn without naming anyone.

### What this says to the present

**These three verses are the most personal place in the sūra and they contain a description of burnout.**

*Makẓūm* — full, and stopped up. This is the state of a person who has worked a long time without response: it has built up inside him, and he cannot pour it out.

**And what the verse does is not to condemn this state.** Jonah's anger is not censured; what is censured is **the departure the anger produced.**

**The difference matters and it works directly in daily life:**

**At work.** Labour that goes long without response produces exhaustion. Exhaustion is not a fault. The fault is the turning of exhaustion into a sudden break.

**In relationships.** Accumulated hurt produces, at some point, the sentence "enough". The sentence itself may be justified; but the moment it is said is generally the worst moment.

**In calling, in teaching.** The conviction that the other side will not change is the commonest ground for withdrawing. And the story of Jonah takes aim at exactly this conviction: his people believed.

**And the command the sūra gives is a single word: *fa'ṣbir* — be patient.**

And the definition of patience stands in the root of the word: **to hold oneself in a place.** Not to wait for the feeling to pass, but not to leave one's place while the feeling stays.

**One further note:** the verse does not say "do not be angry". Anger is found in a prophet in the forty-eighth verse and it is not named as a sin. What is commanded is not the absence of the feeling but **the continuation of the act.**

## 68/51-52 — وَإِن يَكَادُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ بِأَبْصَٰرِهِمْ لَمَّا سَمِعُوا۟ ٱلذِّكْرَ وَيَقُولُونَ إِنَّهُۥ لَمَجْنُونٌ ۝ وَمَا هُوَ إِلَّا ذِكْرٌ لِّلْعَٰلَمِينَ

*Wa in yakādu'lladhīna kafarū la-yuzliqūnaka bi-abṣārihim lammā samiʿū'l-dhikra wa yaqūlūna innahu la-majnūn — wa mā huwa illā dhikrun li'l-ʿālamīn*
"When they heard the Reminder, those who deny would almost have felled you with their looks, and they say, 'he is certainly possessed'. Yet it is nothing but a reminder for all beings."

### وَإِن يَكَادُ — "would almost"

**The verb كَادَ** was treated in the discussion of al-Mulk 67/8: *"kāda describes what does not happen. The man who says 'he almost fell' has not fallen."*

**And it is the same here: the looks do not fell the Prophet; they stop at the threshold of felling.**

**And there is an overlap between the two sūras, as my own reading:** at al-Mulk 67/8 the Fire "would almost burst apart with rage". At al-Qalam 68/51 the deniers "would almost fell you with their looks". **In both places the same verb, in the same function: a threshold that is not crossed.**

**إِن** is here taken not as the negative particle but as the lightened *inna*, and together with the *lām* it forms an emphasis: *wa in yakādu… **la**-yuzliqūnaka.*

### لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ بِأَبْصَٰرِهِمْ — to make slip with a look

**Root: ز-ل-ق.** And the concrete sense of the root is **to slip, to make slip**. *Zaliqa* — he slipped; *zalaq* — slippery ground, ground on which one cannot stand.

**The Qurʾān uses this word in another place:** at al-Kahf 18/40 it speaks of a garden becoming *ṣaʿīdan **zalaqā*** (slippery, bare soil).

**And this occurrence is worth noting, because it is akin to this very sūra's garden narrative:** in both garden narratives in al-Kahf too the garden is destroyed in a single night and turns into slippery ground.

**A variant reading:** the word has been read in the forms **لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ** and **لَيُزْهِقُونَكَ**; the second comes from the root *z-h-q* and gives the sense "to destroy, to take the life out of". **I record it, but since I cannot state with certainty which reading belongs to which authority, I write no name.**

**And what the expression describes is disputed. There are two main readings:**

| Reading | Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The evil eye** | The look actually doing harm; the reading adopted by most of classical exegesis | The literal sense of the word: to make slip with their looks |
| **A hostile look** | To look with rancour and anger; the look is itself a form of attack | The note *yakādu* (almost): the act does not occur; the expression may be a hyperbole |

**On this I state no definite preference, and I write the ground plainly:**

**The matter of the evil eye is a subject on which there are reports and which has a place in the tradition.** In this commentary it is neither denied nor set up as a doctrine — because either would be to say more than this verse says.

**What I can draw from the wording of the verse is this:** a look can be intense enough to unseat a person. And the verse records that that intensity **did not take effect** (*yakādu*).

**And I record one further observation, as my own reading:** the context of the verse is not the physical effect of a look but **being hemmed in**. Throughout the sūra the Prophet has been targeted with words (the charge of being possessed), pressed towards compromise (*idhān*), had a portrait drawn around him. The last verse describes the quietest form of pressure: **the look.**

**A word can be answered; a look cannot.**

### لَمَّا سَمِعُوا۟ ٱلذِّكْرَ — "when they heard the Reminder"

**And the cause of the look is stated: hearing.**

**سَمِعَ — root س-م-ع.** Treated in the discussion of al-Mulk 67/10, where this finding was made: *"The Qurʾān uses this word on two levels: the act of the ear, and acceptance."*

**And here the first level applies: they heard, but they did not accept.**

**It is worth comparing the sentence at al-Mulk 67/10:** there those in the Fire were saying *"had we listened or used our reason"*. Here, in this world, they hear — and their response is a look.

**What emerges when the two verses are read together is this, as my own reading:** the opposite of hearing is not deafness but **hostility**. Those in the Fire will say "we did not hear"; but they did hear, and they responded. Responding is the proof of hearing.

### وَيَقُولُونَ إِنَّهُۥ لَمَجْنُونٌ — the accusation comes back

**And the sūra closes with the word of its second verse.**

| Verse | Sentence |
|---|---|
| **68/2** | *mā anta bi-niʿmati rabbika bi-**majnūn*** — you are not possessed |
| **68/51** | *wa yaqūlūna innahu la-**majnūn*** — they say "he is certainly possessed" |

**The sūra opens with the same word and closes with the same word.** In Arabic rhetoric this structure is called **radd al-ʿajuz ʿalā'l-ṣadr** (returning the end to the beginning).

**And the difference between the two verses is one of mood:**

- **The second verse:** a negative declarative sentence — it rejects.
- **The fifty-first verse:** **doubly reinforced** with *inna* and the *lām* — the other side insists.

**So for fifty verses proofs have been set out, a portrait has been drawn, a narrative told, questions asked — and the other side still says the same thing. And louder.**

**I record this as my own reading and add the following:** the sūra's ending in this way is not a declaration of failure; it is a **record of reality**. Proofs are not obliged to persuade. And the sūra had already said this in the fifth verse: *"soon you will see, and they will see."*

### وَمَا هُوَ إِلَّا ذِكْرٌ لِّلْعَٰلَمِينَ — the last sentence

**And the last sentence of the sūra lifts the whole dispute off its ground.**

**The structure of the sentence: *mā… illā* — restriction (*ḥaṣr*).** "It is nothing but…"

**ذِكْر — root ذ-ك-ر.** To mention, to remember, to call to mind. And beneath the root lie **holding in memory and uttering**.

**And the word occurs twice in these two verses:**

- 51: *lammā samiʿū'l-**dhikr*** — when they heard the Reminder.
- 52: *wa mā huwa illā **dhikrun*** — it is nothing but a reminder.

**The same word: the one the name of what they heard, the other the description of that thing.**

**And now what the sentence does has to be seen.**

**Throughout the sūra the dispute has run through a person:** is he possessed or not? What is his character like? Is he asking a fee?

**The last sentence changes the subject: *wa mā huwa* — "it".**

And who is "it"? Not the Prophet — **the message.**

| | **Throughout the sūra** | **The last verse** |
|---|---|---|
| What is disputed | The person (is he possessed?) | **The message** (what is it?) |
| Its scope | Mecca, those addressed | ***li'l-ʿālamīn*** — all beings |

**The sūra opens with a personal accusation and closes with a universal description.**

**ٱلْعَٰلَمِين — all beings.** Treated in the discussion of al-Fātiḥa.

**And this phrase is used in another place in the Qurʾān of the Prophet:** *"We have sent you only as a mercy for all beings"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/107).

**Setting the two verses side by side is instructive:**

| | **al-Anbiyāʾ 21/107** | **al-Qalam 68/52** |
|---|---|---|
| What | *raḥmatan li'l-ʿālamīn* | *dhikrun li'l-ʿālamīn* |
| For whom / of what | **The messenger** | **The message** |

**Both have the same scope: all beings.** The one describes the one sent, the other what is sent.

### The opening and the close of the sūra

**I record this as an observation:**

| **68/1** | **68/52** |
|---|---|
| *nūn, wa'l-qalami wa mā yasṭurūn* | *wa mā huwa illā dhikrun li'l-ʿālamīn* |
| An oath by what is **written** | A description as what is **remembered** |
| Record | Reminder |

**The sūra opens with writing and closes with remembrance. And the two are two faces of one work:** writing is remembrance taken outside. The pen records, the reminder recalls.

**The finding recorded in the discussion of al-ʿAlaq is completed here:** *"In an oral culture a word lives as long as those who memorised it live. Writing changes this: knowledge becomes independent of the lifespan of the one who knows."*

**Sūrat al-Qalam turns this into a circle:** what is written is a reminder for all beings. The record makes universality possible.

**I offer this as my own reading;** but the word-choices of the first and last verses are a datum of the text.

## The sūra as a whole

### Two portraits, two measures

**The structure of the sūra is built on a comparison, and it needs gathering into a table:**

| | **The Prophet (2-4)** | **The opponent (10-16)** |
|---|---|---|
| Mind | *mā anta bi-majnūn* | (not in dispute) |
| Character | *khuluqun ʿaẓīm* — **one word** | Nine attributes |
| Source | *bi-niʿmati rabbika* — given | *dhā mālin wa banīn* — possessed |
| Return | *ajran ghayra mamnūn* — not cut off | *sa-nasimuhu* — a brand |
| Word | *wa mā yasṭurūn* — what is written | *asāṭīru'l-awwalīn* — rejected as old writing |

**The sūra leaves the measure the other side uses (the mind) and proposes another: character.**

**And the reason for this, as my own reading:** a claim about the mind cannot be measured; character can be observed. The sūra carries the dispute onto measurable ground.

### Pen, brand, record: the objects of the sūra

**There is a theme that recurs in the sūra and I record it as my own reading: *leaving a trace*.**

| Verse | Word | Object | Who leaves it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *qalam*, *yasṭurūn* | Writing | Indefinite |
| 13 | *zanīm* | The mark on the ear | (the trace of a lineage) |
| 15 | *asāṭīr* | Old writings | Those who came before |
| 16 | *sa-nasimuhu* | A brand on the nose | God |
| 37 | *kitābun fīhi tadrusūn* | A book | (none — a question) |
| 47 | *yaktubūn* | Writing | (none — a question) |
| 52 | *dhikr* | A reminder | God |

**In seven distinct places through the sūra a record or a trace is at issue.** And the distribution is meaningful: on the side of the sūra's owner there are real records (pen, brand, reminder), and on the other side records that **do not exist** (have you a book? do you write?).

**This gives the shape of the sūra's argument:** the dispute runs on the question of what each side has in hand.

### The place of the narrative: the function of the example

**The expression *kamā balawnā* (as We tried) in the seventeenth verse sets the narrative up as a comparison.** And this comparison has been placed in the middle of the sūra.

**The structure is as follows:**

1. **Accusation and answer** (1-7) — abstract
2. **The offer of compromise and the portrait** (8-16) — a type
3. **The narrative** (17-33) — **a concrete example**
4. **Examination** (34-47) — abstract
5. **Jonah and the close** (48-52) — a concrete example

**The sūra goes from the abstract to the concrete, then back to the abstract, then to the concrete again. Two narratives, between two abstract sections.**

**And the function of the two narratives is opposite, as my own reading:**

| | **The garden narrative (17-33)** | **The narrative of Jonah (48-50)** |
|---|---|---|
| An example for whom | **Those addressed** | **The Prophet** |
| What it says | "Do not be like this" | "Do not be like this" |
| The fault | Keeping out the poor man, the oath without exception | Leaving the task |
| The end | *ʿasā rabbunā* — hope | *fa'jtabāhu* — he was chosen |

**Both narratives contain a warning and both end with a hope or a deliverance.**

**And the balance of the sūra lies here:** a warning is given to the other side and to its own side alike. The sūra is not a one-sided piece of self-defence.

### The main idea of the sūra

**I gather here the reading I stated at the outset, and I state again that it is my own reading:**

**Sūrat al-Qalam is a dispute about reputation, and it resolves the dispute by changing the ground.**

The other side takes aim at the person: *majnūn*. This is an accusation hard to answer — because the defence can be counted as proof of the accusation.

**The sūra does three things:**

**One. It rejects the accusation but does not return it.** The sixth verse does not say "it is you"; it says "it will be seen in which of you it lies".

**Two. It changes the measure.** Character in place of the mind. And because character can be observed, the dispute ceases to be closed.

**Three. It defers the verdict.** *Fa-sa-tubṣiru wa yubṣirūn* — you will both see.

**And all of this is framed with a pen:** the sūra opens by swearing by writing, and the greatest property of writing is that it can carry a verdict forward in time. What is written stays put after the dispute is over.

### The question of the poor: where three sūras meet

**The offence in the sūra's narrative attaches to one of the Qurʾān's most persistent themes, and this needs gathering up.**

| Sūra | Expression | Stage |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Māʿūn 107/2** | *yaduʿʿu'l-yatīm* — he repels the orphan | The one who comes is turned away |
| **al-Māʿūn 107/3** | *lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi'l-miskīn* | Not even the urging is done |
| **al-Māʿūn 107/7** | *yamnaʿūna'l-māʿūn* | Not even the smallest thing is given |
| **al-Fajr 89/17-18** | *lā tukrimūna*, *lā taḥāḍḍūna* | Inaction |
| **al-Qalam 68/24** | *lā yadkhulanna… miskīn* | **His coming is prevented** |

**Five verses, five stages. And the one in al-Qalam is the furthest:** the arising of the request is prevented.

**And the common structure of the three sūras is this:** in all of them the relation established with the poor is used as a **sign of belief**. In al-Māʿūn the one who denies the religion; in al-Fajr the one who leaves the day of reckoning out of his account; in al-Qalam the one who counts the verses tales.

**The finding recorded in the discussion of al-Māʿūn holds here too:** *"great claims are tested in small things."*

## What this says to the present — a general assessment

### First: the dispute that targets the person

**The sūra's opening problem is still today the commonest form of disputation: arguing about the one who makes a claim rather than about the claim.**

And the attraction of this form is plain — it is easier, it produces quicker results, and it leaves a stronger impression on the audience.

**The three answers the sūra gives can be used today as well:**

**One. Reject the accusation, but do not use the same method.** The sūra does not declare the other side "mad".

**Two. Move to measurable ground.** Mental health is disputable; behaviour can be observed.

**Three. Leave the verdict to time.** And this is possible only for someone who believes that time will bring a verdict.

### Second: *idhān* — oiling the rough places

**The word treated in the eighth and ninth verses is the sūra's most direct look at the present.**

*Idhān* is **making a difference invisible without removing it.**

**And its danger lies in how good it feels.** When a dispute softens everyone relaxes; and relaxation feels like a sign that the right thing has been done.

**The measure the sūra sets is this: look at the source of the relief.** Did you relax because agreement was reached, or because the matter was not discussed?

**And the boundary must be repeated:** the verse rejects not compromise but the concealment of the difference. Courtesy is not *idhān*; silence is not always *idhān*. *Idhān* is knowing that a thing is wrong and leaving the impression that it is not wrong.

### Third: the nine attributes and the veil of wealth

**The fourteenth verse is the sūra's most sociological sentence: nine faults are tolerated thanks to wealth and sons.**

**And this is a measurable observation:** how much a person's faults are talked about is inversely proportional to that person's power.

**What the sūra does is to read the list anyway.** And to read it without naming a name.

**The caution from the discussion of al-Humaza must be repeated here:** to read this portrait and fit one's acquaintances into it is the very act the portrait describes. Four of the nine attributes are faults committed with the tongue: swearing, faultfinding, talebearing.

**The portrait is applied first to oneself.**

### Fourth: "if God wills" — a note about reality

**The eighteenth verse describes an omission of a single sentence, and that sentence is one of the most worn-out religious expressions there is today.**

**What was missing in the narrative was not a formula but a note:** the future is not in my hands.

**And this note has a practical function, as my own reading:** plans built in the mood of certainty destroy a person when they fail — because he has regarded the plan as a right. Plans built with the note produce disappointment when they fail, but not collapse.

The reaction of the men in the narrative shows this: first denial ("we have come to the wrong place"), then grievance ("we have been deprived"), then blame, then acceptance.

### Fifth: being drawn on by degrees — when things go well

**The forty-fourth verse gives the sūra's most uncomfortable concept: that a person's prospering may be a punishment.**

**And the concept's present-day counterpart is direct:** success is counted a proof that a road is the right one. "If it works, it is right" is one of the commonest criteria of modern life.

**Being drawn on by degrees annuls that criterion.** That a thing works does not show that it is right.

**And the finding recorded in the discussion of al-Layl is worth repeating here:** *"growing easier is no proof that the direction taken is the right one."*

**A boundary has to be drawn, because this concept is easily abused:** being drawn on by degrees is not an instrument for interpreting other people's success. To look at a man whose affairs are going well and say "this may be a drawing-on" is the very posture of judgement the sūra forbids.

**The concept is given for a person to look at his own condition.** And the question a person can ask about his own condition is this: where is this course bringing me closer to?

### Sixth: Jonah — the threshold of leaving

**The forty-eighth verse is the most human place in the sūra and it is a description of burnout.**

*Makẓūm* — full, and stopped up.

**And the most striking thing about the verse is that it does not blame the feeling.** Jonah's anger is not named as a sin. What is censured is the **departure** the anger produced.

**And Yūnus 10/98 completes this:** the people he left believed.

**The concrete measure that follows is this, as my own reading:** if a decision to withdraw from a work rests on a conviction about the other side, care is needed — because the truth of that conviction is not known.

Exhaustion is real and legitimate. But the conviction exhaustion produces ("these people will not change") is less reliable than the exhaustion itself.

### Seventh: what the last sentence does

**For fifty-one verses the sūra defended a person. In the fifty-second it changed the subject.**

*Wa mā huwa illā dhikrun li'l-ʿālamīn.*

**And this is the soundest way to close a dispute:** to carry the matter from the person to the message.

**Because a person can be disputed — and the dispute does not end.** The message, on the other hand, stands on its own; whose it is, who carries it, what kind of man the carrier is, are separate questions.

**The sūra opened with a pen and closed with a reminder.** Record and remembrance. Both outlast the lifespan of a person.

## Where this section does not speak with certainty

At several points in the text definite language was deliberately not used; the reader needs to know them:

- **No occasion of revelation was given.** It was stated that there are reports that the portrait in verses 10-13 means a particular person, but **no name was written,** because I cannot vouch for the soundness of the reports. And per the method: what the verse describes are **attributes.**
- **The order of revelation was not used as a proof;** it was recorded that in the widespread lists the sūra comes after al-ʿAlaq, and it was stated that the precision of those lists is disputed.
- **Views that part of the sūra came down later** were noted but not transmitted, because I cannot vouch for them.
- **For the meaning of the letter نٓ** the note at al-Baqara 2/1 was relied on: **it is not known with certainty.** The "fish" and "inkwell" explanations were recorded but **not adopted;** the *nūn/ḥūt* overlap within the sūra was noted as an observation, not presented as exegesis.
- **The observation about sound** (that all fifty-two verses end in *nūn* or *mīm*) was recorded not as a claim of a miracle or a numerical reckoning but as a countable fact.
- **The subject of the verb وَمَا يَسْطُرُونَ** is disputed; four views were given, an inclination was stated, and it was written that it is not binding.
- **The two senses of غَيْرَ مَمْنُونٍ** (not cut off / not held over one's head) were given and **no preference was adopted;** it was stated that both are genuine senses of the root.
- **The construction بِأَييِّكُمُ ٱلْمَفْتُونُ** is disputed grammatically; four resolutions were given, a preference was stated with its ground, and it was recorded that it is not binding.
- **The variant reading in the expression أَن كَانَ ذَا مَالٍ** (statement / question) was recorded, but **which reading belongs to which authority was not written.** The same note holds for the **لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ / لَيُزْهِقُونَكَ** difference.
- **Where the sentence أَن كَانَ attaches** is disputed; three views were given, a preference was stated, and it was written that it is not binding.
- **In treating the meaning of the word زَنِيم,** the explanation that gives the word the sense "born illegitimate" was recorded as a disagreement but **was not dwelt on;** the ground was written plainly: **the Qurʾān condemns no one for his birth.** The reading built on the word's sense of "attached, joined later" was offered as **my own inference**.
- **The time of سَنَسِمُهُ** (in this world / in the hereafter / figurative) is disputed; three views were given and **no preference was adopted.**
- **What وَلَا يَسْتَثْنُونَ describes** is disputed; two views were given with their grounds, a preference was stated with its ground, and it was written that it is not binding.
- **What the *ṭāʾif* of فَطَافَ عَلَيْهَا طَائِفٌ was** — the classical conjectures about it **were not transmitted,** because the verse does not say and its not saying looks deliberate.
- **On the meaning of the word كَٱلصَّرِيمِ** four views were given and **no preference was adopted;** it was stated that all of them come to the same outcome.
- **The meaning of the word حَرْد** is disputed; four descriptions were given, a preference was stated with its ground, and it was written that it is not binding.
- **The meaning of أَوْسَطُهُمْ** (middle in age / most equitable) is disputed; two views were given, a preference was stated with its ground, and it was recorded that it is not binding.
- **What لَوْلَا تُسَبِّحُونَ corresponds to** is disputed; two views were given, a preference was stated, and it was written that it is not binding.
- On **whether the repentance in the garden narrative was accepted**, **no preference was adopted;** it was recorded that the text is silent.
- **The place and the community of the narrative** — the reports about them **were not transmitted,** because I cannot vouch for them.
- **In the verse يَوْمَ يُكْشَفُ عَن سَاقٍ no theological side was taken.** The literal data of the verse (passive verb, indefinite noun, absence of annexation) were recorded; the idiomatic use in Arabic was transmitted as a linguistic datum; **the stances of tafwīḍ and taʾwīl were given in a table and no preference was adopted between them.** The reports on this matter **were not transmitted,** because I cannot vouch for their soundness or their wording.
- **In the expression لَيُزْلِقُونَكَ بِأَبْصَارِهِمْ** on the matter of the evil eye **no definite stance was taken;** two readings were given, it was neither denied nor set up as a doctrine, and the ground was written plainly.
- **The overlaps established with al-Kahf 18/23-24, al-Aʿrāf 7/182-183, al-Ṣāffāt 37/145 and al-Muṭaffifīn 83/12-13** were each recorded as **data**; no claim was made that there is a deliberate reference from one text to another.
