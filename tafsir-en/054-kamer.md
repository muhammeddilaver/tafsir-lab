# 54. Sūrat al-Qamar

Fifty-five verses. Meccan. It takes its name from the last word of the first verse: **ٱلْقَمَر** — the moon.

This sūra is a **refrain sūra**, and the third of them in this commentary. In the section on al-Mursalāt it was shown that a sentence of judgement repeated ten times divides the text into nine parts; in the section on al-Raḥmān it was treated how a question repeated thirty-one times sets the sūra on a double rhythm from beginning to end. Al-Qamar uses the same technique with **two separate refrains**, and at a far lower density than either of the earlier two.

The two refrains are these:

> **وَلَقَدْ يَسَّرْنَا ٱلْقُرْءَانَ لِلذِّكْرِ فَهَلْ مِن مُّدَّكِرٍ**
> *"We have indeed made the Qurʾān easy for remembrance; is there anyone who will remember?"* — **17, 22, 32, 40**

> **فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ**
> *"How then were My punishment and My warnings!"* — **16, 18, 21, 30**

But these two lists are incomplete, and until the incompleteness is recorded the structure of the sūra does not come into view. The **second half** of the first refrain — *fa-hal min muddakir* — occurs **six** times in the sūra: four inside the full refrain (17, 22, 32, 40), and two at the end of another sentence (15 and 51). The **second half** of the second refrain — *ʿadhābī wa nudhur* — likewise occurs **six** times: four in the form of a question (*fa-kayfa kāna*, 16, 18, 21, 30), and two in the form of a command (*fa-dhūqū*, 37, 39).

So the sūra has not two refrains but **two refrain cores**, each of which returns six times. I give the table of this below.

The second defining feature of the sūra lies in its content. Al-Qamar is the Qurʾān's **list of the peoples destroyed**: Noah (Nūḥ), ʿĀd, Thamūd, Lot (Lūṭ) and the house of Pharaoh. Five peoples, thirty-four verses. And not one of these five accounts is told here from beginning to end; each of them is told at length elsewhere in the Qurʾān. What happens here is **the drawing up of a list** — and at the end of the list the text turns to the one addressed: *"Are your disbelievers better than those?"* (43).

The third feature is in the sound, and I take it up in a separate section: **all fifty-five verses of the sūra end with the letter ر.** In the Qurʾān, so unbroken a unity of verse-ending across so long a sūra is rare.

Since the basmala was treated in earlier sections it is not taken up again here; the verses are numbered without it.

---

## Meccan or Medinan

There is wide agreement that the sūra is Meccan. The style supports this: short verses, a single-sounding verse-ending, accounts of destroyed peoples, a focus on resurrection and reckoning, the absence of any legal ruling, and the position of the one addressed as *mukadhdhib* (one who denies).

In some sources it is transmitted that **verse 45** (*"That host shall be routed, and they shall turn their backs in flight"*) was revealed at Medina, or that it points to Badr. That is a separate matter and I take it up under that verse; it does not change the judgement about the sūra as a whole.

**The number of verses** in the common counting is fifty-five.

---

Looking at a sūra's verse-ending (the sound that closes each verse) is something this commentary has done from the start. In al-Qamar the map of verse-endings is easy to draw, because **it has no exceptions.**

**The final letter of all fifty-five is ر.** And more than that: nearly all of them close on a single syllable of the form *-ur / -ir / -ar*.

This has two consequences, and both of them govern how the text is read aloud.

**One — the verse-ending is selecting the word-forms.** In several places in the sūra one can see that, of two words that could have given the same sense, the one ending in ر has been chosen:

| Verse | The form in the sūra | The other form in the Qurʾān | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | *yawmun **ʿasir*** | *yawmun **ʿasīr*** (al-Muddaththir 74/9) | Quasi-participle / intensive form |
| 16, 18, 21, 30, 37, 39 | ***nudhur*** (with the yāʾ dropped) | *nudhurī* — this is the original | Elision of the yāʾ of annexation |
| 45 | ***al-dubur*** (singular) | *adbārahum* (plural, al-Anfāl 8/15-16) | Generic noun in the singular |
| 54 | ***nahar*** (singular) | *anhār* (plural, everywhere else) | Treated separately below |

**The elision of the yāʾ of annexation** deserves attention, because it concerns the most frequently repeated word of the sūra. The *nudhur* of the phrase *ʿadhābī wa nudhur* is in fact *nudhurī* — "my warnings". The final *yāʾ* has been dropped for the sake of the verse-ending, and a kasra is left behind: **نُذُرِ**. The same elision, for the same reason, appears twice in Sūrat al-Mulk: *kayfa kāna **nakīr*** (67/18) and *kayfa kāna **nadhīr*** (67/17) — both are in fact *nakīrī* and *nadhīrī*. This was treated in the section on al-Mulk.

So there is an interesting situation: **the pronoun "my" is erased from the writing for the sake of the rhyme, but stands in the meaning.** Two words side by side: *ʿadhābī* — its yāʾ in place; *nudhur* — its yāʾ dropped. In the same sentence, with the same owner, visible in one word and invisible in the other.

**Two — the sūra is built to be recited in a single breath.** Because the verse-ending does not change, the reciter descends on the same sound for fifty-five verses. The refrains too end on that same sound (*nudhur*, *muddakir*). That is, between a refrain verse and a non-refrain verse **there is no boundary in sound**; the boundary is in the wording alone.

This must be compared with al-Raḥmān. There the refrain had a sound of its own — *tukadhdhibān* — which was in tune with the sūra's general dual sound. In al-Qamar the refrain has no separate sound; it dissolves into the sound of the sūra.

**I record this as my own reading**, but its ground is the table above and can be verified: in al-Qamar you have to look at the wording to notice the refrain; in al-Raḥmān the ear is enough.

**One caveat.** I draw no claim of a miracle from this unity of verse-ending. Rhyme of this kind is a widespread technique in Arabic poetry and in rhymed prose, and in the Qurʾān every sūra has an order of its own. What is worth recording here is the **absence of exceptions** and the **length of the sūra**; both are countable data of the text.

## The structure of the sūra

| Section | Verses | What it does | Closing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opening** | 1-3 | The Hour has drawn near, the moon has split; turning away; *"every matter comes to rest"* | — |
| **The state of the warning** | 4-5 | The reports have come, the wisdom is complete; yet the warnings do not avail | — |
| **The scene of rising** | 6-8 | The caller calls; the emergence from the graves like locusts | — |
| **I. Noah** | 9-14 | They denied; the prayer; the flood from two waters; the ship | 15 + **16** + **17** |
| **II. ʿĀd** | 18-20 | **Opens with the refrain**; the *ṣarṣar* wind; people torn up | **21** + **22** |
| **III. Thamūd** | 23-29, 31 | Objection to the messenger; the she-camel and the turns at the water; the hamstringing; a single cry | **30** (in the middle) + **32** |
| **IV. Lot** | 33-38 | The *ḥāṣib*; the family's rescue at daybreak; the wiping out of eyes | **37**, **39** + **40** |
| **V. The house of Pharaoh** | 41-42 | Two verses. The warnings came; they denied them all; the seizure | **no refrain** |
| **Return to the one addressed** | 43-48 | *"Are your disbelievers better?"*; the rout of the host; the Hour | — |
| **Measure and command** | 49-50 | Everything by a measure; the command is one, like the blink of an eye | — |
| **The record** | 51-53 | Your likes have been destroyed; everything is written down line by line | 51 (partial) |
| **Closing** | 54-55 | The godfearing in gardens; in the presence of *a Sovereign, All-Powerful* | — |

Three things emerge from the table.

**One — the fifth account has no refrain.** Four accounts close with *wa-laqad yassarnā*; the fifth does not. I take up separately, under verses 41-42, what this corresponds to in the structure of the sūra.

**Two — the section on reward is two verses long.** Of fifty-five verses, fifty-three are warning, punishment, account and reckoning; two are garden. In al-Raḥmān thirty-two verses were garden. **Two refrain sūras have placed the weight at exactly opposite ends.**

**Three — the accounts are not equal in length, and the sequence is not a descending curve:** Noah 6 verses, ʿĀd 3 verses, Thamūd 7 verses, Lot 6 verses, Pharaoh 2 verses. The longest account is thus in the middle (Thamūd), the shortest at the end (Pharaoh). The list shows no smooth diminution; there is **an abrupt cut at the end**.

## The placement of the refrains — the full table

The table below shows which refrain opens each account, which closes it, and where in the account the refrain falls. This table is the real map of the sūra.

| Account | Opening sentence | Where the punishment refrain falls | The remembrance refrain | Position of the refrain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Noah** (9-14) | *kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin* (9) | **16** — after the account | 15 *(partial)* + **17** *(full)* | At the end, both in turn |
| **ʿĀd** (18-20) | *kadhdhabat ʿĀdun* (18) | **18** *(inside the opening)* and **21** | **22** | **At the beginning and at the end — the account is enclosed** |
| **Thamūd** (23-31) | *kadhdhabat Thamūdu bi'l-nudhur* (23) | **30** | **32** | **BEFORE the description of the punishment** |
| **Lot** (33-38) | *kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭin bi'l-nudhur* (33) | **37** and **39** — *fa-dhūqū* | **40** | Inside the account, twice, **in the imperative** |
| **The house of Pharaoh** (41-42) | *wa-laqad jāʾa āla Firʿawna'l-nudhur* (41) | — | — | **Neither one** |
| **Closing** (43-55) | *a-kuffārukum khayrun* (43) | — | **51** *(partial, "your likes")* | At the end of the sūra, turned towards the one addressed |

What this table says is: **the refrain is not mechanical.** The same sentence does five different jobs in five accounts.

**One — enclosure in ʿĀd.** Verse eighteen stands alone as this: *"ʿĀd denied; how then were My punishment and My warnings!"* That is, the sentence is set at the **opening** of the account. Then comes a description of two verses (19-20), and then the same sentence comes again, **alone**, at verse twenty-one. The account of ʿĀd is thus **framed** by the same sentence placed on either side of a three-verse description.

**Two — in Thamūd the question comes before the answer.** Verse twenty-nine relates the crime (they hamstrung the camel). Verse thirty asks: *"How then was My punishment!"* Verse thirty-one answers: *"We sent against them a single cry."* That is, the sūra asks **the question before its answer**. In the other accounts the question comes after the event has been told; here it cuts in.

**Three — in Lot the mood changes.** In three of the four accounts the punishment refrain is a question: *fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur* — "how then was My punishment". In the account of Lot it occurs twice, and both times it is not a question but a **command**: *fa-dhūqū ʿadhābī wa nudhur* — "so taste My punishment and My warnings". A question speaks about a third party; a command turns directly to the one addressed. In the fourth account the sūra turns its language towards speaking with the one addressed — and in the next section (from 43 onward) it will turn to him entirely. **The change of mood is therefore a preparation.**

I record these three points **as my own reading.** The verse numbers of the refrains and their moods are data of the text and can be verified; the reading of their function that I draw from them is mine.

## The refrain of al-Raḥmān and the refrain of al-Qamar

These two sūras stand side by side in the codex, and both are refrain sūras. The comparison is therefore unavoidable. In **`055-rahman.md`** the refrain of al-Raḥmān was treated under four headings: where it occurs, what the word *ālāʾ* means, why the verb is dual, and what it means that it comes after the sections on punishment as well. I do not repeat those here; I set al-Qamar on top of that analysis.

| | **al-Raḥmān 55** | **al-Qamar 54** |
|---|---|---|
| Refrain | *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi Rabbikumā tukadhdhibān* | *wa-laqad yassarnā'l-Qurʾāna li'l-dhikri fa-hal min muddakir* |
| How many times | **31** | **4** (full) / **6** (partial *fa-hal min muddakir*) |
| What fraction of the sūra | 40% | 7% (full) / 11% (including the partial) |
| Type of sentence | Question only | **Statement + question** |
| Subject of the question | **Favour** — which of them do you deny | **Remembrance** — is there anyone who takes it |
| Addressee of the question | **Dual**: *tukadhdhibān* — "you two" | **Indefinite**: *min muddakir* — "anyone at all" |
| Agent | Not stated | *yassarnā* — **"We"**, explicitly |
| A second refrain | None | Yes: *ʿadhābī wa nudhur* (6 times) |
| Is an answer given | No | No |
| Dividing function | Divides the text into 31 parts | Divides the text into 4 accounts |

Now let us look at what the differences mean.

**One — the one points with a finger, the other sweeps the room.**

The question of al-Raḥmān is in the **dual**, and its addressee is known: the two weighty kinds, humankind and jinn. The question is put to them directly, and every time it is put it returns to the same two addressees. The pronoun of address occurs twice in the sentence: *Rabbi-kumā* and *tukadhdhibā-n*.

The question of al-Qamar looks for **an indefinite subject**: *hal min muddakirin* — "is there anyone who will remember?" The sentence contains **no** pronoun of address. The question is asked of no one; it is asked into the open.

And this grammatical difference matches the subject of the two sūras exactly:

- **A favour is given to someone.** In counting a favour, al-Raḥmān has to point at the one addressed: this was given to you, that was given to you. Without an owner there is no favour.
- **A lesson stands in the open.** The four accounts of al-Qamar belong to no one; they lie before everyone. The question is therefore not "why do you not take the lesson" but "**is there anyone who takes it**".

**I record this as my own reading.** The grammatical structure of the two sentences is verifiable data; the connection between them is my inference.

**Two — al-Raḥmān only asks, al-Qamar first states.**

The refrain of al-Raḥmān is a question from beginning to end; it carries not a single declarative sentence. The **first half of al-Qamar's refrain is a statement**: *"We have indeed made the Qurʾān easy for remembrance."* The question comes only after that statement.

This narrows the scope of the question. Al-Qamar's question is not "why do you not take heed"; it is "**given that it has been made easy**, is there anyone who takes heed". The question, that is, closes off the excuse in advance. No excuse of difficulty remains.

Al-Raḥmān's question does not close off an excuse; it **closes off a count**: it asks which favour, and the list of favours it faces has already been enumerated.

**Three — neither sūra gives an answer, but their silences differ.**

The observation recorded in the section on al-Raḥmān was this: the question is asked thirty-one times and in none of them is an answer given. So too in al-Qamar: *fa-hal min muddakir* is asked six times, and not once is it said "yes, there was such a one" or "no, there was no one".

But the two silences are not of the same kind:

- **In al-Raḥmān you cannot answer.** The question "which favour do you deny" has no honest answer; it is asked precisely because it makes an answer impossible.
- **In al-Qamar you can answer** — and the one who answers becomes the answer. The question asks "is there anyone"; the answer is whether the one asked places himself in that empty place or not.

So al-Raḥmān's question is a **closing** question, al-Qamar's an **open-ended** one.

**Four — the punishment refrain does not exist in al-Raḥmān.**

Al-Qamar's second refrain — *fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur* — has no counterpart in al-Raḥmān. There the sections on punishment also close with the same question about favour; there is no separate sentence of punishment. This was dwelt on in the section on al-Raḥmān: that "which favour" is asked even after the description of the Fire was the ground for the second sense of the word *ālāʾ* that arises from the context.

Al-Qamar has split the job in two: there is no language of favour, there is a language of warning; and the warning has a sentence of its own.

**To put it in one sentence:** al-Raḥmān divides a list of favours with one question; al-Qamar divides a list of destructions with two. The same technique on two different grounds.

## The verb "to send" in the sūra — an observation about word order

The root **ر-س-ل** occurs four times in the sūra, and in all four it is in the form of a verb or an active participle:

| Verse | Expression | What is sent |
|---|---|---|
| 19 | *innā arsalnā ʿalayhim rīḥan ṣarṣaran* | **A wind** |
| 27 | *innā mursilū'l-nāqati fitnatan lahum* | **A she-camel** |
| 31 | *innā arsalnā ʿalayhim ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan* | **A cry** |
| 34 | *innā arsalnā ʿalayhim ḥāṣiban* | **A stone-hurling wind** |

**In none of the four is what is sent a prophet.** Three are instruments of punishment, one an instrument of trial.

And more interesting still: **the word رَسُول does not occur in the sūra at all.** The messengers are named in only two places (Noah — 9, Lot — 33-37), and Noah is called *"Our servant"* (*ʿabdanā*), not "Our messenger". The names of Ṣāliḥ, Hūd and Moses do not occur at all. The presence of the messengers in the sūra is fitted into a single word: **ٱلنُّذُر.**

This word occurs eleven times in the sūra:

| Verse | Context |
|---|---|
| 5 | *fa-mā tughni'l-nudhur* — the warnings do not avail |
| 16 | *ʿadhābī wa nudhur* |
| 18 | *ʿadhābī wa nudhur* |
| 21 | *ʿadhābī wa nudhur* |
| 23 | *kadhdhabat Thamūdu **bi'l-nudhur*** |
| 30 | *ʿadhābī wa nudhur* |
| 33 | *kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭin **bi'l-nudhur*** |
| 36 | *fa-tamāraw **bi'l-nudhur*** |
| 37 | *ʿadhābī wa nudhur* |
| 39 | *ʿadhābī wa nudhur* |
| 41 | *wa-laqad jāʾa āla Firʿawna'**l-nudhur*** |

**The word has two readings, and both are recorded by the lexicographers:**

- **نُذُر**, the plural of *nadhīr*: **warners** — that is, the prophets.
- **نُذُر**, the plural with the sense of a verbal noun, from *nudhr* / *nadhīr*: **warnings** — that is, the messages brought.

According to the context, one or the other comes forward. *"Thamūd denied the warners"* is a correct reading, and so is *"they denied the warnings"*. The Qurʾān does not separate the two senses here, and **its not separating them is functional**: to deny the warner and to deny the warning are, in the language of this sūra, the same act.

**I record this observation as my own reading.** The two senses of the word are lexical data; that the word *rasūl* does not occur in the sūra at all is data of the text; the conclusion I draw from the two is mine.

## What is absent from the sūra

I apply here the method applied in the sections on Nūḥ and al-Raḥmān: to see what a sūra does, look first at what it does not do.

- **No detail of the splitting of the moon.** When, where, how many saw it, how they reacted — none of this is said. One sentence: *"and the moon has split."*
- **Almost no prophets' names.** Noah and Lot occur. Ṣāliḥ, Hūd and Moses do not; their peoples do.
- **No building of the ship.** Only its material is named (13). Noah's building of it is at Hūd 11/37-38.
- **Noah's son, his wife, the length of his mission: absent.** These are in other sūras and were itemised in the section on Sūrat Nūḥ.
- **How the she-camel came: absent.** As was recorded in the section on al-Shams, the Qurʾān nowhere says this.
- **The city of Lot's people is not named.** The same note was made in the section on al-Ḥāqqa.
- **No account of Pharaoh.** Even his name does not occur; *āla Firʿawn* occurs. Moses, the staff, the sea, the drowning — none of it.
- **No legal ruling.** There is a single command in the sūra, and it is directed at the Prophet: *"so turn away from them"* (6), *"watch and be patient"* (27, spoken as said to Ṣāliḥ).
- **No description of the Garden.** Two verses, a description of four words: *jannātin wa nahar* + *fī maqʿadi ṣidqin ʿinda malīkin muqtadir.*

All of these exist elsewhere in the Qurʾān. So this is not a lack but **a choice**. This sūra uses not the content of the accounts but their **listability**. What is told is not the events; it is the fact that the events **came one after another**.

## 54/1 — ٱقْتَرَبَتِ ٱلسَّاعَةُ وَٱنشَقَّ ٱلْقَمَرُ

*Iqtarabati'l-sāʿatu wa'nshaqqa'l-qamar*
"The Hour has drawn near and the moon has split."

One of the most debated opening sentences in the Qurʾān. I shall look first at the words, then at the grammar, then at the disagreement — because the disagreement arises precisely from the grammar.

### ٱقْتَرَبَتْ — has drawn near

**Root: ق-ر-ب.** To be near. This root has been treated earlier in this commentary — in **`070-mearic.md`** by way of *qarīb* (near), and in **`078-nebe.md`** and **`051-zariyat.md`** again in the context of nearness in time. The issue here is not the sense of the root but its **form**.

The verb is on the pattern **إِفْتِعَال** (*iftiʿāl*, Form VIII): *iqtaraba*. The basic load this form carries in Arabic is **that the act is realised upon the agent himself, with an added effort or intensity**. *Kasara* (he broke) → *iktasara* (he broke for himself, he took by breaking); *jamaʿa* (he gathered) → *ijtamaʿa* (they gathered together).

The difference between *qaruba* and *iqtaraba*, in the form the lexicographers transmit it, is this: *qaruba* states being near, *iqtaraba* states **the movement of drawing near** — it is approaching, the distance is closing.

The Qurʾān uses this verb of the Hour in two places, and both are on the same pattern:

> *"The reckoning of mankind **has drawn near**, while they turn away in heedlessness."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/1)
> *"**The Hour has drawn near** and the moon has split."* (al-Qamar 54/1)

As was recorded in **`102-tekasur.md`**, sūras that open straight into a statement without any preamble are few, and these two are on that list. The reader is set down in the middle of the scene.

Another trace of the same root in this sūra: the form **مُقْتَرِب** is not present here, but the pattern of *iqtaraba* is the same as that of **مُقْتَدِر** in verses 42 and 55 (the active participle of Form VIII). This is a relation of pattern, not of root; I draw no meaning from it, I only record it.

### ٱنشَقَّ — has split

**Root: ش-ق-ق.** This root was treated in full in **`084-insikak.md`** and I do not repeat it here. Of what was recorded there, this is what is needed for this verse:

- The concrete sense of the root is **to split, to divide in two, to break something apart by cracking it**.
- **شِقّ** (*shiqq*) is one of the two pieces of what has been split; the Turkish word *şık*, "an option, one of the alternatives", comes from it.
- **مَشَقَّة** (*mashaqqa*) is hardship — on the lexicographers' explanation, what splits a person.
- **شِقَاق** (*shiqāq*) is separation, the division between communities (al-Baqara 2/137).
- **شَقِيق** (*shaqīq*) is a full brother — one split off from the same stock.

And the threefold distinction established in the section on al-Inshiqāq holds here too: **falq opens what is closed; faṭr splits and founds out of nothing; shaqq divides what has already been founded.** The splitting of the moon is told with **shaqq** of these three: there is no birth and no founding here; there is the dividing of something that stood together.

The verb is again on the pattern **إِنْفِعَال** (*infiʿāl*, Form VII): *inshaqqa*. As was recorded in the section on al-Inshiqāq, this form conveys **the middle sense** — undergoing an outside action, submitting to it. The moon does not crack of itself; it **yields to something done to it.** The agent is not named, but the form implies that there is one.

This verse already appeared in the table in the section on al-Inshiqāq:

| Verse | What splits | What it opens onto |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Qamar 54/1** | **the moon** | **a sign** |
| ʿAbasa 80/26 | the earth | grain, provision |
| Qāf 50/44 | the earth | the coming forth of the dead |
| al-Furqān 25/25 | the sky | the descent of the angels |
| al-Raḥmān 55/37 | the sky | a change of colour |
| al-Ḥāqqa 69/16 | the sky | dissolution |

What is striking in the table is this: **the only heavenly body on the list is the moon.** In all the other uses of the root what splits is either the earth or the sky itself. The moon is the one single body.

### Word order — two perfect verbs, one connective

The sentence consists of two verbs in the perfect and a *wāw* between them:

```
ٱقْتَرَبَتِ ٱلسَّاعَةُ   وَ   ٱنشَقَّ ٱلْقَمَرُ
```

And the whole disagreement arises from this. For in Arabic **a perfect verb can also relate an event that has not yet happened** — and it does so on two different grounds:

**One — as an expression of certainty.** A future event whose occurrence is certain is related as though it had happened. The Qurʾān uses this often:

> *"**The command of God has come**; do not seek to hasten it."* (al-Naḥl 16/1) — the command has not yet come.
> *"And **the trumpet was blown**."* (al-Zumar 39/68) — the scene of the resurrection in the perfect.
> *"And Hell **was brought** that day."* (al-Fajr 89/23) — treated in the section on al-Fajr.

This is not a stylistic preference but a settled possibility of Arabic, and it is treated under a heading of its own in the classical books on the language.

**Two — the relating of an event that really is past.** This is the perfect verb's own proper job, and it requires no separate justification.

Now the question is this: **the first verb (*iqtarabat*) is beyond dispute of the first kind** — the Hour has not yet come, yet "has drawn near" is said. And the second verb? Is *inshaqqa*, coming in the same sentence, with the same connective, in the same tense, also of the first kind, or of the second?

This is where the two great readings arise.

### The disagreement: what does the splitting of the moon relate?

**On this question I issue no definite ruling.** Per the method (`USLUP.md`) I give the disagreement with its evidence and I do not impose a preference. There are two main views and one reconciling view.

| | **(a) An event that occurred in the past** | **(b) A sign of the Hour related in the perfect** |
|---|---|---|
| What it says | The moon actually split at Mecca in the Prophet's time; the verse reports that event | The moon will split on the Day of Resurrection; the perfect is for certainty |
| The tense of the verb | Perfect, in its proper job: past | Perfect, for certainty: future |
| Its relation to the first verb | The two verbs are **in two different times**: one future (the drawing near), one past (the splitting). The *wāw* sequences them, it does not equate them | The two verbs are **on the same temporal plane**: both future, both in the mood of certainty |
| Its strongest evidence | **The second verse.** *"If they see a sign they turn away and say, 'a passing magic'."* For a thing to be called "magic" requires that the thing **have been seen** | **The internal consistency of the sentence.** For two verbs joined by a single connective in the same sentence to be in different times is forced |
| Its second evidence | The transmitted material. Reports stating that the moon split are found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, and in classical exegesis these reports are widely attached to this verse | The sūra as a whole opens with the Hour and closes with it (*al-sāʿa* in 1 and 46); the splitting of the moon fits that frame |
| Its weak side | That two verbs in the same sentence should be in different times requires explanation | Calling it *"magic"* becomes difficult: no one calls an event that has not yet happened "magic" |
| Who holds it | **The majority of the exegetes** | A minority. In the classical sources it is generally transmitted as ascribed to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī |

**A note is required.** The ascription of the second view to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī is widely transmitted in the classical commentaries; **I do not speak with certainty about the soundness of the ascription**, since I have no verified chain of transmission before me. The view itself is present in the sources; whose it is is another matter.

**There is also a third, reconciling reading**, adopted by a not inconsiderable number of exegetes: **the event occurred in the past and is at the same time a sign that the Hour has drawn near.** On this reading the two verbs are joined not as cause and effect but as **sign and thing signified**: the Hour has drawn near; as a token of this the moon has split. This reading tries to hold together both the transmitted ground of the first view and the concern for the unity of the sentence in the second.

**No preference is adopted in this commentary.** Both readings have a ground in the text, and neither blocks the reading of the rest of the sūra: whichever reading you take, from the second verse onward what the sūra says does not change — a sign is shown, it is turned away from, it is called "magic".

### An explicit note about a prohibition

There is a kind of claim that circulates widely around this verse: the claim that some geological formation on the surface of the moon (most often a fissure or a *rille*) is the "scientific proof" of this verse, together with the various accounts that accompany it.

**This commentary does not use these claims.** The ground is an explicit clause of `USLUP.md`: *"No hunting for scientific miracles. No modern piece of knowledge is forced onto a verse."*

Beyond that there are two further practical reasons:

**One.** The claim itself has no support — what is said about the dating of formations on the lunar surface and about the mechanisms that produced them is not of a kind that can be made a support for this claim.

**Two**, and more important: **the claim works against the verse, not for it.** If you tie the truth of a text to a current scientific interpretation, then when the interpretation changes you have brought the text down with it. This is in any case not the Qurʾān's own answer in the second verse; that verse relates what those who see a sign do — not the physical explanation of the sign.

**I record one further observation, and I state that it is my own reading:** the verse itself does not **relate** the event, it only **records** it. Two words. No date, no place, no witness, no duration, no manner. If the purpose of the text were to present a miracle, the least it would do is to describe the event. The text does not do this; in the very next verse it moves to the **reaction**. What the verse is concerned with, then, is not the sign itself but **the stance taken towards the sign.**

## 54/2 — وَإِن يَرَوْا۟ ءَايَةً يُعْرِضُوا۟ وَيَقُولُوا۟ سِحْرٌ مُّسْتَمِرٌّ

*Wa in yaraw āyatan yuʿriḍū wa yaqūlū siḥrun mustamirr*
"If they see a sign they turn away and say, 'a passing magic'."

### Word order — a conditional sentence, and time

The sentence is a conditional structure: *in* (if) + *yaraw* (they see) → *yuʿriḍū* (they turn away) + *yaqūlū* (they say). All three verbs are **imperfect** and in the jussive.

And at just this point a datum falls into the disagreement over the first verse. The first verse was in the perfect; the second is in the imperfect. The text is therefore moving **from an event to a habit**: this happened once; and **whenever** these people see a sign they do this.

The conditional particle chosen is **إِنْ**, not *إِذَا*. The difference the lexicographers record: *idhā* is used for a condition expected or certain to occur, *in* for a condition that is possible or rare. The use of *in* here implies that their seeing a sign is a rare situation — and this fits the **exceptional character** of the event the first verse relates.

I record this as an observation about the grammar; I do not claim that the *in* / *idhā* distinction works absolutely everywhere, since there are places in the Qurʾān where it is relaxed.

### يُعْرِضُوا۟ — to turn away

**Root: ع-ر-ض.** Concrete sense: **breadth, width.** *ʿArḍ* — the width of a thing (as against its length). *ʿArīḍ* — wide.

Two branches come out of this, and both are in active use in the Qurʾān:

- **عَرَضَ** — to turn a thing broadside and **present** it, to set it forth. *ʿArḍ*, *maʿrūḍ*, *maʿriḍ* (an exhibition), *iʿrāḍ*.
- **أَعْرَضَ** (Form IV) — **to turn away.** The lexicographers' explanation: to turn not one's face but one's **side**. That is, you show the one before you not your face but your width.

This detail matters. The English expression "to turn away" gives the movement correctly but not the picture inside the word. The picture in Arabic is this: **the face-to-face stance is broken, the person turns himself sideways.** To refuse, one need not speak; changing one's stance is enough.

And read together with the "presenting" sense of the same root, a contrast emerges: *ʿarḍ* is opening a thing to the one before you; *iʿrāḍ* is closing yourself to him. **The same root names both the offering and the refusal of what is offered.** This is a datum of the language; the reading of it as a contrast is mine.

### ءَايَة — sign

This word has been treated in many places in this commentary and I do not repeat it. Only one point here: **a sign exists not for its own sake but for what it points to.** To see an *āya* is not to watch a display; it is to receive a direction. For the one who takes the sign and does not look where it points, the *āya* ceases to be an *āya*; only an event is left.

Further on in the sūra the same word will occur twice more: in verse 15, *"and We left it as a sign"*; in verse 42, *"they denied all Our signs"*.

### سِحْر — magic

**Root: س-ح-ر.** This root was treated in this commentary in **`074-muddessir.md`** and **`051-zariyat.md`**. What was recorded there and is decisive here is this: the root yields two distinct words —

- **سِحْر** (*siḥr*) — magic.
- **سَحَر** (*saḥar*) — the last part of the night, the time just before dawn. Its plural is *asḥār*.

As was recorded in the section on al-Dhāriyāt, the lexicographers ground the relation between these two senses in **the hour when light and darkness mix and the eye can be deceived**. That explanation is ascribed to the lexicographers; it is not offered as a settled etymology, and I do not offer it as one here either.

**But there is something that must be recorded for this sūra.** Both words of the root occur in it:

| Verse | Word | About whom |
|---|---|---|
| **2** | **سِحْر** — magic | What the deniers say about a sign |
| **34** | **سَحَر** — the hour before dawn | The hour at which Lot's family was saved |

So at the beginning of the sūra one group calls what it sees *siḥr*; thirty-two verses later another group is saved at *saḥar*. The same three letters.

**I record this as my own reading, and I mark its limit:** that the two words are from the same root is a datum of the language and can be verified. The **claim** that the two are set opposite one another in the sūra and that this is a deliberate choice **is not mine, and I do not make it.** I only record an overlap found in the text.

### مُّسْتَمِرّ — continuing, or passing away?

**Root: م-ر-ر.** The concrete sense of the root: **to pass, to go by from one place to another.** *Marra / yamurru* — he passed. *Murūr* — passage. *Mamarr* — a passageway.

The word is the active participle of the pattern **إِسْتِفْعَال** (*istifʿāl*, Form X): *mustamirr*. And this form opens here in two different directions:

**First reading: "continuing, going on."** The verb *istamarra* is settled in Arabic in the sense "it continued, it went on". On this reading what they say is: *"This is unceasing magic — one ends and the next begins."* That is, the addressees count the signs that come one after another as a single continuous act of sorcery.

**Second reading: "passing, soon to be over."** From *murūr*, the root's proper sense: this too will pass. On this reading what they say is: *"This is magic that will pass — it is not worth dwelling on."*

**A third reading is also transmitted:** from the root's *مِرَّة* branch (strength, firmness) — *"a strong, thoroughly solid magic."* The Qurʾān uses this word at al-Najm 53/6: *dhū mirratin fa'stawā.*

| Reading | Branch of the root | What they are saying | Their stance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Continuous** | *istamarra* — to continue | "It does not stop, it keeps coming" | Weariness, exhaustion |
| **Passing** | *marra* — to pass by | "This too will pass" | Belittlement, indifference |
| **Strong** | *mirra* — strength | "Solid magic" | Accepting the effect, rejecting the source |

**All three are recorded and I do not impose a preference.** But one thing can be said: **all three readings arrive at the same result.** Whether they say continuous, or passing, or strong, one thing is common to all three — **the refusal to let the sign be a sign.** The word's several senses are not an ambiguity here; they are three separate tones of refusal.

That last sentence is my own reading.

### The root م-ر-ر three times in the sūra

The root occurs in three places in the sūra, and the three must be read together:

| Verse | Word | Who says it / what it qualifies |
|---|---|---|
| **2** | *siḥrun **mustamirr*** | **They** say it — it qualifies the sign |
| **19** | *yawmi naḥsin **mustamirr*** | **The text** says it — it qualifies the day of ʿĀd's destruction |
| **46** | *wa'l-sāʿatu adhā wa **amarr*** | **The text** says it — it qualifies the Hour |

The **أَمَرّ** of verse forty-six is from another branch of the root: *murr* — **bitter** (in taste). *Amarr* is the comparative: "more bitter".

**Whether the senses "to pass by" and "to be bitter" derive from the same root is a matter the lexicographers dispute, and I issue no definite ruling here.** The classical dictionaries give both under م-ر-ر; whether the link between them is genuinely etymological or was drawn later is another question.

**But the observation about word order within the sūra can be recorded and verified:** in the second verse the deniers qualify the sign as *mustamirr*; in the nineteenth verse a people's day of destruction is qualified as *mustamirr*; in the forty-sixth verse the Hour is qualified as *amarr*. **The word they themselves used comes back twice.**

I record this as my own reading. The sūra will weave this kind of "return of a word to its owner" once more: in verse twenty-five what Thamūd say of Ṣāliḥ, *kadhdhābun ashir*, is made definite in verse twenty-six and turned back on them; and what they say in verse twenty-four, *ḍalālin wa suʿur*, is repeated word for word in verse forty-seven as the state of the guilty. **The sūra does the same thing three times: it takes the denier's word and hands it back to him.**

## 54/3 — وَكَذَّبُوا۟ وَٱتَّبَعُوٓا۟ أَهْوَآءَهُمْ ۚ وَكُلُّ أَمْرٍ مُّسْتَقِرٌّ

*Wa kadhdhabū wa'ttabaʿū ahwāʾahum; wa kullu amrin mustaqirr*
"They denied and followed their own cravings. And every matter comes to rest."

### Two verbs, two stages

*Kadhdhabū* — they denied. **Root: ك-ذ-ب**, and the verb is on the pattern **تَفْعِيل** (*tafʿīl*, Form II). Here that pattern conveys **ascription**: to call someone a liar, to count a word a lie. *Kadhaba* is to lie; *kadhdhaba* is to count someone else a liar. Two distinct acts.

This verb is one of the backbone verbs of the sūra; all four of the accounts open with it (9, 18, 23, 33) and the fifth continues with it (42).

*Ittabaʿū ahwāʾahum* — they followed their cravings. **The root of هَوَىٰ / هَوَاء: ه-و-ي.** This root was treated in **`101-karia.md`** and **`079-naziat.md`**: *hawā* is **to fall from a height downward**. *Hāwiya* — that which falls, or the deep abyss into which one falls.

And **هَوًى** (*hawā*) — a person's inclination, his craving — is from the same root. The explanation the lexicographers transmit: craving is what **drags a person downward**; the self falls towards it. I transmit this explanation; I do not offer it as a settled etymology.

The plural form of the word (*ahwāʾ*) is worth dwelling on. **Guidance in the Qurʾān is singular** (*ṣirāṭ*, *sabīl*, *hudā*), **while craving is here plural.** The same distribution appears elsewhere in the Qurʾān: *"This is My straight path, so follow it; and do not follow the other ways, for they will divide you from His way"* (al-Anʿām 6/153) — one path, many ways.

**The order of the two verbs is worth recording.** First the denying, then the following of craving. That is, the verse arranges craving not as the **cause** of denying but as its **consequence**. The order could have been built the other way; it was not.

**I record this as my own reading:** after a thing has been rejected no emptiness remains; something takes the place of what was rejected. The verse says what fills that place.

### وَكُلُّ أَمْرٍ مُّسْتَقِرٌّ — every matter comes to rest

The sentence is a nominal sentence and it states a rule. There is no verb; there is continuity.

**مُسْتَقِرّ — Root: ق-ر-ر.** This root was treated in **`075-kiyame.md`**, **`076-insan.md`** and **`077-murselat.md`**. Its concrete sense: **to settle in a place, to become fixed, to stand there.**

Its derivatives:
- **قَرَار** — a place of standing, a fixed place, a base. *"He made the earth a place of rest (*qarār*)"* (al-Naml 27/61).
- **مُسْتَقَرّ** (with *fatḥa*, a noun of place) — the **place** where something comes to rest.
- **مُسْتَقِرّ** (with *kasra*, the active participle) — that which comes to rest, which settles into place.
- **قُرَّة** — coolness of the eye. The lexicographers' explanation: the state in which the eye, having found what it sought, **becomes still**. *Qurrat ʿayn*.

The form in the verse is the active participle: *mustaqirr* — **coming to rest in its place.**

What the sentence says is this: **every matter arrives somewhere, stops there and stays there.** There is no matter left hanging; what has been postponed is not without an outcome.

And the load carried by Form X (*istifʿāl*) here is worth recording. This form generally conveys **seeking** (*istaghfara* — he sought forgiveness), but it also has the sense of **"to come into that state"** (*istaḥjara* — it turned to stone; *istaghnā* — he became rich). *Istaqarra* is of the second kind: to come into a state of rest, to settle.

**Its counterpart within the sūra.** The word occurs once more, in verse thirty-eight: *ʿadhābun mustaqirr* — **"a settled punishment"** that came upon the people of Lot in the morning. That is, a word set down in the third verse as a **rule** comes back in the thirty-eighth as an **event**.

| Verse | Expression | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **3** | *wa kullu amrin **mustaqirr*** | The rule: every matter comes to rest |
| **38** | *ʿadhābun **mustaqirr*** | The instance: a punishment that has come to rest |

This is a small but clear example of how the sūra works: **it states the rule at the beginning and gives the instance afterwards.** I record it as my own reading.

### What this says to the present

The practical import of the sentence *kullu amrin mustaqirr* is an understanding of time.

Most of human life is lived without the outcome being seen. A word is spoken and its effect emerges years later; a deed is done and one passes on without seeing its return. This does not mean that there is no outcome; it means that **it has not yet come to rest.**

The verse separates these two: **an outcome unseen and an outcome absent are not the same thing.** And it says this not as a sentence of threat but as a description of how things work — the sentence is nominal, it addresses no one, it issues no ruling; it describes an order.

## 54/4-5 — وَلَقَدْ جَآءَهُم مِّنَ ٱلْأَنۢبَآءِ مَا فِيهِ مُزْدَجَرٌ · حِكْمَةٌۢ بَٰلِغَةٌ ۖ فَمَا تُغْنِ ٱلنُّذُرُ

*Wa-laqad jāʾahum mina'l-anbāʾi mā fīhi muzdajar — ḥikmatun bālighatun fa-mā tughni'l-nudhur*
"There has indeed come to them of the tidings that in which there is deterrence. A wisdom that has reached its full. Yet the warnings do not avail."

### ٱلْأَنۢبَآء — tidings

**Root: ن-ب-أ.** This root was treated in detail in **`078-nebe.md`**, and the distinction recorded there holds here too: a *nabaʾ* is not just any piece of news. It is **a report that has weight, that carries benefit, a great report**. Ordinary news is called *khabar*; *nabaʾ* is a layer of its own.

Its plural is *anbāʾ*. In the verse it comes with **من**: *mina'l-anbāʾ* — "of the tidings". This *min* is of the kind the lexicographers call partitive: it indicates a part of the whole. What has come, then, is not **all** the tidings but **a portion of them.** The sūra will give the list of them shortly: Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Lot, Pharaoh.

Which tidings? The verse does not say; but the stretch running from verse nine to verse forty-two is exactly the unfolding of this sentence. **Verse four is the heading, and 9-42 the text.**

### مُزْدَجَر — that which deters

**Root: ز-ج-ر.** Its concrete sense: **to drive off with a shout, to send away with a rebuke.** A herdsman drives an animal off with his voice; that is *zajr*. At the centre of the word there is **a sound, and that sound repelling something.**

This root was treated in **`079-naziat.md`**, and the two verses of this sūra were already cited there. Here I dwell on the pattern.

**مُزْدَجَر** is the passive participle / noun of place / *mīmī* verbal noun of the pattern **إِفْتِعَال** (Form VIII). The letter ت of the pattern, under the influence of the ز before it, has turned into د: *muztajar* → **muzdajar**. This is a regular assimilation in Arabic; the same change occurs in *uzdujira* in verse nine.

More than one solution is transmitted for the word's grammatical position:

| Solution | Sense |
|---|---|
| ***Mīmī* verbal noun** | "In which there is **deterrence**" |
| **Noun of place** | "In which there is **that from which / whereby one is deterred**" |
| **Passive participle** | "In which there is **that which has been deterred**" |

In point of sense all three come out at the same place, and no preference is required.

**The root's second occurrence in the sūra is worth recording.** In verse nine, of Noah: *wa'zdujira* — "and he was driven off / rebuked". The same root, the same form, in the passive.

| Verse | Form | Who / what |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | ***muzdajar*** — deterrence | What was in the **tidings that came** to them |
| **9** | ***uzdujira*** — he was driven off | What they **did to the messenger** |

**I record this as my own reading:** the same root names, in one place, the thing that ought to have turned them back, and in the other, the person they turned back. *Zajr* had come; but the one who underwent *zajr* was the bearer of the tidings. This reversal stands in the root itself; the conclusion I draw from it is mine.

### حِكْمَةٌۢ بَٰلِغَةٌ — a wisdom that has reached its full

**بَالِغَة — Root: ب-ل-غ.** Its concrete sense: **to arrive somewhere, to reach, to attain a goal.** *Balagha* — he reached. *Bulūgh* — maturity, a person's arrival at the point of ripeness. *Balāgh* — delivery, the thing conveyed. *Balīgh* — one who brings his speech to its target.

*Bāligha* is the active participle: **arrived, attained, come to its farthest point.**

Two distinct readings can be made and both are recorded:

- **The wisdom itself is complete** — there is nothing lacking in it, it has arrived at the point of ripeness.
- **The wisdom has reached them** — it has come to them, it has passed into their hands; no excuse of its not having arrived remains.

The second reading seems more in keeping with the following clause: the wisdom was there, it arrived, **and yet** it did not avail. Still, the first reading is not contrary to the text, and I do not impose a preference.

More than one solution is also given for the word's case-analysis; the most widespread is to count it a substitute for the *mā* of the preceding verse, or the predicate of an elided subject (*hiya ḥikmatun bāligha*). The sense does not change.

### فَمَا تُغْنِ ٱلنُّذُرُ — the warnings do not avail

**أَغْنَىٰ — Root: غ-ن-ي.** Its concrete sense: **not to be in need of a thing, to have enough.** *Ghinā* — wealth, that is, the state of not standing in need of another. *Ghanī* — one who is not in need. *Istighnāʾ* — to regard oneself as self-sufficient.

The construction **أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُ** is settled in the Qurʾān and means: **to take another's place, to ward something off from him, to suffice him.** *"His wealth did not avail him"* (al-Masad 111/2 — *mā aghnā ʿanhu māluhu*), *"They can ward off nothing from them"* (al-Jāthiya 45/10).

There is a disagreement over the **مَا** at the head of the sentence:

| Reading | The kind of *mā* | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Negation** | *mā* of negation | "The warnings **do not** avail" — a statement |
| **Question** | *mā* of interrogation | "**What do** the warnings avail?" — a rhetorical question |

Both readings arrive at the same result; the second is sharper. **I do not impose a preference.**

**A grammatical detail:** the verb comes in the form **تُغْنِ**, with the final *yāʾ* dropped (not *tughnī*). This elision appears in the orthography of the codex and is connected with the harmony of the verse-ending. The same elision occurs in the words **يَدْعُ** and **ٱلدَّاعِ** in verse six, and I take it up separately there.

**ٱلنُّذُر** — this was dealt with above in the section on the backbone of the sūra: it can be read both as "warners" and as "warnings", and the sūra does not separate the two.

### The work the two verses do together

Verses four and five set up a **frame** for the rest of the sūra, and the frame is logical:

1. The tidings came (*jāʾahum*).
2. In the tidings there was deterrence (*fīhi muzdajar*).
3. The wisdom was complete and had arrived (*ḥikmatun bāligha*).
4. It did not avail (*fa-mā tughni'l-nudhur*).

That is, **the lack is not on the sender's side.** For three clauses the quality of what was sent is enumerated: the tidings, the deterrence, the complete wisdom. Then at the fourth step the result is given.

**And this is the logic of the first refrain as well:** *"We have made the Qurʾān easy for remembrance — is there anyone who will remember?"* The refrain has the same structure: first the quality of what was given, then the question about the result.

That is, the fifth verse is the refrain that begins at verse seventeen **said in advance.** I record this as my own reading.

## 54/6 — فَتَوَلَّ عَنْهُمْ ۘ يَوْمَ يَدْعُ ٱلدَّاعِ إِلَىٰ شَىْءٍ نُّكُرٍ

*Fa-tawalla ʿanhum; yawma yadʿu'l-dāʿi ilā shayʾin nukur*
"So turn away from them. On the day the caller calls to a thing unrecognisable…"

### فَتَوَلَّ — you too, turn away

**Root: و-ل-ي.** Its concrete sense: **to be near, to be adjacent.** *Walī* — one who is near, a friend, a protector. *Wilāya* — nearness and the authority that comes with it. *Tālī* — one who follows behind.

And something interesting comes of this: **تَوَلَّىٰ** (Form V) carries two opposite senses —

- **تَوَلَّىٰ عَنْ** — to turn away from someone, to withdraw.
- **تَوَلَّىٰ** (without the preposition) — to take a matter on, to assume charge of it.

The same verb, when it takes the preposition *ʿan*, conveys withdrawal; without it, taking charge. Here it comes with *ʿan*: withdrawal.

**The one commanded is the Prophet**, and this is the only command the sūra gives him. In the second verse the deniers were said to perform *iʿrāḍ*; in the sixth *tawallī* is commanded of the Prophet. **Two different roots, a similar movement** — but one is censured and the other commanded.

Where is the difference? In its source. Their turning away is flight from a sign; the Prophet's turning away is **stopping where the delivering of the message ends.** Another form of the same command is at al-Dhāriyāt 51/54: *"So turn away from them; you are not to blame"* — and in **`051-zariyat.md`** that verse was treated in detail in this commentary. The note recorded there holds here too: this command is not to abandon the delivering of the message but **not to take on responsibility for the outcome.**

### يَوْمَ يَدْعُ ٱلدَّاعِ — a point of orthography

Two elisions of the codex's orthography come together in this expression:

- **يَدْعُ** — its original is *yadʿū* (يدعو). The final **و** is not written.
- **ٱلدَّاعِ** — its original is *al-dāʿī* (الداعي). The final **ي** is not written.

Elisions of this kind are found in several places in the written codex, and the letter is not pronounced in recitation. **I record this as a datum of the language; I draw from it no meaning and no numerical claim whatsoever.** The prohibition in `USLUP.md` on letter-and-number computation holds here too.

**ٱلدَّاعِ** — the caller. **Root: د-ع-و**, and this root was treated in detail in **`071-nuh.md`** as the backbone of that sūra: it was shown there that the root does two jobs at once in Arabic (to call and to pray).

Who the caller is is not said in the verse. In classical exegesis it is widely said to be the angel who blows the trumpet; **the Qurʾān does not name him** and neither do I. That the word comes definite (*al-dāʿī*) shows that a caller known to the one addressed is meant; it does not give his identity.

The same expression occurs at Ṭāhā 20/108: *"On that day they will follow the caller, from whom there is no swerving."*

### شَىْءٍ نُّكُرٍ — a thing unrecognisable

**نُكُر — Root: ن-ك-ر.** This root was treated in **`067-mulk.md`**, **`051-zariyat.md`**, **`058-mucadele.md`** and **`060-mumtehine.md`**. Its concrete sense: **not to recognise, not to know, to find strange.**

Its derivatives show the field of the root:

- **نَكِرَ / أَنْكَرَ** — he did not recognise, he denied. To deny is to say "I do not recognise this".
- **مُنْكَر** — what is not approved, what is rejected. And its exact opposite is **مَعْرُوف** — "what is known, what is recognised". **In Arabic, that is, good is named "the recognised" and evil "the unrecognised".**
- **نَكِير** — repudiation, rejection. At al-Mulk 67/18: *fa-kayfa kāna nakīr* — treated in the section on al-Mulk.
- **نَكِرَة** — an indefinite noun (in grammar). Unrecognised, not determined.
- **تَنَكَّرَ** — he disguised himself, he became unrecognisable.

The form **نُكُر** is an adjective and means **"unrecognisable, unfamiliar, without precedent"**.

And this is exactly what the verse says: **what they are called to resembles nothing known.** The verse does not describe what that day is; it states **that it cannot be described.**

**This says something about the method of description itself, and I record it as my own reading.** There are two ways of conveying a thing: to liken it, or to say that it is like nothing. The Qurʾān uses both — and in this sūra the two stand **side by side**:

| Verse | Method | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| **6** | **Unlikeness** | *shayʾin **nukur*** — a thing unrecognisable |
| **7** | **Likeness** | *ka-annahum **jarādun muntashir*** — like locusts spread abroad |

The sixth verse says "it resembles nothing"; the seventh immediately makes a comparison. Not a contradiction but **a division of labour**: what resembles nothing is **the event itself**; what is likened is **the appearance of the people.** The nature of the resurrection cannot be described; but the state people will be in on that day can be.

**Interpreting the Qurʾān by the Qurʾān.** The same root, in the same sense, occurs in the account of al-Khiḍr in Sūrat al-Kahf: *"You have done a most **munkar** thing"* (al-Kahf 18/74) and *"You have done a **nukr** thing"* (al-Kahf 18/71). There too the sense runs towards "unheard-of, hard to take in".

## 54/7 — خُشَّعًا أَبْصَٰرُهُمْ يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ ٱلْأَجْدَاثِ كَأَنَّهُمْ جَرَادٌ مُّنتَشِرٌ

*Khushshaʿan abṣāruhum yakhrujūna mina'l-ajdāthi ka-annahum jarādun muntashir*
"With their eyes cast down they come forth from the graves as though they were locusts spread abroad."

### خُشَّعًا — variant reading and grammar

**Root: خ-ش-ع.** Its concrete sense: **to be lowered, the dropping of the voice, the descent of the eye.** *Khushūʿ* is submission, but first of all it is **a physical lowering**: the head bows, the eye drops, the voice falls. At Ṭāhā 20/108: *"Voices are **hushed** (*khashaʿat*) before the All-Merciful."*

In **`088-gasiye.md`** the word's use of the eye was treated and this verse was cited there; I do not repeat it.

**There is a variant reading.** The word is read in two forms:

| Reading | Pattern | Difference in sense |
|---|---|---|
| **خُشَّعًا** | *fuʿʿal* — a broken plural | "With eyes cast down" (plural) |
| **خَاشِعًا** | active participle, singular | "With eye cast down" (singular) |

**Since I cannot state with certainty which reading belongs to which imām, I write no names.** This is the same note applied in the section on al-Ḥāqqa.

**There is no difference in sense**, and there is a grammatical reason for this: in Arabic an adjective coming **before** its plural noun may remain singular. So *khāshiʿan abṣāruhum* also means "with their eyes cast down". The two readings give the same picture.

The word's case-analysis is that of a *ḥāl* (an accusative of state): "they come forth in the state of…".

**A point of word order:** the sentence begins with *khushshaʿan* — that is, **the appearance comes before the verb.** The normal order would be *yakhrujūna khushshaʿan abṣāruhum*. What is brought forward is the eyes. The reader sees the eyes first and the coming forth second.

### ٱلْأَجْدَاث — the graves

**Root: ج-د-ث.** A grave. Its singular is *jadath*.

Arabic has several words for a grave and the Qurʾān uses them distinctly:

| Word | Root | Its emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| **قَبْر** | ق-ب-ر | The general name; the place of burial |
| **جَدَث** | ج-د-ث | The pit itself; the dug-out place |
| **رَمْس** | ر-م-س | A place covered over, its trace effaced |

**جَدَث** occurs in only three places in the Qurʾān, and **in all three in the same scene**: the coming forth from the grave.

| Verse | Expression |
|---|---|
| Yā Sīn 36/51 | *mina'l-ajdāthi ilā Rabbihim **yansilūn*** — they hasten forth |
| **al-Qamar 54/7** | *yakhrujūna mina'l-**ajdāth*** — they come forth |
| al-Maʿārij 70/43 | *yakhrujūna mina'l-ajdāthi **sirāʿan*** — they come forth swiftly |

This root was treated in **`070-mearic.md`**. What is to be added here is this: in all three verses **there is movement, and the movement is fast** — *yansilūn* (to walk quickly), *sirāʿan* (as if running). The word is never used in the Qurʾān in the context of "the dead lying"; always in the context of **coming forth**.

I record this as an observation about usage; the wording of the three verses can be verified.

### جَرَادٌ مُّنتَشِرٌ — locusts spread abroad

**جَرَاد — Root: ج-ر-د.** Its concrete sense: **to strip, to lay bare, to peel off the covering.** *Jarada* — he stripped. *Ajrad* — hairless, bare. *Jarīda* — a palm branch stripped of its leaves (and from this the sense "newspaper" arose later).

**And the lexicographers explain the giving of this name to the locust as follows: it strips the place it passes over and leaves it bare.** That is, the word itself carries the work the creature does. I transmit this explanation ascribing it to the lexicographers.

*Jarād* is a generic noun; its singular is *jarāda*.

**مُنتَشِر — Root: ن-ش-ر.** This root was treated in **`067-mulk.md`**, **`081-tekvir.md`**, **`077-murselat.md`** and **`052-tur.md`**. Its concrete sense: **to spread, to lay out, to open.**

Its derivatives and their uses in the Qurʾān:

- **نَشَرَ** — he spread, he opened. To open something folded.
- **مَنشُور** — spread open (of a page). *"Pages spread open"* (al-Muddaththir 74/52).
- **نُشِرَتْ** — *"When the pages are spread open"* (al-Takwīr 81/10).
- **ٱلنُّشُور** — **the resurrection.** *"To Him is the rising and the gathering"* (al-Mulk 67/15).
- **مُنتَشِر** — the active participle of Form VIII: spread abroad, scattered.

**And there is an overlap here worth recording:** the coming forth of people from the graves is called *nushūr*; and at the moment of that coming forth the people are likened to *muntashir* locusts. **The same root gives both the name of the event and the adjective of the comparison.**

I record this **as my own reading, and I mark its limit**: that the two words are from the same root is verifiable linguistic data. I do not claim that this was a deliberate choice.

### The *farāsh* of al-Qāriʿa and the *jarād* of al-Qamar

In **`101-karia.md`** the comparison at al-Qāriʿa 101/4 was treated in detail: *ka'l-farāshi'l-mabthūth* — "like scattered moths/butterflies". It was recorded there that rendering *farāsh* into English is a matter of choice and that the Arabic word covers both. The seventh verse of this sūra was moreover already cited in the section on al-Qāriʿa.

I now set the two comparisons side by side, because **the same event is told with two different creatures** and the choices say different things.

| | **al-Qāriʿa 101/4** | **al-Qamar 54/7** |
|---|---|---|
| What is likened | People | People |
| The likeness | **فَرَاش** — moth / butterfly | **جَرَاد** — locust |
| The adjective | **مَبْثُوث** (ب-ث-ث) — scattered | **مُنتَشِر** (ن-ش-ر) — spread abroad |
| Proper sense of the root | ف-ر-ش: **to spread, to lay out** | ج-ر-د: **to strip bare** |
| The creature's salient trait | **Lightness and directionlessness** — erratic flight towards light or fire | **Density and number** — a swarm that covers the ground |
| The feeling the scene gives | The loss of weight | The crushing multitude of a crowd |
| What follows in the sūra | The balance is set up, weight is measured | They run to the caller, "a hard day" is said |

**The two comparisons are not telling the same thing.**

**Al-Qāriʿa is telling of lightness.** As was shown in the section on al-Qāriʿa, that sūra first empties out all the weight in the world: the lightest creature (the moth) and the heaviest body (the mountain) come to the same state; then at exactly that point the balance is set up and a single question is asked — whose scales are heavy? **The moth comparison is a preparation for the balance.**

**Al-Qamar is telling of multitude.** In al-Qamar there is no balance; there is a **crowd**. The next verse of the sūra says *"they run with necks outstretched towards the caller"*; its forty-fourth verse relates their claim *"we are a host that cannot be defeated"*; its forty-fifth announces the scattering of that host. **From beginning to end the sūra is concerned with crowds** — peoples, hosts, *jamʿ*, *ashyāʿ*. A swarm of locusts is the crowd itself.

**And here it can be seen plainly that the comparison is chosen for the one addressed.** For desert and desert-edge societies a swarm of locusts is **not an abstract image.** A locust invasion finishes a region's yearly crop in a few hours; it darkens the sky; it covers the ground; it can be heard. This is **the densest and most threatening image of a crowd** that such a society has seen.

The Qurʾān itself also names the locust as a punishment: *"So We sent upon them the flood, the **locusts**, the vermin, the frogs and the blood"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/133) — and this is about the people of Pharaoh; that is, the people who will be named in the forty-first verse of this sūra.

**In one sentence:** for al-Qāriʿa people are like winged things flung into a fire; for al-Qamar they are like **a swarm covering the ground**. One gives weightlessness, the other numberlessness.

**I record this comparison as my own reading.** The wording of the two verses, the senses of the two roots and the continuation of the two sūras are verifiable data; the difference in function I draw from the choice of comparisons is my interpretation.

## 54/8 — مُّهْطِعِينَ إِلَى ٱلدَّاعِ ۖ يَقُولُ ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ هَٰذَا يَوْمٌ عَسِرٌ

*Muhṭiʿīna ila'l-dāʿ; yaqūlu'l-kāfirūna hādhā yawmun ʿasir*
"Straining their necks towards the caller. The disbelievers say, 'This is a hard day.'"

### مُّهْطِعِين — to run with the neck outstretched

**Root: ه-ط-ع.** This root was treated in **`070-mearic.md`**. The description the lexicographers give: **to go quickly with the head raised and the eye fixed on a single point, the neck stretched forward.**

Three elements are together in the word:

1. **Speed** — not walking but hastening.
2. **The stretching of the neck** — the body inclined forward, the head thrust out.
3. **The fixing of the eye** — the gaze does not leave a single point.

All three of its occurrences in the Qurʾān are in the same scene:

- *"Their heads raised, **hastening**; their gaze does not return to them"* (Ibrāhīm 14/43 — *muhṭiʿīna muqniʿī ruʾūsihim*).
- **al-Qamar 54/8** — here.
- *"They come hurrying towards you **with necks outstretched**"* (al-Maʿārij 70/36).

**And there is a tension that must be recorded.** In the seventh verse the eyes are **cast down** (*khushshaʿan abṣāruhum*); in the eighth the gaze is **fixed and the neck stretched out** (*muhṭiʿīn*). The two descriptions appear to conflict.

The classical explanation is this: *khushūʿ* here describes the abasement, *ihṭāʿ* the compulsion. The eye is lowered, but the body is being dragged towards the call. **There is no resistance, and no desire either.** A person is going quickly to a place he does not want to go, unable to raise his eyes.

I transmit this explanation; it is widely reconciled in this way in the sources.

### يَقُولُ ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ — the disbelievers say

The sentence is in the **imperfect**: *yaqūlu* — "they say". The verbs around it (*yakhrujūn*, *muhṭiʿīn*) are on the same plane; the scene is told as it happens.

**That the speaker is specified is worth recording.** The verse does not say "people say", it says *"**al-kāfirūn** say"*. This is not, then, a sentence everyone will say on that day; it is a sentence a particular group will say.

Elsewhere the Qurʾān says how the same day will be for the believers: *"The greatest terror will not grieve them"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/103), *"On that day you will see the believing men and the believing women, their light running before them"* (al-Ḥadīd 57/12 — treated in **`057-hadid.md`**).

### يَوْمٌ عَسِرٌ — a hard day

**Root: ع-س-ر.** Its concrete sense: **hardship, difficulty.** *ʿUsr* — straitness, distress. And its exact opposite is **يُسْر**.

This opposition was treated in detail in this commentary in **`094-insirah.md`** (*inna maʿa'l-ʿusri yusrā*) and I do not repeat it.

**But the encounter within the sūra must be recorded, and it is the most striking weave of words in it:**

| Verse | Word | Root | What it says |
|---|---|---|---|
| **8** | *yawmun **ʿasir*** | **ع-س-ر** | That day is **hard** |
| **17, 22, 32, 40** | *wa-laqad **yassarnā**'l-Qurʾān* | **ي-س-ر** | The Qurʾān has been **made easy** |

**The sūra places the sentences "a hard day" and "a book made easy" inside the same text** — and repeats the second four times.

**I record this as my own reading.** The opposition of the two roots is linguistic data; the presence of the two expressions in the same sūra is data of the text; the relation I set up between them is mine. And it is this: the sūra sets an ease and a hardship side by side and says **which one comes first.** What has been made easy is in hand now; the hard thing comes after. What joins them is the question of the refrain — *"is there anyone who will remember?"*

**The difference between عَسِر and عَسِير.** At al-Muddaththir 74/9 the same day is called *yawmun **ʿasīr***. That verse was treated in **`074-muddessir.md`**. The difference is in the pattern: *ʿasir* is a quasi-participle, *ʿasīr* an intensive form; the second is heavier. **The verse-ending has a share in the choice here** — all the verses of this sūra descend to the sound *r* on short syllables, and *ʿasīr* does not sit on that pattern. I gave this in tabular form in the section on verse-endings above.

## I. Noah — 54/9-17

## 54/9 — كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ فَكَذَّبُوا۟ عَبْدَنَا وَقَالُوا۟ مَجْنُونٌ وَٱزْدُجِرَ

*Kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin fa-kadhdhabū ʿabdanā wa qālū majnūnun wa'zdujir*
"Before them the people of Noah denied. They denied Our servant and said, 'a madman', and he was driven off."

### The opening of the list

Verse nine opens the second section of the sūra, and the manner of the opening is decisive: **the name of the people comes after the verb, while "before them" comes immediately after it.**

*Kadhdhabat **qablahum** qawmu Nūḥin.*

That is, the second word of the sentence is an adverb of time. Before the reader hears the name of the people he hears the words **"before you"**. In its very first sentence the list establishes its link with the one addressed.

The same pattern is not repeated in the other three accounts; there the start is straight from the name of the people (*kadhdhabat ʿĀdun*, *kadhdhabat Thamūdu*, *kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭin*). **"Before them" is only in the first account** — because establishing the time once is enough.

### كَذَّبَتْ قَوْمُ — a grammatical point

The verb comes in the **feminine**: *kadhdhabat*. Yet the word *qawm* is masculine.

In Arabic, **broken plurals** and **collective nouns** can be treated as either masculine or feminine. *Qawm* comes both ways in the Qurʾān. The feminine here is referred to the sense of "community" (*jamāʿa*, *umma*, *ṭāʾifa*).

The same feminine is repeated four times in the sūra: *kadhdhabat ʿĀdun* (18), *kadhdhabat Thamūdu* (23), *kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭin* (33). The names ʿĀd and Thamūd too become feminine when counted as tribal names. All four on the same pattern.

**The fifth account falls outside this pattern**, and that is no accident: *wa-laqad **jāʾa** āla Firʿawna'l-nudhur* (41) — the verb changes, the subject changes, the pattern is broken. I take this up under verse forty-one.

### عَبْدَنَا — Our servant

Noah is named here not as **"Our messenger"** but as **"Our servant"**.

**Root: ع-ب-د.** This root has been treated in many places in this commentary and I do not repeat it. Our concern here is **the choice of word.**

The Qurʾān uses several designations for the prophets and they do not all do the same job:

| Designation | What it looks at |
|---|---|
| **رَسُول** | The mission — his having been sent |
| **نَبِيّ** | The message — his receiving revelation |
| **عَبْد** | **The relation** — whose he is |

And the designation *ʿabd* is an expression of **honour** in the Qurʾān; it is used at the highest stations. At al-Isrāʾ 17/1, in the account of the Night Journey, *bi-ʿabdihi* is said; at al-Jinn 72/19, *ʿabdullāh*; at al-Kahf 18/65 al-Khiḍr is named *ʿabdan min ʿibādinā*.

**Its counterpart within the sūra:** in verse nine Noah is set opposite the deniers as "Our servant". That is, the verse defines both sides by their relations — one is a **servant**, the others **deniers**.

**And this detail of word order matters:** after saying *"the people denied"* the sentence says again *"they denied Our servant"*. The same verb twice. In the first there is no object, in the second there is. First the act of denying is set down absolutely, then its object is named — and the name of the object is not "Noah" but **"Our servant."**

I record this as my own reading: **the one denied is named by his relation to God.** Whoever the act may be aimed at, seen through the relation, whose the denied thing is changes.

### مَجْنُونٌ — a madman

**Root: ج-ن-ن.** Its concrete sense: **to cover, to conceal.** *Janna'l-layl* — the night covered. From this root: *jinn* (the unseen creature), *janna* (the garden whose trees cover the ground), *janīn* (the one covered in the womb), *junna* (a shield), *majnūn*.

**Majnūn** — passive participle: **covered over.** The Arabic picture of madness, that is, is of a covering drawn over the mind.

As was recorded in **`051-zariyat.md`**, this accusation has become a set formula in the Qurʾān: at al-Dhāriyāt 51/52 it is said, *"No messenger came to those before them without their saying, 'a sorcerer or a madman'"* — and the link between that verse and this sūra is direct: **at al-Qamar 54/2 "magic" was said; at 54/9 "madman" is said.** The two words of al-Dhāriyāt stand distributed across two verses of al-Qamar.

I record this link as an observation; the wording of the two sūras can be verified.

### وَٱزْدُجِرَ — and he was driven off

**Root: ز-ج-ر**, treated at verse four. There are two new points here.

**One — the verb is passive.** *Uzdujira* — "he was driven off, he was rebuked". The agent is not named. The same method was recorded in the section on al-Ḥāqqa for *uhlikū* (they were destroyed): the passive, by concealing the agent, brings the event itself forward. Here the concealed agent is obvious (his people), but not naming him **generalises** the act: it is not asked who did it; it is said that it was done.

**Two — a disagreement over the sentence's construction.** To what does the verb *wa'zdujira* attach?

| Solution | Sense |
|---|---|
| **A separate sentence** | They said "a madman" **and** Noah was driven off (two distinct events) |
| **Coordinated with *qālū*** | They said "a madman" **and drove him off** — the driving off is also their act |
| **A complement of *majnūn*** | They said, "he is a madman, the jinn have driven/struck him" — that is, a continuation of the accusation |

The third solution is striking: on this reading *uzdujira* falls inside their speech and means "driven off / prodded by the jinn"; that is, it spells out the accusation.

**All three solutions are transmitted and I do not impose a preference.** The first is the simplest and fits best with the verb's being passive; but the third is not weak, since it makes use of the closeness in sense between *majnūn* and *zajr*.

## 54/10 — فَدَعَا رَبَّهُۥٓ أَنِّى مَغْلُوبٌ فَٱنتَصِرْ

*Fa-daʿā Rabbahu annī maghlūbun fa'ntaṣir*
"So he called upon his Lord: 'I am overcome; give help.'"

### The shortest prayer in the Qurʾān

Noah's prayer is **three words**: *annī maghlūbun fa'ntaṣir.*

In **`071-nuh.md`** this verse was cited and this note was made: the imprecation at the end of Sūrat Nūḥ is *"not an imprecation made from a position of power; it is the cry of a man who has been beaten"*, and it was stated that this is morally decisive. Here I add to that note.

**فَدَعَا — Root: د-ع-و.** In the section on Sūrat Nūḥ it was shown that this root is the backbone of that sūra: there Noah was calling **his people** (*daʿawtu qawmī*, 71/5); here he calls **his Lord**. The same root, the same man, two directions. That the root does both jobs at once was already recorded in the section on Sūrat Nūḥ.

**And its link with verse six is worth recording:** there, *yadʿu'l-dāʿī* — on the Day of Resurrection **the caller** calls. The root occurs three times in the sūra, in three distinct positions: a calling angel, a calling prophet, a called Lord.

### أَنِّى مَغْلُوبٌ — "I am overcome"

**Root: غ-ل-ب.** Its concrete sense: **to prevail, to overpower, to come out on top.** *Ghalaba* — he overcame. *Ghālib* — the one who overcomes. *Maghlūb* — passive participle: **the one overcome.**

This root was treated in **`080-abese.md`**.

The side of the sentence to dwell on is this: **Noah speaks not about his people but about himself.**

Think of the things he could have said: "They are wrongdoers", "They denied", "They drove me off". All would have been true, and all have already been said in the preceding verse. Noah says none of them. **His one-sentence report is about himself: "I am overcome."**

**I record this as my own reading:** this is not a complaint but a **statement of condition**. There is no accusation in the sentence, no epithet, no verdict. There is only a balance-sheet — and the one who draws it up is the losing side.

And the passive form of *maghlūb* strengthens this. He does not say "they overcame me" (*ghalabūnī*); he says "I am one overcome" (*maghlūb*). **The name of the victor does not appear.**

### فَٱنتَصِرْ — give help

**Root: ن-ص-ر.** This root was treated in **`059-hasr.md`**, **`061-saff.md`**, **`071-nuh.md`**, **`072-cin.md`** and **`086-tarik.md`**. What the lexicographers record about its concrete sense: *naṣr* is **help**, but especially **help that brings victory**. Not merely lending support; support that changes the outcome.

The verb is on the pattern **إِفْتِعَال** (Form VIII): *intaṣara*. Here that form can give two senses:

- **To take vengeance for oneself / to obtain one's right.**
- **To help, to give victory** (in some usages close to *naṣara*).

The imperative: *fa'ntaṣir* — "prevail / take my right for me."

**And now we come to one of the most striking weaves in the sūra.** The same root occurs once more in the sūra, in verse forty-four — and this time **from the mouths of the deniers**:

| Verse | Expression | Who says it | Its sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| **10** | *innī **maghlūbun** fa'**ntaṣir*** | **Noah** — to his Lord | "I am **overcome**; give help" |
| **44** | *naḥnu jamīʿun **muntaṣir*** | **The deniers** — about themselves | "We are a host **that cannot be beaten**" |
| **45** | *sa-yuhzamu'l-jamʿu wa yuwallūna'l-dubur* | **The text** — the answer | "That host **shall be routed**" |

**One man says "I am overcome" and receives help; a host says "we cannot be beaten" and is routed.** And the word the two sides use is two forms of the same root: *intaṣir* and *muntaṣir*.

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording of the three verses and the shared root are verifiable data of the text; the conclusion I draw from the contrast is mine.

## 54/11-12 — فَفَتَحْنَآ أَبْوَٰبَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ بِمَآءٍ مُّنْهَمِرٍ · وَفَجَّرْنَا ٱلْأَرْضَ عُيُونًا فَٱلْتَقَى ٱلْمَآءُ عَلَىٰٓ أَمْرٍ قَدْ قُدِرَ

*Fa-fataḥnā abwāba'l-samāʾi bi-māʾin munhamir — wa fajjarna'l-arḍa ʿuyūnan fa'ltaqa'l-māʾu ʿalā amrin qad qudir*
"So We opened the gates of heaven with water pouring down. And We made the earth burst forth in springs. And the water met upon a matter already decreed."

These two verses are the most tightly built word order in the sūra, and they cannot be read without seeing the symmetry.

### The meeting of two waters — the symmetry in the word order

Let us first set the two verses side by side:

| | **Verse 11 — from above** | **Verse 12 — from below** |
|---|---|---|
| Connective | **فَـ** — immediately after | **وَ** — and at the same moment |
| Verb | **فَتَحْنَا** — We opened | **فَجَّرْنَا** — We made burst forth |
| Form of the verb | **Form I** (bare) | **Form II** (*tafʿīl* — intensive/multiplicative) |
| Object | **أَبْوَابَ ٱلسَّمَاءِ** — the gates of heaven | **ٱلْأَرْضَ** — the earth (itself) |
| How the water is named | **بِمَاءٍ مُّنْهَمِرٍ** — water pouring down | **عُيُونًا** — in springs |
| Direction | **Descent** | **Ascent** |
| State of the water | Poured, flowing | Bursting, under pressure |

**Four separate contrasts work at once:** heaven/earth, opening/splitting, descent/ascent, pouring/bursting.

And then comes the third clause: **فَٱلْتَقَى ٱلْمَآءُ** — "the water met".

**And here is the real point: the water is SINGULAR.**

The verse does not say *"the waters met"* (*al-miyāh*). **الْمَاءُ** — definite, singular. What comes from two separate sources is, at the moment of meeting, named **a single water**.

What the word order does, then, is this: **it sets up a duality and ties it to a unity.** Two verbs, two directions, two descriptions — and then a single noun.

**I record this as my own reading.** That the word is singular and definite is data of the text; the reading of "from duality to unity" that I draw from it is mine.

In **`082-infitar.md`** this verse was cited and it was recorded that the resurrection may be described as a repetition of the flood on a universal scale; in **`078-nebe.md`** in the context of the gates of heaven; in **`080-abese.md`** in the context of *fajjarnā*. I rely on all three and do not repeat them.

### فَتَحْنَا — to open

**Root: ف-ت-ح.** In **`077-murselat.md`** this root was distinguished from the other roots that describe the opening of the sky, and the distinction recorded there is decisive here:

> *ف-ت-ح is also an opening, but it is **the opening of a door**; it does not convey splitting.*

This is why the verse speaks of **gates**. The root wants a door.

**أَبْوَابَ ٱلسَّمَاء** — the gates of heaven. The Qurʾān uses this expression elsewhere too:

- *"The gates of heaven will not be opened for them"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/40) — about the deniers.
- *"And the sky is opened and becomes gates"* (al-Nabaʾ 78/19) — treated in **`078-nebe.md`**.

**The comparison with al-Aʿrāf 7/40 is worth recording.** There the gates of heaven are **not opened** for the deniers; here they **are** opened for the deniers — but what comes through is not prayer, it is water. The same gate works in two directions.

I record this as a reading of the Qurʾān by the Qurʾān; the wording of the two verses can be verified, the contrast between them is my observation.

### مُّنْهَمِر — pouring down

**Root: ه-م-ر.** *Hamara* — he poured, he made flow. *Inhamara* — it poured out, it came down in a downpour.

The word is the active participle of the pattern **إِنْفِعَال** (Form VII): *munhamir*. And Form VII, as was recorded in the section on al-Inshiqāq, conveys **the middle sense**: undergoing an outside action.

That is, the water does not pour of itself; **it is released to pour.** And this fits the preceding verb: the gates were opened, the water poured out. Another opened the gate; the water poured.

**The word occurs only here in the Qurʾān.** The note used in this commentary for words of this kind holds here too: a word with a single occurrence is read by its lexical sense, and no intra-Qurʾānic weave of usage is sought for it.

### فَجَّرْنَا — to make burst forth

**Root: ف-ج-ر.** In **`097-kadr.md`** this root was treated by way of *fajr*, and what was recorded there is this: the root's proper sense is **to split, to crack, to open**; *infijār* (an explosion), *fajjara* (to make a spring gush), *tafjīr* are from the same root. **The name of the dawn also comes from here**: the light that splits the darkness of the night and comes forth.

The verb here is on **Form II** (*tafʿīl*), and this form conveys **multiplicity**: not splitting once, but **splitting all over.**

**عُيُونًا — a grammatical point.**

The object of the sentence is **the earth**, not the springs: *fajjarna'l-**arḍa*** — "We split the earth". What, then, is *ʿuyūnan*?

*ʿUyūnan* is a **specifier** (an accusative of specification). That is, the sentence does not say *"we opened springs in the earth"*; it says **"we split the earth into springs"**.

The difference matters, and it can also be seen:

| Construction | The picture it gives |
|---|---|
| "We opened springs in the earth" | Water coming out at particular points |
| **"We split the earth into springs"** | **The whole of the earth turning into water** |

The verse says the second. Not a part of the earth but the earth itself becomes a spring.

The same specifier structure occurs elsewhere in the Qurʾān: *"The head has **taken flame** with old age"* (Maryam 19/4 — *wa'shtaʿala'l-raʾsu **shayban***) — there too what burns is the head, and old age is the specifier; not "my head has whitened" but "my head has caught fire with old age".

**عَيْن — Root: ع-ي-ن.** This word was treated in **`055-rahman.md`**, **`056-vakia.md`**, **`067-mulk.md`**, **`052-tur.md`** and **`077-murselat.md`**: it means both "eye" and "spring". The same word will come back two verses later (14) in the sense of **"Our eyes"**.

| Verse | *ʿAyn* | Its sense |
|---|---|---|
| **12** | *ʿuyūnan* | **Springs** |
| **14** | *bi-aʿyuninā* | **Eyes** |

One word, two verses apart, in two senses. I record this and draw no meaning from it; the polysemy of the word is settled in Arabic.

### عَلَىٰٓ أَمْرٍ قَدْ قُدِرَ — upon a matter already decreed

And the sentence ends here. The flood, the gates, the springs — all are tied to a single expression.

**أَمْر — Root: أ-م-ر.** Command, matter, decree. This word occurs **three times** in the sūra, and all three in decisive positions:

| Verse | Expression | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **3** | *wa kullu **amrin** mustaqirr* | Every matter comes to rest |
| **12** | *ʿalā **amrin** qad qudir* | The flood, upon a matter that has been measured |
| **50** | *wa mā **amrunā** illā wāḥidatun* | Our command is but one |

In verse three the **rule**, in verse twelve the **instance**, in verse fifty the **source**. The word does the same job on three separate layers of the sūra.

**قَدْ قُدِرَ — Root: ق-د-ر.** And here the backbone root of the sūra enters for the first time.

This root was treated in detail in **`097-kadr.md`** in its three senses, and below, under verse forty-nine, I shall compare two of its uses. Here I record only this: the verb is **passive** (*qudira* — it was decreed) and is reinforced with **قَدْ**. *Qad* + the perfect conveys **confirmation** in Arabic: "decreed for certain".

That is, the verse says: **the flood is not an overflowing; it is a measured matter.**

And this is the most repeated theme of the sūra. The table below shows the root's four occurrences in it:

| Verse | Form | Whose it is |
|---|---|---|
| **12** | *qad **qudir*** — decreed | **The flood** |
| **42** | *akhdha ʿazīzin **muqtadir*** — the seizing of One mighty, All-Powerful | **The destruction of Pharaoh** |
| **49** | *khalaqnāhu **bi-qadar*** — We created it by a measure | **Everything** |
| **55** | *ʿinda malīkin **muqtadir*** | **Where the godfearing are seated** |

**The sūra's first destruction and its last reward are sealed with the same root.** I take this circle up again under verse forty-nine.

## 54/13-14 — وَحَمَلْنَٰهُ عَلَىٰ ذَاتِ أَلْوَٰحٍ وَدُسُرٍ · تَجْرِى بِأَعْيُنِنَا جَزَآءً لِّمَن كَانَ كُفِرَ

*Wa ḥamalnāhu ʿalā dhāti alwāḥin wa dusur — tajrī bi-aʿyuninā jazāʾan li-man kāna kufir*
"And We carried him upon a thing of planks and nails. It ran under Our eyes — as a recompense for the one who had been denied."

### The ship is not named

The first thing to be noticed in this verse is **the word that is not said.**

The Qurʾān uses several words for a ship: *fulk* (Hūd 11/37 — *wa'ṣnaʿi'l-fulk*), *safīna* (al-Kahf 18/79), *al-jāriya* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/11). None of these is in this verse.

In their place there is a **description**: **ذَاتِ أَلْوَاحٍ وَدُسُرٍ** — "(that which is) possessed of planks and nails."

*Dhāt* in Arabic means "possessor of", and what it qualifies has been elided. There is in fact no noun in the sentence; **there is a list of materials.**

**I record this as my own reading:** the Qurʾān here does not call the ship a ship; **it names it by taking it apart.** The thing that saves a world is described as consisting of wood and nails. And the next clause will say what carried it: eyes.

### أَلْوَاح — planks

**Root: ل-و-ح.** In **`085-buruc.md`** this root was treated in detail (in the context of *al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ*), and this verse was cited there. The note made there holds here too:

> *"The same word is used of the planks of a ship. That is, *lawḥ* is not a sacred term; it is the name of a material. A flat, level piece."*

The concrete sense of the root is **to gleam, to appear** (*lāḥa* — it appeared, it gleamed), and from this **a flat, broad surface that can be written on.** The *alwāḥ* given to Moses is the same word (al-Aʿrāf 7/145).

So the same word occurs in three separate places in the Qurʾān: **the tablet on which revelation is written**, **the tablets of Moses**, and **the planks of Noah's ship.**

### دُسُر — nails, or ropes?

**Root: د-س-ر.** *Dasara* — **to push, to thrust in, to drive in, to pierce.** At the centre of the root is **thrusting in with force.**

The word occurs only here in the Qurʾān and its sense is disputed:

| View | What *dusur* is | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Nails** | Plural of *disār*: the nails that hold the planks together | Directly in keeping with the root's sense of "to drive in"; the view of the majority |
| **Ropes / fibres** | Cords of palm fibre binding the planks together | In old Arab and Indian Ocean shipbuilding, planks were joined by **sewing**; and a cord too is a thing "thrust through" |
| **Prow / keel** | The forward part of the ship that **pushes** the water | From the root's sense of "to push" |

**All three views are recorded and I do not impose a preference.** But it should be stated that the second view has a historical ground: **the technique of joining planks by sewing them with palm-fibre cords rather than with nails is a known shipbuilding technique** on the Arabian Peninsula and around the Indian Ocean, and it was used in the region for a long time. To read the word as "rope", then, corresponds to a technique the one addressed would have known.

I transmit this historical information as a datum of shipbuilding; **I do not claim** that the verse intends this technique, and I draw no miracle from it.

**Whatever the view, the common point is this:** both words are elements of **joining**. *Alwāḥ* are the parts; *dusur* is what holds them together. In the verse the ship is described as **parts + what holds the parts.**

### وَحَمَلْنَاهُ — "We carried him"

The subject of the verb is **God**: *ḥamalnā* — "We carried". Noah's building of the ship is not mentioned here.

In **`069-hakka.md`** the "three carryings" of this commentary were set out, and *ḥamalnākum fi'l-jāriya* at al-Ḥāqqa 69/11 was treated there in detail. The only thing to be added here is this:

In al-Ḥāqqa it is **"We carried you"** — to the one addressed. In al-Qamar it is **"We carried him"** — Noah. The two sūras name the same event with two different pronouns.

| | **al-Ḥāqqa 69/11** | **al-Qamar 54/13** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *ḥamalnā**kum*** | *ḥamalnā**hu*** |
| Whom it carries | **You** — the one addressed | **Him** — Noah |
| How the ship is named | *al-jāriya* — that which runs | *dhāti alwāḥin wa dusur* — possessed of planks and nails |
| Emphasis | **The addressee's line of descent** | **The material of the vessel** |

Al-Ḥāqqa ties the event to the reader; al-Qamar reduces the event to its material. **This fits the general bearing of the two sūras:** al-Ḥāqqa puts the addressee inside the event, al-Qamar sets the event before him as a list.

I record this as my own reading.

### تَجْرِى بِأَعْيُنِنَا — "it was running under Our eyes"

**تَجْرِي — Root: ج-ر-ي.** To run, to flow, to move along. It was treated in the section on al-Ḥāqqa by way of *al-jāriya*.

The verb is **imperfect** — among the perfect verbs around it. For a moment, that is, the scene becomes **live**: we carried… it is running. The same technique will be used in verse twenty (*tanziʿu'l-nās* — it tears people out).

**بِأَعْيُنِنَا — and here a note on transcendence must be made.**

Rendered word for word the expression means "with Our eyes / within Our eyes". And expressions of this kind — those that ascribe hand, eye, face or throne to God — are among the most disputed matters in the history of theology.

The note made in **`069-hakka.md`** for *ʿarsha Rabbika* and in **`089-fecr.md`** for the same question holds here in exactly the same way, and I do not repeat it: in the classical tradition there are two basic stances — **tafwīḍ** (referring the meaning to God, not speaking about the modality) and **taʾwīl** (interpreting the expression within the figurative possibilities of the language). **This commentary takes no side in theology.**

What can be said on the side of the language, however, is this, and it is not disputed: **"to be before someone's eyes" is a settled idiom in Arabic** and means **to be under protection, to be watched over.** English carries the same field with expressions such as "under one's eye" and "to keep an eye on".

The Qurʾān uses the same expression in three places and in all three with the same function:

- *"Build the ship **under Our eyes** and by Our revelation"* (Hūd 11/37) — the building of the ship.
- **al-Qamar 54/14** — the running of the ship.
- *"You are **under Our eyes**; so glorify your Lord with praise"* (al-Ṭūr 52/48) — treated in **`052-tur.md`**.
- And in the singular form as well: *"So that you might be reared **under My eye**"* (Ṭāhā 20/39) — the chest of Moses.

**In all four of these occurrences the context is a context of protection.** And in all four what is described is **a carrying under threat**: the ship being built, the ship running, the prophet under threat, the infant set upon the river.

I record this as an observation about usage; the wording and contexts of the four verses can be verified.

**A detail of form:** here the **plural** (*aʿyun*) is used, at Ṭāhā 20/39 the **singular** (*ʿaynī*). The use of the plural to express majesty is settled in Arabic (as in *ḥamalnā*, *fataḥnā*, *innā*). I draw from this no number and no modality.

### جَزَآءً لِّمَن كَانَ كُفِرَ — "a recompense for the one who had been denied"

The last piece of the sentence, and it is disputed.

**جَزَاء** — **Root: ج-ز-ي.** Recompense, an equivalent payment. The word is used both of reward and of punishment; it is neutral. Its case-analysis is that of an object of purpose (an accusative of cause): "as a recompense for…".

**كُفِرَ** — **Root: ك-ف-ر**, and the verb is **passive**: "he was denied / he was met with ingratitude."

Who, then, is *"the one denied"*?

| View | Who *man kāna kufir* is | Sense of the sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **Noah** | The messenger who was denied, rejected | The flood is the recompense for the wrong done **to Noah** |
| **God** | The Lord who was met with ingratitude, denied | The flood is the recompense for the ingratitude shown **to God** |

**Both views are transmitted in the classical sources.**

The ground of the first is the context: in the preceding verses Noah was denied, called "a madman", driven off; and he prayed. The sentence stands like the answer to his prayer.

The ground of the second is the dominant use of the root ك-ف-ر in the Qurʾān: the root is used not for a denial between people but mostly for a stance taken towards God.

**I do not impose a preference.** Both readings are in keeping with the rest of the sūra and **the practical result is the same**: the flood is named not as a natural event but as a **recompense**. The verse gives the event together with its agent and its ground.

**An observation about word order.** The verb *kufir* is passive; and the agent of the passive verb — those who denied — is not named. Yet they are the whole subject of the sentence. **The sūra does not mention the drowned.** As was recorded in the section on Sūrat Nūḥ, the flood was fitted there into a single word (*ughriqū*); here it is **not mentioned at all.** The verse tells of the ship, of the water, of the recompense — but not of the drowning.

I record this as my own reading.

## 54/15 — وَلَقَد تَّرَكْنَٰهَآ ءَايَةً فَهَلْ مِن مُّدَّكِرٍ

*Wa-laqad taraknāhā āyatan fa-hal min muddakir*
"And We left it as a sign. Is there anyone who will remember?"

### Half of the refrain

This verse is **not the full refrain.** It carries the refrain's second half (*fa-hal min muddakir*), not its first. This occurs twice in the sūra: here and in verse fifty-one.

| Verse | First half | Second half |
|---|---|---|
| **15** | *wa-laqad **taraknāhā āyatan*** | *fa-hal min muddakir* |
| 17, 22, 32, 40 | *wa-laqad **yassarnā'l-Qurʾāna li'l-dhikri*** | *fa-hal min muddakir* |
| **51** | *wa-laqad **ahlaknā ashyāʿakum*** | *fa-hal min muddakir* |

**Three first halves, one single second half.** And all three begin with **وَلَقَدْ**.

So in the sūra the question *fa-hal min muddakir* is attached to **three different things**:

1. **A sign left behind** (15) — the remnant of an event in the past.
2. **A Qurʾān made easy** (17, 22, 32, 40) — the text in hand.
3. **Destroyed counterparts** (51) — those of the addressee's own kind.

The question rests, then, on three separate sources: **the event, the text, and the addressee himself.** And in all three it ends with the same sentence.

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording and distribution of the three verses are data of the text; the threefold classification is mine.

### تَرَكْنَاهَا — "We left it"

**Root: ت-ر-ك.** To leave, to abandon, to leave behind. *Turāth* / *tarika* — an inheritance, the property left behind.

The verb states that a thing was **not removed**. The verse does not say "we made it"; it says **"we did not take it away"**. The sign is what remains of the event.

**To what does the pronoun هَا refer?** The verse does not say, and there is disagreement:

| View | The referent | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The ship** | The nearest concrete object mentioned (13-14) | The description of the ship is in the context |
| **The event / the account** | The whole of the flood | What is a *sign* is not an object but an occurrence |
| **The punishment** | The destruction itself | The subject of the sūra is punishment |

**I do not impose a preference.** The second view is the one that rubs least against the text, since *āya* in the Qurʾān generally names an event or a proof. But the reading "the ship" is not weak either; elsewhere the Qurʾān speaks as though pointing at a remnant of the ship: *"We saved him and the people of the ship, and We made it a sign for all beings"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/15).

**Al-ʿAnkabūt 29/15 is worth reading together with this verse:** there *jaʿalnāhā* (We made it), here *taraknāhā* (We left it). **To make is to do; to leave is not to do.** The two verbs say the same result from two different directions.

### مُّدَّكِر — one who remembers

**Root: ذ-ك-ر.** This is one of the roots most often treated in this commentary — in **`087-ala.md`**, **`074-muddessir.md`**, **`080-abese.md`**, **`069-hakka.md`**, **`051-zariyat.md`** and in many other places. Its concrete sense: **to remember, to call to mind, to keep in mind.**

The issue here is the word's **form**, and it requires attention.

**مُدَّكِر** is in fact **مُذْتَكِر**: the active participle of the pattern **إِفْتِعَال** (Form VIII), from the verb *iddakara*.

The sound change occurred in two steps:
1. The letter ذ of the root and the letter ت of the form come side by side: *mudhtakir*.
2. The two letters assimilate to one another and merge into **د** (assimilation): **muddakir.**

There are two more changes of the same kind in this sūra: **مُزْدَجَر** (4) and **ٱزْدُجِرَ** (9) — there, under the influence of ز, ت became د. And **ٱصْطَبِرْ** (27) — there, under the influence of ص, ت will become ط.

**The sūra thus displays three separate sound-assimilations of Form VIII together:** *muzdajar* (ز→د), *muddakir* (ذ→د), *iṣṭabir* (ص→ط). I record this as a datum of the language; I draw no meaning from it.

**What Form VIII contributes in sense is this:** here the pattern conveys **effort and acquisition**. *Dhakara* is to remember; *iddakara* is **to take a lesson, to draw a moral**. The word describes not a passive remembering but **taking a thing and making it one's own.**

### فَهَلْ مِن مُّدَّكِرٍ — how the question is built

The question consists of three elements, and all three are meaningful.

**هَلْ** — the interrogative particle. The difference the lexicographers record between *a-* (hamza) and *hal*: *hal* asks a **yes-or-no question** (is there / did it happen), while the hamza can ask both a yes-or-no and an **identifying** question (is it this one or that one). Here it is *hal*: the question is not "who" but **"is there"**.

**مِنْ** — this *min* is of the kind the lexicographers call **exhaustive** or **redundant**. The same structure was treated in the section on al-Ḥāqqa (*fa-hal tarā lahum min bāqiya*). Its function is **to make a negation or a question fully comprehensive**: "anyone at all", "so much as one person".

**مُّدَّكِرٍ** — indefinite. It is not a name that is sought but a quality.

**Putting the three together, the question becomes:** *"Is there so much as one person who takes the lesson?"*

And here exactly the difference from the refrain of al-Raḥmān appears. **In al-Raḥmān's question there is a pronoun of address** (*Rabbi-kumā*, *tukadhdhibā-n*): the question is put to you. **In al-Qamar's question there is no pronoun at all**: the question is put into the open, and whoever is to answer places himself in that empty space.

In the section "The refrain of al-Raḥmān and the refrain of al-Qamar" above I gave this difference in tabular form; here I show its counterpart in the grammar.

## 54/16 — فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ

*Fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur*
"How then were My punishment and My warnings!"

### The first occurrence of the second refrain

This sentence occurs six times in the sūra; four in this form (16, 18, 21, 30), two in the imperative (37, 39).

### كَيْفَ — a question or not

**كَيْفَ** is a particle that asks after a state: "how". But here it is not a genuine request for information.

The lexicographers give such questions separate names:

- **The confirmatory question** — one whose answer is known, asked in order to make the addressee concede it.
- **The question of wonder** — one that conveys astonishment and dread.
- **The question of threat** — one that conveys a threat.

All three have been defended for this sentence, and there is no sharp boundary between them. In rendering it into English, an exclamation mark rather than a question mark falls closer to the function of the sentence: *"How was My punishment!"*

In **`067-mulk.md`** two sentences of exactly this pattern were treated: *fa-kayfa kāna **nakīr*** (67/18) and *kayfa kāna **nadhīr*** (67/17). I rely on the analysis there and do not repeat it.

### كَانَ — the perfect tense and the position of the addressee

The verb is **perfect**: *kāna* — "how **was** it".

And this is the most striking side of the sentence. **The sentence is said to people who have not yet seen the punishment, about people who have** — but the tense states that the event is over.

**I record this as my own reading:** this choice of tense places the addressee **after the event.** He hears, in the past tense, of something that has not yet come to him in the state in which it came to others. This is what the sūra does: it shows the future in the language of the past.

And this belongs to the same family as the grammatical question of the first verse. There *iqtarabat* was perfect and related something that had not yet happened. Here *kāna* is perfect and shows something that has happened to a community to whom it has not yet happened. **The sūra bends the tenses in both directions at once.**

### عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ — two words, one owner

*ʿAdhābī* — "my punishment". *Nudhur* — as was shown above in the section on verse-endings, its original is *nudhurī*; **the yāʾ has been dropped for the verse-ending.**

The same owner is thus present twice in the sentence, but visible once.

**That the two words stand side by side is worth recording.** *ʿAdhāb* is punishment; *nudhur* is warning. One is the outcome, the other what preceded it. The sentence gathers the two into one question: **"How was My punishment — and how were My warnings!"**

And what follows from this is: **the warning is named as part of the punishment.** The warnings too are the subject of the question "how was it". The verse asks, that is, not only about the severity of the penalty but about whether what was done before the penalty had also been carried out.

I record this as my own reading; its ground is that the two words are joined under a single attribution, and this is verifiable data of the word order.

## 54/17 — وَلَقَدْ يَسَّرْنَا ٱلْقُرْءَانَ لِلذِّكْرِ فَهَلْ مِن مُّدَّكِرٍ

*Wa-laqad yassarnā'l-Qurʾāna li'l-dhikri fa-hal min muddakir*
"We have indeed made the Qurʾān easy for remembrance. Is there anyone who will remember?"

### The full form of the first refrain

This sentence occurs **four times** in the sūra in exactly the same form: 17, 22, 32, 40. And all four are at the end of an account.

### وَلَقَدْ — a threefold reinforcement

The sentence opens with three elements of reinforcement:

1. **وَ** — here counted as the *wāw* of the oath; it shows that the sentence is the answer to an oath.
2. **لَ** — the *lām* of the answer: the reinforcing lām that enters the answer to an oath.
3. **قَدْ** — **confirmation** at the head of a perfect verb.

The sentence is therefore rendered "We have indeed, most certainly", and this is not exaggeration; it is the counterpart of three separate grammatical elements.

**And this triple occurs in every sentence in the sūra that begins with *wa-laqad*.** There are eight verses in the sūra opening with *wa-laqad*: 4, 15, 17, 22, 32, 36, 37, 38, 40, 41, 51. The most heavily reinforced sentences in the sūra, that is, are the joints of the accounts.

### يَسَّرْنَا — We have made easy

**Root: ي-س-ر.** This root was treated in **`094-insirah.md`**, **`092-leyl.md`**, **`087-ala.md`**, **`084-insikak.md`**, **`073-muzzemmil.md`**, **`080-abese.md`** and **`051-zariyat.md`**. Its concrete sense: **ease, suitability, being able to be done without strain.**

In **`092-leyl.md`** precisely this verse was cited: *"In Form II, يَسَّرَ — to make easy. In the Qurʾān: 'We have indeed made the Qurʾān easy for remembrance' (al-Qamar 54/17, repeated four times in the sūra)."* I rely on that.

Two things are to be added here.

**One — the pattern.** The verb is on **Form II** (*tafʿīl*): *yassara*. The load this form carries here is **transitivising** and **gradual**: to **make** a thing easy. This does not mean that the Qurʾān was not easy and was made so afterwards; it means that **it was given as easy.**

The Qurʾān uses the same verb of itself once more: *"We have made it easy in your tongue"* (al-Dukhān 44/58; Maryam 19/97 — *fa-innamā **yassarnāhu** bi-lisānik*).

**Two — what was made easy.** The verse does not simply say *"We made the Qurʾān easy"*; it says **"We made it easy للذِّكْرِ"**. The ease, that is, was given **for a particular work**.

**لِلذِّكْرِ** — the lām conveys purpose: "for remembrance". And *dhikr* here can be read in two senses, both of which are transmitted:

| Reading | What *dhikr* means | What was made easy |
|---|---|---|
| **To memorise / hold in mind** | The text's staying in the memory | **The wording** — easy to memorise |
| **To take heed / draw a lesson** | Taking the lesson | **The meaning** — easy to understand and to draw a lesson from |

**Both readings are widely transmitted and I do not impose a preference.** But the context of the sūra appears to support the second: the word *muddakir* in the continuation of the sentence is from the same root and means **one who draws a lesson**. Since *dhikr* and *muddakir* are from the same root, then, what was made easy and what is expected are two faces of the same work.

**I record this not as a preference but as an observation about the context; it is not binding.**

### The logic of the sentence

The two halves of the refrain build a logic, and this logic covers all four accounts of the sūra:

**The first half is a claim:** the instrument was made easy.
**The second half is a question:** is there anyone using it?

And what comes between them is **the accounts.** The sūra does this: it relates the destruction of a people, then asks *"this is easy to understand — is there anyone who understands?"*

**Four times.**

And in all four the same thing is said, but **the material piled up behind it changes.** At verse seventeen there is one account behind it; at twenty-two, two; at thirty-two, three; at forty, four.

**This is the reverse of the "first pile up, then ask item by item" observation recorded in the section on al-Raḥmān.** Al-Raḥmān piles up a list of twelve verses and then makes each item in turn the subject of a question. Al-Qamar **asks the same question after each account**, and each time the question rides on more material.

| | **al-Raḥmān 55** | **al-Qamar 54** |
|---|---|---|
| Method | First pile up, then ask item by item | Ask the same question after each part |
| Material behind the refrain | **One item** each time | **One account more** each time |
| Weight of the question | Constant | **Increasing** |

**I record this as my own reading.** The distribution of the refrains in the two sūras has been given in tabular form and is verifiable data; the interpretation "increasing weight" is mine.

## II. ʿĀd — 54/18-22

## 54/18 — كَذَّبَتْ عَادٌ فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ

*Kadhdhabat ʿĀdun fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur*
"ʿĀd denied. How then were My punishment and My warnings!"

### The account opening with the refrain

This verse is the most striking choice in the structure of the sūra, and I gave it in tabular form above: **the punishment refrain has been placed at the beginning of the account, not at its end.**

In the other three accounts the order is: name of the people → offence → punishment → refrain. Here the order is: name of the people → **refrain** → description of the punishment → **refrain.**

The result: the account of ʿĀd becomes nothing but a three-verse description with the same sentence set on either side of it.

```
18  كذبت عاد    فكيف كان عذابي ونذر
19  إنا أرسلنا عليهم ريحا صرصرا في يوم نحس مستمر
20  تنزع الناس كأنهم أعجاز نخل منقعر
21              فكيف كان عذابي ونذر
```

**I record this as my own reading:** this arrangement puts verses nineteen and twenty **inside a parenthesis.** The question is asked and answered, then the question is asked again. The reader therefore reads the description as the answer to a question asked twice.

And this has to do with ʿĀd's being **the shortest account.** A narration of three verses may have been framed so that it should not be lost. This last sentence is a conjecture and I present it as one.

### About ʿĀd

The people of ʿĀd and Iram were treated in detail in **`089-fecr.md`**: where the people lived, the meaning of the expression *dhāt al-ʿimād*, the disagreements about them and the historical background are given there. In **`069-hakka.md`** the destruction of ʿĀd was taken up in detail: the singular form of *rīḥ*, *ṣarṣar*, *ʿātiya*, *ḥusūman*, *ṣarʿā*, *aʿjāzu nakhlin khāwiya*.

**I do not repeat these two.** Below I give only what belongs to al-Qamar.

In the section on al-Ḥāqqa this observation was recorded and it holds here too: **al-Fajr named ʿĀd by what they did, al-Ḥāqqa by what befell them.** Al-Qamar does a third thing: **it mentions neither what they did nor what they built.** In al-Qamar ʿĀd has a single act: *kadhdhabat* — they denied.

| Sūra | How ʿĀd is named |
|---|---|
| **al-Fajr 89/6-8** | **By what they did** — the pillars, buildings the like of which were not created |
| **al-Ḥāqqa 69/6-7** | **By what befell them** — the wind, seven nights and eight days, those laid low |
| **al-Qamar 54/18-20** | **By a single act** — they denied |

Three sūras take three different faces of the same people. And the face al-Qamar takes is the one that suits this sūra's list: the sūra lists peoples **by their common act.**

## 54/19 — إِنَّآ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ رِيحًا صَرْصَرًا فِى يَوْمِ نَحْسٍ مُّسْتَمِرٍّ

*Innā arsalnā ʿalayhim rīḥan ṣarṣaran fī yawmi naḥsin mustamirr*
"We sent against them a howling wind on a day of unrelenting misfortune."

### رِيحًا صَرْصَرًا

In **`069-hakka.md`** these two words were treated in detail and I do not repeat them. Two of the things recorded there hold here as well:

- **رِيح comes in the singular**, and in the Qurʾān the singular *rīḥ* occurs mostly in contexts of punishment, the plural *riyāḥ* mostly in contexts of mercy. The section on al-Ḥāqqa drew attention to this distribution and **did not present it as a rule, since it could not be said with certainty whether it has exceptions.** The same note holds here.
- **صَرْصَر** is a reduplicated pattern (a doubled quadriliteral) and has two explanations: **howling** (*ṣarīr* — a shrill continuous sound) and **freezing** (*al-ṣirr* — biting cold). Many exegetes combine the two. **No preference was imposed there; I do not impose one here either.**

The section on al-Ḥāqqa also carried this note: *ṣarṣar* occurs in the Qurʾān only of the people of ʿĀd; **it is almost the name of a single event.**

### فِى يَوْمِ نَحْسٍ مُّسْتَمِرّ — a note that must be dwelt on

**نَحْس — Root: ن-ح-س.** The word means **misfortune, a state turning to the bad**, and its exact opposite is **سَعْد** (good fortune, felicity).

The same root yields one further word: **نُحَاس** — molten copper, or a smoke-like substance. The Qurʾān uses this at al-Raḥmān 55/35: *yursalu ʿalaykumā shuwāẓun min nārin wa **nuḥāsun***. That verse was treated in **`055-rahman.md`**.

**I do not speak with certainty about the link the lexicographers draw between the two senses.** Explanations connecting the tarnishing of copper with misfortune are transmitted in the sources; I do not present these explanations as etymological data.

**And here a note is required.**

This verse and its counterpart at Fuṣṣilat 41/16 (*fī ayyāmin **naḥisāt***) have been made a support, throughout history, for teachings about lucky and unlucky days. **It should be stated that the verse establishes no such system**, and the ground for this is in the Qurʾān itself.

**One.** The verse does not speak of a kind of day; it speaks of **the day of a particular event**. *Naḥs* qualifies that day by what it was for them: that day went badly for them. The sentence is not a piece of calendar lore but the description of an event.

**Two.** The Qurʾān explicitly rejects the practice of tying events to days and omens:

> *"When good came to them they said, 'this is ours'; and when an evil befell them they **took it as an ill omen** from Moses and those with him. Know that **their omen is with God**."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/131)
> *"They said, 'We have **met with ill fortune** because of you.' They said, '**Your ill fortune is from yourselves**.'"* (Yā Sīn 36/18-19)

The root used in both verses is **ط-ي-ر** (*ṭiyara* — to take auguries by releasing birds), and in both verses **the stance rejected is the same**: attaching the cause of an event to some outward sign.

**Three.** The verse itself already names the agent: *innā arsalnā* — **"We sent."** The misfortune of the day is not a power; it is a characterisation of the event.

I give these three points together with the notes of `USLUP.md` that *"the link with science, history and social science must not be forced"* and that *"where certainty is not spoken must be stated openly."* All three rest on the text of the Qurʾān; the stance in the conclusion is mine.

### مُّسْتَمِرّ — the second occurrence in the sūra

The word treated at verse two comes back here, and in exactly the same form: **مُّسْتَمِرّ.**

| Verse | Expression | Who says it |
|---|---|---|
| **2** | *siḥrun **mustamirr*** | **The deniers** — about a sign |
| **19** | *yawmi naḥsin **mustamirr*** | **The text** — about the day of ʿĀd's destruction |

At verse two the word could be read in three distinct senses (continuous / passing / strong). At verse nineteen **only one sense works: continuous.** For al-Ḥāqqa 69/7 gives the duration of the same event: seven nights and eight days.

In **`069-hakka.md`** the relation between these two verses was already taken up and this was recorded: *"The classical explanation is that the note* mustamirr *in al-Qamar conveys continuity and that the two verses do not conflict; the 'day of misfortune' gives the day the event began, while al-Ḥāqqa gives its duration."* I rely on that note and do not repeat it.

**The only thing I add here is the meeting of the two occurrences, and it is my own reading:** the deniers had qualified a sign as "going on and on" and passed by. At verse nineteen the same adjective is given to a people's day of destruction. **The word that enters the language of the sūra is the word that first came out of their mouths.**

This is the first of the three times the sūra does this. The others will come at verse twenty-six (*al-kadhdhābu'l-ashir*) and verse forty-seven (*ḍalālin wa suʿur*).

## 54/20 — تَنزِعُ ٱلنَّاسَ كَأَنَّهُمْ أَعْجَازُ نَخْلٍ مُّنقَعِرٍ

*Tanziʿu'l-nāsa ka-annahum aʿjāzu nakhlin munqaʿir*
"Tearing people out as though they were the trunks of uprooted palms."

### What has already been treated

In **`069-hakka.md`** this verse was treated in detail in comparison with al-Ḥāqqa 69/7. What was given there and I shall not repeat:

- That the word **أَعْجَاز** comes from the root ع-ج-ز and means the **trunk** of a palm tree; and that the root's relation to the branch meaning *incapacity* is transmitted as ascribed to the lexicographers.
- Why the choice of **نَخْل** is meaningful: the palm is Arabia's tallest, most enduring tree, at the centre of the economy; **the tree of permanence and of wealth.**
- That the two verses show **two moments of the same event**: al-Qamar in the middle of it (while the wind blows), al-Ḥāqqa at the end of it (after the wind has passed).
- That the verb **تَنزِعُ** comes from the root ن-ز-ع and that the same root is used at al-Maʿārij 70/16 (*nazzāʿatan li'l-shawā*). That link was taken up in **`070-mearic.md`**.
- The table of differences between **خَاوِيَة** (hollow) in al-Ḥāqqa and **مُنقَعِر** (torn up by the roots) here.

Below I give only the three things not taken up in the section on al-Ḥāqqa.

### مُنقَعِر — the root itself

**Root: ق-ع-ر.** And this root was not taken up in the section on al-Ḥāqqa.

Its concrete sense: **the very bottom of a thing, its base.** *Qaʿru'l-biʾr* — the bottom of a well. *Qaʿru'l-ināʾ* — the bottom of a vessel. *Qaʿīr* — one whose bottom is deep.

The verb is on the pattern **إِنْفِعَال** (Form VII): *inqaʿara* — **it broke away from its base, it was torn out together with its foundation.** Its active participle is *munqaʿir*.

**And this is exactly what the word says: the tree is not broken, it comes out from its base.**

The difference matters, and it can be seen:

| State | What happens | What is left behind |
|---|---|---|
| **Breaking** | The trunk parts at some point | The root and stump remain in the ground |
| **مُنقَعِر — being torn out from the base** | **The tree comes out of the ground with its foundation** | **A pit is left behind** |

The picture the verse gives, then, is not of a fallen tree but of **a tree taken out of its place.** Its bond with the soil severed, trunk and root together in the air.

**This must be read together with the *khāwiya* of al-Ḥāqqa**, and the two words together give a complete description: al-Qamar says the root has broken away, al-Ḥāqqa says the inside was hollow. **What was torn out from outside was already empty inside.**

I record this joining as my own reading.

**And Form VII conveys the middle sense here too** — for the fourth time in this sūra: *inshaqqa* (1), *munhamir* (11), *munqaʿir* (20), and below, not *muḥtaẓir* but a similar structure. The sūra names everything **that undergoes an outside action** with this form. The moon was split, the water was released, the tree was torn out — none of the three of itself.

I record this as an observation about the pattern, and it can be verified.

### تَنزِعُ — the imperfect tense

The verb is **imperfect**, among the perfect verbs around it: *arsalnā* (perfect) — *tanziʿu* (imperfect).

The same technique was there in verse fourteen: *ḥamalnāhu* (perfect) — *tajrī* (imperfect).

**The same work is done in both places:** while the narrative advances in the past, a single scene is drawn into the present. For a moment the reader steps inside the event.

And note what the two scenes are: one is **rescue** (the ship running), the other **destruction** (the wind tearing out). The sūra brings both to life with the same choice of tense.

I record this as my own reading; the tenses of the two verbs are data of the text.

### ٱلنَّاس — "people", definite

The verse does not say *"them"*, it says *"people"* — and the word is **definite** (*al-nās*).

This is not a generalisation; the context is the people of ʿĀd. But the choice of word is worth recording: instead of the name of the people, **the name of the species** is used. The wind tears out not "ʿĀd" but "people".

**I record this as my own reading:** the choice of word enlarges the asymmetry in the scene. What stand opposite one another are named not a wind and a people but **a wind and man**.

## 54/21-22 — فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ · وَلَقَدْ يَسَّرْنَا ٱلْقُرْءَانَ لِلذِّكْرِ فَهَلْ مِن مُّدَّكِرٍ

*Fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur — wa-laqad yassarnā'l-Qurʾāna li'l-dhikri fa-hal min muddakir*

### Two refrains in succession

Verses twenty-one and twenty-two are one of the places where the two refrains come **one after the other**. This occurs twice in the sūra:

| Place | Order |
|---|---|
| **16-17** | Punishment refrain → Remembrance refrain |
| **21-22** | Punishment refrain → Remembrance refrain |

And in both places the order is the same: **punishment first, remembrance second.**

**I record this as my own reading:** the order could have been built the other way and was not. The text first asks "how was My punishment", then "is there anyone who will remember". That is, **the event is set down first, and then what is to be drawn from it is asked.** The second question rides on the first.

In the accounts of Thamūd and Lot this order is kept but other verses come between (30 → 31 → 32; 39 → 40). The paired sequence is unbroken, that is, only in the first two accounts.

Verse twenty-two is the **second** full occurrence of the refrain. The analysis given under verse seventeen holds here as well; I do not repeat it. The only difference is the material piled up behind it: here there are two accounts.

## III. Thamūd — 54/23-32

The longest account in the sūra: nine verses. And the only one with dialogue — the people's speech is reported (24-25), an answer is given (26), and instructions are given to the messenger (27-28).

In **`091-sems.md`** what the Qurʾān says and does not say about the account of Thamūd was itemised in detail; the historical frame of the people (the Assyrian records, al-Ḥijr, their not being confused with the Nabataean structures) was also given there. **I do not repeat these.** Two verses of this sūra (54/28 and 54/29) were already cited in the section on al-Shams.

## 54/23 — كَذَّبَتْ ثَمُودُ بِٱلنُّذُرِ

*Kadhdhabat Thamūdu bi'l-nudhur*
"Thamūd denied the warnings."

### A change in the pattern

In the two preceding accounts the verb had no object: *kadhdhabat ʿĀdun* — "ʿĀd denied", simply. Here there is an object: *bi'l-nudhur*.

And this object will be repeated in the remaining two accounts as well:

| Verse | People | Verb | Object |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | The people of Noah | *kadhdhabat* | **none** (then *ʿabdanā*) |
| 18 | ʿĀd | *kadhdhabat* | **none** |
| **23** | **Thamūd** | *kadhdhabat* | ***bi'l-nudhur*** |
| **33** | **The people of Lot** | *kadhdhabat* | ***bi'l-nudhur*** |
| 42 | (The house of Pharaoh) | *kadhdhabū* | ***bi-āyātinā kullihā*** |

**There is an intensification in the sequence, and I record it as my own reading.** In the first two accounts the verb has no object — the denying is set down absolutely. In the third and fourth the object is *nudhur*. In the fifth the object is *āyātinā kullihā* — "all Our signs". The scope of the denying, that is, widens as the list advances.

A note on the particle **بِ**: in the Qurʾān the verb *kadhdhaba* takes both a direct object (*kadhdhabū rusulanā*) and a *bi*-object (*kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā*). When it comes with *bi*, the object is mostly **a thing** (a sign, a book, the Hour, the religion); when it comes directly, mostly **a person** (a messenger, a prophet). I draw attention to this distribution but **do not present it as a rule**; it may have exceptions.

If the distribution holds, then in the expression *bi'l-nudhur* the sense "warnings" (a thing) comes forward and the sense "warners" (persons) stays in the background. This is an inclination, not a ruling.

## 54/24-25 — فَقَالُوٓا۟ أَبَشَرًا مِّنَّا وَٰحِدًا نَّتَّبِعُهُۥٓ إِنَّآ إِذًا لَّفِى ضَلَٰلٍ وَسُعُرٍ · أَءُلْقِىَ ٱلذِّكْرُ عَلَيْهِ مِنۢ بَيْنِنَا بَلْ هُوَ كَذَّابٌ أَشِرٌ

*Fa-qālū a-basharan minnā wāḥidan nattabiʿuhu innā idhan la-fī ḍalālin wa suʿur — a-ulqiya'l-dhikru ʿalayhi min bayninā bal huwa kadhdhābun ashir*
"They said: 'A single human being from among us — are we to follow him? Then we should indeed be in error and madness. Has the remembrance been cast upon him alone out of all of us? No, he is a brazen liar.'"

### Three objections, three words

The people's speech is spread over two verses and contains **three distinct objections**. Each rests on a single word:

| Objection | Key word | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **بَشَرًا** — a human being | "He is a human being like us" |
| **2** | **وَاحِدًا** — a single one | "And only one person" |
| **3** | **مِنْ بَيْنِنَا** — from among us | "And why **him**?" |

The three objections stand on three separate grounds: **kind** (that he is human), **number** (that he is one), **choice** (that it is he).

**And all three are objections repeated elsewhere in the Qurʾān:**

- **The objection of kind:** *"This is nothing but a human being like yourselves"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/33-34); *"Should an angel not have been sent down to us?"* (al-Anʿām 6/8).
- **The objection of choice:** *"Has the remembrance been sent down upon him out of all of us?"* (Ṣād 38/8 — **exactly the same objection**); *"Should this Qurʾān not have been sent down to a great man of one of the two cities?"* (al-Zukhruf 43/31).

**The comparison with Ṣād 38/8 is worth recording**, because the wording is almost identical and there the speech belongs to **the Meccans**, here to **Thamūd**. The sūra, that is, makes its addressees hear their own sentence from the mouth of an earlier people.

**I record this as my own reading.** The closeness in wording between the two verses is verifiable data of the text.

### أَبَشَرًا — a point of word order

**بَشَرًا** is in the accusative (*a-basharan*), yet the verb of the sentence comes afterwards: *nattabiʿuhu*.

This is the structure Arabic calls *ishtighāl*: the object is brought forward, remains in the accusative, and attaches to an elided verb (*a-nattabiʿu basharan… nattabiʿuhu*).

**Its function is emphasis.** The first word heard in the sentence becomes *basharan*. The centre of the objection, that is, has been brought to the front: **"A human being!"**

And the order is: *basharan* (a human being) → *minnā* (from among us) → *wāḥidan* (a single one) → *nattabiʿuhu* (are we to follow). **The verb is left to the very end.** All the grounds of the objection are enumerated first, and then what is being asked of them is said.

**بَشَر — Root: ب-ش-ر.** Its concrete sense: **skin, the outer surface of the hide.** *Bashara* — the skin. And *bashar* is the naming of the human being by his **visible, touchable** side. The word *insān*, on the other hand, comes from the root of intimacy/forgetting and looks in a different direction.

The objection, then, shows in the choice of word too: they speak of the messenger by way of his **skin**. Whoever says *bashar* counts the one before him as nothing more than what is visible.

I record this as my own reading; the root sense of the word is a datum of the language.

### ضَلَٰلٍ وَسُعُرٍ — the sharpest return in the sūra

**ضَلَال — Root: ض-ل-ل.** Its concrete sense: **to lose the way, to be lost, to miss where one is going.** *Ḍalla* — he was lost. From the same root *ḍāll* (one who has lost his way) and — strikingly — *ḍalla'l-shayʾu fi'l-shayʾi*: one thing being lost inside another.

**سُعُر — Root: س-ع-ر.** Its concrete sense: **to kindle a fire, to make it blaze, to make it fierce.** *Saʿara'l-nār* — he made the fire blaze up.

And the word yields two distinct senses:

| Sense | How it arises |
|---|---|
| **Madness, derangement** | The "kindling" of the mind — the lexicographers' explanation: a mind heated up and thrown off balance. *Nāqa masʿūra* — a maddened she-camel |
| **Blazing fires** | The plural of *saʿīr*. The Qurʾān uses *saʿīr* of the Fire (al-Mulk 67/5, 67/10-11) |

**In the mouth of Thamūd the first sense is at work:** "If we follow him we shall be in error and **madness**."

**And now look at verse forty-seven:**

> **إِنَّ ٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ فِى ضَلَٰلٍ وَسُعُرٍ**
> *"The guilty are in error and blazing fires."* (54/47)

**The two expressions are identical: ضَلَالٍ وَسُعُرٍ.** The same two words, in the same order, in the same letters.

The difference lies only in whose the sentence is:

| | **54/24** | **54/47** |
|---|---|---|
| Who says it | **Thamūd** | **The text** |
| About whom | Themselves (hypothetically) | **The guilty** |
| Mood of the sentence | *innā idhan la-fī…* — conditional, hypothetical | *inna'l-mujrimīna fī…* — certain, declarative |
| In which sense *suʿur* | **Madness** | **Blazing fires** — or both at once |

**Thamūd had said, "if we follow him we shall become so." The sūra says, "those who do not follow him already are so"** — and it says it with their own sentence.

And the word's two senses become functional here: they had said "madness"; in verse forty-seven the same word can also be read as "blazing fires". **The word stays the same, its sense changes place.**

**This is the clearest example of the "handing the word back to its owner" that the sūra performs three times.** The others:

| # | Their word | Where it comes back |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *mustamirr* (2) — "a magic that goes on" | 19 — ʿĀd's day of misfortune |
| 2 | *kadhdhābun ashir* (25) — "a brazen liar" | 26 — made definite and handed back |
| 3 | *ḍalālin wa suʿur* (24) — "error and madness" | 47 — the state of the guilty |

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording and verse numbers of the three pairs are verifiable data of the text; gathering the three under a single technique is my reading.

### أَءُلْقِىَ ٱلذِّكْرُ عَلَيْهِ — "has the remembrance been cast upon him?"

**أُلْقِيَ — Root: ل-ق-ي.** *Laqiya* — he met, he encountered. *Ilqāʾ* (Form IV) — **to cast, to let fall, to set before someone.**

The verb is **passive**: *ulqiya* — "it was cast". The agent is not named.

**The Qurʾān uses this verb of revelation:**

- *"You did not expect that the Book would be **cast** to you"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/86 — *an yulqā ilayka'l-kitāb*).
- *"He **casts** the spirit of His command upon whom He wills of His servants"* (al-Muʾmin 40/15 — *yulqi'l-rūḥa min amrih*).
- *"We shall **cast** upon you a weighty word"* (al-Muzzammil 73/5) — treated in **`073-muzzemmil.md`**.

The verb, then, is the Qurʾān's own verb for revelation. **In making their objection Thamūd use the right verb** — what they object to is not that revelation came but **to whom it came.**

I record this distinction as my own reading and I count it important: the sentence does not reject the possibility of revelation; it rejects **the choice of recipient.** The expression *min bayninā* — "from among us" — says exactly this.

### كَذَّابٌ أَشِرٌ — two epithets

**كَذَّاب** is, from the root ك-ذ-ب, the **فَعَّال** pattern, and in Arabic this pattern conveys **intensity and a trade or habit**: *najjār* (a carpenter), *khayyāṭ* (a tailor), *kadhdhāb* (one who makes lying his business). It means, then, not "someone who has told a lie" but **"one who has made lying his habit"**.

**أَشِر — Root: أ-ش-ر.** *Ashar* — **the insolence that comes of abundance of favour, self-satisfaction, excess.** The word does not convey a haughtiness rooted in the self; it conveys **an excess that comes of being sated.**

Words close in sense: *baṭaran* (al-Anfāl 8/47 — going astray through ease), *fariḥīn* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/76 — rejoicing insolently), *maraḥ* (Luqmān 31/18 — walking with a swagger).

Thamūd say two things of Ṣāliḥ, then: **a liar** and **insolent.** The second is interesting: to count a prophet "insolent" is to read his claim as **overstepping his place**.

## 54/26 — سَيَعْلَمُونَ غَدًا مَّنِ ٱلْكَذَّابُ ٱلْأَشِرُ

*Sa-yaʿlamūna ghadan mani'l-kadhdhābu'l-ashir*
"Tomorrow they will know who the brazen liar is."

### The word made definite

This verse carries the clearest point of word order in the sūra, and it is built on a single letter.

In verse twenty-five Thamūd had said: **كَذَّابٌ أَشِرٌ** — indefinite, with tanwīn. "A liar, an insolent one."

Verse twenty-six answers: **ٱلْكَذَّابُ ٱلْأَشِرُ** — **definite**, with the article. "**The** liar, **the** insolent one."

| | **Verse 25** | **Verse 26** |
|---|---|---|
| Who says it | Thamūd | The text |
| Form | **نكرة** — indefinite | **معرفة** — definite |
| Its sense | "A liar" | "**The** liar" |
| About whom | Ṣāliḥ | **Unspecified** — "they will know who it is" |

**The only difference between the two verses is the definite article.** And that article turns an epithet from a description into an **identity**.

And the sentence does not say whose it is. It asks with *man* (who): *"they will know who the liar and the insolent one is."* The verse, that is, does not refute the claim; **it leaves who the claim belongs to for the future.**

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording of the two verses can be verified; the function of the article is grammatical data; the conclusion I draw from the two is mine.

### غَدًا — "tomorrow"

**Root: غ-د-و.** *Ghad* — tomorrow. *Ghuduww* — to set out in the morning. *Ghadāt* — the morning hour.

**The word here is not a calendar "tomorrow".** In Arabic *ghad* has a settled use for **the near future**; the English "one of these days" does the same job.

The Qurʾān makes this use elsewhere too: *"No soul knows what it will earn tomorrow"* (Luqmān 31/34) — there too no particular day is meant.

**The word's function here is to shorten the distance.** The sūra names a future of uncertain arrival "tomorrow". And it says this to Thamūd — for Thamūd that "tomorrow" really was near: at Hūd 11/65 it is recorded that a term of three days was given.

I give this detail on the basis of Hūd 11/65; it was also recorded in **`091-sems.md`** among the things the Qurʾān says about Thamūd.

## 54/27-28 — إِنَّا مُرْسِلُوا۟ ٱلنَّاقَةِ فِتْنَةً لَّهُمْ فَٱرْتَقِبْهُمْ وَٱصْطَبِرْ · وَنَبِّئْهُمْ أَنَّ ٱلْمَآءَ قِسْمَةٌۢ بَيْنَهُمْ ۖ كُلُّ شِرْبٍ مُّحْتَضَرٌ

*Innā mursilū'l-nāqati fitnatan lahum fa'rtaqibhum wa'ṣṭabir — wa nabbiʾhum anna'l-māʾa qismatun baynahum; kullu shirbin muḥtaḍar*
"We are sending the she-camel as a trial for them. So watch them and be steadfast. And tell them that the water is divided between them; at each turn of drinking the one whose turn it is shall be present."

### مُرْسِلُوا۟ ٱلنَّاقَةِ — the pattern

**مُرْسِلُو** is an active participle in **annexation**; hence the plural *nūn* has been dropped (its original is *mursilūna*).

And the active participle conveys **the future** here: "we shall send / we are sending". In Arabic the active participle may be past, present or future; the context decides. Here, since the command has not yet been given, it is future.

**And the observation I recorded above is at work here:** the root **ر-س-ل** occurs four times in the sūra, and in none of the four is what is sent a prophet. Here what is sent is **a she-camel.**

### فِتْنَةً — a trial

**Root: ف-ت-ن.** This root was treated in **`085-buruc.md`**, **`068-kalem.md`**, **`074-muddessir.md`**, **`072-cin.md`** and **`051-zariyat.md`**. Its concrete sense deserves attention.

*Fatana'l-dhahaba* — **he put the gold into the fire.** This is the root's proper sense: to cast a metal into the fire and separate the pure from the adulterated. *Fattān* — the goldsmith who melts gold.

The senses that arise from this: **trial** (separating out), **turmoil / sedition** (the state of being cast into fire), **torment** (at al-Burūj 85/10, *fatanū* — they burned).

**And the naming of the she-camel a "trial" is worth dwelling on.**

The camel is a miracle; but the verse does not call it a *miracle*, it calls it a **trial**. The function of the sign, that is, is not to offer an irresistible proof; it is **to separate.**

This is in keeping with the Qurʾān's understanding of a sign and is directly connected with the second verse of the sūra: there a sign was shown and "magic" was said. **A sign is not something that convinces everyone who sees it; it is something that divides those who see it in two.**

I record this as my own reading. The sense of the root and the choice of word in the verse are verifiable data.

### فَٱرْتَقِبْ and وَٱصْطَبِرْ — two commands, two Form VIIIs

**ٱرْتَقِبْ — Root: ر-ق-ب.** Its concrete sense: **the neck** (*raqaba*), and from this **to stretch out one's neck and watch.** *Raqīb* — one who watches, who oversees. *Murāqaba* — surveillance. *Irtiqāb* — to wait and observe.

The picture in the word is thus the same bodily movement as the *muhṭiʿīn* of verse eight: **the stretching of the neck.** There a compelled running, here a deliberate waiting.

**ٱصْطَبِرْ — Root: ص-ب-ر.** This root was treated in **`068-kalem.md`**, **`070-mearic.md`**, **`073-muzzemmil.md`**, **`076-insan.md`**, **`052-tur.md`** and **`074-muddessir.md`**. Its concrete sense: **to hold a thing in a place, to confine it, to bind it.**

**The issue here is the pattern, and it matters.**

The Qurʾān generally gives the command to be patient with **Form I**: **ٱصْبِرْ** (*iṣbir*) — al-Qalam 68/48, al-Muzzammil 73/10, al-Maʿārij 70/5, al-Rūm 30/60.

Here, however, **Form VIII** is used: **ٱصْطَبِرْ** (*iṣṭabir*). Its original is *iṣtabir*; the letter ت, under the influence of the root's ص letter, has turned into **ط**.

The contribution of Form VIII in sense is **effort and acquisition**. *Ṣabara* is to be patient; *iṣṭabara* is **to take patience upon oneself, to make patience one's work.**

**And this pattern occurs in only three places in the Qurʾān:**

| Verse | Expression | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Maryam 19/65 | *fa'ʿbudhu wa'**ṣṭabir** li-ʿibādatih* | Patience in persevering in service |
| Ṭāhā 20/132 | *wa'mur ahlaka bi'l-ṣalāti wa'**ṣṭabir** ʿalayhā* | Patience in prayer and in directing one's family |
| **al-Qamar 54/27** | *fa'rtaqibhum wa'**ṣṭabir*** | **Patience in awaiting the outcome of a trial** |

**What all three have in common is the duration of the patience.** In all three what is asked is not a brief endurance but **a continuous bearing**: perseverance in service, perseverance in prayer, perseverance in waiting.

I record this observation; the wording of the three verses can be verified.

**The order of the two commands is worth recording:** first watch, then be steadfast. Patience here is therefore **not inaction**; it is being steadfast while watching. The man does not withdraw, he goes on looking.

## The turn at the water — on 54/28

This verse is the only one in the sūra that carries a concrete question of law, and it deserves separate treatment.

### نَبِّئْهُمْ — tell them

**Root: ن-ب-أ**, treated at verse four: a *nabaʾ* is a report that has weight.

The verb is in the imperative of **Form II** (*tafʿīl*): *nabbiʾ* — "make it known to them". The task given to Ṣāliḥ, then, is not to lay down the rule but **to convey it**.

### قِسْمَةٌ بَيْنَهُمْ — "is a division between them"

**Root: ق-س-م.** Its concrete sense: **to divide, to separate into parts, to apportion.** Its derivatives: *qism* (a share), *qisma* (both the dividing and the share), *taqsīm*, *qāsim* (one who divides), *muqsim*.

The same root also yields the word *qasam* (an oath) — treated in **`068-kalem.md`** and **`051-zariyat.md`**. The link the lexicographers draw: an oath is what **separates** truth from falsehood. I transmit this explanation; I do not offer it as a settled etymology.

**And there is a point of word order here.**

The verse does not say *"the water has been divided"* (*al-māʾu **maqsūmun***). It says **"the water is a division between them"** (*al-māʾu **qismatun** baynahum*) — that is, it makes the verbal noun the predicate directly.

In Arabic, making a verbal noun the predicate conveys **intensity**: the thing is identified with the act itself. *Zaydun ʿadlun* — "Zayd is justice" (not just, but justice itself).

Its counterpart here is this: **the water is not a thing that has been divided; it is the division itself.** What is said is not what the water is but how it is ordered. Water is no longer a substance; it is an **order**.

**I record this as my own reading.** That a verbal noun made predicate conveys intensity is grammatical data; its application here is my reading.

### كُلُّ شِرْبٍ مُّحْتَضَرٌ — at every turn there is attendance

**شِرْب — Root: ش-ر-ب.** And here there is a distinction between words that requires attention:

| Form | Its sense |
|---|---|
| **شُرْب** (*shurb*, with *ḍamma*) | **The act of drinking** |
| **شِرْب** (*shirb*, with *kasra*) | **A share of drinking / a turn at drinking / one's turn** |
| **شَرَاب** (*sharāb*) | **The thing drunk** |

The form in the verse is **شِرْب**: not the act but the **right.** Not "every drinking", that is, but **"every turn"**.

The Qurʾān uses the same word at al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/155, and there it gives the arrangement explicitly:

> *"This is a she-camel; **she has a turn at drinking**, and you have a turn at drinking on a known day."* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/155)

In **`091-sems.md`** this verse was already recorded among the things the Qurʾān says about Thamūd.

**مُحْتَضَر — Root: ح-ض-ر.** Its concrete sense: **to be present, to be at a place.** *Ḥaḍara* — he was present. *Ḥāḍir* — one who is present. *Ḥaḍāra* — settled life (the opposite of nomadism: *badāwa*). *Iḥtiḍār* — being present.

This root was treated in **`081-tekvir.md`**.

The word is the **passive participle of Form VIII**: *muḥtaḍar* — **that at which one is present.** Every turn, that is, is an occasion at which the one whose turn it is **stands present.**

**What the sentence says is how the order works:** the water is there; whoever's turn it is comes and takes it that day; the other does not come.

### The sharing of a water right — a concrete question of desert law

This verse is the most concrete and most worldly sentence in the sūra and is worth dwelling on, because what is related is not a miraculous prohibition; it is **an arrangement for managing a resource.**

**The historical frame.** On the Arabian Peninsula, and in arid regions generally, water has throughout history been the most tightly regulated resource. In oases and around wells the use of water is run **on a system of turns**: each household, each herd, each field has access to the water for a determined span of time. In Arabic such arrangements are generally called *nawba* (a turn) and *qismat al-māʾ* (the division of the water); similar arrangements are also documented in the irrigation systems of Iran, North Africa and South Arabia.

**This is a piece of social history and I transmit it as such.** I do not claim that the verse intends one of these systems; I say that the arrangement the verse describes **would not have been strange to the one addressed.**

**And the character of the arrangement matters.** The verse does not say that the water belongs to the she-camel; it says that it is **divided.** What is laid down, then, is not a prohibition but a **turn.** No one is left without water; each takes on his own day.

**What the offence is therefore changes.** What Thamūd did was not merely to kill an animal; it was **to abolish the division.** When the camel is killed, the water becomes one side's again. The hamstringing, that is, is an act of abolishing an order.

**I record this as my own reading.** The words of the verse (*qismatun*, *shirb*, *muḥtaḍar*) and the description at al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/155 are verifiable data of the text; defining the offence in this way is my reading.

### What this says to the present

What this verse says to the present lies not in the word "camel" but in **the word "division".**

When more than one party must have access to a shared resource, what has to be regulated before the resource itself is **the turn.** The verse first states that a division exists (*qismatun baynahum*), then says that everyone will be present at his own turn (*kullu shirbin muḥtaḍar*). Two sentences, two distinct pieces of work: **the recognition of a right** and **the keeping of a turn.**

And the violation the sūra relates begins not with a denial of the right but with **the breaking of the turn.** The camel is killed because it has a turn.

This yields a general observation about how shared resources — water, pasture, common ground, common time — are ruined: **ruin usually comes not through the denial of a right but through the abolition of the turn.** No one says "this has no right"; they simply do away with the turn.

That last sentence is my own reading, and I record it as an observation, not a ruling.

## 54/29 — فَنَادَوْا۟ صَاحِبَهُمْ فَتَعَاطَىٰ فَعَقَرَ

*Fa-nādaw ṣāḥibahum fa-taʿāṭā fa-ʿaqar*
"They called their companion; he reached out and took, and hamstrung her."

### Three *fāʾ*s, three verbs

The verse consists of four words and three of them are verbs. All three are joined with **فَ**.

The connective **فَ** conveys **immediate sequence** in Arabic: coming one after the other without a gap. *Wāw* only joins; *thumma* puts an interval between; *fāʾ* means **immediately after.**

The verse therefore gives the three acts as **a single sequence of movement**:

```
فَنَادَوْا    →  فَتَعَاطَىٰ  →  فَعَقَرَ
they called     he reached out    he hamstrung
```

No pause between them, no thinking, no hesitation. **This is the fastest verse in the sūra.**

And this speed must be read together with the length of the *ṣarṣar* wind in verse nineteen (seven nights and eight days) and the instantaneity of the *ṣayḥa* in verse thirty-one. The sūra gives different durations for different events; here the duration is **close to zero.**

I record this as my own reading; the function of the connective *fāʾ* is grammatical data.

### فَنَادَوْا — plural

The verb is **plural**: *nādaw* — "they called". The calling, then, belongs to the group.

**Root: ن-د-و / ن-د-ي.** *Nidāʾ* — to call out, to summon. *Nādī* — a meeting place, an assembly (at al-ʿAlaq 96/17, *nādiyah* — treated in **`096-alak.md`**).

And note: the root here is not **د-ع-و**. In verse six there was *yadʿu'l-dāʿī*, in verse ten *fa-daʿā Rabbahu*; both were from the root of *daʿwa*. Here the root *nidāʾ* is used.

The difference the lexicographers record: *nidāʾ* emphasises **calling with the voice** (shouting from a distance); *duʿāʾ* looks to **the content of the call**. I transmit this distinction; I do not claim that it works absolutely everywhere.

### صَاحِبَهُمْ — "their companion"

**Root: ص-ح-ب.** *Ṣaḥiba* — to be together with, to keep company. *Ṣāḥib* — a companion, one who is present with another, the owner of a thing.

**The choice of word is worth recording.** The verse names the man who hamstrung the camel neither by his name, nor by his offence, nor by an epithet. It says **"their companion"** — that is, it defines him **by his relation to the group.**

In the classical sources this man's name is transmitted, and as was recorded in **`091-sems.md`** that name **does not occur in the Qurʾān.** The note made in the section on al-Shams holds here too; I do not repeat the name.

**And the sūra's way of distributing the guilt shows here:**

| Verb | Who does it | Number |
|---|---|---|
| *nādaw* — they called | **The group** | Plural |
| *taʿāṭā* — he reached out and took | **One man** | Singular |
| *ʿaqar* — he hamstrung | **One man** | Singular |

**The plural verb comes before the singular verbs.** The act begins with the group's call and ends by one man's hand.

In **`091-sems.md`** this singular/plural difference was already recorded: al-Qamar uses the singular (*ʿaqar*), al-Shams the plural (*fa-ʿaqarūhā*, 91/14), and al-Aʿrāf and Hūd also use the plural (7/77; 11/65). In the section on al-Shams this question was moreover treated under the heading *"a singular agent, a collective crime"*.

**The only thing I add here, and it is my own reading:** in al-Qamar both the singular and the plural occur in the same verse. Al-Shams names the singular agent in a separate verse (*inbaʿatha ashqāhā*, 91/12); al-Qamar joins the two in a single sentence. And the manner of the joining makes the call **a part** of the hamstringing: *fa-nādaw… fa-ʿaqar.* The same chain of *fāʾ*.

### فَتَعَاطَىٰ — "he reached out and took"

**Root: ع-ط-و.** Its concrete sense: **to stretch out one's hand and take, or give.** *Aʿṭā* (Form IV) — he gave. *ʿAṭāʾ* — a gift, the thing given.

This root was treated in **`092-leyl.md`**, **`093-duha.md`**, **`108-kevser.md`** and **`078-nebe.md`** — and there it always had the sense of **giving**: *fa-ammā man aʿṭā* (al-Layl 92/5), *innā aʿṭaynāka'l-kawthar* (al-Kawthar 108/1).

Here **Form VI** (*tafāʿul*) is used: *taʿāṭā*.

Form VI generally conveys **reciprocity** (*taqātalū* — they fought one another). But it also has the sense of **giving oneself to a task, setting about it.** *Taʿāṭā* is of the second kind: **he stretched out his hand to a thing, he took the work upon himself.**

**And the most striking side of the verse is here: the object of the verb is not named.**

*Taʿāṭā* — took what? The verse does not say. A sword, a knife, some other implement — none of it is written.

In the classical commentaries the object is widely supplied as "a sword". **The Qurʾān does not say this.**

**I record this as my own reading:** the ellipsis here is a choice of narration. The text does not name the implement; it names only **the movement of the hand.** And the next verb already gives the result. The verse is concerned not with the implement but with **the hand.**

The word's kinship with the sense of "giving" is also worth recording: the same root names a gift in the Qurʾān; here it names a hamstringing. **What they have in common is the stretching out of the hand; what it is stretched out for is not written in the root.**

That last sentence is my own reading; the two senses of the root are data of the language.

### فَعَقَرَ — he hamstrung

**Root: ع-ق-ر.** Its concrete sense deserves attention, because the English "to slaughter" does not give the word.

*ʿAqara'l-baʿīra* — **he struck the camel in the legs, cut its leg and brought it down.** *ʿAqr*, that is, is first of all **the destruction of the power to move**; slaughtering at the throat (*dhabḥ*) is a separate word.

The root's derivatives show where the sense opens out:

- **عَاقِر** — a woman or man who bears no child. In the Qurʾān: *"My wife is barren"* (Maryam 19/5, 19/8), *"I am a barren old woman"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/29). **The lexicographers' explanation: one whose line has been cut.**
- **عُقْر الدار** — the middle of a house, its core, its main body.
- **عَقِير** — an animal struck in the legs.

**The common core of the root: to cut off the continuance of a thing.** The camel's walking is cut off, the woman's line is cut off, and the centre of a house is the place where continuance comes to a stop.

This root was treated in **`091-sems.md`**. The only thing I add here is that this link between *ʿāqir* and *ʿāqir* **is functional in this sūra, and I record it as my own reading:** what Thamūd cut was not an animal but **the continuance of an order.** Verse twenty-eight had set up the order (*qismatun baynahum*); the verb of verse twenty-nine cuts off that order's continuing. The root names exactly this.

## 54/30 — فَكَيْفَ كَانَ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ

*Fa-kayfa kāna ʿadhābī wa nudhur*

### The question that comes before the answer

This is the most striking placement I recorded in the table of refrains above, and I take it up separately here.

The order is this:

```
29  فنادوا صاحبهم فتعاطى فعقر          ← THE OFFENCE
30  فكيف كان عذابي ونذر                ← THE QUESTION
31  إنا أرسلنا عليهم صيحة واحدة ...     ← THE ANSWER
32  ولقد يسرنا القرآن للذكر ...         ← THE REMEMBRANCE REFRAIN
```

In the other three accounts the refrain comes **after** the description of the punishment. Here it comes **in between**.

**The result is this:** the reader hears the question "how was the punishment" before he learns what the punishment was. The question opens a gap; verse thirty-one fills it.

**I record this as my own reading:** this placement increases the force of verse thirty-one. An answer that has been asked for is heard more loudly than one that has not. And the account of Thamūd is the longest account of the sūra; the punishment of the longest account is given as the answer to a question that was asked.

This is a statement about a choice; the verse numbers can be verified.

---

## 54/31 — إِنَّآ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً فَكَانُوا۟ كَهَشِيمِ ٱلْمُحْتَظِرِ

*Innā arsalnā ʿalayhim ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan fa-kānū ka-hashīmi'l-muḥtaẓir*
"We sent against them a single cry, and they became like the dry brushwood of the maker of a fold."

### صَيْحَةً وَٰحِدَةً — a single cry

**صَيْحَة — Root: ص-ي-ح.** *Ṣāḥa* — he shouted, he cried out. *Ṣayḥa* — a loud sound made once. The pattern is **مَرَّة** (*faʿla*), and in Arabic this pattern conveys **a single occurrence**: *ḍarba* (one blow), *naẓra* (one look), *ṣayḥa* (one cry).

The word already carries the sense "once", then. **The adjective وَاحِدَة reinforces this further.**

The Qurʾān uses different words for the destruction of Thamūd in different sūras, and in **`091-sems.md`** the itemisation of this was given: *ṣayḥa* (Hūd 11/67), *rājifa* / *ṣāʿiqa* (al-Aʿrāf 7/78; Fuṣṣilat 41/17), *damdama* (al-Shams 91/14), *ṭāghiya* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/5 — treated in **`069-hakka.md`**). I do not repeat that table.

Al-Qamar's contribution is the word **وَاحِدَة**.

**And this word will occur once more in the sūra** — in verse fifty, in an entirely different context:

| Verse | Expression | What it qualifies |
|---|---|---|
| **31** | *ṣayḥatan **wāḥidatan*** | **The destruction of Thamūd** — a single cry |
| **50** | *wa mā amrunā illā **wāḥidatun*** | **God's command** — a single word |

**I record this as my own reading:** verse fifty is the general rule of verse thirty-one. The destruction of Thamūd came by a single sound; verse fifty says that this is not an exception but the **rule**: the command is always one. And in verse fifty-one it will be said *"We have destroyed your likes"* and the refrain will be repeated.

In **`069-hakka.md`** a similar weave was recorded under the heading *"the repetition of* wāḥida *carrying the difference of speed between the two sections of the sūra"*; there *nafkhatun wāḥida* and *dakkatan wāḥida* did the same job. **The same word does the same job in two sūras:** it reduces the duration of the event to zero.

### كَهَشِيمِ ٱلْمُحْتَظِرِ — the dry brushwood of the maker of a fold

The most original comparison in the sūra, and it is taken from a scene of shepherding.

**هَشِيم — Root: ه-ش-م.** Its concrete sense: **to break something dry and brittle into small pieces, to crumble it.** *Hashama* — he broke, he crushed.

**هَشِيم** is on the *faʿīl* pattern with the sense of a passive participle: **broken, crumbled, dried up and scattered plant matter.**

The Qurʾān uses the word once more, and there it gives the process as well:

> *"Set forth to them the likeness of the life of this world: it is like water We send down from the sky, and the plants of the earth mingle with it; then it becomes **dry stubble that the winds scatter** (*hashīman tadhrūhu'l-riyāḥ*)."* (al-Kahf 18/45)

*Hashīm*, then, is a plant that **was once alive and has dried and crumbled.** There is a past inside the word: this thing was once green.

**A note on the language.** It is widely transmitted that the name **Hāshim**, borne by one of the Prophet's forefathers, comes from this root and is given the sense *"one who crumbles bread for broth"*. I transmit this as a piece of lexical information; it has no bearing on the sense of the verse, and I make none.

**ٱلْمُحْتَظِر — Root: ح-ظ-ر.** And this word is the real key.

*Ḥaẓara* — **to enclose a thing, to draw a fence around it, to close it off.** And from this:

- **حَظِيرَة** (*ḥaẓīra*) — **a fold.** A shepherd weaves a fence of dry thorn and brushwood to gather his animals at night; the area this fence encloses is called a *ḥaẓīra*.
- **مَحْظُور** (*maḥẓūr*) — **forbidden, fenced off.** In the Qurʾān: *"The gift of your Lord is not **withheld**"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/20 — *mā kāna ʿaṭāʾu Rabbika **maḥẓūrā***).

**The same root, then, gives both "to make a fold" and "to forbid".** The link is plain: to forbid is to fence something around.

**ٱلْمُحْتَظِر** is the active participle of Form VIII: **one who makes a fold, who weaves a fence.**

The exact rendering of the expression is therefore: **"the dry brushwood of the one who makes a fold."**

**The picture is this.** The shepherd weaves a fence of thorn and brushwood to protect his animals. In time the brushwood dries, breaks, and is crumbled underfoot by the animals. What collects at the base of the fence is **the dust of broken dry thorn** — no use for fuel, no use for fencing.

**There is a variant reading.** The word has also been read as **ٱلْمُحْتَظَر** (*muḥtaẓar*, with *fatḥa*); on that reading it is a passive participle / noun of place and means **"the fold itself"**: "the dry brushwood of the fold". **Since I cannot state with certainty which reading belongs to which imām, I write no names.** Between the two readings **the picture does not change.**

### The comparison taken from the life of herding

This comparison is the clearest of the sūra's comparisons chosen to suit its addressee, and it works on the same logic as the locust comparison of verse seven.

Let us set the four great comparisons of the sūra side by side:

| Verse | Comparison | Where it is taken from | What it gives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **7** | *jarādun muntashir* — locusts spread abroad | **Agriculture / a desert scourge** | Number, density, covering the ground |
| **20** | *aʿjāzu nakhlin munqaʿir* — trunks of uprooted palms | **Palm cultivation** | Permanence coming out by the roots |
| **31** | *hashīmu'l-muḥtaẓir* — the dry brushwood of the fold | **Herding** | Becoming worthless, crumbling |
| **48** | *yusḥabūna fi'l-nāri ʿalā wujūhihim* | (not a comparison but a description) | — |

**All three comparisons are taken from the addressee's own way of making a living:** the crop, the palm grove, the fold. What the Qurʾān does here is to speak in a community's **everyday images.**

And there is something common to all three: **each of them is the dead state of a thing.** A swarm of locusts finishes the crop; an uprooted palm ceases to be a tree; dried brushwood ceases to be a fence.

**I record this as my own reading.**

### A people who built, turned to the dust of a fence

One last note, and it is my own reading.

As was recorded in **`091-sems.md`**, Thamūd are named in the Qurʾān as the people who **carved houses out of the mountains** (al-Aʿrāf 7/74; al-Ḥijr 15/82; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/149), and at al-Fajr 89/9 they occur as *"those who hollowed the rocks in the valley"*.

A people who made houses out of rock is likened to **a shepherd's dry fence.**

This contrast is the same as the one drawn for ʿĀd in the section on al-Ḥāqqa: there too the comparison was to the dead part of the palm, the tree of permanence, and it was recorded that what was raised up in al-Fajr and what was brought down in al-Ḥāqqa were set opposite one another.

**Al-Qamar does the same thing a third time** — and this time the material chosen is the thing furthest from stone that there could be: dry thorn.

I record this as a reading; the wording of the verses can be verified, the contrast is a reading I have set up.

## IV. Lot — 54/33-40

## 54/33-34 — كَذَّبَتْ قَوْمُ لُوطٍۭ بِٱلنُّذُرِ · إِنَّآ أَرْسَلْنَا عَلَيْهِمْ حَاصِبًا إِلَّآ ءَالَ لُوطٍ ۖ نَّجَّيْنَٰهُم بِسَحَرٍ

*Kadhdhabat qawmu Lūṭin bi'l-nudhur — innā arsalnā ʿalayhim ḥāṣiban illā āla Lūṭin; najjaynāhum bi-saḥar*
"The people of Lot too denied the warnings. We sent against them a stone-hurling wind — except the family of Lot; them We saved at daybreak."

### A break in the sequence

This account differs from the other three at one point: **the description of the punishment comes before the description of the offence.**

Verse thirty-four gives the punishment (*ḥāṣib*), verse thirty-seven gives the offence (*rāwadūhu ʿan ḍayfih*). The order, that is, has been built **in reverse**.

In the other three accounts the order was always the same: denial → (offence) → punishment. Here: denial → **punishment** → rescue → warning → **offence** → punishment again.

**I record this as my own reading:** this break appears to have to do with the intervention of the sentence about the rescue (34-35). To relate those who were saved, the sūra must first state the destruction; a rescue can only be related as an exception within a destruction. This is already visible in the grammar of verse thirty-four: the particle *illā* (except).

### حَاصِب — hurling stones

**Root: ح-ص-ب.** Its concrete sense: **gravel, small stones.** *Ḥaṣbāʾ* — pebbles.

This word was treated in **`067-mulk.md`** and this verse was cited there: *"We sent against them a stone-hurling wind (al-Qamar 54/34) — about the people of Lot."* The section on al-Mulk also gave in tabular form the contrast between a threat coming from the sky (*ḥāṣib*) and one coming from the earth (*yakhsifa*). I do not repeat these.

Two things are to be added here.

**One — the form of the word.** *Ḥāṣib* is an **active participle**: "hurling gravel". The word names not the wind but **the work the wind does.** Rendered into English as "a rain of stones", the agent's movement is lost.

**Two — the root's second use.** The same root occurs once more in the Qurʾān, in a completely different place:

> *"You and what you worship besides God are the **fuel** of Hell (*ḥaṣabu jahannam*)."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/98)

There *ḥaṣab* is what is **thrown** into the fire. The root names both the thrown stone and the thrown fuel. The common core: **being thrown.**

I record this as a datum of the language.

**The Qurʾān relates the destruction of the people of Lot with other words in other sūras** and I do not repeat them here; in **`069-hakka.md`** *al-muʾtafikāt* (the overturned cities) was treated in detail and it was recorded there that the Qurʾān does not give the names of the cities. That note holds here too.

### إِلَّآ ءَالَ لُوطٍ — the family of Lot

**آل — its root**, according to the majority of the lexicographers, is **أ-و-ل** (*awl* — to return, to arrive); according to some it is a transformation of the word *ahl*. I issue no definite ruling.

The sense of the word: **someone's circle, his household, those attached to him.** It is not mere blood kinship; it conveys **attachment.**

**And this word occurs twice in the sūra — and the two occurrences look at one another:**

| Verse | Expression | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **34** | ***āla Lūṭin*** — the family of Lot | **They were saved** |
| **41** | ***āla Firʿawna*** — the people of Pharaoh | **They were seized** |

**The same word at the two ends of what befell two peoples.** One names those attached to a prophet, the other those attached to a tyrant.

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording of the two verses is verifiable data of the text; setting the two opposite one another is my reading.

And the word's sense of "attachment" is functional here: elsewhere the Qurʾān says that Lot's wife was not saved (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/32-33; al-Taḥrīm 66/10 — treated in **`066-tahrim.md`**). *Āl* here, then, is not determined by blood. **It is possible to be in the same household and stay outside the list.**

### نَّجَّيْنَٰهُم — We saved

**Root: ن-ج-و.** Its concrete sense: **to bring a thing up to a high place, to set it apart.** *Najwa* — high ground the floodwaters do not reach. And from this **to be saved**: to **rise above and away** from danger.

From the same root *najwā* (a secret conversation — treated in **`058-mucadele.md`**): the **setting apart** of a conversation from the group.

The verb is on the pattern of **Form II** (*tafʿīl*): *najjā* — he saved. Here that form conveys both transitivity and **multiplicity** (number, thoroughness).

### بِسَحَرٍ — at daybreak

**Root: س-ح-ر**, treated at verse two, where it was recorded that the two words (سِحْر / سَحَر) come from the same root.

**سَحَر** — the **last** part of the night, the time just before dawn. The darkest and quietest hour of the night.

The word was treated in **`051-zariyat.md`**, and al-Dhāriyāt 51/18 was cited there: *wa bi'l-asḥāri hum yastaghfirūn* — "and in the hours before dawn they seek forgiveness."

**A grammatical detail.** The word here is **indefinite** and carries tanwīn: *bi-**saḥarin***. There is a nicety the lexicographers record: when the *saḥar* of **a particular day** is meant, the word *saḥar* becomes diptote and takes no tanwīn (*saḥarā*); when an unspecified daybreak is meant, it takes tanwīn. **I transmit this nicety as ascribed to the lexicographers**; I do not claim that it works absolutely everywhere.

If it holds, then what the verse says is "at **a** daybreak"; no particular date is spoken of.

### The meaning of the hour — an hour between rescue and destruction

And now verse thirty-four must be read together with verse thirty-eight, because both **give an hour:**

| Verse | Time | To whom |
|---|---|---|
| **34** | ***bi-saḥarin*** — at daybreak, before dawn | **Lot's family is saved** |
| **38** | ***bukratan*** — in the morning, as the sun comes up | **The people are destroyed** |

**Between the two events there is about an hour.**

The rescue takes place a short time before the destruction — and the two times are neighbours. *Saḥar* is the last slice of the night, *bukra* the first slice of the day. Between them is sunrise.

**I record this as my own reading.** The senses of the two words are lexical data; the wording of the two verses is data of the text; the temporal relation between them is my inference.

And this is something the sūra does elsewhere too: **it gives durations.** The *ṣarṣar* lasted seven nights and eight days (the information al-Ḥāqqa gives); the *ṣayḥa* was a single instant (31); the flood came from two waters (11-12); the people of Lot were taken at a morning hour (38). **The sūra gives events with their hours** — and in verse fifty it will say where this comes from: *ka-lamḥin bi'l-baṣar.*

## 54/35 — نِّعْمَةً مِّنْ عِندِنَا ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِى مَن شَكَرَ

*Niʿmatan min ʿindinā; kadhālika najzī man shakar*
"As a favour from Us. Thus do We recompense the one who gives thanks."

### The rescue named a "favour"

This verse carries the most striking choice of word in the sūra, and to see it one must recall the sūra as a whole.

**Al-Qamar is a sūra of punishment.** The greater part of its fifty-five verses is denial, warning, penalty and reckoning. The word "favour" occurs **once** in the sūra — and for a rescue.

**نِعْمَة — Root: ن-ع-م.** Its concrete sense: **softness, ease.** *Naʿima* — it became soft and easy. *Nāʿim* — soft, pleasant. *Niʿma* — a good thing given. *Naʿīm* — abundance, well-being. From the same root *naʿam* (yes) and *anʿām* (livestock — property given).

**And the word's case-analysis is worth recording:** *niʿmatan* is in the **accusative** and is an **object of purpose** (an accusative of cause). That is, "We saved them — **as / because of** a favour from Us."

The rescue could thus have been named in two different languages, and the text chose one:

| Possible language | What it says | How the rescue is understood |
|---|---|---|
| **The language of justice** | "We saved them because they deserved it" | The paying of a **debt** |
| **The language of favour** *(the one chosen)* | "As a favour from Us" | A **gift** |

**And the difference is preserved in the continuation of the sentence too.** The verse says *"thus do We recompense"*, but the name of what is being recompensed is not **obedience** or **faith**: it is **شُكْر.**

### كَذَٰلِكَ نَجْزِى مَن شَكَرَ — three "thus do We recompense"

This pattern occurs in several places in the Qurʾān, and in **`055-rahman.md`** two of them were compared. I now complete the table by adding a third:

| Verse | Sentence | Who or what is recompensed |
|---|---|---|
| al-Mursalāt 77/44 | *innā kadhālika najzi'l-**muḥsinīn*** | **The doers of good** — persons |
| al-Raḥmān 55/60 | *hal jazāʾu'l-**iḥsāni** illa'l-iḥsān* | **Doing good** — the act |
| **al-Qamar 54/35** | *kadhālika najzī **man shakar*** | **The one who gives thanks** — a person defined by his act |

In **`055-rahman.md`** the difference between the first two was recorded: *"al-Mursalāt names the agent, al-Raḥmān the act."* Al-Qamar takes a third path: **it defines the person by his act** — "whoever gives thanks".

And the relative pronoun **مَنْ** is indefinite here: not a name but a quality. This accords with the principle recorded in `USLUP.md`: *"what the verse describes are qualities; whoever bears that quality is included in it."*

### Why "thanks"?

And here the most direct link with al-Raḥmān is made.

**شُكْر — Root: ش-ك-ر.** The explanation the lexicographers transmit about its concrete sense: *shakara'l-dābbatu* — the animal **grew fat** on little fodder, its condition became visible. *Shukr*, that is, is **the becoming visible of what has been received.** From the same root *shakūr* (much given to thanks) and *mashkūr* (that for which a return is made).

And the exact opposite of thanks is **كُفْر**: to cover. **Thanks makes visible, ingratitude covers over.**

**Now look at the refrain of al-Raḥmān:**

> *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi Rabbikumā **tukadhdhibān*** — "Which of your Lord's **favours do you deny**?"

Al-Raḥmān's whole question is about **the denial of a favour.** And in **`055-rahman.md`** it was recorded that *ālāʾ* means "favour" and that the favour is "something asked to be remembered, something that can be forgotten": *"The name of a favour carries within it the possibility of being forgotten."*

**Al-Qamar 54/35 is the exact other side.** Here there is a favour (*niʿmatan min ʿindinā*) and the attitude that is recompensed is **thanks** — that is, making the favour visible.

| | **al-Raḥmān 55** | **al-Qamar 54/35** |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a favour | Yes, counted thirty-one times | Yes, once, and for a single event |
| The addressee's attitude | **تَكْذِيب** — denial | **شُكْر** — thanks |
| What the root does | Covering / rejecting | **Making visible** |
| Outcome | The question is repeated, no answer | **A recompense is given** |

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording and word-roots of the two sūras are verifiable data; setting up the contrast is my reading.

**And one further conclusion follows, which I also record as my own reading:** in describing the rescue al-Qamar uses **the language not of law but of relation.** In the language of punishment there are "seizing" (*akhdh*), "punishment" (*ʿadhāb*), "We sent" (*arsalnā*). In the language of rescue there are "favour" and "thanks".

**The sūra works, that is, with two separate vocabularies:** a vocabulary of act and force for destruction, a vocabulary of gift and return for rescue. And the second vocabulary lasts only one verse in the sūra — until verses fifty-four and fifty-five. There the same vocabulary will come back: *jannāt*, *maqʿadi ṣidqin*, *ʿinda malīkin muqtadir.*

## 54/36 — وَلَقَدْ أَنذَرَهُم بَطْشَتَنَا فَتَمَارَوْا۟ بِٱلنُّذُرِ

*Wa-laqad andharahum baṭshatanā fa-tamāraw bi'l-nudhur*
"He had indeed warned them of Our seizing; but they turned the warnings into a dispute."

### بَطْشَة — a seizing

**Root: ب-ط-ش.** This root was treated in detail in **`085-buruc.md`** (*inna baṭsha Rabbika la-shadīd*, 85/12), and the description recorded there holds here too:

> *Baṭsh*: **to grip a thing violently and harshly, to fall upon it and seize it.** The word describes the movement of the hand — clawing, snatching, taking hold and not letting go.

In the section on al-Burūj three elements of the word were enumerated and the first was **suddenness**: not a slow encirclement but a lunge. I do not repeat these.

**What is to be added here is the pattern.** The word comes here in the form **بَطْشَة**, that is, on the *faʿla* pattern: **a single occurrence.** The same pattern as *ṣayḥa* in verse thirty-one.

| Verse | Word | Pattern | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| **31** | *ṣayḥa* | *faʿla* | **A single** cry |
| **36** | *baṭsha* | *faʿla* | **A single** seizing |
| 85/12 | *baṭsh* | verbal noun | Seizing (in general) |

**Al-Qamar, that is, names the destruction of both Thamūd and the people of Lot with the "single occurrence" pattern.** In al-Burūj the same root came as a verbal noun, that is, in a form conveying continuity.

I record this as an observation about the pattern; the wording of the three verses can be verified.

The Qurʾān uses the same pattern once more: *"The day We shall seize with the greatest seizing (*al-baṭshata'l-kubrā*)"* (al-Dukhān 44/16).

### أَنذَرَهُمْ — "he warned"

The subject of the verb is singular: *andhara-hum* — "**he** warned them". Who? Lot.

**And this is the only verb in the sūra of which a messenger is the agent.**

The messengers' acts in the sūra are these: Noah **prayed** (10), Ṣāliḥ **was commanded to watch and to be steadfast and to give notice** (27-28), Lot **warned** (36). In all three accounts, then, what the messenger does is to convey or to pray — in none of them is there any use of force, any intervention, any producing of an outcome.

I record this as an observation; the verbs of the verses can be verified.

And the verb is on the pattern **إِفْعَال** (Form IV): *andhara*. The sense of the root **ن-ذ-ر** in this form is **"to make known by way of a frightening report"**. The root was treated in **`051-zariyat.md`**, **`071-nuh.md`**, **`074-muddessir.md`** and **`077-murselat.md`**; I do not repeat it.

**The root's turn within the sūra:** immediately after the verb *andharahum* (he warned) comes *bi'l-nudhur* (the warnings). The same root twice in the same sentence: **the act done and the name of the act done.**

### فَتَمَارَوْا۟ — they turned it into a dispute

**Root: م-ر-ي.** And a caution is needed here: **this root is NOT the root م-ر-ر of verse two.**

The two roots are close in sound, distinct in sense:

| Root | Derivative | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **م-ر-ر** | *mustamirr* (2, 19), *amarr* (46) | To pass by / to be bitter |
| **م-ر-ي** | *tamāraw* (36), *mirya* | **To doubt, to dispute** |

**I record this distinction explicitly**, because the sūra's use of both invites confusion. A note of the same kind was made in the section on al-Ḥāqqa between *wāhiya* and *wahn*, and in the section on al-Qāriʿa between other pairs. **I draw no meaning from the closeness in sound between the two roots.**

**The concrete sense of the root م-ر-ي:** *marā'l-ḍarra* — to **pull and squeeze** the udder in order to milk it. And from this, figuratively: to **pull a matter back and forth**, to drag it this way and that, to wrangle over it.

Its derivatives:
- **مِرْيَة / مُرْيَة** — doubt. In the Qurʾān: *"Be not in **doubt** concerning it"* (Hūd 11/17; al-Sajda 32/23 — *fa-lā taku fī **miryatin** minh*).
- **إِمْتِرَاء** — to fall into doubt, to dispute. Maryam 19/34: *"…the word of truth concerning which they **fall into dispute**."*
- **تَمَارِي** — Form VI: **to dispute with one another, to wrangle together.**

**The form in the verse is Form VI**, and Form VI's conveying of reciprocity is fully functional here: *tamāraw* — **they disputed with one another.**

**And this is the subtlest reaction the sūra describes, and it is worth dwelling on.**

The reactions of the deniers are enumerated in the sūra:

| Verse | Reaction | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | *yuʿriḍū* | **They turn away** — they do not look |
| 2 | *"siḥrun mustamirr"* | **They give it a name** — they supply an explanation |
| 9 | *"majnūn"* | **They qualify the person** |
| 24-25 | *"basharan… kadhdhābun ashir"* | **They object** — they enumerate grounds |
| **36** | ***tamāraw*** | **They dispute** — they open the matter to debate |

**The fifth differs from the others, and I record this as my own reading.** Turning away, naming, objecting — all are open expressions of a stance. *Tamārī*, however, is not an open rejection; it is **making the matter debatable.**

And note what it is that is disputed in the verse: *fa-tamāraw **bi'l-nudhur***. What they dispute is **the warnings.** A warning is turned into a subject of debate.

Form VI's reciprocity completes this: the dispute is conducted **among themselves.** Not with the warner, but with one another.

**What this says to the present.** The stance this verse describes is a less visible form of open rejection: to neutralise a warning, without rejecting it, **by turning it into a topic.** So long as the discussion goes on, no decision has to be made.

That last sentence is my own reading, and it is not a ruling but an observation drawn from the movement the word carries.

## 54/37 — وَلَقَدْ رَٰوَدُوهُ عَن ضَيْفِهِۦ فَطَمَسْنَآ أَعْيُنَهُمْ فَذُوقُوا۟ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ

*Wa-laqad rāwadūhu ʿan ḍayfihi fa-ṭamasnā aʿyunahum fa-dhūqū ʿadhābī wa nudhur*
"They had indeed pressed him for his guests. So We wiped out their eyes. 'Taste My punishment and My warnings!'"

### رَاوَدُوهُ عَنْ — the pattern and the preposition

**Root: ر-و-د.** Its concrete sense: **to go back and forth in search of a thing, to range about.** *Rād* — the scout who searches for pasture. *Rāʾid* — one sent out on behalf of a people to look for a place. *Irāda* — to want; to turn towards something.

This root was treated in **`085-buruc.md`**, **`086-tarik.md`** and **`075-kiyame.md`**.

The verb is on the pattern of **Form III** (*mufāʿala*): *rāwada*. This form conveys **persistence and mutual pressure**: not asking once but **asking insistently, again and again.**

**And the key to the pattern is the preposition: عَنْ.**

*Rāwada fulānan **ʿan** shayʾin* is a settled construction in Arabic, and its exact sense is: **to try to make someone give up something, to press him in order to prise that thing from him.**

The Qurʾān uses this construction once more, and there its sense is beyond dispute:

> *"The woman in whose house he was **pressed him against his will** (*rāwadathu'llatī huwa fī baytihā **ʿan** nafsih*)."* (Yūsuf 12/23)

The use in Sūrat Yūsuf and the use here are **the same construction**, and the difference lies in the word that comes after the preposition: there *ʿan nafsihi* (from himself), here *ʿan ḍayfihi* (from his guest).

### ضَيْف — guest

**Root: ض-ي-ف.** *Ḍāfa* — he became a guest, he lodged. *Aḍāfa* — he received as a guest; and in grammar *iḍāfa* (making one noun **a guest of** another, annexation) is from the same root.

**The word is a generic noun:** it is used in the same form for the singular and the plural. The Qurʾān does this at al-Dhāriyāt 51/24 as well: *ḍayfi Ibrāhīma'l-mukramīn* — "Abraham's honoured guests" (a singular word, a plural adjective). That verse was treated in **`051-zariyat.md`**.

**And the choice of the word is decisive, and I record this as my own reading.**

In describing the offence the verse could have done three things: it could have named the act, characterised the perpetrators, or named the victim. **The path it chose is the third, and the name it chose is "guest".**

The offence, that is, is framed in the language of the sūra as **the violation of guesthood.** And this frame is one of the gravest violations in the addressee's own law: in Arab custom the protection of a guest is the host's personal obligation, and that obligation is binding in tribal law. To touch the guest is to touch the host.

**This is a piece of social history and I transmit it as such.** That hospitality (*ḍiyāfa*) was an institutional obligation in Arab society is widely documented in pre-Islamic poetry and in the later sources.

**And the word order of the verse supports this:** the direct object of the verb is Lot (*rāwadū-**hu***), while the guest comes with a preposition (*ʿan ḍayfihi*). In the grammar, that is, **the one pressed is Lot.** The offence is directed at **the host** before it reaches the guest.

**I record that the Qurʾān names the act of the people of Lot more explicitly in other sūras** (al-Aʿrāf 7/80-81; al-Naml 27/54-55; al-ʿAnkabūt 29/28-29), and I do not go into the detail of those verses here, because what al-Qamar does is different: the sūra names the offence by way of **the right of a guest.**

### فَطَمَسْنَآ أَعْيُنَهُمْ — We wiped out their eyes

**Root: ط-م-س.** This root was treated in full in **`077-murselat.md`** and this verse was cited there. I take the finding there as it stands:

> *"The idea beneath the root: to destroy the trace, the shape, the distinguishing lines of a thing. To erase a writing, to blur a road, to flatten a face. … This is the root's most decisive feature: not to destroy but to render indistinguishable."*

I likewise do not repeat the three Qurʾānic examples given in the section on al-Mursalāt: Yūnus 10/88 (*iṭmis ʿalā amwālihim* — the property is made useless), Yā Sīn 36/66 (*la-ṭamasnā ʿalā aʿyunihim* — the function of sight is removed), and this verse.

**Two things I add here.**

**One — the matching of offence and penalty.**

The offence begins with a look and the penalty takes the eye as its target.

| | The offence | The penalty |
|---|---|---|
| Organ | **The eye** — which sees the guest, which desires | **The eye** — which is wiped out |
| Act | *rāwadū* — to press insistently | *ṭamasnā* — to erase the trace |

In **`069-hakka.md`** a *"table of offence-and-return matching"* was set up in this commentary (al-Ḥāqqa 69/33-34 ↔ 35-36). The same method is at work here.

**And the kind of act is worth recording:** *ṭams* is not destruction but **the ending of a function.** The eye remains; **it cannot distinguish what it sees.** The penalty does not take the organ; it takes the organ's work.

**Two — the difference from Yā Sīn 36/66.**

In Yā Sīn the verb comes with **لَوْ** (a conditional): *"Had We willed, We would have wiped out their eyes"* — that is, a possibility that did not occur is spoken of. In al-Qamar the verb is **perfect**: *ṭamasnā* — it occurred.

The same verb, then, occurs in one place in the Qurʾān as a warning and in another as an event.

I record this as an observation about usage; the wording of the two verses can be verified.

### فَذُوقُوا۟ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ — the change of mood

And here the sūra's second refrain **changes mood.**

| Verse | Form | Mood | Addressee |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16, 18, 21, 30 | *fa-**kayfa kāna** ʿadhābī wa nudhur* | **Question**, perfect | About a third party |
| **37, 39** | *fa-**dhūqū** ʿadhābī wa nudhur* | **Command** | **Second person — direct** |

**ذُوقُوا — Root: ذ-و-ق.** *Dhāqa* — he tasted. The word is the sense of the tongue and conveys **the nearest, most direct contact.**

The Qurʾān often gives punishment with this verb: *"Taste the punishment"* (al-Anfāl 8/50; al-Sajda 32/14; al-Qamar 54/48).

**And who is commanded?** The people of Lot have long since been destroyed; no command can be given to them. Two solutions are transmitted:

| Solution | To whom it is said |
|---|---|
| **To them, at that moment** | The sentence is counted as said to them at the moment of the event; an address inside the narrative |
| **To the present addressee** | The sentence is an address directed at those hearing the sūra; what befell an earlier people is shown to the hearer in the imperative |

**I do not impose a preference**; both are recorded.

But something can be said about its function within the sūra, and **I record this as my own reading:** the change of mood comes exactly **just before the turning point of the sūra.** In verse forty-three the text will turn to the addressee and ask *"are your disbelievers better than those?"* The imperative in verses thirty-seven and thirty-nine is the first sound of that turn.

The refrain, that is, prepares **by mood** for the last section of the sūra.

## 54/38-39 — وَلَقَدْ صَبَّحَهُم بُكْرَةً عَذَابٌ مُّسْتَقِرٌّ · فَذُوقُوا۟ عَذَابِى وَنُذُرِ

*Wa-laqad ṣabbaḥahum bukratan ʿadhābun mustaqirr — fa-dhūqū ʿadhābī wa nudhur*
"And in the early morning a settled punishment came upon them. 'Taste My punishment and My warnings!'"

### صَبَّحَهُمْ — "it came upon them in the morning"

**Root: ص-ب-ح.** *Ṣubḥ* — morning. *Ṣabāḥ* — the morning hour. *Miṣbāḥ* — a lamp (what turns darkness into morning). *Ṣabūḥ* — a morning drink.

The verb is on the pattern of **Form II** (*tafʿīl*), and in Arabic this pattern, when built from nouns of time, gives the sense **"to come at that time / to do something at that time"**: *ṣabbaḥa* — he came in the morning; *massā* — he came in the evening.

The literal rendering of the sentence, then, is: **"the punishment morninged them."** English has no such structure; it is rendered "came in the morning", and the time inside the verb is lost.

In **`100-adiyat.md`** this verse was cited (*"And a settled punishment came upon them in the morning"*).

**بُكْرَةً — Root: ب-ك-ر.** *Bukra* — the first hour of the day, early morning. *Bākir* — one who is early. *Bikr* — first-born, untouched (the Turkish *bakir*, "virgin, untouched", comes from this).

Coming together with the verb *ṣabbaḥa*, the word **narrows** the time: not morning, but the first moment of morning.

**And the observation about the hour that I recorded above is completed here:** in verse thirty-four the rescue is *bi-saḥarin* (before dawn), in verse thirty-eight the destruction *bukratan* (as the sun comes up). Between them there is a space of time.

### عَذَابٌ مُّسْتَقِرّ — the sūra's first circle closes

**مُسْتَقِرّ** — treated at verse three: from the root ق-ر-ر, **coming to rest, becoming fixed.**

And this word links directly with the sentence of verse three:

| Verse | Expression | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **3** | *wa kullu amrin **mustaqirr*** | **The rule:** every matter comes to rest |
| **38** | *ʿadhābun **mustaqirr*** | **The instance:** a punishment that has come to rest |

**The rule set down in the third verse of the sūra is realised in an event in the thirty-eighth.** The distance between them is thirty-five verses.

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording of the two verses and the word's occurring in the same form are verifiable data of the text; the rule-and-instance relation is my reading.

**Two readings of the word's sense here are transmitted:**

| Reading | What *mustaqirr* means |
|---|---|
| **Lasting** | The punishment is not temporary; it is continuous, it reaches into the hereafter |
| **Come to its place** | The punishment has settled exactly into place; it has found its target |

**I do not impose a preference.** The sense of the root will bear both, and the use in the third verse stands closer to the second reading.

Verse thirty-nine repeats the command of verse thirty-seven **in exactly the same words.** This is the only place in the sūra where the punishment refrain occurs twice within one account (in ʿĀd it also occurred twice, but one at the opening and one at the close).

## 54/40 — وَلَقَدْ يَسَّرْنَا ٱلْقُرْءَانَ لِلذِّكْرِ فَهَلْ مِن مُّدَّكِرٍ

The **fourth and last** full occurrence of the refrain. Behind it are four accounts: Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, Lot.

And this is the sūra's last *wa-laqad yassarnā*. After it the fifth account will come, but it will not close with the refrain.

## V. The house of Pharaoh — 54/41-42

## 54/41-42 — وَلَقَدْ جَآءَ ءَالَ فِرْعَوْنَ ٱلنُّذُرُ · كَذَّبُوا۟ بِـَٔايَٰتِنَا كُلِّهَا فَأَخَذْنَٰهُمْ أَخْذَ عَزِيزٍ مُّقْتَدِرٍ

*Wa-laqad jāʾa āla Firʿawna'l-nudhur — kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā kullihā fa-akhadhnāhum akhdha ʿazīzin muqtadir*
"And the warnings came to the house of Pharaoh too. They denied all Our signs; so We seized them with the seizing of One mighty, All-Powerful."

### Why is the best known told the most briefly?

This is the question most often asked about the structure of the sūra, and it **can be answered by an observation about word order.** Below, without conjecture, I set out five observations from the text's own data.

**One — this account falls outside the pattern.**

The four accounts opened with the same verb: *kadhdhabat*. The fifth opens with a different verb: **جَاءَ.**

| Account | Opening verb | Subject | What it says |
|---|---|---|---|
| Noah | *kadhdhabat* | **The people** | What they did |
| ʿĀd | *kadhdhabat* | **The people** | What they did |
| Thamūd | *kadhdhabat* | **The people** | What they did |
| Lot | *kadhdhabat* | **The people** | What they did |
| **Pharaoh** | ***jāʾa*** | ***al-nudhur*** | **What came to them** |

**In the fifth account the subject is not the people but the warnings.** The agent of the sentence changes: in four accounts peoples were doing something; here something comes to a people.

**Two — this account has no refrain.**

All four closed with *wa-laqad yassarnā*. The fifth does not. Verse forty-three turns straight to the addressee: *a-kuffārukum khayrun min ulāʾikum?*

**Three — this account does not name the instrument of destruction.**

In the four accounts the instrument of destruction was given by name:

| Account | Instrument | Verse |
|---|---|---|
| Noah | **Water** — from the sky and from the earth | 11-12 |
| ʿĀd | **Wind** — *rīḥan ṣarṣaran* | 19 |
| Thamūd | **A cry** — *ṣayḥatan wāḥidatan* | 31 |
| Lot | **A stone-hurling wind** — *ḥāṣiban* | 34 |
| **Pharaoh** | **None** | — |

In place of the instrument, verse forty-two names **the one who seizes**: *akhdha **ʿazīzin muqtadir***. The drowning is not mentioned; the sea is not mentioned; the staff is not mentioned. In the fifth account the sūra brings forward **not the instrument but the agent.**

**Four — in this account the object of the denying is widened.**

Thamūd and Lot had denied *bi'l-nudhur* (the warnings). Here the object is **بِآيَاتِنَا كُلِّهَا** — "**all** Our signs".

The word **كُلِّهَا** occurs once in the sūra, here, and it is worth recording: elsewhere the Qurʾān counts the signs given to Moses as **nine** (al-Isrāʾ 17/101; al-Naml 27/12). The longest list of signs, that is, belongs to this people — and the sūra gathers it into **a single word.**

**Brevity is balanced by scope.** An account of two verses takes in the widest list by saying "all of them".

**Five — this account closes the list, it does not narrate.**

Four accounts narrate something: an offence, an instrument, an outcome. The fifth **does not narrate**; it **enumerates.** It has two sentences: a warning came, they denied. And then the seizing.

**The conclusion that follows, and I record it as my own reading:**

**The account of Pharaoh is not an account here but an item on a list.** Having told four accounts the sūra closes the list, and in closing it uses the best-known example — because the best-known example is the one that **needs telling least.**

The Qurʾān relates the account of Pharaoh at length in al-Aʿrāf, Yūnus, Ṭāhā, al-Shuʿarāʾ, al-Qaṣaṣ and many other sūras. Al-Qamar's addressee knows this account. **What is known need not be told; naming it is enough.**

And what the sūra is doing is in any case not telling but **listing**. As I recorded above under the heading "What is absent from the sūra", this sūra uses not the content of the accounts but their coming one after another. The fifth item shows that the list is complete.

**This is an observation about word order and it is not conjecture**; its ground is the five points above, and all of them can be verified from the text. I draw from it no claim about the order of revelation or the arrangement of the text.

### أَخْذَ عَزِيزٍ مُّقْتَدِرٍ — an absolute object

**أَخْذَ** is the verbal noun of the verb *akhadhnāhum* and is in the accusative as an **absolute object**. But it is not an ordinary absolute object; it is in **annexation**: *akhdha ʿazīzin muqtadir* — "with the seizing of One mighty and All-Powerful".

In Arabic this structure does the work of **qualification**: it says **how** the act was done, by reference to the identity of the agent.

In **`069-hakka.md`** a structure of the same kind was treated: *akhadhahum akhdhatan rābiya* (69/10) — there the verbal noun was qualified by an adjective, here by an annexation.

| Verse | Structure | How it is qualified |
|---|---|---|
| al-Ḥāqqa 69/10 | *akhdhatan **rābiya*** | **By an adjective** — a mounting seizure |
| **al-Qamar 54/42** | *akhdha **ʿazīzin muqtadir*** | **By an annexation** — by the identity of the one seizing |

**عَزِيز — Root: ع-ز-ز.** Its concrete sense: **to be hard, firm, unwearing.** *Arḍun ʿazāz* — hard ground that cannot be dug. And from this: **to be unconquerable, to be superior, to be precious.**

The word carries two things at once: **power** and **worth.** For a thing to be *ʿazīz* is both that it cannot be reached and that it is precious. From the same root *ʿizza*, *iʿzāz*, *muʿāzza*.

**مُقْتَدِر — Root: ق-د-ر**, the active participle of Form VIII: **one whose power suffices to its measure, possessed of full power.**

And this word is **the same as** the last word of the sūra (55). I take up that circle below.

**That the two attributes come together is worth recording, and I record it as my own reading:** *ʿazīz* conveys irresistibility, *muqtadir* conveys **measure.** The seizing, that is, is both unstoppable and **measured.** Without both together the sentence would be incomplete: with *ʿazīz* alone, invincibility would be understood; with *muqtadir* alone, sufficiency. The two together describe **a power that does not miss its measure.**

And this is precisely what will be said in verse forty-nine: *innā kulla shayʾin khalaqnāhu bi-qadar.*

## The turn to the addressee — 54/43-48

## 54/43 — أَكُفَّارُكُمْ خَيْرٌ مِّنْ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكُمْ أَمْ لَكُم بَرَآءَةٌ فِى ٱلزُّبُرِ

*A-kuffārukum khayrun min ulāʾikum am lakum barāʾatun fi'l-zubur*
"Are your disbelievers better than those? Or do you have an acquittal in the scriptures?"

### The turning point of the sūra

Verse forty-three opens the second half of the sūra. Having spoken about the third person for forty-two verses, the text turns here to **the second person** and does not turn back.

A count of the pronouns shows this: from 43 to 53 the suffix **-kum** occurs five times (*kuffārukum*, *ulāʾikum*, *lakum*, and in 51 *ashyāʿakum*). Before verse forty-three the pronoun of address occurs in only two places in the sūra (the command to the Prophet in 6, and *dhūqū* in 37 and 39).

### أَكُفَّارُكُمْ — "your disbelievers"

**The construction is unusual and worth dwelling on.**

*Kuffār* is the plural of *kāfir*. And with the suffix **-kum** it is ascribed to a community: "your disbelievers."

To whom is it said? If the addressees are themselves the deniers, "your disbelievers" falls strangely. Two solutions are transmitted:

| Solution | Who is addressed | Who the *kuffārukum* are |
|---|---|---|
| **Addressed to the Arabs** | The people of Mecca generally | Those among them who deny |
| **Addressed to the deniers** | The deniers themselves | Themselves; *-kum* meaning "those of your kind" |

**I do not impose a preference.** Both arrive at the same result: a comparison is being made.

**And the subject of the comparison is power, not morality.** The question is not "are you better" but, in context, "are you stronger" — because what is compared are those destroyed in the four accounts. The logic behind the sentence is this: if they were destroyed, what is there in you that would save you?

The word **خَيْر** is a comparative here, and in Arabic the comparatives of *khayr* / *sharr* are used without the hamza (not *akhyar* but *khayr*).

### أَمْ لَكُم بَرَآءَةٌ — "or do you have an acquittal?"

**أَمْ** in Arabic is of two kinds: the connected (a continuation of the first question, "or is it that…") and the disjunctive (opening a new question, "rather…"). Here it is commonly read as connected: two possibilities are being offered.

**And what the two possibilities are matters:**

1. **Actual superiority** — "are you better?"
2. **A written exemption** — "or do you have a document?"

The sūra, that is, enumerates two kinds of guarantee and turns both into a question: **being strong in fact** or **being exempt in law.** No third possibility is offered.

**I record this as my own reading;** its ground is that the particle *am* sets out two possibilities, and that is grammatical data.

**بَرَاءَة — Root: ب-ر-أ.** This root was treated in **`082-infitar.md`**, **`059-hasr.md`**, **`057-hadid.md`** and **`060-mumtehine.md`**. Its concrete sense: **to separate from a thing, to be freed of it, to move away from it.**

And the root has two great branches:

- **بَرِئَ مِنْ** — to keep away from a thing, to sever connection with it. *Barāʾa* — a document of severance. *Barīʾ* — innocent, having no connection.
- **بَرَأَ** — to create, to bring forth from nothing. *al-Bāriʾ* — the Creator. The lexicographers' explanation: to create is to **separate** a thing out of non-being.

The sense in the verse is the first: **acquittal, exemption, a document of release.**

**And the picture the word carries is documentary.** In Arabic, and in old legal language generally, a *barāʾa* is **a written guarantee** given to someone, or a **certificate of discharge**. The Turkish *beraat etmek*, "to be acquitted", comes from this same word.

The question, then, is: **do you hold a written exemption?**

And from here one passes straight to the next word.

### ٱلزُّبُر — what is written

**Root: ز-ب-ر.** And this root is the key root of the last section of the sūra; it will come back in verse fifty-two.

Its concrete sense: **to write by carving into stone or a hard surface; to record firmly and lastingly.**

Its derivatives show the field of the root:

- **زَبَرَ** — he wrote; and especially he wrote **by incising, by carving.**
- **زُبْرَة** — a block of iron, a hard piece. In the Qurʾān: *"Bring me **blocks** of iron"* (al-Kahf 18/96 — *zubara'l-ḥadīd*).
- **زَبَرَ ٱلْبِئْرَ** — he lined a well with stone, he made it firm.
- **زَبُور** — a written text; and specifically the book given to David (al-Nisāʾ 4/163; al-Isrāʾ 17/55; al-Anbiyāʾ 21/105).
- **زُبُر** — the plural of *zabūr* or of *zibr*: **written pages, books.**

**The common core of the root: hardness and permanence.** The block of iron, the stone-lined well and the incised writing all come from the same place. **This root, that is, ties the verb "to write" not to paper but to stone.**

And this connects directly with the verse's question: *"do you have an acquittal in the scriptures?"* — what is asked after is **a record that will not be erased.**

**The difference between ز-ب-ر and س-ط-ر** must be dwelt on separately, and I shall do so at verse fifty-three; both convey "writing", but from different places.

**The word occurs twice in the sūra, and the two occurrences answer one another:**

| Verse | Expression | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **43** | *am lakum barāʾatun **fi'l-zubur*** | **Question:** do you have an exemption in the records? |
| **52** | *wa kullu shayʾin faʿalūhu **fi'l-zubur*** | **Answer:** everything you have done is in the records |

**The question is asked in verse forty-three, the answer given in verse fifty-two. Nine verses lie between them.**

And the form of the answer is worth recording: the question asks "is there an exemption"; the answer does not say "there is no exemption". It says **what is in the records**: everything that has been done. There is a record, then — but what is inside it is not an acquittal but an **account.**

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording of the two verses and the repetition of the same word in the same form are verifiable data of the text; the question-and-answer relation is my reading.

## 54/44-45 — أَمْ يَقُولُونَ نَحْنُ جَمِيعٌ مُّنتَصِرٌ · سَيُهْزَمُ ٱلْجَمْعُ وَيُوَلُّونَ ٱلدُّبُرَ

*Am yaqūlūna naḥnu jamīʿun muntaṣir — sa-yuhzamu'l-jamʿu wa yuwallūna'l-dubur*
"Or do they say, 'We are a host that cannot be beaten'? That host shall be routed, and they shall turn their backs in flight."

### Two verses, one lesson in grammar

These two verses carry the finest point of word order in the sūra, and the point lies **in the shift from singular to plural.**

Let us look first at the claim:

> **نَحْنُ جَمِيعٌ مُّنتَصِرٌ**

- **نَحْنُ** — "we", a plural pronoun.
- **جَمِيعٌ** — a **singular** noun. A host, a whole gathered together.
- **مُّنتَصِرٌ** — a **singular** adjective.

The sentence is built, that is, as: **a plural subject + a singular predicate + a singular adjective.**

This is not incorrect in Arabic; *jamīʿ* is a collective noun and takes a singular adjective. But **what it does in point of sense is this: it makes the multitude speak as a single being.** "We are not separate persons; we are **one** whole, and that whole cannot be beaten."

**Now look at the answer:**

> **سَيُهْزَمُ ٱلْجَمْعُ وَيُوَلُّونَ ٱلدُّبُرَ**

- **سَيُهْزَمُ** — a **singular** verb, passive.
- **ٱلْجَمْعُ** — **singular**, and now **definite** (with the article).
- **وَيُوَلُّونَ** — a **PLURAL** verb.
- **ٱلدُّبُرَ** — a **singular** object.

**And here is the point.**

The first half of the sentence meets the claim in its own language: **singular.** "That host shall be routed." The claim had said "we are one whole"; the answer says "that whole shall be beaten."

In the second half of the sentence **the verb turns plural**: *yuwallūna* — "they turn their backs."

| | **The claim (44)** | **The answer — first half (45)** | **The answer — second half (45)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number | **Singular** — *jamīʿun muntaṣir* | **Singular** — *yuhzamu'l-jamʿ* | **Plural** — *yuwallūna* |
| What it relates | The claim of unity | The defeat of the unity | **The scattering of the unity** |

**The host, that is, is beaten as a singular and flees as a plural.**

**I record this as my own reading.** The numbers of the verbs and nouns are verifiable grammatical data of the text; the reading "the scattering of the unity" that I draw from them is mine.

And this reading fits what the sūra has been concerned with from the start: **the crowd.** In verse seven people were likened to a swarm of locusts; in verse fifty-one *ashyāʿ* (likes, partisans) will be said. Throughout the sūra the collective is a theme.

### مُنتَصِر — and the prayer of Noah

I complete here the table given under verse ten.

The root **ن-ص-ر** occurs twice in the sūra:

| Verse | Form | Who | Sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **10** | ***ٱنتَصِرْ*** — imperative | **Noah**, to his Lord | *"I am overcome (**maghlūb**); give help"* |
| **44** | ***مُنتَصِر*** — active participle | **They**, about themselves | *"We are a host that cannot be beaten (**muntaṣir**)"* |

**The same root, the same form (VIII), two opposite positions.**

And the outcomes too are reversed: Noah's prayer is answered and the flood comes (11-14); their claim is refuted in verse forty-five.

**I record this as my own reading, and let me put it into a sentence:** using the same root, the sūra sets two stances opposite one another — **the one who asks for help and the one who says he has no need of it.** And the pattern of the verb is the same in both; the only thing that changes is who the agent is. In Noah's sentence the agent is God (*intaṣir* — You do it); in theirs the agent is themselves (*muntaṣir* — we are the ones).

### سَيُهْزَمُ — passive and future

**Root: ه-ز-م.** What the lexicographers transmit about its concrete sense: *hazama'l-shayʾa* — he **broke** a thing, cracked it, caved it in. *Hazīm* — cracked, giving off a sound. And from this **to rout**: to break the order of an army.

The word, that is, conveys not killing or defeating but **the breaking of an order.** And this fits exactly with the next verb (to scatter and flee).

**The verb is passive:** *yuhzamu* — "shall be routed". The agent is not named. And with **سَـ** it is future.

The difference the lexicographers record between **سَـ** and **سَوْفَ**: *sa-* conveys the near future, *sawfa* the distant. I transmit this distinction; I do not claim that it works absolutely everywhere.

### ٱلدُّبُر — the back

**Root: د-ب-ر.** Its concrete sense: **the back of a thing, its end.** *Dubr* — the back. *Dābir* — the last, the root. *Tadbīr* — to think of the **end** of a matter. *Mudabbir* — one who orders the end.

This root was treated in **`079-naziat.md`** and this verse was cited there.

**The word comes in the singular** (*al-dubur*), though the subject is plural. This is common with generic nouns in Arabic and is also connected with the harmony of the verse-ending — I recorded it in the table of verse-endings above. At al-Anfāl 8/15-16 the same expression is used in the **plural**: *lā tuwallūhumu'l-**adbār***.

### A note on the historical background

In the classical commentaries it is widely transmitted that this verse was realised at the battle of **Badr**; since the sūra is Meccan, this would be a report given in advance.

**I do not go into the detail of that transmission, and I make this note:** I cannot speak with certainty about the text and the chain of these reports; I therefore do not relay any text ascribing it to a transmitter or a source. I record that the transmitted view exists; I do not make it a support.

**And this should be stated:** the reading of the verse does not depend on that report. The sentence is an answer to a claim, and the refutation of the claim stands within the text independently of what event it was realised in. Some exegetes have in any case read the verse without tying it to a particular event, as a general ruling.

As I recorded above under the heading "Meccan or Medinan", some sources also transmit that this verse is Medinan or was revealed at Medina. **I record this disagreement and I do not impose a preference.**

## 54/46 — بَلِ ٱلسَّاعَةُ مَوْعِدُهُمْ وَٱلسَّاعَةُ أَدْهَىٰ وَأَمَرُّ

*Bali'l-sāʿatu mawʿiduhum wa'l-sāʿatu adhā wa amarr*
"No — the Hour is their appointed time. And the Hour is a far greater calamity and far more bitter."

### The sūra's first circle closes

This verse joins directly with the **first verse** of the sūra.

| Verse | Expression |
|---|---|
| **1** | *iqtarabati'**l-sāʿatu*** — the Hour has drawn near |
| **46** | *bali'**l-sāʿatu** mawʿiduhum wa'**l-sāʿatu** adhā wa amarr* — the Hour is the appointed time |

The word **ٱلسَّاعَة** occurs **three times** in the sūra: once at the opening, twice here. And between them stand forty-five of its fifty-five verses.

**I record this as my own reading:** the sūra draws a circle. At the opening "the Hour has drawn near" is said and the splitting of the moon is named; then for forty-four verses destructions that took place **in the past** are enumerated; at verse forty-six the text turns back and says *"the real appointed time is the Hour."*

**The four accounts, that is, are set between two "Hours".**

And the meaning of this is: the destructions enumerated are not the final event; **they are examples of the final event.** Verse forty-six says this outright: the Hour is **greater** and **more bitter.**

### بَلْ — "no, rather"

**بَلْ** is the particle of retraction in Arabic: it cancels the preceding sentence and puts a new one in its place.

What does it cancel? In the preceding verse a rout was announced: *sa-yuhzamu'l-jamʿ.* Verse forty-six says *"no"* — but not to deny the rout; **to make it small.**

The order, that is, is:

1. "We are a host that cannot be beaten" (44)
2. "That host shall be routed" (45)
3. **"No — the real appointed time is the Hour"** (46)

**The sentence is not a correction but a change of scale.** And I record this as my own reading.

### مَوْعِد — the appointed time

**Root: و-ع-د.** *Waʿd* — a promise, the giving of one's word. *Mawʿid* is on the pattern of a **noun of place or time**: **the promised time or place, the point of meeting.**

From the same root *mīʿād* (an appointed term), *īʿād* (a threat — the promise of something bad).

The word's function here: the Hour is named not as a threat but as an **appointment.** A time determined, agreed, known to both parties.

### أَدْهَىٰ — a greater calamity

**Root: د-ه-ي.** And this root occurs **only here** in the Qurʾān.

What the lexicographers record about its concrete sense is interesting, because the root opens in two directions at once:

- **دَاهِيَة** (*dāhiya*) — **an unforeseen great disaster**, a calamity that catches a person unprepared.
- **دَهَاء** (*dahāʾ*) — **cunning, sharpness of mind, mastery in taking measures.** The Turkish word *dâhi*, "a genius", comes from this.

**The link between the two senses is this (and I transmit it as ascribed to the lexicographers):** a *dāhiya* is a disaster that outruns the mind — what you cannot calculate the coming of, what you cannot guard against. And the *dāhī* stands in the same place from the other side: the person who calculates what others cannot.

**At the centre of the root, that is, lies "outrunning calculation".** On one side a disaster that outruns calculation, on the other an intelligence that does.

In the **comparative**: *adhā* — "a greater calamity."

**And the word's function here is worth recording, and I record it as my own reading.** The sūra has related the destruction of four peoples; each of them was something that could not be calculated. Verse forty-six says: **even those remain calculable.** The Hour is the thing that most outruns calculation.

### أَمَرُّ — more bitter

**Root: م-ر-ر**, treated at verse two. And I complete here the table I recorded there:

| Verse | Word | Who / what |
|---|---|---|
| **2** | *siḥrun **mustamirr*** | What the deniers said of a sign |
| **19** | *yawmi naḥsin **mustamirr*** | ʿĀd's day of destruction |
| **46** | *wa'l-sāʿatu adhā wa **amarr*** | **The Hour** |

The word is from the root **مُرّ** (bitter) and is a comparative: *amarr* — "more bitter."

**I issue no definite ruling on whether the senses "to pass by" and "to be bitter" come from the same root**; I recorded this at verse two as well.

**But the observation about word order can be recorded:** at verse two the deniers used, of a sign, a word that can mean "it will pass / it goes on". At verse forty-six the same three letters return, of the Hour, in the sense of "bitter".

**That the two adjectives come together is also worth recording.** *Adhā* conveys **magnitude**, *amarr* conveys **taste**. One looks to the scale of the event, the other to how it is undergone. By setting these two side by side the Qurʾān describes it both from outside and from within.

## 54/47-48 — إِنَّ ٱلْمُجْرِمِينَ فِى ضَلَٰلٍ وَسُعُرٍ · يَوْمَ يُسْحَبُونَ فِى ٱلنَّارِ عَلَىٰ وُجُوهِهِمْ ذُوقُوا۟ مَسَّ سَقَرَ

*Inna'l-mujrimīna fī ḍalālin wa suʿur — yawma yusḥabūna fi'l-nāri ʿalā wujūhihim dhūqū massa saqar*
"The guilty are in error and blazing fires. On the day they are dragged on their faces into the Fire: 'Taste the touch of Saqar!'"

### ضَلَٰلٍ وَسُعُرٍ — the word returning to its owner

I treated this matter in detail under verse twenty-four and do not repeat it. I record here only this: **verse forty-seven repeats the two words of verse twenty-four exactly.**

| | **Verse 24** | **Verse 47** |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence | *innā **idhan** la-fī ḍalālin wa suʿur* | *inna'l-mujrimīna **fī** ḍalālin wa suʿur* |
| Mood | **Conditional, hypothetical** — *idhan* (in that case) | **Certain, declarative** |
| About whom | Thamūd, about themselves | **The guilty** |
| *Suʿur* | Madness | **Blazing fires** (or both at once) |

The word **إِذًا** (in that case) of Thamūd's sentence falls away; when the condition falls away, the judgement remains.

### ٱلْمُجْرِمِين — the guilty

**Root: ج-ر-م.** Its concrete sense: **to cut, to pluck off.** *Jarama'l-nakhla* — he harvested the palm, plucked the fruit. *Jirm* — the body of a thing, its mass.

And from this **jurm**: a crime. The lexicographers' explanation: a crime is the act that **cuts** a person off from what he belongs to.

**The word's position in the sūra is worth recording.** The sūra named four peoples one by one: the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, the house of Pharaoh. At verse forty-seven the names end and a single **quality** remains: *al-mujrimūn.*

**The principle recorded in `USLUP.md` here appears in the text's own working:** *"No blanket judgement is issued about an ethnic or religious group; what the verse describes are qualities, and whoever bears that quality is included in it."* The sūra itself makes the same transition: from the names of peoples to a quality.

I record this as an observation.

### يُسْحَبُونَ — they are dragged

**Root: س-ح-ب.** Its concrete sense: **to drag by pulling, to draw along the ground.** *Saḥaba* — he dragged.

And from the same root **سَحَاب** — **a cloud.** The lexicographers' explanation: a cloud is what is **dragged** across the sky; the wind pulls it along.

This root was treated in **`052-tur.md`**.

**The picture the word gives is decisive:** *saḥb* is not making something walk but **pulling** it. What is dragged has no motion of its own; it is carried.

And this sets up a contrast with the *muhṭiʿīn* of verse eight:

| Verse | Movement | Whose movement |
|---|---|---|
| **8** | *muhṭiʿīn* — running with necks outstretched | **They** are moving (however compelled) |
| **48** | *yusḥabūna* — being dragged | **They are being moved** |

The sūra shows a descent between the two scenes: first a compelled running, then a dragging.

I record this as my own reading.

### عَلَىٰ وُجُوهِهِمْ — on their faces

The Qurʾān uses this expression in several places: *"On the Day of Resurrection We shall gather them on their faces"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/97), *"Those who will be gathered on their faces to Hell…"* (al-Furqān 25/34).

**The face is the organ by which a person is recognised** — the bearer of identity. And that the dragging is upon the face brings this organ to the ground.

And this makes a link with verse thirty-seven: there the eyes of Lot's people were wiped out; here the faces are scraped along the ground. **The sūra places both punishments in the region of the face.**

I record this as an observation; the wording of the two verses can be verified.

### مَسَّ سَقَرَ — the touch of Saqar

**مَسّ — Root: م-س-س.** Its concrete sense: **to touch** — and especially **to make light contact with the hand.**

**And the word's use here is a technique of style.**

The word that could be used for the **lightest** contact of fire has been chosen. It does not say "taste the burning of the fire" or "taste the fire"; it says **"taste the touch of the fire."**

In rhetoric this counts as **understatement in the wording**, or a kind of elliptical expression: to say little and convey much. The English "just a taste of it" does a similar job.

The Qurʾān uses the same technique elsewhere: *"If a **breath** (*nafḥa*) of your Lord's punishment were to touch them…"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/46). There too the smallest measure is chosen.

**I record this as my own reading**; that *mass* means "light contact" is lexical data.

**سَقَر — Root: س-ق-ر.** This word was treated in full in **`074-muddessir.md`** and this verse was cited there. The notes there:

> *"Its concrete sense: the scorching of the sun, its burning and altering. *Saqarat-hu'l-shams* — the sun scorched him, his skin changed. In the Qurʾān the word is used almost as a proper name and occurs in four places: al-Muddaththir 74/26, 74/27, 74/42 and al-Qamar 54/48. And a grammatical point: the word is diptote (it takes no tanwīn) — which shows that it is treated as a proper name."*

I do not repeat these.

**The only thing I add here is the meeting of the two words, and it is my own reading:** *mass* is Arabic's lightest word for contact, while *saqar* is a name whose root is **scorching**. The sentence sets the two side by side. **The lightest verb comes before the heaviest noun.**

## Measure, command, record — 54/49-53

## 54/49 — إِنَّا كُلَّ شَىْءٍ خَلَقْنَٰهُ بِقَدَرٍ

*Innā kulla shayʾin khalaqnāhu bi-qadar*
"We have created everything by a measure."

### How the sentence is built

The sentence has the *ishtighāl* structure, and this must be seen:

```
إِنَّا   كُلَّ شَىْءٍ   خَلَقْنَٰهُ   بِقَدَرٍ
inna    kulla shayʾin   khalaqnā-hu    bi-qadar
```

**كُلَّ شَىْءٍ** is in the **accusative** (*kulla*, with *fatḥa*). Yet the verb already has an object: *khalaqnā-**hu*** — the pronoun *hu* at the end.

There are thus **two** objects in the sentence: once as an explicit noun brought forward (*kulla shayʾin*), and once as a pronoun attached to the verb (*hu*).

This is the structure Arabic calls *ishtighāl*: the noun brought forward is counted the object of an elided verb and stays in the accusative; then the same object is repeated by a pronoun. **Its function is emphasis.**

**And there is a variant reading, and it must be recorded.**

The word has also been read as **كُلُّ** (*kullu*, with *ḍamma*). On that reading the structure of the sentence changes: *kullu shayʾin* becomes the **subject** and *khalaqnāhu bi-qadar* the predicate.

| Reading | Structure | Its English rendering |
|---|---|---|
| **كُلَّ** (accusative) | *Ishtighāl* — object of an elided verb | "We — **everything** — We created it by a measure" |
| **كُلُّ** (nominative) | Subject + predicate | "Everything — We created it by a measure" |

**Since I cannot state with certainty which reading belongs to which imām, I write no names.** The accusative reading is the widespread one.

**And there is a theological debate here.** In the classical period the question of divine decree was entered by way of the grammatical structure of this verse; it was argued that the accusative reading makes the scope complete (nothing is left outside), while a limitation might be read into the nominative.

**This commentary takes no side in theology.** The same note was made in **`069-hakka.md`** and **`089-fecr.md`**. One further thing outside our task must also be stated: **the question of divine decree cannot be settled under this verse**, and it is not attempted here. Below I look only at what the word says.

### بِقَدَرٍ — the proper sense of the root

**Root: ق-د-ر.** And this root was treated in detail in **`097-kadr.md`** in **its three senses**. The table there was this:

| Sense | Which aspect of the night in Sūrat al-Qadr |
|---|---|
| **Measure / determining** | What was done |
| **Worth / honour** | What it accomplished |
| **Narrowness / fullness** | What it was filled with |

And in the section on al-Qadr precisely this verse was adduced as the evidence for the first sense: *"We have created everything according to a measure (bi-qadar) (al-Qamar 54/49)."* The same verse was also cited in the same context in **`077-murselat.md`**, **`087-ala.md`** and **`057-hadid.md`**.

**I now compare the two uses.**

| | **al-Qadr 97/1-5** | **al-Qamar 54/49** |
|---|---|---|
| Form of the word | **ٱلْقَدْر** — a definite noun | **قَدَر** — indefinite, with *bi-* |
| What it names | **A night** | **The quality of the creating** |
| How many senses are at work | **All three** — shown in the section on al-Qadr | **One only: measure/quantity** |
| The word's place in the sentence | Second term of an annexation (*laylatu'l-qadr*) | An accusative of state / a qualifier (*bi-qadar*) |
| The power branch of the root | The sense *qudra* is attached to the night indirectly | **It comes with a separate word**: *muqtadir* (42, 55) |

**And the last line is important, and I record it as my own reading.**

In Sūrat al-Qadr the three branches of the root were gathered into a single word; the word's polysemy was a density. In al-Qamar the **root is divided into branches:**

- **بِقَدَرٍ** (49) — the **measure** branch.
- **مُقْتَدِر** (42, 55) — the **power** branch.

The sūra has distributed the two aspects of the root, that is, over **two separate words.** For this reason there is no ambiguity in how the word *qadar* is to be read in verse forty-nine: the sūra itself has given the sense of power to another word.

**This makes it easier to read verse forty-nine independently of the debate about divine decree** — and that is what this commentary does here.

**The word's sense here: a measure, a quantity, a determined amount.**

The Qurʾān's own usage confirms this:

> *"Everything with Him is by a **measure** (*bi-miqdār*)."* (al-Raʿd 13/8)
> *"There is nothing whose stores are not with Us; and We send it down only in **a known measure**."* (al-Ḥijr 15/21) — cited in **`057-hadid.md`**.
> *"He created everything and determined it by a **measure** (*fa-qaddarahu taqdīrā*)."* (al-Furqān 25/2)

**In all three verses the word looks to quantity.**

### The theme of measure within the sūra

Verse forty-nine turns a theme the sūra has been building from the start into a **sentence of rule**. The table below gathers the expressions in the sūra that convey measure and number:

| Verse | Expression | What is measured |
|---|---|---|
| **3** | *kullu amrin mustaqirr* | **Where** every matter arrives |
| **12** | *ʿalā amrin qad **qudir*** | The flood's **having been decreed** |
| **19** | *fī yawmi naḥsin mustamirr* | The **day** of the destruction |
| **27-28** | *qismatun baynahum · kullu shirbin muḥtaḍar* | The water's **share and turn** |
| **31** | *ṣayḥatan **wāḥidatan*** | The **number** of the penalty: one |
| **34** | *bi-**saḥarin*** | The **hour** of the rescue |
| **38** | *ṣabbaḥahum **bukratan*** | The **hour** of the destruction |
| **49** | *kulla shayʾin khalaqnāhu **bi-qadar*** | **The measure of everything** |
| **50** | *illā **wāḥidatun** ka-lamḥin bi'l-baṣar* | The **number and duration** of the command |
| **53** | *kullu ṣaghīrin wa kabīrin mustaṭar* | The **scope** of the record |

**From beginning to end the sūra gives measures:** day, hour, number, share, turn, duration. And in verse forty-nine it says where these come from.

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording of the verses in the table can be verified; gathering the table under one theme is my reading.

### What this says to the present

It is possible to read the word's sense of "measure" in a practical place, independently of the theological debate, and I record this as an observation.

The expression **بِقَدَرٍ** says that the **quantity of a thing is determined.** And a thing whose quantity is determined has two features: it is **limited** and it is **sufficient.**

Limited — because it has been measured; it is not infinite. Sufficient — because it was given not at random but cut to size.

Its counterpart in a person's own life is the fact that resources and spans are limited: time is measured, strength is measured, opportunity is measured. The verse names this not as a lack but as an **order.**

This is not the sort of dressing-up of a verse in modern knowledge that `USLUP.md` forbids; it follows directly from the lexical sense of the word. And I record it not as a definite ruling but as a reading.

## 54/50 — وَمَآ أَمْرُنَآ إِلَّا وَٰحِدَةٌ كَلَمْحٍۭ بِٱلْبَصَرِ

*Wa mā amrunā illā wāḥidatun ka-lamḥin bi'l-baṣar*
"And Our command is but one, like the blink of an eye."

### وَاحِدَةٌ — its coming in the feminine

**وَاحِدَة** is a feminine adjective, but the word *amr* is masculine.

The reason is that **the qualified noun has been elided**: the adjective belongs to an unstated feminine noun. Two supplyings are transmitted:

| Supplied noun | Sense |
|---|---|
| **كَلِمَة وَاحِدَة** | "A single **word**" |
| **مَرَّة وَاحِدَة** | "A single **time**" |

**I do not impose a preference.** Both are recorded and they make no practical difference: the command is either a single word or a single time; on both supplyings what is described is **indivisibility.**

And the word joins with *ṣayḥatan **wāḥidatan*** in verse thirty-one. As I recorded under that verse above: the destruction of Thamūd came by a single sound; verse fifty says that this is the rule.

In **`069-hakka.md`** it was recorded that the same word (*wāḥida*) occurs twice in al-Ḥāqqa and carries the difference of speed between the two sections of that sūra (*nafkhatun wāḥida*, *dakkatan wāḥida*). It does the same job in al-Qamar.

### كَلَمْحٍۭ بِٱلْبَصَرِ — like the blink of an eye

**لَمْح — Root: ل-م-ح.** And this root occurs in **only two places** in the Qurʾān: here and at al-Naḥl 16/77.

Its concrete sense: **a quick look, a glance from the corner of the eye, a glimpse.** *Lamaḥa* — he glanced. *Lamḥa* — the moment of a single look. *Lamḥatu'l-baṣar* — the time the eye takes to open and close.

And another branch of the root: **لَمَحَ** — it gleamed, it flashed. In sound it is close to **ل-م-ع** (*lamʿ*, a gleam). **I do not speak with certainty about the relation between the two roots**; I draw no meaning from closeness in sound.

### The movement of the eye as the shortest measure of time

**And what must be dwelt on here is what the unit of measure is.**

The verse does not use a unit of time. There is no second, no instant, no abstract span. **The measure used is a bodily movement: the opening and closing of the eye.**

The reason lies in language itself and is a general fact: **in older languages the shortest measures of duration are taken from the body's fastest movements.** In English, "in the blink of an eye", "in a heartbeat"; in Arabic, *lamḥu'l-baṣar*, *ṭarfatu ʿayn* (the blink of an eye). When there is no instrument of measure, the unit of measure is the body.

**This is an observation about language and I record it as such;** I do not claim that the verse intends it.

**And the organ chosen matters, and I record this as my own reading.** Throughout the sūra the eye is a recurring organ:

| Verse | The eye |
|---|---|
| **7** | *khushshaʿan **abṣāruhum*** — their eyes cast down |
| **12** | ***ʿuyūnan*** — springs (the same word) |
| **14** | *bi-**aʿyuninā*** — under Our eyes |
| **37** | *fa-ṭamasnā **aʿyunahum*** — We wiped out their eyes |
| **50** | *ka-lamḥin **bi'l-baṣar*** — like the blink of an eye |

**The eye in five separate verses.** And the five in five separate roles: the sign of abasement, the source of water, the expression of protection, the target of punishment, and the measure of time.

I record this as an observation about the distribution of a word; the count can be verified.

### The difference from al-Naḥl 16/77 — a question of a particle

The Qurʾān uses this expression once more, and **the wording is not exactly the same**:

> *"The matter of the Hour is **like the blink of an eye**, or nearer."* (al-Naḥl 16/77 — *ka-**lamḥi'l-baṣari** aw huwa aqrab*)
> *"Our command… **like the blink of an eye**."* (al-Qamar 54/50 — *ka-lamḥin **bi'l-baṣari***)

**The difference is in one letter:**

| Verse | Structure | Grammatical status |
|---|---|---|
| al-Naḥl 16/77 | *lamḥi'**l-baṣar*** | **Annexation** — "the eye's glance" |
| **al-Qamar 54/50** | *lamḥin **bi'l-baṣar*** | **With the particle *bāʾ*** — "a glance made with the eye" |

Annexation makes the glance **belong** to the eye: the eye's *lamḥa*. The *bāʾ*, on the other hand, gives the eye the function of an **instrument**: a *lamḥa* done with the eye.

**The difference is small and does not spoil the sense; but I record it**, because in this commentary differences of wording are recorded. On the second construction the eye is not the owner of the movement but **its instrument.**

In **`079-naziat.md`** this verse was cited: *"And Our command is but one, like the blink of an eye (al-Qamar 54/50)."*

### The work the two verses do together

Verses forty-nine and fifty build a pair together, and the two sides of the pair are these:

| Verse | What it looks at | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **49** | **The created** | Everything is **measured** |
| **50** | **The Creator** | The command is **one** and **instantaneous** |

**The measure, that is, is in the object, not in the command.** What is created is measured; the act of creating is indivisible and without duration.

**I record this as my own reading** and I draw this conclusion: the events related in the sūra's four accounts — the flood, the wind, the cry, the stones — were events with durations and measures. Verses forty-nine and fifty say where those measures come from, and add that **the source itself is without measure.**

## 54/51 — وَلَقَدْ أَهْلَكْنَآ أَشْيَاعَكُمْ فَهَلْ مِن مُّدَّكِرٍ

*Wa-laqad ahlaknā ashyāʿakum fa-hal min muddakir*
"And We have destroyed your likes. Is there anyone who will remember?"

### The refrain's last occurrence — and the entry of the pronoun

This is the **sixth and last** occurrence of the question *fa-hal min muddakir* in the sūra. And it differs from the earlier ones at one point.

| Occurrence | First half | Pronoun of address |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | *wa-laqad **taraknāhā** āyatan* | None |
| 17, 22, 32, 40 | *wa-laqad **yassarnā'l-Qurʾāna** li'l-dhikr* | None |
| **51** | *wa-laqad **ahlaknā ashyāʿa-KUM*** | **Present: -kum** |

**In five occurrences there was no pronoun of address; in the sixth there is.**

This was the observation I recorded in the section "The refrain of al-Raḥmān and the refrain of al-Qamar" above: al-Raḥmān's question pointed at the addressee (*Rabbi-kumā*, *tukadhdhibā-n*), al-Qamar's was asked into the open.

**And towards its close the sūra closes that difference.** In the last occurrence the question is no longer asked into the open; **the addressee is inside the sentence.**

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording of the six occurrences is verifiable data of the text; the reading "at the close the sūra brings the addressee into the sentence" is mine.

### أَشْيَاع — "your likes"

**Root: ش-ي-ع.** Its concrete sense: **to spread, to disperse, to go in every direction.** *Shāʿa'l-khabaru* — the news spread. *Shuyūʿ* — spreading (the Turkish *şuyû bulmak*, "to become widely known", is from this).

And from this the second sense: **a group that follows behind someone, a party of adherents.** *Shīʿa* — a group that follows a person and acts with him. Its plurals are **أَشْيَاع** and **شِيَع**.

Its uses in the Qurʾān show the field of the word:

- *"He had divided them into **factions** (*shiyaʿan*)"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/4) — about Pharaoh's people.
- *"Those who split up their religion and became **factions**…"* (al-Anʿām 6/159; al-Rūm 30/32).
- *"As was done before with **their likes**…"* (Sabaʾ 34/54 — *ka-mā fuʿila bi-**ashyāʿihim** min qabl*).

**The choice of the word is worth recording, and it does two things.**

**One — it ties the peoples to the addressee.** The verse does not say "we destroyed those before them"; it says **"your *ashyāʿ*."** The protagonists of the four accounts, that is, are counted as belonging to **the addressee's own group.**

**Two — it does not specify the kind of tie.** A *shīʿa* is not a bond of descent; it is a bond of **acting together.** What is said to the addressee, that is, is not "they are your forefathers" but **"they are those who behaved as you do".**

And this accords with the principle recorded in `USLUP.md`: the tie is established not by identity but by **quality.**

**I record this as my own reading.** The sense of the root is a datum of the language; the reading "a tie of quality" that I draw from it is mine.

### أَهْلَكْنَا — We destroyed

**Root: ه-ل-ك.** *Halaka* — he perished, he was destroyed. *Ahlaka* (Form IV) — he destroyed.

In **`069-hakka.md`** the passive use of this root (*uhlikū* — they were destroyed) was treated, and it was recorded that the passive, by concealing the agent, brings the event forward.

**In al-Qamar the verb is active and the agent is explicit:** *ahlaknā* — "We destroyed."

| Sūra | Form | Agent |
|---|---|---|
| al-Ḥāqqa 69/5-6 | *uhlikū* — **passive** | **Not named** |
| **al-Qamar 54/51** | *ahlaknā* — **active** | ***We*** |

This fits the general bearing of the two sūras. **In al-Qamar the agent is always explicit:** *arsalnā* (19, 31, 34), *fataḥnā* and *fajjarnā* (11-12), *ḥamalnā* (13), *ṭamasnā* (37), *akhadhnā* (42), *najjaynā* (34), *ahlaknā* (51), *khalaqnā* (49), *yassarnā* (17, 22, 32, 40). **The sūra uses a first-person plural verb in close to fifteen places.**

I record this as an observation from counting; the verbs can be verified.

## 54/52-53 — وَكُلُّ شَىْءٍ فَعَلُوهُ فِى ٱلزُّبُرِ · وَكُلُّ صَغِيرٍ وَكَبِيرٍ مُّسْتَطَرٌ

*Wa kullu shayʾin faʿalūhu fi'l-zubur — wa kullu ṣaghīrin wa kabīrin mustaṭar*
"Everything they have done is in the records. Every small and great thing is written down line by line."

### كُلُّ شَىْءٍ — a second time, three verses apart

**This construction occurs twice in the sūra, and the two occurrences complete one another:**

| Verse | Expression | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **49** | *innā **kulla shayʾin** khalaqnāhu bi-qadar* | Everything **created** |
| **52** | *wa **kullu shayʾin** faʿalūhu fi'l-zubur* | Everything **done** |

**In verse forty-nine, creating; in verse fifty-two, action. The scope of both is "everything."**

And the structure of the two sentences differs:

- In 49 *kulla* is in the **accusative** (on the widespread reading) — the object of the verb.
- In 52 *kullu* is in the **nominative** — the subject, with *fi'l-zubur* as its predicate.

**I record this as my own reading:** the two verses set two distinct completenesses side by side. The completeness of creating: nothing was created without measure. The completeness of the record: no act stayed outside the record. **Both begin with "everything."**

### فِى ٱلزُّبُرِ — the answer to verse forty-three

The root **ز-ب-ر** was treated under verse forty-three, and I complete here the circle I recorded there:

| Verse | Sentence | Its function |
|---|---|---|
| **43** | *am lakum **barāʾatun** fi'l-zubur* | **Question:** is there an exemption in the records? |
| **52** | *wa kullu shayʾin **faʿalūhu** fi'l-zubur* | **Answer:** your acts are in the records |

**The question sought a document; the answer states the document's contents.**

And the two verses end with **exactly the same phrase**: *fi'l-zubur.* The same two words, nine verses apart.

**The root's sense of "writing by carving into stone" becomes functional here** — because what is asked after is not an erasable record. Verse forty-three was looking for a **document of exemption**; verse fifty-two says that something else is found in that same place.

### مُسْتَطَر — written down line by line

**Root: س-ط-ر.** This root was treated in **`052-tur.md`**, **`068-kalem.md`**, **`083-mutaffifin.md`** and **`088-gasiye.md`**. Its concrete sense: **a row, a series, a line.**

Its derivatives:
- **سَطْر** — a line; and more generally **an even row** (a row of trees, a row of people, is also *saṭr*).
- **سَطَرَ** — he wrote in lines.
- **مُسَطَّر** — written. In the Qurʾān: *"A Book **written in lines** on unrolled parchment"* (al-Ṭūr 52/2-3 — *kitābin **masṭūr***). Treated in **`052-tur.md`**.
- **أَسَاطِير** — the word rendered "tales of the ancients". The root is the same: **that which has been written out in lines.** In the Qurʾān it occurs as the accusation of the deniers: *"This is nothing but the **lines** of the ancients"* (al-Anfāl 8/31; al-Anʿām 6/25; al-Qalam 68/15 — treated in **`068-kalem.md`**).

**The form in the verse is مُسْتَطَر**: **the passive participle of Form X (*istifʿāl*).**

And there are two explanations of Form X's function here:

| Explanation | What Form X adds |
|---|---|
| **Intensification** | *Mustaṭar* = the same sense as *masṭūr*, but stronger: **written out fully, without omission** |
| **Seeking / deserving** | That which calls for writing, which is a fit subject of record |

**The first is widespread and I do not impose a preference.**

### ز-ب-ر and س-ط-ر — two roots for writing

Two consecutive verses use two different roots for writing, and the difference between them is worth recording:

| Root | Concrete sense | What it looks to |
|---|---|---|
| **ز-ب-ر** | To **incise** on a hard surface; a block of iron, a stone-lined well | **Permanence** — inerasability |
| **س-ط-ر** | A **line**, a row, a series | **Order** — not scattered but arranged |

**Verses fifty-two and fifty-three, that is, state the two properties of the record separately: that it is inerasable and that it is orderly.**

**I record this distinction as my own reading.** The lexical senses of the two roots are data of the language; the division of labour between them is a reading I have set up. In **`084-insikak.md`** the same kind of division of labour was set up for the three roots of splitting (ش-ق-ق / ف-ط-ر / ف-ل-ق), and the same note was made there too.

### صَغِير وَكَبِير — stating the whole by its two ends

This is a widespread technique in Arabic and in languages generally: **to name the two opposite ends and thereby take in everything between them.** In English "sooner or later", "more or less", "high and low" do the same job.

The Qurʾān uses the same technique elsewhere:

> *"What a Book this is — it leaves out nothing **small** or **great** but has counted it!"* (al-Kahf 18/49)
> *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it; and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."* (al-Zilzāl 99/7-8) — treated in **`099-zilzal.md`**.

**And the technical difference here is worth recording:** al-Kahf 18/49 covers by **negation** ("has left nothing out"), al-Zilzāl covers by **the smallest unit of measure** ("an atom"), while al-Qamar covers by **the two ends** ("small and great").

Three different paths, one result: **the completeness of the scope.**

I record this as a comparison of techniques; the wording of the three verses can be verified.

### What this says to the present

The practical side of these two verses lies in the idea of the "record" itself, and I record it as an observation, not as a ruling.

The greater part of human life consists of things that are **not recorded**: words spoken and forgotten, behaviour no one saw, decisions with no witness. And whether a piece of behaviour is going to be recorded often determines how it is done.

What verse fifty-three says is that the record is not made **according to size.** The record does not select what is important; it takes things in without distinguishing **small from great.**

And this joins with the sentence of the third verse: *wa kullu amrin mustaqirr* — every matter comes to rest. The sūra had said this at the beginning; at verse fifty-three it adds that the record too has the same scope.

## 54/54 — إِنَّ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ فِى جَنَّٰتٍ وَنَهَرٍ

*Inna'l-muttaqīna fī jannātin wa nahar*
"The godfearing are amid gardens and rivers."

### The sūra's only verse of good tidings

Fifty-three of the fifty-five verses are warning, account, punishment and reckoning. **The reward is fitted into two verses** — and both are the sūra's last two.

Compared with al-Raḥmān this is the sūra's most conspicuous feature, and I recorded it above in the section on structure. Here I complete the table:

| | **al-Raḥmān 55** | **al-Qamar 54** |
|---|---|---|
| Total verses | 78 | 55 |
| Garden / reward verses | **32** (46-77) | **2** (54-55) |
| Their proportion | **41%** | **4%** |
| Description of the garden | Springs, fruits, couches, spouses, colours, vessels, pavilions | **None** — two nouns and a place |
| Punishment / warning verses | 15 (31-45) | **~50** |

**Two refrain sūras have piled their material at exactly opposite ends.**

And this fits the subject of the two sūras' refrains:

- **Al-Raḥmān's refrain is about favour** (*ālāʾ*) — and the sūra counts favours.
- **Al-Qamar's refrain is about warning** (*nudhur*) and asks about heeding (*muddakir*) — and the sūra counts warnings.

**The refrain has determined what the sūra will count.** I record this as my own reading; the distribution of verses in the two sūras is a verifiable count.

### ٱلْمُتَّقِين — those who guard themselves

**Root: و-ق-ي.** Its concrete sense: **to protect, to shield, to guard oneself from harm.** *Wiqāya* — protection. *Wiqāʾ* — the cover placed over the mouth of a vessel. *Ittiqāʾ* (Form VIII) — **to take oneself into protection.**

This root has been treated in many places in this commentary and I do not repeat it. The one point here is this:

**The word occurs here for the first and last time in the sūra.** Throughout the sūra the side described is always the deniers: *al-kāfirūn* (8), *al-mujrimūn* (47), *kuffārukum* (43). The other side is **never named** in the sūra — until verse fifty-four.

**And the choice of word is worth recording.** The other side is named not *al-muʾminūn* (the believers) but ***al-muttaqūn*** (those who guard themselves).

**I record this as my own reading:** *ittiqāʾ*, by its root, is **to protect oneself from something** — that is, from what the sūra has been relating from the start. For forty-four verses the sūra enumerated what must be guarded against; at the close it names those who guarded themselves. **The word is the counterpart of the sūra's content.**

### جَنَّٰتٍ وَنَهَرٍ — plural and singular

And here there is an oddity of word order: **جَنَّات is plural, نَهَر singular.**

The Qurʾān's standard expression is *jannātin **tajrī min taḥtihā'l-anhār*** — "gardens beneath which **rivers** flow", and there both words are plural. Here the second word comes in the singular.

**More than one explanation of this oddity is transmitted:**

| Explanation | What *nahar* means | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **A generic noun** | "River" — as a genus, with plural sense | In Arabic generic nouns can give a plural sense in the singular form; and **the harmony of the verse-ending** requires the singular |
| **A lightened form of نُهُر** | *Nuhur*, the plural of *nahr*, read in lightened form as *nahar* | Transmitted in the sources |
| **Breadth / openness** | *Nahar*, from the root's branch meaning "to widen, to open out": **a broad expanse of water** | *Nahara* — it widened, it flowed |
| **Light** | Related to *nahār* (daytime): **light, brightness** | Shared root; *nahār* is also from the root ن-ه-ر |

**A note is required on the fourth explanation.** *Nahār* (daytime) and *nahr* (river) are counted as being from the same root, and the link the lexicographers draw is one of **opening out / widening**: daytime is the time in which light spreads; a river is the bed in which water spreads. **I transmit this explanation as ascribed to the lexicographers; I do not offer it as a settled etymology.** And the reading "light" is a minority one.

**The first explanation is the most widespread and is supported by the data of the verse-endings:** as I showed in the table of verse-endings above, all fifty-five verses of the sūra end on the sound *r*, and the form *anhār* does not sit on that pattern.

**I do not impose a preference;** but I state that the first explanation has a ground in the text: the order of the verse-endings is a verifiable datum.

## 54/55 — فِى مَقْعَدِ صِدْقٍ عِندَ مَلِيكٍ مُّقْتَدِرٍ

*Fī maqʿadi ṣidqin ʿinda malīkin muqtadir*
"In a seat of truth, in the presence of a Sovereign, All-Powerful."

The sūra's last verse, and every word in it carries something.

### مَقْعَد — a place to be seated

**Root: ق-ع-د.** *Qaʿada* — he sat. **مَقْعَد** is a noun of place: **a place where one sits, a station, a seat.**

This root was treated in **`085-buruc.md`** and **`072-cin.md`**, and this verse was cited in the section on al-Burūj.

**And the word's other occurrences in the Qurʾān are worth recording:**

| Verse | Expression | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Āl ʿImrān 3/121 | *tubawwiʾu'l-muʾminīna **maqāʿida** li'l-qitāl* | **Battle positions** |

In **`072-cin.md`** that verse was treated, and it was recorded there that the jinn were driven from *"the places where they sat to listen"*.

In al-Qamar's last verse it names **an abiding seat.** The word is the same; **the duration differs.** All the others are temporary: a position is abandoned, one is driven from a listening post, and the one who sits behind finally gets up.

### صِدْق — a Qurʾānic pattern

**Root: ص-د-ق.** This root was treated in **`051-zariyat.md`**, **`057-hadid.md`**, **`059-hasr.md`**, **`061-saff.md`**, **`062-cuma.md`**, **`070-mearic.md`**, **`075-kiyame.md`**, **`092-leyl.md`** and **`063-munafikun.md`**. Its concrete sense: **soundness, reality, a word's matching what is real.** *Ṣidq* — truthfulness. *Ṣadaqa* — alms (what shows the truth of faith). *Ṣadāq* — the marriage gift. *Ṣādiq*, *ṣiddīq*.

**And here the word is the second term of an annexation: *maqʿadi ṣidqin*.**

This is a settled pattern in the Qurʾān and is repeated in several places:

| Verse | Construction | Its sense |
|---|---|---|
| Yūnus 10/2 | *qadama **ṣidqin*** | **A footing of truth** — a sound standing |
| al-Isrāʾ 17/80 | *mudkhala **ṣidqin** … mukhraja **ṣidqin*** | **An entering and a coming forth of truth** |
| al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/84 | *lisāna **ṣidqin** fi'l-ākhirīn* | **A tongue of truth** — a good name among those who come after |
| Maryam 19/50 | *lisāna **ṣidqin** ʿaliyyā* | The same |
| **al-Qamar 54/55** | *maqʿada **ṣidqin*** | **A seat of truth** |

**The function of the pattern is this:** when the word *ṣidq* is annexed to a noun, it makes that noun **"real, genuine, not to be taken back."** *Qadama ṣidqin* — a firm footing; *lisāna ṣidqin* — a genuinely good name; *maqʿada ṣidqin* — **really a seat.**

**I record this as my own reading:** the pattern does not qualify what is promised in return; it qualifies **the reality of the promise.** It says not "a good place" but **"really a place".**

**And a contrast within the sūra is worth recording.** The sūra is the sūra of the root ك-ذ-ب. Its occurrences:

| Form | Verses | Number |
|---|---|---|
| **Verb** (*kadhdhaba / kadhdhabū / kadhdhabat*) | 3, 9 *(twice)*, 18, 23, 33, 42 | **7** |
| **Noun** (*kadhdhāb / al-kadhdhāb*) | 25, 26 | **2** |
| **Total** | | **9** |

**And in the sūra's last verse the root ص-د-ق occurs once.**

| Root | Number of occurrences in the sūra | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **ك-ذ-ب** — to deny | **9** | Spread from beginning to end |
| **ص-د-ق** — truth | **1** | **In the last verse** |

**The sūra fills with nine denials and closes with a single truth.**

I record this as my own reading. The count can be verified; the reading I draw from it is mine.

### عِندَ — "in the presence of"

**عِنْد** is an adverb of place in Arabic, but it conveys not only physical nearness; it conveys **belonging, ownership and presence.** *ʿIndī* — in my view, according to me. *Min ʿindillāh* — from God.

The Qurʾān uses the same word of the martyrs: *"They are alive with their Lord (*ʿinda Rabbihim*), provided for"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/169).

**And the word order of the verse is worth recording:** first the **place** is stated (*fī maqʿadi ṣidqin*), then **in whose presence** it is (*ʿinda malīkin muqtadir*). The description is thus in two layers: a place, and a presence.

**I record this as my own reading:** in verse fifty-four the sūra gave a scene (*jannātin wa nahar*); in verse fifty-five it leaves the scene and states **the relation.** And the sūra's last word is not the name of a place but an **attribute.**

### مَلِيك — an intensive pattern

**Root: م-ل-ك.** This root was treated throughout the whole of **`067-mulk.md`**, and was also taken up in **`114-nas.md`**, **`082-infitar.md`**, **`078-nebe.md`**, **`076-insan.md`**, **`059-hasr.md`** and **`062-cuma.md`**.

**The issue here is the pattern, and it requires attention.**

The Qurʾān uses three separate forms from this root:

| Form | Pattern | Its sense |
|---|---|---|
| **مَلِك** | *faʿil* | **King** — al-Ḥashr 59/23, al-Nās 114/2, Ṭāhā 20/114 |
| **مَالِك** | active participle | **Owner, possessor** — al-Fātiḥa 1/4 (with a variant reading), Āl ʿImrān 3/26 |
| **مَلِيك** | ***faʿīl*** — **intensive** | **al-Qamar 54/55** |

The **فَعِيل** pattern conveys in Arabic **intensity and the quasi-participle**: the quality being settled and concentrated in its bearer. *ʿAlīm* (knowing much), *Qadīr*, *Samīʿ*, *Raḥīm*.

*Malīk*, then, is weightier than *malik*: **one whose dominion is settled and complete.**

**And in this pattern the word is commonly recorded as occurring only here in the Qurʾān.** I record it in that form and make no absolute claim about the count.

**The *mālik/malik* variant reading treated in the section on al-Fātiḥa does not attach here**; it was treated there and I do not repeat it.

**I record this as my own reading:** the sūra's last verse names God with the attribute of sovereignty in its most concentrated pattern. And the sūra's addressee was a host that said in verse forty-four *"we are a host that cannot be beaten"* and was scattered in verse forty-five. **The host was scattered; the dominion stands.**

### مُقْتَدِر — and the sūra's circle closes

**Root: ق-د-ر**, the active participle of Form VIII. Treated under verse forty-two.

**And now the sūra's tightest circle is completed.**

```
42   فَأَخَذْنَٰهُمْ أَخْذَ عَزِيزٍ مُّقْتَدِرٍ
49   إِنَّا كُلَّ شَىْءٍ خَلَقْنَٰهُ بِقَدَرٍ
55   فِى مَقْعَدِ صِدْقٍ عِندَ مَلِيكٍ مُّقْتَدِرٍ
```

Three verses, one root, three separate jobs:

| Verse | Form | What the root is doing |
|---|---|---|
| **42** | *ʿazīzin **muqtadir*** | **The one who seizes** — at the close of the list of destructions |
| **49** | *bi-**qadar*** | **Measure** — the quality of everything's creation |
| **55** | *malīkin **muqtadir*** | **The one in whose presence they sit** — at the close of the sūra |

**The same word — مُقْتَدِر — occurs twice in the sūra: in verses forty-two and fifty-five.**

**And the position of the two occurrences is decisive:**

- **Verse 42:** the **last verse** of the five-account list of destructions.
- **Verse 55:** the **last verse** of the sūra.

The same attribute, that is, seals two lists at once: **the list of penalties** and **the sūra as a whole.**

**And verse forty-nine stands between them**, with the root's third form: *bi-qadar.* The order, then, is:

1. **Power** closes the punishment (42).
2. **Measure** describes the creating (49).
3. **Power** closes the sūra (55).

**The root occurs three times in the last third of the sūra, each time in a closing position.**

**I record this as my own reading.** The wording of the three verses, the forms of the word and the verse numbers are verifiable data of the text; the reading of the circle is mine.

**And one further detail:** in verse forty-two *muqtadir* comes together with **عَزِيز** (*ʿazīzin muqtadir*); in verse fifty-five with **مَلِيك** (*malīkin muqtadir*). Two companions, in two different directions:

| Verse | Accompanying attribute | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| **42** | *ʿazīz* — unconquerable, unreachable | **Irresistibility** — suited to a context of punishment |
| **55** | *malīk* — sovereign | **Being someone whose presence can be entered** — suited to a context of reward |

**The same power shows two different faces through two different attributes.** This too I record as my own reading.

## The sūra's argument — step by step

It is possible to reduce the fifty-five verses of the sūra to a single piece of reasoning, and I offer this as an attempt at a reading:

**1.** A time has drawn near and a sign was shown. (1)

**2.** They saw the sign and rejected it; in rejecting it they gave it a name. (2)

**3.** After the rejection no emptiness remained; craving took its place. (3a)

**4.** But every matter comes to rest. (3b — **the rule**)

**5.** Deterring tidings came to them; the wisdom was complete and it arrived. It did not avail. (4-5)

**6.** A day will come; on that day they will be called to a thing unrecognisable and will find it hard. (6-8)

**7.** And this is not happening for the first time. **Here is the list:**
- The people of Noah denied → the flood from two waters. (9-14)
- ʿĀd denied → the uprooting wind. (18-20)
- Thamūd denied → they broke the order, a single cry. (23-31)
- The people of Lot denied → they violated the guest, a stone-hurling wind. (33-38)
- Warnings came to the house of Pharaoh → they denied them all, they were seized. (41-42)

**8.** After each account the same two questions: **how was My punishment** and **is there anyone who will remember.** (16-17, 18/21-22, 30/32, 37/39-40)

**9.** Now to you: are your disbelievers stronger than those? Or do you have a written exemption? (43)

**10.** Do you say "we are a host that cannot be beaten"? That host will be scattered. (44-45)

**11.** And the real appointed time is not even this: **the Hour.** Greater and more bitter. (46)

**12.** The state of the guilty is described in Thamūd's own words. (47-48)

**13.** **Because:** everything was created by a measure and the command is a single act. (49-50 — **the source**)

**14.** **And:** your likes have been destroyed; everything you have done is in inerasable records, line by line. (51-53)

**15.** As for those who guard themselves: in gardens, in a real seat, in the presence of One whose dominion is complete. (54-55)

**The backbone of the argument is four steps, and I record this as my own reading:**

| Step | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **The rule is set down** | 3 | *Kullu amrin mustaqirr* |
| **The instances are enumerated** | 9-42 | Five peoples |
| **The source is stated** | 49-50 | *Bi-qadar* and *wāḥidatun* |
| **The record is shown** | 52-53 | *Fi'l-zubur* and *mustaṭar* |

And the turn to the addressee (43-48) and the closing (54-55) are set among these four.

## What this says to the present

Per the method (`USLUP.md`), what is said in this section is **my own reading**; it is not offered as transmitted material and it is not binding.

**One — the question is not answered.**

The sūra's refrain is asked six times and not once answered: *"Is there anyone who will remember?"*

In the section on al-Raḥmān this was recorded of the same situation: the question is asked thirty-one times and in none of them is an answer given. But the two silences differ, and I recorded the difference above: al-Raḥmān's question has no honest answer; al-Qamar's **does**, and the one who answers becomes the answer.

For a text to ask a question six times and never answer it is for it to leave that question's answer outside the text.

**Two — knowing and drawing a lesson are not the same thing.**

The refrain's word is not *ʿālim* but *muddakir* — and as I showed above the word is in Form VIII: **one who takes the lesson and makes it his own.**

The sūra relates the story of four peoples, and these are stories the addressee **already knows.** That in verse forty-one it reduces the best-known of them to two sentences shows this: there is no need to tell it, it is known.

The sūra's question, then, is not "do you know". It puts a word between knowledge and knowledge's being of use.

**Three — "your likes."**

Verse fifty-one ties the four peoples to the addressee's own group: *ashyāʿakum.* And the tie the word establishes is not one of descent but of **behaviour.**

Its practical consequence is that it blocks reading the accounts as being about other people. As was recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn: *"The sūra is an instrument of diagnosis, and the diagnosis is applied first to oneself."* Al-Qamar does this with a single word.

**Four — the abolition of the turn.**

The observation I recorded under verse twenty-eight is the sūra's most concrete point of contact with the present.

Thamūd's offence is not related as the killing of an animal. In verse twenty-eight an order is set up (*qismatun baynahum*, *kullu shirbin muḥtaḍar*); in verse twenty-nine that order is cut off (*ʿaqar*).

Shared resources are usually not ruined by an open denial of a right; they are ruined **by the abolition of the turn.** No one says "this has no right"; the turn simply stops working.

**Five — opening it to debate.**

The verb *tamāraw* in verse thirty-six names the sūra's subtlest reaction: to neutralise a warning, without rejecting it, **by making it debatable.**

To turn away is a visible stance; to give something a name is a visible stance. To open it to debate looks like taking no stance at all — and so long as the debate goes on, no decision has to be made.

**Six — the record's not being made according to size.**

Verse fifty-three says that the record makes no distinction of *"small and great"*.

Knowing whether a piece of behaviour is going to be recorded often determines how it will be done. What the verse says is that this distinction does not operate.

**Seven — being able to say "I am overcome."**

Noah's prayer in verse ten is three words and contains not a single accusation: *innī maghlūbun fa'ntaṣir.*

And in verse forty-four there is exactly the opposite sentence: *naḥnu jamīʿun muntaṣir.*

The two sentences are built from the same root and their outcomes are reversed. This is the sūra's quietest contrast, and it is not stated in a sentence of judgement; it is stated **by the two sentences sharing a root.**

**Eight — the name of the rescue.**

Verse thirty-five names the sūra's one sentence of rescue a "favour", and names the attitude recompensed "thanks".

From beginning to end the sūra works with a vocabulary of punishment and force: We sent, We seized, We destroyed. In a single verse the vocabulary changes. And in that verse the quality of those saved is neither faith nor works; it is **thanks** — that is, making visible what has been received.

This is the exact opposite of al-Raḥmān's refrain, and at this point the two sūras look at one another.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

Per the method (`USLUP.md`), I list openly the places where, throughout the text, definite language was deliberately not used.

- **What the splitting of the moon in 54/1 relates** — an event that occurred in the past, or a sign of the Hour related in the perfect — **is disputed.** Two great readings and one reconciling reading were given in tabular form, with their evidence and their weak sides. **No preference was adopted and no definite ruling was issued.**
- **The ascription of the second view to al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī** is widely transmitted in the classical sources; **certainty was not claimed about the soundness of the ascription**, because no verified chain can be given.
- **That reports about the splitting of the moon are found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim** was recorded; **the text of no report was relayed, no chain of transmitters was given and no single report was made a support.**
- **Claims of "scientific proof" drawn from geological formations on the moon's surface were rejected**, and the ground was written openly: `USLUP.md`'s prohibition on hunting for scientific miracles, the claim's own lack of support, and the fact that such a link works against the text.
- **On the sense of مُّسْتَمِرّ** (going on / about to pass / strong) three readings were given; **no preference was imposed.** The sentence that all three arrive at the same result is **my own reading.**
- **On whether the senses "to pass by" and "to be bitter" in the root م-ر-ر come from the same root, no definite ruling was issued.**
- **That the roots م-ر-ر and م-ر-ي are distinct was recorded explicitly**; **no meaning was drawn** from the closeness in sound between the two roots.
- **No meaning was drawn from the closeness in sound between ل-م-ح and ل-م-ع.**
- **On the kind of *mā* in فَمَا تُغْنِ ٱلنُّذُرُ** (negative / interrogative) two readings were given, **no preference was stated.**
- **On the grammatical position of مُزْدَجَر** (*mīmī* verbal noun / noun of place / passive participle) three solutions were given; it was recorded that in point of sense they come out at the same place.
- **On the sense of حِكْمَةٌ بَٰلِغَة** (the completeness of the wisdom / the wisdom's having arrived) two readings were given; an inclination to the second was stated and **not imposed.**
- **On the construction of وَٱزْدُجِرَ** (a separate sentence / coordinated with *qālū* / a complement of *majnūn*) three solutions were given, **no preference was imposed.**
- **On who "the one denied" is in جَزَآءً لِّمَن كَانَ كُفِرَ** (Noah / God) two views were given, **no preference was imposed**; it was recorded that the practical result is the same.
- **On the referent of the pronoun in تَرَكْنَٰهَا** (the ship / the event / the punishment) three views were given; it was stated that the second rubs least against the text and it was **not presented as binding.**
- **What دُسُر is** (nails / ropes / the ship's prow) is disputed; three views were given, **no preference was imposed.** The shipbuilding technique of sewing with palm fibre was **transmitted as a historical datum**; it was **not claimed** that the verse intends this technique, and **no miracle was drawn** from it.
- **No theological side was taken over the expression بِأَعْيُنِنَا.** The tafwīḍ/taʾwīl distinction given in the sections on al-Ḥāqqa and al-Fajr was preserved and not repeated; nothing was said about modality. **No number or modality was drawn from the plural form.**
- **On the sense of لِلذِّكْرِ** (memorising / taking heed) two readings were given; it was stated that the context inclines to the second and it was **recorded as not binding.**
- **The variant reading خُشَّعًا / خَاشِعًا** was transmitted; **since it could not be stated with certainty which reading belongs to which imām, no names were written.** The same note holds for the differences **ٱلْمُحْتَظِر / ٱلْمُحْتَظَر** and **كُلَّ شَىْءٍ / كُلُّ شَىْءٍ**.
- **No theological side was taken in the debate about divine decree conducted by way of the variant reading in 54/49.** The question of decree was not settled under this verse and no attempt was made to settle it; only the word's sense of "measure/quantity" was given.
- **It was not claimed** that the **إِنْ / إِذَا distinction** operates absolutely everywhere.
- **The distribution whereby the object of *kadhdhaba* with بِ is "a thing" and its direct object "a person"** was given as an inclination; **it was not presented as a rule.**
- **For the رِيح / رِيَاح distribution** the note of the section on al-Ḥāqqa was preserved as it stands: **since it cannot be said with certainty whether it has exceptions, it was not presented as a rule.**
- **For the sense of صَرْصَر** (howling / freezing) the refusal to rule from the section on al-Ḥāqqa was preserved.
- **It was not claimed** that the **سَـ / سَوْفَ distinction** operates absolutely everywhere.
- **The grammatical nicety drawn from the indefiniteness of بِسَحَرٍ** was transmitted as ascribed to the lexicographers; **it was not presented as an absolute rule.**
- **What نَهَر means** (a generic noun / a lightened form of *nuhur* / breadth / light) is disputed; four explanations were given, it was stated that the first is supported by the data of the verse-endings, and **no preference was imposed.**
- **On the sense of مُسْتَقِرّ in verse 38** (lasting / come to its place) two readings were given, **no preference was imposed.**
- **On the function of Form X in مُسْتَطَر** (intensification / seeking) two explanations were given, **no preference was imposed.**
- **On the elided noun qualified by وَاحِدَةٌ** (*kalima* / *marra*) two supplyings were given, **no preference was imposed.**
- **On the addressee of أَكُفَّارُكُمْ** two solutions were given, **no preference was imposed.**
- **On the addressee of فَذُوقُوا۟** (those destroyed / the present hearers) two solutions were given, **no preference was imposed.**
- **The reports that 54/45 was realised at Badr were recorded but not made a support**; since certainty could not be spoken about the text and chain of the reports, **no text was relayed ascribed to a source.** The disagreement that this verse is Medinan was also recorded and **no preference was imposed.**
- **No teaching of "lucky and unlucky days" was drawn from the word نَحْس;** on the contrary, the Qurʾān's stance on *ṭiyara* (al-Aʿrāf 7/131; Yā Sīn 36/18-19) was given as evidence. **Certainty was not spoken about the link between نَحْس and نُحَاس.**
- **The name of the man of Thamūd who hamstrung the camel was not given;** the note of the section on al-Shams was preserved. **How the camel came, its size and the details of the reports were not relayed.**
- **The names, number and locations of the cities of the people of Lot were not given;** the note of the section on al-Ḥāqqa was preserved.
- **That the word مَلِيك occurs only here in the Qurʾān** was stated on the basis of the widespread note; **no absolute claim about the count was made.** The same caution holds for *munhamir* and *adhā*.
- **Explanations of roots:** ه-و-ي (falling / craving), ع-ق-ر (cutting / barrenness), ح-ظ-ر (fold / prohibition), ز-ب-ر (writing / a block of iron), س-ح-ب (dragging / cloud), د-ه-ي (disaster / intelligence), س-ع-ر (madness / flame), ج-ن-ن (covering / madness), ن-ه-ر (river / daytime), ه-ش-م (breaking / the name *Hāshim*), ج-ر-د (stripping / locust), ع-ط-و (giving / reaching out and taking), and the difference between ن-د-و and د-ع-و — **all of these were transmitted as relations set up by the lexicographers**, not offered as settled etymological rulings.
- **The elisions in the orthography of the codex** (*yadʿu*, *al-dāʿī*, *tughni*) were recorded as data of the language; **no meaning, number or letter-computation whatsoever was drawn from them.** `USLUP.md`'s prohibition on abjad/letter computation was preserved.
- **No claim of a miracle was drawn from the unity of the verse-endings;** it was stated explicitly that rhyme of this kind is a widespread technique in Arabic poetry and rhymed prose.
