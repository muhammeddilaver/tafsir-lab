# 95. Sūrat al-Tīn

Eight verses. The sūra opens with a sequence of oaths three verses long, in the fourth verse issues a ruling about man, in the fifth turns that ruling on its head, in the sixth opens a door, and in the last two binds the one addressed to the reckoning.

The two densest sentences the Qurʾān says about man are in this sūra, and they come one after the other: **"We created man in the finest stature"** and **"then We turned him back to the lowest of the low."** Between the two sentences there is not even a connective — only *thumma*, that is, an interval of time or of rank.

The whole matter of the sūra is that interval.

### The name of the sūra

It takes its name from its first word: **al-Tīn**, the fig. The word occurs nowhere else in the Qurʾān. The sūra is thus named by a word that occurs only once in the whole Qurʾān.

In the sources the sūra is also found named by its full opening formula, *Wa'l-tīn*. In the early period sūras were referred to not by official names but by their opening words; this usage is a trace of that.

### Meccan or Medinan

The majority say Meccan. A view holding it Medinan is also transmitted, and its ground is that the phrase "this secure land" in the third verse looks as though it were said from outside Mecca — if the one who says "this" is not there, then the pointing is being done from a distance.

That ground is weak, because in Arabic *hādhā* also points to what is present to the mind; moreover the sūra's short verse-endings, its sharp rhyme and its direct man–creation–reckoning axis are the character of the early Meccan sūras. But I issue no firm ruling; this distinction does not change how the sūra is understood.

### The skeleton of the sūra

| Section | Verses | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Oath (*qasam*) | 1-3 | An oath by four things |
| The answer to the oath | 4 | The ruling in man's creation |
| The counter-ruling | 5 | The fall |
| Exception | 6 | Those left outside the fall |
| The concluding question | 7 | "So then…" |
| The close | 8 | The ruling bound to its owner |

The structural resemblance to Sūrat al-ʿAṣr is plain: oath → a negative ruling about man → exception. The differences that come out when the two sūras are compared explain both the better; I shall return to this at the sixth verse.

The basmala was treated in earlier sections and is not taken up again here; the verse numbers are counted without it.

---

## The four oaths: fruits or places?

The oldest and most fruitful debate on this sūra is in its first three verses, and before taking the verses one by one the frame of the debate has to be set up — because the frame determines how the words are to be read.

The four things sworn by are these: **the fig**, **the olive**, **Mount Sīnīn**, **this secure land**.

The last two members of the series are indisputably **places**. Ṭūr is a mountain; "this secure land" is a city. But what are the first two?

**The first reading — actual fruits.** The fig and the olive are exactly what they appear to be: two plants, two crops. This reading rests on the dictionary sense of the words and needs no further assumption.

**The second reading — place names / centres of revelation.** The fig and the olive stand for the region in which they grow: the Syria–Palestine country. That region is the land of the chain of prophets running from Abraham (Ibrāhīm) to Jesus (ʿĪsā). Sinai is the mountain on which Moses (Mūsā) received revelation. "This secure land" is the city to which the revelation came down to Muhammad. The four members are thus the four stations of revelation, and the series moves in historical order.

**The third reading — the combining one.** The words are both fruits and signs; the language of the Qurʾān carries both layers at once.

### Which reading is the more consistent?

This is genuinely a question that can be decided, and the grounds have to be laid out openly.

| | The fruit reading | The place reading |
|---|---|---|
| The unity of the series | The first two members are plants, the last two places — the series changes kind within itself | All four are places; the series is of one kind |
| The function of the *wāw*s | An oath by four separate things, each on its own | A consecutive oath by four things of one kind |
| The link between oath and answer | The link between the fig and "the finest stature" stays indirect | There is a direct link between the centres of revelation and "man's stature and his fall": what keeps man's stature is revelation |
| From the side of the language | Direct; requires no added assumption | No settled usage can be shown in Arabic for the words *tīn* and *zaytūn* as names of a region |
| Support from within the Qurʾān | The olive occurs elsewhere as an actual tree | *Ṭūr* and *balad* occur in the Qurʾān always as actual places |

**My assessment — and I state that it is not binding:** the internal consistency of the series supports the second reading. If in a four-membered oath series the last two members are certainly places, reading the first two as places as well makes the series of one kind; and it establishes a direct link between the answering part of the sūra (man's rise and his fall) and the history of revelation. That link cannot be established on the fruit reading — the fig's connection with man's stature can only be made by forcing it.

But **the weak side of the second reading is real too and must not be concealed**: we know of no settled naming in Arabic such as "the fig region" or "the olive region". This reading is a conclusion drawn from the logic of the series; it does not rest on a usage recorded in the language. The first reading, then, is the closer to the language, the second the closer to the structure of the text.

So the most honest thing to say is this: **both readings are possible and neither invalidates the other.** In treating the verses below I keep both of them in place.

One more thing has to be added: those who defend the "place reading" often make a mistake — distributing the four members one to one among four prophets and presenting that as certain. The Qurʾān does not make that distribution. Even if the place reading is accepted, which region stands for which prophet is interpretation, not transmitted report.

## 95/1 — وَٱلتِّينِ وَٱلزَّيْتُونِ

*Wa'l-tīni wa'l-zaytūn*
"By the fig and by the olive."

### The particle of the oath

The **وَ** at the head is not a conjunction but the particle of the oath; it was treated in detail in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr. The *wāw* at the head of the second word, on the other hand, is the *wāw* of conjunction: the oath is established once, and the second member is attached to it.

The question of God's swearing by a created thing was likewise taken up in the section on al-ʿAṣr; I do not repeat it here. Let me only recall the criterion I found strongest there: **the thing sworn by is the witness to the ruling that is about to be uttered.** That criterion will work here too — one has to ask what the four things sworn by testify about man's stature and his fall.

### ٱلتِّين — tīn

**Root: ت-ي-ن.** The word's family of derivatives in Arabic is thin; *tīn* is a noun that stands almost on its own. This is usual with the names of fruits.

The word **occurs only here in the Qurʾān.** A hapax legomenon.

The fig is one of the oldest cultivated plants of the Mediterranean basin. Archaeobotanical research records finds suggesting that the domesticated form of the fig may reach back in the Near East to dates earlier than wheat and barley; this is a debated matter in the history of agriculture, and I make no claim about a definite ordering. What is certain is this: the fig is among the oldest and most familiar trees of this country.

**A line has to be drawn here.** In the exegetical literature, and especially in popular writing, long accounts circulate about the nutritional value of the fig: it contains this vitamin, it is good for that illness, and therefore the verse is announcing the fact. This method is wrong and will not be used in this text. The verse says nothing about the contents of the fig. That a thing is sworn by is not a claim about its chemical composition.

What is genuinely remarkable about the fig lies elsewhere: **that it is a fruit which can be eaten both fresh and dried, can be kept a long time, and is eaten whole, from seed to skin.** There is no part of it to be picked out. Dried figs were a portable food in the caravan economy. In that world, then, the fig was not an ordinary member of the category "fruit" but a privileged product in respect of being storable and portable. This is not something the verse says; it is information about the object the word points to.

### ٱلزَّيْتُون — zaytūn

**Root: ز-ي-ت.** From the same root comes **zayt** (oil). What is striking is this: in Arabic the general name for oil and the name of the olive derive from the same root. In this culture, that is, the first thing that comes to mind at the word "oil" is olive oil; the word has recorded it.

*Zaytūn* is the name of both the tree and the fruit.

The olive, unlike *tīn*, occurs in several places in the Qurʾān, and the places where it occurs give the word weight:

- *"With it He brings forth for you crops, olives, date palms, grapes and fruit of every kind"* (al-Naḥl 16/11) — in a list of favours.
- *"and the olive and the pomegranate, alike and unlike…"* (al-Anʿām 6/99; similarly 6/141) — in the context of the variety of creation.
- *"Vineyards and gardens, olives and date palms"* (ʿAbasa 80/28-29) — in the enumeration of provision.

But the real occurrence is **al-Nūr 24/35**:

> *"…kindled from a blessed tree, an olive neither of the east nor of the west, whose oil would almost give light though no fire had touched it…"*

This verse stands inside one of the densest similitudes in the Qurʾān — the verse of light — and there the olive tree is called **"blessed"**. This is not an incidental attribute: blessing, by the sense in its root, is a thing's continuing steadily and increasingly. The olive tree lives for hundreds of years and goes on bearing as it grows old; this is the concrete ground for giving it the word.

The qualification **"neither of the east nor of the west"** in the verse of light is meaningful too. In the classical explanations this phrase is taken to mean that the tree stands in an open place that takes the sun all day — that is, it is not left in shade for half the day; the oil of such a tree is purer. I transmit this as an observation about cultivation.

It is possible to draw a link between the olive's being the subject of an oath in this sūra and its occurring in the context of "light" in Sūrat al-Nūr: olive oil is the substance that opens the darkness in that world. It is the fuel of the lamp. I record this not as a ruling of exegesis but as an association the word carries.

### The two fruits standing side by side

Both are the produce of trees, both are among the basic plants of this country, both can be stored. Their being named together is not a customary pairing in the language — nowhere else in the Qurʾān do these two occur together.

This is the strongest support of those who defend the place reading: **had the aim been to enumerate favours, one would have expected the date-and-grape pair that is always named together in the Qurʾān's lists of favours.** In enumerating provision the Qurʾān names dates and grapes side by side many times (for example al-Naḥl 16/67, al-Muʾminūn 23/19). Here it is not those but another pair that has been chosen. That choice is a sign — what it is a sign of is the disputed part.

## 95/2 — وَطُورِ سِينِينَ

*Wa ṭūri sīnīn*
"By Mount Sīnīn."

### طُور — ṭūr

It means "mountain"; but it is not a word native to Arabic. The majority of the lexicographers accept that the word comes from the Syriac/Aramaic **ṭūr** (mountain). In the Qurʾān this word is used almost always for **one particular mountain**, the mountain on which Moses received revelation.

That a word of foreign origin should be used precisely for the mountain of a foreign prophet is worth noticing. In relating an event outside the Arab country, the Qurʾān takes that country's own word.

### سِينِين — Sīnīn

There is a question of grammar and of reading here.

The Qurʾān names the same mountain elsewhere in a different form:

> *"A tree that comes forth from Mount Sīnāʾ, bringing forth oil and a relish for those who eat…"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/20)

There **طور سيناء** (*Ṭūri Sīnāʾ*), here **طور سينين** (*Ṭūri Sīnīn*). The same place, two different forms.

There are two explanations for this, and they do not exclude one another:

**The first — two forms in the language.** The word came into Arabic from outside, and it is usual for proper names of foreign origin to acquire more than one pronunciation in Arabic. The classical lexicographers record several ways of saying such names.

**The second — the requirement of the verse-ending (rhyme).** Every verse of the sūra ends in the pattern **-īn / -īm**: *zaytūn, sīnīn, amīn, taqwīm, sāfilīn, mamnūn, dīn, ḥākimīn.* A long vowel followed by a nasal *nūn* or *mīm*. A sound unbroken across eight verses. The form *Sīnāʾ* would not enter this series; *Sīnīn* does.

This second explanation shows the importance the Qurʾān gives to its order of sound. But this has to be added as well: the meaning does not change for the sake of the sound — the mountain meant is the same mountain, and only one of the two forms of its name recorded in Arabic is being used.

What the word *Sīnīn* means has been debated separately: senses such as "blessed", "wooded", "beautiful" are transmitted. These explanations do not agree with one another and none of them is certain. The word is in all likelihood not an Arabic adjective but a part of a place name; it is not necessary to look for a meaning.

### Why Sinai?

Sinai is, in the Qurʾān's narrative, **the place where revelation was given directly**. Moses was called there, was made to speak there, received the tablets there. The Qurʾān makes this mountain the subject of an oath in another sūra as well (Sūrat al-Ṭūr 52/1).

That a mountain should be named as a centre of revelation is the most concrete datum strengthening the place reading of the series: Ṭūr is not a fruit and cannot be; and it is identified beyond dispute with a prophet. In the middle of the series, that is, stands a "place of revelation" that nobody disputes.

## 95/3 — وَهَٰذَا ٱلْبَلَدِ ٱلْأَمِينِ

*Wa hādhā'l-baladi'l-amīn*
"And by this secure land."

### هَٰذَا — "this"

The most striking word in the series. The first two oaths were made with common nouns (*the fig, the olive*), the third with the name of a particular mountain, and the fourth with **a demonstrative pronoun**.

*Hādhā* in Arabic indicates what is near. What is indicated is therefore either beside the speaker or present in the mind of the one addressed. This separates the last member of the series from the other three: the fig and the olive are far off, Sinai is very far off, **but this land is where the foot is standing.**

We saw the same demonstrative used of the House in Sūrat Quraysh: *"let them serve the Lord of this House"* (Quraysh 106/3). The two uses do the same work: taking the evidence from directly in front of the eyes of the one addressed. Sūrat al-Fīl had the same method — instead of distant peoples, the history of the addressee's own city.

There is also the direction of the series. The series comes **from far to near**: the Palestine–Syria region → Sinai → Mecca. Geographically as well as historically, a movement narrowing towards the point where the one addressed stands. For those who accept the place reading this is the strongest proof of all; because four things chosen at random would not be expected to form such a direction.

### ٱلْبَلَد — balad

**Root: ب-ل-د.** The original sense of the root is a place's having definite boundaries, an area's being marked off from what surrounds it. *Balad*: a settled place, a town, a country. Its plural is *bilād*. From the same root Turkish has borrowed *belediye* — the municipality, the body that runs the affairs inside a city's boundaries.

The word has one further interesting derivative: **balīd** — a heavy, sluggish, unresponsive person. The sense of "being fixed and bound to a place" in the root turns to the negative when it is applied to a human being. I record this as a nicety and do not include it in the meaning of the verse.

Here the definite article (*al-*) and the demonstrative come together: *hādhā'l-balada*. Not "a city", then, but "that known city". The name is not given, but it is pointed at.

This is a method the Qurʾān often uses for Mecca. In Sūrat al-Fīl too the name of the city did not appear; the event was told, the place was not shown. There is no need to give a name — the one addressed is there.

### ٱلْأَمِين — amīn

**Root: ء-م-ن.** The wide analysis of this root was done in Sūrat Quraysh: *amn* (safety), *amān* (inviolability), *amāna* (what is left in trust), *amīn* (trustworthy) and *īmān* all come from the same root. I do not repeat the criterion recorded there; I add to it.

The question here is this: **how is the word *amīn* predicated of the city as an attribute?**

In Arabic the *faʿīl* pattern can be used in an active sense (active participle) and in a passive sense (passive participle) alike. Two readings therefore arise:

| Reading | Meaning | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Passive**: a land given safety, a protected land | The city has been brought to safety; those inside it are safe | The affair of the Elephant, the inviolability of the sanctuary, "He secured them against fear" in Quraysh 106/4 |
| **Active**: a land that gives safety, that makes whoever enters it secure | The city gives safety to whoever takes refuge in it | The actual function of the sanctuary: even a man with a blood claim upon him was inviolable there |
| **Reciprocal**: a land faithful to what is entrusted to it, a land that is trusted | The place where a person leaves himself and which does not betray him | By analogy with the use of *amīn* of a person |

All three have been defended in the classical sources and in practice they lie close to one another. In my view the second reading sits best with the context of the sūra, because the verse seems to bring forward not something that **has happened to** the city but something the city **does**. This is a preference; it is not binding.

### "The secure land" — the Qurʾān's own testimony

This expression meets us in other places in the Qurʾān as well, and all of them point to the same city:

- *"My Lord, make this a secure land (**baladan āminan**) and provide its people with fruits"* (al-Baqara 2/126) — Abraham's prayer.
- *"My Lord, make this land secure (**hādhā'l-balada āminan**)"* (Ibrāhīm 14/35) — the same prayer, in almost the same words.
- *"Have they not seen that We made an inviolable and secure place (**ḥaraman āminan**) while people are snatched away all around them?"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/67).
- *"Have We not settled them in a secure sanctuary to which the fruits of all things are brought?"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/57).

These occurrences say something: **the safety of the city is a thing that was asked for in a prayer and given.** It did not come about of itself, it is not the gift of geography, and it is certainly not the achievement of Quraysh.

This was exactly the matter treated in Sūrat Quraysh: the city's trade and its safety were not their own work, and for that reason the one to whom thanks were owed was not themselves but the Lord of the House. Sūrat al-Tīn now makes the same city the subject of an oath. The two sūras complete one another: one states the **source** of the city's safety, the other calls that safety as a **witness**.

One further connection: **Sūrat al-Balad (90) swears by the same city, with the same word, but in exactly the opposite tone.** There it says *"I swear by this land — and you are dwelling in this land / it will be made lawful to you"*, and immediately after comes *"We created man in hardship."* In al-Tīn "the finest stature" follows "the secure land"; in al-Balad "hardship" follows the same land. The two sūras set two different descriptions of man beside the same city. I shall come back to this pairing in a separate section at the end of the sūra.

## 95/4 — لَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ فِىٓ أَحْسَنِ تَقْوِيمٍ

*Laqad khalaqna'l-insāna fī aḥsani taqwīm*
"Truly, We created man in the finest stature."

The answer to the oath. The three verses of oath had been set up for this sentence.

### لَقَدْ — a double emphasis

The sentence opens with two particles of emphasis: **لَ** (the *lām* that comes on the answer to an oath) and **قَدْ** (the particle that conveys certainty at the head of a perfect verb). *Qad* + the perfect verifies that the act has taken place.

This pattern marks grammatically that the sentence is the answer to the oath: in Arabic, when an oath is set up, its answer is expected to be attached with *lām* or with *inna*. The weight of the oaths in the first three verses, that is, falls directly upon this sentence.

In Sūrat al-ʿAṣr the same work was done by *inna* + *lām*: *inna'l-insāna la-fī khusr*. Here it is *laqad* + the verb. The two sūras build the same structure with two different instruments.

### خَلَقْنَا — "We created"

**Root: خ-ل-ق.** The concrete sense of the root is to give a thing measure, to shape it, to cut and fit it. From the same root come *khalq* (creation), *khuluq / akhlāq* (disposition — that is, man's inner making), *khallāq* (the one who creates much).

That *akhlāq* and *khalq* come from the same root is especially meaningful for this verse: in Arabic the name of the outward form and the name of the inner character derive from one root. When it is debated which of the two "creating in the finest stature" looks to, this fact about the language must be kept in mind — the root already carries both at once.

**The verb is plural.** Not "I created" but "We created". In Arabic this is the plural of majesty (*taʿẓīm*): a single agent speaking of himself in the mode of greatness. The Qurʾān uses this mode particularly with verbs that convey power — creating, sending down, sending forth.

### ٱلْإِنسَٰن — al-insān

The analysis of this word was done in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr: the two views about its root (*ʾ-n-s* — familiarity; *n-s-y* — to forget), and the definite article's covering the whole of the kind (*istighrāq*).

The article here is all-inclusive too, and the proof of that from inside the text is again the same: **the exception in the sixth verse.** An exception can only be made from a general ruling.

But there is something here different from al-ʿAṣr, and the difference matters. In al-ʿAṣr "al-insān" was the subject of a **negative** ruling: he is in loss. Here he is the object of a **positive** one: he was created in the finest stature. The same word, in the same scope, carries two opposite rulings.

This is not a contradiction but the Qurʾān's two-layered description of man: **high in respect of his creation, at a loss in respect of his course.** Sūrat al-Tīn gives these two layers in a single sūra, in two consecutive verses — which is why the sūra is the most compressed text of the Qurʾān's conception of man.

### فِى — "in"

The verse does not say "man **as** the finest stature" or "**with** the finest stature". It says **"*inside* the finest stature"**.

*Fī* conveys adverbial containment: being encompassed, being inside something. We saw the same particle used with *khusr* (loss) in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr: *la-fī khusr* — inside loss.

Set the two uses side by side:

- *fī aḥsani taqwīm* — inside the finest stature
- *la-fī khusr* — inside loss

**The same particle, two opposite surroundings.** That man can be inside both a stature and a loss is what these two sūras say together. And the choice of *fī* says this: neither of them is an event that befalls man; both are **states he is inside**.

A grammatical note: some lexicographers read the *fī* here in the sense of "with" and understand the sentence as "We created man with the finest stature". This reading too is possible, but it moves the particle away from its original sense; the adverbial reading is the stronger.

### تَقْوِيم — taqwīm

The key word of the sūra.

**Root: ق-و-م.** The concrete sense of this root is **to stand, to be set upright, to be made straight**. The analysis of the root was done in al-Fātiḥa by way of *mustaqīm* (the straight path, the path that stands upright) and in al-Baqara by way of *iqāma* (holding the prayer standing, setting it up); I rely on that treatment, and add here only what belongs to this word.

The derivatives of the root say the following:

- **qiyām** — to rise to one's feet; the part of the prayer that is performed standing
- **qawm** — a community that stands together (those who stand together)
- **maqām** — the place where one stands
- **istiqāma** — going upright and straight
- **qīma** — the amount that **stands** as the equivalent of a thing; its value
- **qiyāma** — the day on which everyone will rise
- **qayyūm** — the one who himself stands and holds everything standing

These last two derivatives are striking. **Qīma** and **qiwām** come from the same root: the value of a thing is what it can stand as the equivalent of. And **al-Qayyūm** is a name of God (al-Baqara 2/255). Man's being created "in the finest stature", that is, is bound at the level of the root to God's being the one who holds standing.

**Pattern: تَفْعِيل (tafʿīl).** This pattern in Arabic conveys causation and intensity. *Qāma* (he rose to his feet) → *qawwama* (he straightened, set right, set up, appraised) → *taqwīm* (straightening, setting right, giving form).

The concrete use of the word is this: to straighten a crooked thing, to true a rod, to bring a structure into a state where it stands upright. From here it widens in two directions: **to give form** and **to set a value**. (Turkish *takvim*, the calendar — the setting in order of the days of the year — is the same word; its sense narrowed later.)

### What does "the finest stature" mean? The disagreement

**أَحْسَن** (aḥsan) is the elative (*ism al-tafḍīl*) from the root *ḥ-s-n*: "more / most beautiful". *Ḥusn* in Arabic conveys both aesthetic beauty and fittingness, aptness, serviceability for a task. So *aḥsanu taqwīm* may be not only "the most splendid appearance" but also **"the most serviceable order"**.

The exegetes differ over what this construct looks to:

| View | What is meant | Ground | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bodily form** | Man's physical build: the proportion of the limbs, symmetry, the face | The concrete sense of *taqwīm* is to give form; elsewhere too the Qurʾān says *"He shaped you and made your shapes beautiful"* (al-Taghābun 64/3) | Taken on its own it makes no link with the rest of the sūra: bodily beauty has no direct bearing on "falling to the lowest of the low" |
| **Upright posture** | Man's standing on two feet with his head above | The original sense of the root is directly this (*qiyām* = standing upright); the most visible difference separating him from the other living creatures | The Qurʾān does not stress this difference openly elsewhere; the inference comes from outside the text |
| **Capacity and equipment** | Intellect, language, discernment, learning, the ability to bear responsibility | The rest of the sūra speaks of a moral fall; a fall is only meaningful through the misuse of a capacity | The dictionary sense of the word *taqwīm* is primarily form |
| **The order of the whole** | The order that body, intellect and moral capacity set up together | The root *khalq* covering both the outward form and the disposition (*akhlāq*); the sense of "fittingness" in *ḥusn* | Being general, it is not distinguishing |

**My assessment — it is not binding:** the fourth reading is the soundest, because it does not exclude the other three; but what is decisive from the point of view of the sūra's internal logic is the third dimension. The reason is this: the next verse speaks of a **fall**. For there to be a fall there must be a height, and for the falling to have anything to do with man's own part there must be a **capacity**. Bodily form does not fall; capacity falls.

The root itself supports this. *Taqwīm* is bringing a thing **into a state where it stands upright**. A thing that stands upright is a thing that can fall. A thing lying flat does not fall. A fragility, then, is already recorded inside the word: what can stand can also be toppled.

I record this as my own reading, not as a classical transmitted report. But it is a possibility the root carries, and it agrees exactly with the fifth verse of the sūra.

### تَقْوِيمٍ — its coming indefinite

The second member of the construct has tanwīn, that is, it is indefinite: *aḥsani taqwīmin* — "the finest of a stature". In Arabic the indefiniteness here conveys magnification (*taʿẓīm*): an order that cannot be described, whose extent cannot be drawn.

One further possibility of interpretation arises, and I offer it as a possibility: the construct may be read not as "the finest of all statures" but as "the finest state of a stature". On this reading the verse does not compare man with all beings and award him the first place; it says **that man was made complete within his own kind**. Not a claim to be "the highest of created things", then, but the finding that he was "equipped with everything necessary for himself".

This distinction is not unimportant. The first is a declaration of superiority and lifts man above the other beings; the second is a declaration of responsibility and calls man to account out of his own equipment. What follows in the sūra accords better with the second: he had everything in hand, and he fell all the same.

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

The care taken in man's creation is treated in the Qurʾān with several different words:

- *"It is He who made beautiful everything He created, and began the creation of man from clay… Then He set him right and breathed into him of His spirit; and He gave you hearing, sight and hearts"* (al-Sajda 32/7-9). Here "making beautiful" (*aḥsana*) and the enumeration of the senses that follows it stand together.
- *"He shaped you and made your shapes beautiful"* (al-Taghābun 64/3; similarly Ghāfir 40/64).
- *"O man! What has deceived you about your generous Lord? He who created you, ordered you, balanced you; He assembled you in whatever form He willed"* (al-Infiṭār 82/6-8). These verses are structurally very close to Sūrat al-Tīn: first the care taken in creation, then a question.
- *"Then We brought him into being as another creation. So blessed be God, the finest of creators"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/14). Here *aḥsan* comes this time as an attribute of God: the finest of those who create.

The last example sets a measure: man's being "in the finest stature" is **the consequence of the Creator's being "the finest of creators".** Beauty is not man's own attribute but a trace left upon him.

The question in al-Infiṭār 82/6 has to be noted as well: *"What has deceived you about your Lord?"* That question is in the same pattern as the one in the seventh verse of Sūrat al-Tīn, *"So what makes you deny the dīn after this?"*: a "what" question asked once the care taken in creation has been enumerated.

## 95/5 — ثُمَّ رَدَدْنَٰهُ أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِينَ

*Thumma radadnāhu asfala sāfilīn*
"Then We turned him back to the lowest of the low."

The most disputed verse of the sūra.

### ثُمَّ — thumma

In Arabic there are three particles that convey sequence, and the difference between them matters:

- **وَ (wāw)** — conveys no order, it only adds.
- **فَ (fāʾ)** — immediately afterwards; following without a gap.
- **ثُمَّ (thumma)** — following **with a gap**; there is a distance between the two events.

The verse uses not *fāʾ* but *thumma*. It is not saying "We created, and immediately afterwards We brought him down"; **there is an interval in between.**

What is this interval? Two readings:

**An interval of time.** Man was created, a stretch of time passed, then he fell. This reading counts the "fall" as something that takes place within life.

**An interval of rank (*thumma'l-rutba*).** In Arabic *thumma* sometimes conveys not time but the distance between two rulings: "and what is more". On this reading the verse would be saying: *We created man in the finest stature — and, astonishingly, We turned him back to the lowest of the low.* The distance between them is not chronological but logical.

The second reading carries the tension between the two verses better. What is astonishing about the sūra is in any case not the order of events but **that two opposite rulings can be issued about the same being.**

### رَدَدْنَا — "We turned back"

**Root: ر-د-د.** Its original sense is **to turn back, to give back, to send back.** From the same root come *radd* (turning back), *irtidād* (turning back on one's tracks), *mardūd* (turned back).

The choice of the word is striking. The verse does not say "We **cast** him down" or "We **brought** him down"; it says **"We turned him back"**.

The verb *radd* conveys a thing's being returned to a place or a state in which it was before. This has a direct effect on the meaning of the verse: turning back is an act of **restitution**. Man had been taken from somewhere; he is being sent back there.

Which "somewhere" that is stands at the very centre of the disagreement below. On the old-age reading what is returned to is **the helplessness of childhood**; on the earth reading, **the earth he was brought out of**; on the moral reading, **an animal level at which the capacity is not used**.

The Qurʾān uses this verb openly in the context of old age:

> *"And some of you are **turned back** (*yuraddu ilā ardhali'l-ʿumur*) to the most abject time of life, so that after having known he comes to know nothing"* (al-Naḥl 16/70; almost the same expression in al-Ḥajj 22/5).

This is the closest kin within the Qurʾān of the verb *radadnā* in our verse, and it is the strongest support of the old-age reading. In both places the same root, the same direction: a turning back from above downwards.

### أَسْفَلَ سَٰفِلِين — asfala sāfilīn

**Root: س-ف-ل.** To be low, to sink. Its opposite is *ʿuluww* (height). From the same root Turkish has borrowed *süflî* (belonging to what is below).

The construct is made of two parts:

- **أَسْفَل** — the elative: "lower / lowest"
- **سَافِلِين** — the plural of the active participle: "those who are low"

Word for word, then: **"the lowest of those who are low."** The English "the lowest of the low" carries this well.

**An observation about the structure.** This construct is **in exactly the same form** as **أَحْكَمِ ٱلْحَاكِمِينَ** (the most judicious of judges) in the last verse of the sūra: elative + a plural active participle from the same root. The sūra uses one and the same grammatical pattern at its two ends — one for the lowest point man can fall to, the other for God's position in issuing judgement. I record this as my own observation; it is a symmetry that stands in the text.

That the word *sāfilīn* is **the plural proper to rational beings** (the sound masculine plural) must be noted as well. In Arabic this plural form is generally used for human beings and possessors of intellect. What "the low" means, then, is not stone and earth but **beings that have fallen**. This is a linguistic datum that strengthens the moral reading of the verse.

### What is the fall? The disagreement

In classical exegesis several distinct views have gathered on this verse:

| View | What it means | Ground | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Old age / the time of decrepitude** | Man is created strong and upright, and at the end of his life grows weak and loses his knowledge | The verb *radda* being used in exactly this sense in al-Naḥl 16/70 and al-Ḥajj 22/5; the sense of restitution in the verb "to turn back" | Old age is not a fault, and the believer grows old too; in that case the exception in verse 6 loses its sense (treated below) |
| **Hell** | The place he is brought down to is in the hereafter; the lowest level | The Qurʾān names the Fire with the word *asfal*: *"The hypocrites are in the lowest level of the Fire"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/145) | The verb is perfect (past tense); for the hereafter a future would be expected. Besides, the context of the sūra is the life of this world |
| **Moral abasement** | Man, by not using the equipment given him, sinks to an animal level | The exception in verse 6 being built out of faith and deed; *sāfilīn* being the plural proper to possessors of intellect | The sense of "turning back" in the verb *radda* is weakened on this reading |
| **The lower layer of creation** | The earth man was brought out of / the world-bound side of the body | The sense of restitution in *radd*; the Qurʾān's expression *"from it We created you, and to it We shall return you"* (Ṭāhā 20/55) | It makes no link with the moral frame of the sūra |

### Which view does the exception support?

The key to this debate is in the next verse, and the matter can be settled at the level of grammar.

The sixth verse says *"except those who believe and do righteous deeds"*. Now the question is this: **what are those excepted being left outside of?**

**If the fall is old age**, the exception cannot be "connected" (*muttaṣil*) — because the believer grows old too, and he too grows weak. In that case the exception has to be counted "disconnected" (*munqaṭiʿ*): that is, *illā* here means "but, however", and the sentence is read: *"…but as for those who believe and do righteous deeds, for them there is a reward unceasing."* They too grow old, that is, but their old age does not cut off the return of their deeds.

There is a grammatical datum supporting this reading: **in the verse a فَ (fāʾ) comes after the exception** — *fa-lahum ajrun*. In Sūrat al-ʿAṣr there was no *fāʾ* after the exception. The presence of the *fāʾ* shows that the sentence moves close to a condition-and-answer relation, and it makes the disconnected reading of the exception easier.

**If the fall is moral abasement**, the exception is straightforwardly connected: the human kind is falling, these are not falling. The sentence reads more fluently, but the presence of the *fāʾ* is left a little in excess.

**My assessment — it is not binding:** both are readings the text can bear and they need not be forced to a choice. From the point of view of the sūra as a whole the moral reading is the more fruitful, because at its end the sūra is bound to the reckoning and to judgement, and old age is not a matter of reckoning. But the support the old-age reading has in the verb *radda* is serious and must not be taken lightly.

This too has to be added: the views do not exclude one another. Man's growing decrepit in body and his sinking morally can be read as two faces of one and the same reality — both are a moving away from "the finest stature". The word is wide enough to carry both, and the verse does not narrow it.

### Whose act?

One point to notice: **the subject of the act of bringing down is God as well.** It is not said "man fell"; it is said "We turned him back". The act of creating and the act of bringing down belong to the same subject, in the same plural mode: *khalaqnā… radadnāhu.*

This does not mean that the verse frees man from responsibility — what follows in the sūra establishes responsibility openly. What it says is more basic: **both the rise and the fall take place inside an order.** Man neither built his own stature nor laid down the rules of the fall. This is the language the Qurʾān prefers in places of this kind: the acts are ascribed to God, the responsibility is laid on man. The relation between the two is one of the longest debates in the history of theology, and I take no side here.

## 95/6 — إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ فَلَهُمْ أَجْرٌ غَيْرُ مَمْنُونٍ

*Illā'lladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilu'ṣ-ṣāliḥāti fa-lahum ajrun ghayru mamnūn*
"Except those who believe and do righteous deeds; for them there is a reward unceasing."

### Comparison with Sūrat al-ʿAṣr

This verse is in the same pattern as al-ʿAṣr 103/3, and setting the two texts side by side explains both.

| | **al-ʿAṣr 103/3** | **al-Tīn 95/6** |
|---|---|---|
| Particle of exception | *illā* | *illā* |
| 1st condition | faith | faith |
| 2nd condition | righteous deed | righteous deed |
| 3rd condition | enjoining the truth | — |
| 4th condition | enjoining patience | — |
| After the exception | it ends directly | *fāʾ* + the announcement of the return |
| The return | not stated | *ajrun ghayru mamnūn* |

There are two differences and both are meaningful.

**The first: four conditions in al-ʿAṣr, two in al-Tīn.** This does not mean that the two texts contradict one another. The ruling the two sūras set up is different: al-ʿAṣr takes as its subject **man's balance-sheet within time**, and there the social dimension is a necessary item — because staying on one's feet within time is not possible alone. Al-Tīn takes as its subject **man's position in his creation**, and there the minimum dividing line is faith and deed.

Even so, one observation is worth recording: the Qurʾān's description of "those who are saved" is not a fixed formula. Sometimes two conditions are enumerated, sometimes four, sometimes — as we shall see at al-Balad 90/17 — three. The common core is always faith and deed; what is added on top varies with the subject of the sūra. **This is not looseness in the formula but the description working together with its context.** To take one list as absolute and reduce the others to it goes against the text's own method.

**The second: al-Tīn states the return, al-ʿAṣr does not.** In al-ʿAṣr what those excepted will receive is not specified; it is only said that they are outside the loss. In al-Tīn there is a promise of a reward. This shows the difference in tone between the two sūras: al-ʿAṣr is a text of balance-sheet, al-Tīn a text of creation and reckoning.

### ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ

The root analysis of these two verbs was done in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr: the link of the root *ʾ-m-n* with safety, and the sense of "repairing" in the root *ṣ-l-ḥ* — that a righteous deed is not a "good work" but a "repairing work".

The only thing to be added here is the particular sense this description takes on in the context of the sūra. The fifth verse spoke of a **corruption**: a turning back downwards from the finest stature. The sixth verse says *ṣāliḥāt*, that is, **works that repair**. The opposite of the root is *fasād*, corruption.

So the sūra sets this up: **what is placed against corruption is repair.** The choice of words is not accidental; there is an opposition at the level of the root between the two verses — fall and corruption against repair. I record this link as my own reading, but the meanings of the two roots stand there in the text.

### أَجْر — ajr

**Root: ء-ج-ر.** Its original sense is **the return for work done, a wage.** From the same root come *ījār* (letting out for hire), *ujra* (wage), *ajīr* (a hired worker). Turkish *icar* (lease) and *ücret* (wage) come from this root.

The word is an economic word and this must not be passed over: an *ajr* is not a gift but **an earned return**. The Qurʾān uses other words too for merit (*thawāb*, *jazāʾ*, *ḥasana*); that it chooses here in particular the word "wage" is to say, in the language of a work relationship, that what has been done will not be left without return.

The word comes **indefinite**: *ajrun* — "a reward". Again it conveys magnification: a return whose amount is not stated, whose frame is not drawn.

### غَيْرُ مَمْنُونٍ — ghayru mamnūn

**Root: م-ن-ن.** This root is used in Arabic in two distinct directions, and both have been read into this construct:

**1. To cut, to diminish, to exhaust.** *Manna'l-ḥabl* — he cut the rope. In this sense *mamnūn* means "cut"; and *ghayru mamnūn* becomes **unceasing, inexhaustible**.

**2. To hold a favour over someone, to throw a kindness in his face.** *Minna* is the reminding of someone of a good turn done him; *manna ʿalayhi* — he laid an obligation upon him. In this sense *ghayru mamnūn* means **not held over one's head**.

That the root also means **to do good** (*manna* — he bestowed; in the Qurʾān God's granting of favour is expressed with this root) makes the matter interesting: in one root there is both "bounty" and "the throwing of bounty in someone's face". Arabic has recorded in the root that kindness is the thing most liable to be held over one's head.

**Which sense?** The classical exegetes mention both, and the majority prefer the first (unceasingness). The ground is this: the word occurs in three places in the Qurʾān and in all three it comes together with *ajr*:

- *"For them there is a reward unceasing"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/8)
- *"For them there is a reward unceasing"* (al-Inshiqāq 84/25)
- *"…for them there is a reward unceasing"* (al-Tīn 95/6)

In all three verses the context is the reward of the hereafter; and about the reward of the hereafter the most meaningful attribute is inexhaustibility.

But it would not be right to throw out the second sense either. **When the two are read together a very apt description emerges: a return that is neither cut off nor held over one's head.** No wage in this world carries both these conditions at once — every wage has an end and every kindness its obligation. The verse uses the word for a worldly wage and then removes both defects of a worldly wage at once.

I record this reading not as a preference but as the result the two senses give together.

## 95/7 — فَمَا يُكَذِّبُكَ بَعْدُ بِٱلدِّينِ

*Fa-mā yukadhdhibuka baʿdu bi'l-dīn*
"So then what, after this, makes you deny the dīn?"

The most disputed sentence of the sūra. Both the meaning of the word and the direction of the address are matters of disagreement, and the two are bound to one another.

### فَ — the particle of consequence

The *fāʾ* at the head announces a consequence of the preceding verses. The sentence, that is, sits on top of an argument that has been built: the stature in creation was set out, the fall was set out, those who are saved were enumerated — **so then**.

### بَعْدُ — "after this"

The word comes **with a ḍamma and without tanwīn** (*baʿdu*). This marks a special situation in Arabic: *baʿda* normally requires a construct ("after such and such"). When the second member drops away and its sense is known in the mind, the word is fixed upon the ḍamma (*mabnī ʿalā'l-ḍamm*).

The verse, that is, does not say "after what" — but it has no need to say it, because what comes "after" is the thing that has just been set out: **the proof in his own creation.**

This small point of grammar carries the whole weight of the verse. The sentence comes to mean: *with all this in plain view, still.*

### مَا — a question or a negation?

| Reading | The meaning of the sentence | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Interrogative (*istifhām*)** | "After this, what makes you deny the dīn?" | The movement of the sūra is a movement of calling to account; a question opening with *fāʾ* is widespread in the Qurʾān (the pattern of al-Infiṭār 82/6) |
| **Negation (*nafy*)** | "After this, nothing can give you the lie about the dīn." | The widespread use of *mā* as a particle of negation; agreement with the tone of certainty at the sūra's close |

The majority adopt the first reading and its ground is strong: the verse has presented a proof and taken its conclusion; if the conclusion comes in the form of a question, the one addressed is obliged to answer. The Qurʾān uses this technique often.

### To whom is the address?

This is the real debate, and there are three main views:

| View | How the sentence is read | Ground | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The addressee: man (in general)** | "O man! After all this, what drives you to deny the reckoning?" | The subject of the sūra has been *al-insān* from the beginning; the verse returns to the same subject. *"What has deceived you about your Lord?"* in al-Infiṭār 82/6 is in the same pattern | In the preceding verses man was always in the third person; here there is a shift to the second person |
| **The addressee: the Prophet** | "After this, who can give you the lie about the dīn?" | In the Qurʾān the singular address "you" is mostly to the Prophet | Since the subject of the verb *yukadhdhibuka* is *mā* (what), the sense "who" is forced; *mā* is used of what has no intellect |
| **The addressee: the one who denies** | "You there, denier! After all these proofs, what still makes you deny?" | Agreement with the harsh close of the sūra | There is no indication in the text that such an addressee has been designated |

**My assessment — it is not binding:** the first reading is the most consistent. For three reasons: (a) the subject of the sūra has been man from the beginning; (b) *mā* is the interrogative particle used for beings without intellect, so what is being asked is not "who" but "what" — that is, what makes him deny is not a person but a cause; (c) in the Qurʾān the passage from the third person to the second (**iltifāt** — a shift in the direction of address) is a widespread manner, and it is used at closings of exactly this kind: a general description is made, and then the reader is seized by the collar.

*Iltifāt* performs a function here: **the sentence takes the reader out of the audience and seats him in the dock.** Up to the fifth verse the talk was about "man"; in the seventh verse the talk is now about "you".

### يُكَذِّبُ — to give the lie

**Root: ك-ذ-ب.** *Kidhb*, a lie. **The *tafʿīl* pattern** (*kadhdhaba*) here means not "to tell a lie" but **"to count a thing false, to give the lie to it"** — that is, to say of a report, "this is a lie".

The distinction matters: *kadhaba* is to tell a lie, *kadhdhaba* is to make another out a liar. The Qurʾān mostly relates denial with the second verb, because the matter is not a want of knowledge but the stance taken towards a report that has come.

It is transmitted in the sources that this verb is also read **يُكْذِبُكَ** (*yukdhibuka*, the *ifʿāl* pattern); on that reading the sense becomes "finds you a liar / makes you out a liar". This reading is not widespread and I issue no firm ruling; I confine myself to recording it.

### ٱلدِّين — dīn

**Root: د-ي-ن.** The analysis of this word was done in detail in Sūrat al-Māʿūn; the three main branches of the root (debt / *dayn*, judgement and requital, the way that is followed) and why the "day of reckoning" reading is the stronger in the context of that sūra were treated there.

The same conclusion holds here, and its ground is even plainer in this sūra: **the next verse closes directly with the words for judgement and judge.** *Dīn* and *ḥākim* lie in the same field of meaning — one the day of judgement, the other the one who gives the judgement. The two verses interpret one another.

So the sentence comes to this: *with all this in plain view, what drives you to deny that you will meet a requital?*

And this is exactly the proof the sūra has been building from the start: **it is not reasonable that a being made with this much care should be left without a reckoning.** The question in Sūrat al-Infiṭār carries the same logic. The order in creation is presented as the proof of the reckoning.

## 95/8 — أَلَيْسَ ٱللَّهُ بِأَحْكَمِ ٱلْحَٰكِمِينَ

*A-laysa'llāhu bi-aḥkami'l-ḥākimīn*
"Is God not the most judicious of judges?"

### أَلَيْسَ — a negative question

In Arabic a negative question is a strong affirmation. The same structure was seen in Sūrat al-Fīl in the pattern *a-lam tara*: "is it not?" = "of course it is so, and you know it."

This pattern places the one addressed in a position: he is not obliged to answer, because the answer is already inside the sentence. To refuse, he would have to refuse not the question but what is plain.

### بِ — the redundant bāʾ

*Bi-aḥkami* — the particle *bāʾ* here is not a particle that adds meaning; it is a "redundant" (*zāʾid*) particle that comes to reinforce the predicate of a negative sentence. In Arabic this *bāʾ*, which comes at the head of the predicate of *laysa*, increases the certainty of the negation.

It cannot be carried over into English, but it is heard: the sentence is not in the tone of "is it not?" but of "is it not indeed?".

### The root ٱلْحُكْم

**Root: ح-ك-م.** The concrete sense of the root has to be dwelt on, because the English word "judgement" has lost it.

The original sense of the root is **to hinder, to hold back, to rein in**. The clearest proof of this is in one of its derivatives: **حَكَمَة (ḥakama)** — the bit put in a horse's mouth, the piece of iron that turns the animal's head. To bit a horse is to keep it from going where it wants.

From here the whole family of the root becomes intelligible:

- **ḥukm** — to bind a matter, to bring it to a decision; to prevent arbitrariness
- **ḥikma** — the knowledge that holds a man back from error; doing a thing in its proper place
- **iḥkām** — making firm, leaving no gap
- **muḥkam** — firm, not to be shifted
- **ḥākim** — both a judge and "one who possesses wisdom"

To give judgement, then, is in the root of Arabic a work of **drawing a boundary**. The judge is the one who prevents the parties from doing what they want.

This gives the last sentence of the sūra a further meaning. The sūra spoke of a fall; the fall is the consequence of boundlessness. And the last verse announces who it is that sets the boundary.

### أَحْكَمِ ٱلْحَٰكِمِينَ

The structure: elative (*aḥkam*) + a plural active participle from the same root (*al-ḥākimīn*). "The most judging of those who judge."

**The expression has two layers:**

**The first — quantity:** the one who judges most, whose judgement stands above everyone's. The court above all courts, that is.

**When the second sense of the root is added:** the one who judges most soundly, in whose judgement there is no gap. *Aḥkam* is not "the one who gives the harshest punishment" but **"the one who decides most aptly, most rightly, most soundly"**. The sense of *iḥkām* (making firm) in the root requires this.

This distinction is not unimportant. The verse can be read like a sentence of threat — "it is He who will settle the account." But the word itself points not to threat but to **aptness**. What is said is this: the judgement to be given will be without defect; neither too much nor too little.

The same expression occurs in one other place in the Qurʾān, and there it is inside a **prayer**:

> *"And Noah (Nūḥ) called upon his Lord: My Lord! My son is of my family, and Your promise is true, and You are the most judicious of judges"* (Hūd 11/45).

This occurrence is valuable for fixing the tone of the expression. Noah says these words not as a threat but as **a taking of refuge**: at a point where he cannot decide about his son's outcome, he applies to the one who decides most aptly. *Aḥkam al-ḥākimīn*, that is, is an attribute one appeals to, not one that is feared.

### The link between taqwīm and ḥukm

In the fourth verse the sūra says **أَحْسَنِ تَقْوِيمٍ** (the finest stature), in the eighth **أَحْكَمِ ٱلْحَٰكِمِينَ** (the most judicious). Both are elative constructs, and they stand at the two ends of the sūra.

The link between them exists at the level of the root as well. *Taqwīm* is **to straighten a thing, to bring it into a state where it stands upright**. *Ḥukm* is **to make a thing firm, to leave no gap in it**. Both are acts of setting up an order; the one on the side of creation, the other on the side of the reckoning.

The sūra is saying, then: **the one who set up the order and the one who settles the account are the same.** This is the answer to the sūra's question in the seventh verse. The reply to "What makes you deny the dīn?" is that the one who gave the stature will give the judgement as well. It is unthinkable that one who arranged a thing with this much care should take no interest in the outcome of the arrangement.

I record this as my own reading; the closeness of the two words, however, stands in the text.

### On the answer "balā"

It is transmitted that when this verse is recited there is a practice of saying "*balā wa anā ʿalā dhālika mina'l-shāhidīn*" (yes, and I am among those who bear witness to that). This report is found in some sources, but there is dispute over its soundness; presenting it as a certain sunna calls for caution. A similar practice is transmitted for the close of Sūrat al-Qiyāma as well.

This much I can say: since the verse is itself a question, it is natural that it should expect an answer, and the reciter's answering it inwardly accords with the structure of the text. But to relate this with a firm ruling, as "one says such and such", goes beyond the knowledge in our hands.

## The sūra as a whole — the logical sequence of the eight verses

| Verse | What it does | What it prepares |
|---|---|---|
| **1-3. The oaths** | Four witnesses are called | The weight of the ruling to be uttered |
| **4. Aḥsan taqwīm** | Man's position in his creation | The measure of the fall — the height he falls from |
| **5. Asfala sāfilīn** | The fall | The necessity of the exception |
| **6. The exception** | Those who do not fall, and their return | That there is a reckoning |
| **7. The question** | Seizing the one addressed by the collar | The asking after the owner of the judgement |
| **8. Aḥkam al-ḥākimīn** | The owner of the judgement | — |

The irreversibility of the sequence lies here: **for the fall to be meaningful, the height has to be established first.** The expression "the lowest of the low" says something only after "the finest stature" has been said. The sūra first raises man, then brings him down; had the order been the other way about, the fall would not have been a loss nor the exception a gain.

And the last two verses bind all of this to a reckoning. The sūra is not giving a lesson in anthropology — it tells what man is in order to show why the reckoning is necessary.

## The axis between al-Tīn and al-Balad

These two sūras carry the two closest sentences about man in the Qurʾān, and they have to be read together.

**A shared pattern.** Both announce man's creation with the same structure:

- **لَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ فِىٓ أَحْسَنِ تَقْوِيمٍ** (al-Tīn 95/4)
- **لَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ فِى كَبَدٍ** (al-Balad 90/4)

The same emphasis (*laqad*), the same verb (*khalaqnā*), the same object (*al-insāna*), the same particle (*fī*). Only the last word changes: **stature** and **hardship**.

The Qurʾān uses this pattern in two sūras at once and completes it with two different things. What that means is this: there are two simultaneous truths about man, and both belong to his creation. **He was created in the finest stature, and he was created in hardship.** The one announces the capacity, the other the condition.

**A shared oath.** Both swear by the same city:

- *"and by this secure land"* (al-Tīn 95/3)
- *"I swear by this land… you are in this land"* (al-Balad 90/1-2)

But the attribute differs. In al-Tīn the city is **secure** — its safety is brought forward. In al-Balad the city is left bare, no attribute is given it, and immediately afterwards the Prophet's situation there is mentioned. Read together with the question of safety treated in Sūrat Quraysh, what emerges is this: the same city is in one sūra the source of safety and in the other the place where safety is called into question.

**Opposite directions.** The route the two sūras trace for man is the reverse of one another:

| | **al-Tīn** | **al-Balad** |
|---|---|---|
| Man's starting point | The finest stature (high) | In hardship (hard) |
| Direction of movement | **Downwards** — *asfala sāfilīn* | **Upwards** — *al-ʿaqaba* (the steep ascent) |
| The problem | Falling | Not climbing |
| The solution | Faith + righteous deed | Throwing oneself at the ascent: the freeing of a slave, feeding, faith, and the enjoining of patience and mercy |
| The close | Who it is that gives judgement | Who stands on which side |

In al-Tīn man **falls**; in al-Balad an **ascent** is set before him and he does not climb. The two sūras hold two sides of one matter: the one the height that is lost, the other the height that is not gained.

There is this as well: the exception in al-Tīn was built on two conditions (faith + deed). Al-Balad's description of the "ascent" is built directly out of **concrete social acts**: to set a slave free, to feed on a day of hunger an orphan who is kin or a poor man cleaving to the dust. Where the phrase "righteous deed" in al-Tīn is abstract, al-Balad fills the same thing in.

Read together, the two sūras complete the Qurʾān's description of man: **a being created high, set down into a hard life, inclined to fall, and with an ascent left before him that he can climb.**

## The recitation of the sūra in prayer

There is a sound record that this sūra was recited by the Prophet in prayer. In the report transmitted from al-Barāʾ b. ʿĀzib it is related that he heard the Prophet recite *"wa'l-tīni wa'l-zaytūn"* in the night prayer, and that he never heard anyone with a finer voice than his. The report is found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

This report shows that the sūra had settled early into the tongue of the community. Its being a short text with a strong sound and an unbroken rhyme made that easier.

## What this says to the present

**First: the debate about the worth of man lies between two verses of this sūra.**

In modern thought two opposed accounts of man circulate. The first exalts him: the bearer of reason, of culture, of rights, the most developed product of the universe. The second reduces him: a chemical accident, an animal that calculates its interest, a mechanism that deceives itself. Both accounts can produce their evidence, because both rest on real data.

The sūra does not see this duality as a contradiction; **it says both of them about the same being and puts a movement between them.** Man is both "the finest stature" and "the lowest of the low" — but these are not two different descriptions of man, they are the two ends of one and the same man. This is a frame that permits neither celebration nor contempt: worth has been given, but it has to be kept.

The practical consequence is this: in speaking about human dignity, to say "man is valuable anyway" is half a sentence. In the language of the sūra, worth is a thing given and losable.

**Second: "stature" is not a fixed state but a balance.**

*Taqwīm* is bringing into a state of standing upright. A thing that stands upright is doing a continuous work of balance. We know that the muscles of a standing person work without pause, making constant small corrections in order not to fall — standing is not a passive state but an active work.

This coincides with the intuition the word carries, and I record it not as a claim of miracle but as a conceptual overlap: the root carries within it that standing upright is not an achievement but a **process**. The same holds at the moral level. Nobody straightens up once and stays that way.

**Third: the real reason for rejecting the reckoning is not a lack of knowledge.**

The seventh verse asks "**what** makes you deny?" — not who, but what. What is being asked, that is, is not a person or a proof but a **cause**. The sūra's assumption is this: the person who rejects the reckoning has no counter-proof in his hand; what makes him reject it is something else.

This is an observation that holds in most moral debates. Most of the time a man does not arrive at a conclusion by proof; he wants to arrive at a conclusion and finds his proof afterwards. The verse's question takes aim at this mechanism: ask not for your ground, but for the thing that drove you to look for a ground.

**Fourth: a judgement's being the most apt is different from its being the harshest.**

The sūra does not end "Is God not the severest of punishers?" It ends **"the most judicious of judges"**, and the sense of the root is aptness, firmness, fitness to the place.

This has a side that looks to the practice of religious life. The idea of the reckoning, when it is used as an instrument of intimidation, either paralyses a person or blunts him. The close of the sūra is built not in the language of intimidation but in the language of **assurance**: the decision to be given will be without defect. Noah's use of the same expression inside a prayer confirms this. To believe in the reckoning is not to believe that something bad is going to happen to one; it is to believe that nothing will be left without finding its place.

**Fifth: what the four oaths have in common.**

Whether we accept the place reading or not, the four members have one side in common: **all of them last.** Fig and olive trees live for centuries; a mountain does not move from its place; the city has stood for a thousand years. All four things sworn by are things time has not been able to wear away.

If we recall the witnessing function of the oath, this becomes meaningful: the things that last are testifying about man, who passes. In telling of man's fall, the sūra has set four unchanging witnesses behind it.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

- Whether the sūra is Meccan or Medinan is disputed; the view of the majority was transmitted, and no firm ruling was issued.
- **What the four oaths are** — fruits or places — is not certain. I stated that I find the place reading the more consistent and gave my grounds, but I wrote plainly that this is not binding and that the reading does not rest on a usage recorded in the language.
- Even if the place reading is accepted, which region stands for which prophet is interpretation; it is not transmitted report.
- The explanations transmitted about the meaning of the word *Sīnīn* do not agree with one another; none of them was preferred.
- Whether the attribute *amīn* is active or passive is disputed; a preference was stated, but it was written that it is not binding.
- **What "the finest stature" looks to** is a matter of disagreement; four views were given in a table and none was presented as certainly correct.
- **What "the lowest of the low" is** is a matter of disagreement; four views were given and it was discussed which reading the verse of exception supports, but no view was made definite.
- Whether the exception in the sixth verse is connected or disconnected depends on what the fall is; both possibilities were left open.
- In which sense the construct *ghayru mamnūn* is to be read is disputed; both senses were left in place.
- To whom the address in the seventh verse is made is disputed; a preference was stated with its ground, and it was written that it is not binding.
- A second reading of the verb *yukadhdhibuka* is transmitted in the sources; since it is not widespread, no ruling was issued.
- The report about saying "balā" when the eighth verse is recited was transmitted, but it was stated that its soundness is disputed.
- The observations about the sound and structural symmetries of the sūra (the structural resemblance between *asfala sāfilīn* and *aḥkami'l-ḥākimīn*, the closeness of root between *taqwīm* and *ḥukm*) were presented as my own reading; not as a classical transmitted report.
