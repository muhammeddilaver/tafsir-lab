# 103. Sūrat al-ʿAṣr

Three verses. One of the shortest sūras in the Qurʾān. It takes its name from its first word.

On the place of revelation the majority say Meccan; some reports say Medinan. In the lists of the order of revelation it is placed among the early sūras — that is, a text belonging to the years of the Meccan period when no community had yet formed and opposition was hardening. This is worth keeping in mind while reading the emphasis on "enjoining upon one another" in the third verse: there is as yet no large body of people to enjoin anything upon one another.

The skeleton of the sūra has three parts, and it is built like a piece of reasoning:

1. **The oath** (*qasam*): "By time."
2. **The answer to the oath** (*jawāb al-qasam*): "Man is truly in loss."
3. **The exception**: "Except those…"

A general ruling is laid down first, and then those who fall outside it are enumerated under four conditions. This is not a common pattern in the Qurʾān; usually the good news comes first and the warning after. Here the order is reversed: the weight of the ruling is established first, and the door is opened only at the very end.

The basmala was treated in the preceding sūras and is not taken up again here; the verse numbers are counted without the basmala.

## 103/1 — وَٱلْعَصْرِ

*Waʾl-ʿaṣr*
"By time."

One of the shortest verses in the Qurʾān. Not even two words: a particle and a noun.

### What an oath (*qasam*) does

The **وَ (wāw)** at the head is not a conjunction here but the particle of oath — the particle used in Arabic when one says *waʾllāhi*. It puts the noun after it in the genitive (*al-ʿaṣri*) and loads the sentence with the sense "I swear that…".

The function of an oath in language is not to give information but to **reinforce it**. You can state a piece of news as it is; when you state it under oath you have told the one addressed something further: I am not opening this for debate, I stand behind it. The classical books of rhetoric divide a statement into three kinds according to the condition of the one addressed — a person with no knowledge of the matter is given the bare statement, a person with doubts is given it with one reinforcement, and a person who denies it with heavy reinforcement. Sūrat al-ʿAṣr has the oath, and *inna*, and the *lām*. The one addressed, then, is not someone ready to accept what is about to be said; he is someone who will object.

### Why does God swear by a created thing?

In this sūra the oath is sworn by something created: by time. There are many like it in the Qurʾān — the sun, the moon, the night, the morning, the fig, the olive, the pen, the star, the city, the soul. Human beings, by contrast, are forbidden this: it is reported that the Prophet said, "Whoever would swear, let him swear by God, or else keep silent" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim, from Ibn ʿUmar). The servant may swear only by God; God swears by whatever He wills.

The exegetes have explained this asymmetry in several ways. The principal answers are these:

**First — magnification (*taʿẓīm*).** To swear by a thing is to announce that the thing matters. When God swears by something He has created, He has drawn attention to it, put it on the agenda. On this reading part of the message is already in the sentence "*waʾl-ʿaṣr*" itself: *look at time.*

**Second — a relation of evidence.** A link is sought between the thing sworn by and the thing said under oath. Time, that is, is the **witness** that man is in loss. This explanation works extraordinarily well in this sūra: time is the living proof that something is continually going out of a person's hands. The oath itself becomes the evidence for the ruling. This is also the reading I find strongest; but it is a preference, and it is not binding.

**Third — speaking in the language of the one addressed.** The Arab held an oath to be a weighty thing; to object to what was said under oath was shameful. The revelation is using the addressee's own habit of speech.

**Fourth — the assumption of an unexpressed construct.** Some scholars hold that in every oath sworn by a created thing there is an unspoken word: "By the *Lord* of time." This view is a minority one and has been judged weak, because the Qurʾān repeats these oaths in dozens of places and in none of them does such a construct appear openly. It is still worth recording, because the anxiety behind it is serious: the anxiety that reverence shown to a created thing may turn into worship of a created thing.

### The root of عصر: to press

This is the most productive place in the sūra.

The concrete, tangible sense of the root **ع-ص-ر** in Arabic is **to press** — to squeeze a thing in the hand or in an instrument so that the liquid inside it comes out. To press grapes, to press olives, to wring a wet cloth until the water runs out.

Its derivatives bear this out:

- **عَصِير (*ʿaṣīr*)** — pressed liquid. This is still the word for fruit juice in Arabic today.
- **مِعْصَرَة (*miʿṣara*)** — the instrument of pressing; a wine-vat, a press, an oil-press.
- **إِعْصَار (*iʿṣār*)** — a whirling storm, a whirlwind. A wind that twists the air and compresses it towards a point. It occurs in the Qurʾān: the disaster that burns a man's garden to ashes is so named (al-Baqara 2/266).
- **ٱلْمُعْصِرَات (*muʿṣirāt*)** — in Sūrat al-Nabaʾ, the things from which rain is sent down (al-Nabaʾ 78/14). The exegetes differ over whether these are the clouds or the winds, but the word itself speaks of compression, of pressing: that which is compressed and about to release its load.
- Twice in the story of Joseph (Yūsuf): the dream of his fellow prisoner — *"I see myself pressing wine"* (Yūsuf 12/36) — and the good news of the year of plenty — *"In that year the people will be given rain, and in that year they will press"* (Yūsuf 12/49).

From the same root, and in the same lexical entry, comes also the sense of **time**: *ʿaṣr* = a time, the age (*dahr*), an epoch; and specifically the hour of the afternoon. The classical dictionaries do not split these two senses into separate entries; they give them side by side.

**So what is the link between pressing and time?**

Care is needed here: not all the lexicographers draw this link in the same way, and some think it unnecessary to draw at all. The explanation repeated in the dictionaries is this: *ʿaṣr* is the hour towards the end of the day when the shadows lengthen and the daylight begins to narrow, to be compressed — the day is being pressed as it gives up its remaining light. That the word should have widened from here to time in the absolute seems plausible.

To this one thing may be added — and I must state plainly that this is an inference of mine, not a classical report: time does to a person what a press does to grapes. It passes over him, presses him as it passes, draws something out of him with each pressing, and leaves the pulp behind. A lifespan is the fruit being pressed. What comes out cannot be put back. This reading joins the second verse of the sūra in a surprising way: what is pressed must necessarily diminish — and the name of that diminution is *khusr*, that is, loss.

I offer this as a fine point, not as a ruling. But it is a possibility lying ready in the root itself; it is not an invention.

**A point not to be confused:** the word **عُسْر (*ʿusr*)**, meaning "hardship, difficulty" — the *ʿusr* of the verse in Sūrat al-Inshirāḥ, "with every hardship there is ease" — is **from a different root** (ʿayn-sīn-rāʾ). *ʿAṣr* is ʿayn-ṣād-rāʾ. In transliteration the two look alike, and they are sometimes confused with one another. There is no etymological kinship between them.

### What does "ʿaṣr" mean here? The disagreement

Because the range of meaning the word carries is wide, the exegetes differ. The views and the grounds on which they rest:

| View | What it means | Ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Time in the absolute / *dahr*** (the majority) | All time, time as it flows | One of the primary senses of the word in the dictionary; there is no indication in the sūra that narrows it; it relates directly to the following verse (loss/depletion) | — |
| **The afternoon hour / the afternoon prayer** | The last portion of the day, or the prayer performed at that hour | That *ʿaṣr* is the proper name of that hour; the reports about the merit of the afternoon prayer | There is no indication in the sūra pointing to prayer; the definite article here may be generic |
| **The age of the Prophet** | The period of the message | An oath is sworn by what is noble; the noblest period is the one in which the revelation came down | There is no sign in the text indicating this; the restriction is left without evidence |
| **The daytime, or the night and the day** | Time in its divided state | That the Qurʾān swears elsewhere by the night and the day | An analogy with oaths in other sūras is not evidence here |

The preference of the majority is the first, and its ground is strong: the word has come *absolute*, not annexed to anything, not delimited. Moreover this reading sits within the sūra's own inner logic — time is the witness, because loss takes place within time.

**What can be said for the afternoon view.** At first sight this view looks forced, but one side of it gives pause: the link between the afternoon and loss is already established in the hadith literature. It is reported that the Prophet said of the person who misses the afternoon prayer that "it is as though he had been bereft of his family and his property" (al-Bukhārī and Muslim). The language of loss is already attached to the hour of the afternoon. This does not prove the afternoon view; but it makes intelligible where the view came from.

This too should be added: the views do not exclude one another. When the word is read as time in the absolute, the afternoon is already inside it. The narrowing views may be taken to have selected one instance from the wide sense.

### What this says to the present

That an oath is sworn by time says that time is **not neutral**. What is sworn by is a witness; and a witness recounts what it has seen. This stands directly against the understanding that takes time for an empty vessel: here time is not a volume to be filled but a witness that keeps a record.

The second point is more concrete: the sense of "pressing" in the root says something about the direction of time. A press works in one direction only. The liquid pressed out of the fruit does not go back in. What physics calls the arrow of time — the irreversibility of processes — looks towards the same place as the intuition this word carries. I record this not as a claim of miracle but as a conceptual overlap; the word is not announcing a law of physics, but it does record the oldest observation human beings have made about time: it cannot be taken back.

## 103/2 — إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَفِى خُسْرٍ

*Innaʾl-insāna la-fī khusr*
"Man is surely in loss."

The answer to the oath. That is, this is the ruling sworn to.

### The density of the reinforcement

In this very short sentence **three separate reinforcements** are laid one on top of another:

1. **The oath** — the preceding verse has already weighted the sentence.
2. **إِنَّ (*inna*)** — "verily". The strongest particle of emphasis in Arabic; it puts its noun in the accusative and loads the sentence with certainty.
3. **لَ (*lām*)** — the lām that comes at the head of the predicate of *inna* and reinforces it once more. The lexicographers call this the displaced lām (*lām al-muzaḥlaqa*): its place is properly at the head of *inna*, and it has been shifted to the head of the predicate so that two particles of emphasis should not stand side by side.

To this is added the fact that the sentence is a **nominal sentence**. In Arabic a verbal sentence conveys occurrence and renewal, a nominal sentence fixity and continuity. It is not said here that "man incurs loss"; it is said that "man is in loss". Loss, that is, is not an event but a state.

Fourfold reinforcement, in a sentence of three words. By the measure of rhetoric this indicates that the one addressed stands in the position of **denial**. Nobody is willing to say "I am at a loss"; least of all someone who supposes himself to be in profit.

### خُسْر — *khusr*

**Root: خ-س-ر.** Its primary sense is **diminution and loss**, and in Arabic this sense has settled particularly in the domain of **trade and weighing**. The melting away of capital, coming out of a transaction at a loss, giving short measure.

The uses of the root in the Qurʾān show this commercial frame directly:

- *"When they measure out or weigh for others, they give less"* (al-Muṭaffifīn 83/3) — the verb here is from this root; a shortfall made at the scales.
- *"Do not give short weight"* (al-Raḥmān 55/9) — again the same root, again the scales.

*Khusr*, then, is not an emotional word. It is an accounting entry. The balance coming out negative when the books are closed.

**The usual renderings show an interesting drift.** The word tends to be rendered in English as **loss** or **ruin**, and both of these have picked up an emotional colouring — disappointment, an inward ache. The Arabic *khusrān* is a cold balance sheet. The English reader, hearing the verse, is inclined to hear a melancholy in "man is in loss"; whereas the text is reporting not a state of feeling but a **result of accounting**.

### فِى — "in"

A single particle, but it changes the picture the sentence gives.

The verse does not say "man has incurred loss". It says "man is **in** loss". **فِى** is the particle that indicates containment, being inside something.

The difference lies here: when it is said "he incurred a loss", the loss is an event that has befallen the man — it comes from outside, and it passes. When it is said "in loss", loss becomes an **environment**. The man is standing inside it, surrounded by it. As one is inside water, as one is inside darkness.

For this reason the verse of exception is not a "rescue" either, but an **extraction**: to be taken out from within.

### خُسْرٍ — its coming indefinite

The word is not *al-khusr*; it has tanwīn: **khusrin**, that is, "a loss". In Arabic indefiniteness sometimes conveys diminution, and sometimes the exact opposite — magnification and dread. The reading here is the second: *such a loss that* — a loss undescribed, its extent left unspecified.

And since its kind is not specified, which loss it is remains open-ended: in the lifespan, in the capital, in the hereafter? The text does not divide them; it leaves the word so as to include them all.

### ٱلْإِنسَٰن — *al-insān*

**The definite article (al-).** The article here is of the kind the lexicographers call *istighrāq*: it encompasses the whole of the genus. Not "a man" or "this man" — the **species** man, all of them.

There is a decisive proof of this from within the text: **the exception in the third verse.** An exception is made only from a general ruling. To say "except these" means that the ruling was covering these too. Had "al-insān" meant a particular person or a particular group, the exception that follows would be meaningless. The tendency of some exegetes to refer this word to a particular Meccan denier is weak for this reason; the structure of the sūra itself does not permit it.

**Its coming singular.** Not "men are in loss" but "man is in loss". When the name of a species is used in the singular, the ruling does not descend on persons one by one; it is written directly into **the definition of the species**. This, then, is not a statistic about the majority; it is the description of the default state. This is the condition that holds unless the contrary is demonstrated.

**On the root of the word** there are two old views, and both are meaningful: on one view it is **ʾ-n-s** (familiarity, growing accustomed, closeness) — man is the creature that grows accustomed and draws close. On the other view it is **n-s-y** (to forget) — man is the creature that forgets; this view is widely ascribed to Ibn ʿAbbās. If the second root is correct, its link with the subject of the sūra is plain: the real problem with time is forgetting that time is passing.

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

The root *khusr* occurs in many places in the Qurʾān, and almost every time it stands in a context of **accounting**. Its two sharpest occurrences:

*"The real losers are those who lose both themselves and their families on the Day of Resurrection"* (al-Zumar 39/15). What suffers the loss here is not property but the person himself. The capital lost is a man's own existence.

*"Say: Shall we tell you who lose most by their deeds? They are those whose every effort in the life of this world has gone astray while they supposed that they were doing good work"* (al-Kahf 18/103-104).

This second verse explains why Sūrat al-ʿAṣr is built with such heavy reinforcement: the most dangerous loss is the loss that is **not noticed**. The person is working, producing, busy, even content — and the balance is negative. That the loss is hidden is what requires it to be announced under oath.

### The sūra's relation to its neighbours

Al-ʿAṣr is placed between two sūras in the order of the codex, and the three take hold of the same subject from different sides:

**Before — al-Takāthur (102).** *"The rivalry for increase distracted you, until you visited the graves"* (al-Takāthur 102/1-2). Here is the diagnosis: man is busy increasing, and this occupation lasts until death.

**Al-ʿAṣr (103).** Here that occupation is given its name: loss. *Takāthur* means "abundance" — while man supposes he is increasing, al-ʿAṣr says he is diminishing. Set the two sūras side by side and a contrast emerges: what is counted rises, the balance falls.

**After — al-Humaza (104).** *"Who amasses wealth and counts it… supposing that his wealth will make him immortal"* (al-Humaza 104/2-3). Here the metaphor of capital is spoken openly in terms of wealth, and the source of the delusion is shown: the supposition that accumulation will halt time.

The same three elements are present in all three: number, wealth, death. Al-ʿAṣr, in the middle of this trio, changes the unit of measure — what must be counted is not wealth but time.

## 103/3 — إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ وَتَوَاصَوْا۟ بِٱلْحَقِّ وَتَوَاصَوْا۟ بِٱلصَّبْرِ

*Illāʾlladhīna āmanū wa ʿamiluʾṣ-ṣāliḥāti wa tawāṣaw biʾl-ḥaqqi wa tawāṣaw biʾṣ-ṣabr*
"Except those who believe and do righteous deeds, and enjoin the truth upon one another, and enjoin patience upon one another."

The centre of gravity of the sūra. A ruling of two verses is opened up by an exception of one.

### The character of the exception

**إِلَّا (*illā*)** here is a connected exception (*istithnāʾ muttaṣil*): those excepted are taken out from within the very set that is the subject of the ruling. A slice is separated from within the sphere of "man".

This has two consequences. First, those taken out are men too — not a different species, not born exempt. Second, the taking out does not happen **of itself**; it happens by four verbs. The whole of the exception is built with verbal clauses: *āmanū, ʿamilū, tawāṣaw, tawāṣaw.* All in the past tense. Not a state, then, but work done.

There is one further striking difference of word order: **the ruling is singular, the exception plural.** "Al-insān" was singular; "alladhīna āmanū" is plural. The text describes loss as the condition of a single man, and deliverance as the condition of a plural body. I record this observation as my own reading, but it stands in the text at the level of grammar and agrees exactly with the last two conditions of the sūra.

### The four conditions

The sequence itself builds a structure:

| # | Condition | Domain | Its direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Faith** | Inward | The heart — acceptance |
| 2 | **Righteous deeds** | Outward | The body — action |
| 3 | **Enjoining the truth** | Towards another | Society — content |
| 4 | **Enjoining patience** | Towards another | Society — endurance |

The first two are individual, the last two social. And since the last two come in the pattern of reciprocity, it is linguistically impossible for them to be fulfilled alone.

This is the most misunderstood place in the sūra. To reduce the conditions of deliverance to two, "belief + worship", is to discard half the verse. The text does not admit to the exception the person who merely puts his own faith and his own deeds in order. **Deliverance is not described as a work that can be completed alone.**

### ءَامَنُوا۟ — faith

**Root: ʾ-m-n.** From the same root come **amn** (safety), **amāna** (trust), **amīn** (trustworthy). The primary sense of the root is the departure of fear, being in safety.

This takes faith out of being merely the acceptance of a piece of information. In Arabic "*āmana bihi*" says more than finding a proposition true: entering into a relation of trust, committing oneself. The word *amīn*, from the same root, describes the side that gives trust; *īmān* describes the side that trusts.

The object of the verb is not stated here: what they believed in is not specified. When this usage is left absolute in the Qurʾān, what is meant is faith itself — whom one is to believe in is taken as already known.

### وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ — righteous deeds

**Root: ṣ-l-ḥ.** The opposite of this root is **fasād** (corruption, spoiling). From the same root come **iṣlāḥ** (putting right) and **ṣulḥ** (peace, the mending of a breach).

The definition that follows from this is important: **a righteous deed is not a "good work" but a "repairing work".** Work that puts right what has been spoiled, that keeps in place what is in place, that renders a thing serviceable. There is an idea of repair inside the word — not an abstract merit, but a mending.

**Note the difference of pattern:** faith came as a **verb** (*āmanū*), and deed too as a **verb** (*ʿamilū*); but the object of the deed is a definite plural: *al-ṣāliḥāt.* Not a single fine action, then, but a set whose kind is specified.

That faith and deed are mentioned **separately** has been the subject of long debate in the history of theology: is deed a part of faith, or is it something distinct from faith and required together with it? The majority of the Sunnīs do not count deed as belonging to the essence of faith but to its perfection; other schools hold different views. I state no preference here; what the sūra says is plainer: **both are enumerated.** The text has not reduced either to the other.

### وَتَوَاصَوْا۟ — they enjoined upon one another

The word in the sūra that most deserves to be dwelt on.

**Root: و-ص-ي.** From this root comes **waṣiyya** (a will, a bequest). Some of the lexicographers give the concrete sense of the root thus: the branches of plants interlocking and joining together; "*wāṣiya* ground" means ground whose plants have grown into one another. Underneath the root, then, lies **binding, holding fast to one another**. A bequest is like this too: the word binds the one who speaks it and the one who hears it to each other; its binding force continues even after death.

English covers the two ends of this root with two unconnected words — a **bequest** at one end and **advice** at the other — and the link between them has become invisible. "Advice" is a light word: a suggestion, an opinion offered, to be taken or left. At the heavy end of the same Arabic root stands the last will and testament. The verse uses the word that lies between these two ends; the lightness of "advise" does not carry the weight of the text.

**The real matter is in the pattern.** The word is in the **تَفَاعَلَ (*tafāʿul*)** form. In Arabic this form conveys **reciprocity** (*mushāraka*) — it shows the verb passing back and forth between at least two parties:

- *qātala* (he killed) → *taqātalū* (they killed one another)
- *ʿarafa* (he knew) → *taʿārafū* (they came to know one another) — the *li-taʿārafū* of al-Ḥujurāt 49/13 is this pattern
- *awṣā* (he bequeathed / he enjoined) → *tawāṣaw* (they enjoined upon one another)

The verse therefore does not say "those who give counsel to others". It says **"those who enjoin upon one another"**. The verb runs in both directions.

The consequence of this difference is large. One-directional counsel sets up a hierarchy: the one who knows and the one who does not, the one who speaks and the one who listens. Mutual enjoining breaks this down; everyone is both speaker and listener. The pattern has lodged inside the grammar the fact that the one who gives counsel also stands in need of it. It says that a community can remain standing only when its members are able to warn one another — in an order of counsel that flows in one direction only, the one who warns receives a warning from no one, and becomes impossible to correct.

**The other places where this pattern occurs in the Qurʾān** support this reading:

- *"Then to be of those who believe and enjoin patience upon one another and enjoin mercy upon one another"* (al-Balad 90/17). Almost the same structure; here the fourth element is mercy in place of patience.
- *"Did they enjoin this upon one another? No, they are a people transgressing all bounds"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/53). This occurrence is striking: the same pattern is used here in a **negative** context. That past communities always said the same two words to their prophets — "sorcerer" or "madman" — is as though they had enjoined it upon one another. The mechanism of mutual enjoining, that is, works in both directions: a community can enjoin the truth upon one another, and it can enjoin denial. The mechanism is neutral; what is decisive is the content. This is why the sūra's stipulation *biʾl-ḥaqq* is necessary.

### بِٱلْحَقِّ — the truth

**Root: ḥ-q-q.** Its primary sense is **to be fixed and in its proper place**; for a thing genuinely to be so. Its opposite is *bāṭil* — empty, groundless, collapsing.

The word carries three senses at once in Arabic, and all three are left open in the verse:

- **The true** — that which is as it is, that which is correct.
- **The right** — what belongs to a person, the share that is owed to him.
- **The duty** — what falls upon one, what must be discharged.

"Enjoining the truth" can therefore mean "speaking what is true" and "rendering to each his right" and "reminding of responsibility" alike. The text does not narrow it.

The particle **بِ (*bāʾ*)** here attaches the verb to its object and conveys an adhesion: what is enjoined is joined to the truth, not separate from it.

### بِٱلصَّبْرِ — patience

**Root: ṣ-b-r.** The concrete sense is **to hold, to confine, to keep back in a place.** "He held his soul in patience" = he held his soul back. In Arabic, killing an animal that has been tied up is called killing *ṣabran* — because the animal is held bound. Underneath the root lies not lamentation but **holding**.

This differs from the passivity that the English "patience" suggests. In English patience mostly speaks of enduring, submitting, waiting. The Arabic root, though, conveys an active grip: keeping the reins in hand, not allowing dispersal.

In the tradition of ethics patience is gathered under three headings, and this classification is widespread: patience **in persevering in worship**, patience **in refraining from sin**, and patience **in the face of affliction**. What is common to all three is a person's holding himself in a place.

### Why "enjoining patience" separately?

This is the finest point of the sūra.

If enjoining the truth were a costless business, the fourth condition would be unnecessary. Its being laid down as a separate condition assumes that speaking the truth **has a cost**. Patience comes here not as a random virtue but as the **natural consequence** of the third condition.

This link is drawn explicitly in another place in the Qurʾān. In Luqmān's counsel to his son the sequence is exactly the same: *"Enjoin what is right, forbid what is wrong, and be patient over what befalls you"* (Luqmān 31/17). First speak the truth, then accept what comes upon you. The second clause is the bill for the first.

From here follows the realism of the sūra: a community that enjoins the truth will meet resistance. If it does not meet resistance, it is probably not enjoining the truth. And a community that meets resistance must hold on to one another so as not to be dispersed — this is why patience too came in the **reciprocal** pattern. No one can carry his own patience alone.

For a sūra revealed in Mecca this condition was not abstract. The first addressees of the text were a small group paying the cost of speaking the truth.

### The unity of the sūra: time, capital, loss

The three verses are woven with a single metaphor, and the metaphor is consistent:

- **Capital:** the lifespan. Its amount is fixed, its total unknown.
- **Expenditure:** time. Uninterrupted, unstoppable, not subject to the will.
- **The balance:** negative by default.

The crucial side of this picture is that **you are at a loss even if you do nothing.** In trade, loss does not begin when capital stands still; there is merely no profit. Here it is not so — because the expenditure side does not depend on your decision. Time goes whether you decide or not. This is why the verse does not say "man may incur loss" but "he is in loss". There is no neutral position. Standing still is not an option.

The exception changes one single thing in this picture: **that something be received in return for the time that goes.** That the juice of the fruit being pressed should run into a vessel. The four conditions are the name of that vessel.

### The saying ascribed to al-Shāfiʿī

There is a saying repeated almost every time this sūra is mentioned: *"If people were to reflect on this sūra alone, it would suffice them."*

Honesty is required about the ascription. This saying is transmitted in the literature of tafsīr with an ascription to al-Shāfiʿī, and it has widely entered circulation by way of Ibn Kathīr's commentary. However:

- There is no firm evidence that this expression occurs in al-Shāfiʿī's own works.
- The ascription is transmitted not in the form of a report with a chain of transmission, but at the level of "it has been said" in the books of tafsīr.

The most accurate statement is therefore this: **it is a saying widely transmitted in the exegetical tradition with an ascription to al-Shāfiʿī.** Its ascription cannot be known with certainty. The saying itself is fine and describes the sūra correctly; but to transmit it in the firm form "al-Shāfiʿī said" would go beyond the information in our hands.

Similarly, a report is transmitted that the Companions used to recite this sūra when parting from one another. This report is found in the classical sources, but there is disagreement over its soundness; presenting it as an established practice calls for caution.

### What this says to the present

**First: how it differs from the culture of productivity.**

The modern world sees time as a resource and says so openly. The sentence "time is money" was written in the eighteenth century by Benjamin Franklin as a piece of advice to tradesmen, and it has been ever since the slogan of thinking of time as a measurable, saleable, optimisable unit. Today we call this "time management", "productivity", "personal optimisation".

Between the sūra's grasp of time and this there is an intersection and a divergence.

**The intersection is real:** both count time as an irrecoverable resource. The economic concept of opportunity cost — that every unit spent is the price of the alternative not taken — works on the same logic as the sūra's description of *khusr*. An hour spent for nothing is not a neutral hour; it is the loss of an alternative.

**The divergence is more important.** At three points:

*The unit of measure is different.* The culture of productivity measures output: how many tasks, how many pages, how many hours. The sūra asks not "how much" but "for the sake of what". The description in al-Kahf sits exactly here: the man who supposes he is doing good work while his effort has gone astray. A productive life can be a life whose balance is negative — and it may go unnoticed, because all the indicators look green.

*The direction is different.* Time management addresses a single subject: your time, your plan, your goals. **Half** of the sūra's conditions of deliverance concern someone else, and stand in the pattern of reciprocity. Someone who manages his own time flawlessly but enters into this relation with no one falls, by the sūra's measure, outside the exception. This is a question that the modern understanding of productivity never asks.

*The status of time is different.* In the culture of productivity time is material — it is filled, cut, shaped. In the sūra time is **a witness** — the party sworn by, that is, the party that testifies. Material you manage; to a witness you render account.

**Second: the cost of doing nothing.**

This is the most practical measure the sūra sets up. Loss does not require doing an evil; letting oneself drift is enough. This stands against the common understanding that defines morality as "not doing bad things". A person may have harmed no one at all and still be in the condition the sūra describes; because the measure works through what is not done as well.

**Third: that deliverance cannot be completed alone.**

This is the hardest side of the sūra. Every understanding that builds religious life as an entirely individual programme of purification runs into a wall in the second half of the third verse. The text lays down, after faith and deed, **two social conditions**, and gives both in the pattern of reciprocity. No one, that is, can be inside this exception alone, saying nothing to anyone and hearing nothing from anyone.

The concrete counterpart of this for the present is: to build a circle of people who can warn one another is not a personal preference but, in the language of the sūra, one of the conditions of deliverance. And for that circle to work it must run in both directions — to be not only the one who speaks but also the one who is open to what is spoken. A person to whom no one can say anything stands outside the structure the sūra describes, however much truth he enjoins.

**Fourth: the shortness of the sūra itself says something.**

Three verses; fourteen words, by the method of counting. A text that speaks of the scarcity of time itself takes up little space. I record this not as a claim of miracle but as the agreement of the text's form with its content: to say what is to be said without drawing it out is a consistent choice for a text whose subject is time.
