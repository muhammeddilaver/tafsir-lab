# 107. Sūrat al-Māʿūn

Seven verses. It stands in the band of short sūras counted as belonging to the Meccan period. It takes its name from a word in the last verse that occurs nowhere else in the Qurʾān: **al-Māʿūn**.

It is transmitted in the sources that the sūra is also called by other names: *al-Dīn* (from the word in the first verse), *Araʾayta* (from the opening word), *al-Yatīm*, *al-Takdhīb*. In the early period most sūras were called not by an official name but by a striking word within them; this multiplicity is the trace of that.

## What does the sūra do?

This sūra takes up a single question and answers it from a wholly unexpected place.

The question is this: **How do you tell that a person really denies the religion?**

The expected answer comes from the field of belief — "he says such and such", "he does not believe in this", "he rejects that". The sūra does not give this answer. Instead it points to two behaviours: **his repelling the orphan** and **his not urging the feeding of the needy.** As the proof of belief it is not belief that is shown but the hand.

Then, in its second half, the sūra turns the picture around. This time the people it takes up are not those who deny the religion but **those who pray.** And it catches them at the same point: a small thing not given.

That is, the two halves of the sūra take two different types of person and bring them together at the same point. The first rejects with his tongue, the second with his hand. The sūra's claim is that there is no practical difference between the two.

## Meccan or Medinan?

One must be honest here: there is no definite answer, and the views that exist rest not on transmitted report but on inference.

| View | Ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| The whole of it **Meccan** | The style is the Meccan style: short verses, sharp rhyme, an opening with a question, an emphasis on the world to come. It occurs in the early ranks in the lists of the order of revelation. | The description of ostentation and of "those who pray" appears to require an environment in which being a Muslim brought a social return. |
| The whole of it **Medinan** | The last four verses describe the hypocrites; the phenomenon of hypocrisy is proper to Medina. For those who count "al-Māʿūn" as the alms-tax (*zakāt*), the institutionalising of the alms-tax likewise takes place in Medina. | The one addressed in the first three verses is plainly someone who denies the religion; in Medina this description sits less well. |
| The first three verses **Meccan**, the last four **Medinan** | The addressee of the two halves differs; style and subject change at this cutting point. | This is an **inference** made from the content of the text; it does not rest on a sound transmitted report. The claim that a sūra came down in two pieces requires a separate proof. |

The third view is often repeated in the books of exegesis, but its being repeated does not make it a transmitted report. In my judgement the cautious position is this: it can be said that the sūra as a whole is a text befitting the last period of Mecca, but the possibility that its second half belongs to Medina must also be taken seriously. This preference is not binding and it does not change the understanding of the sūra either — because what is described is not a period but an attitude.

## The reports of the occasion of revelation

In the reports it is said that the first verses came down concerning the following people: Abū Jahl (that he withheld the due of an orphan under his protection), al-ʿĀṣ b. Wāʾil al-Sahmī, al-Walīd b. al-Mughīra, Abū Sufyān (that he drove off an orphan who asked something of him while he was slaughtering a camel). For the last four verses, ʿAbd Allāh b. Ubayy and the hypocrites are mentioned.

These reports must be looked at with caution, and there is a concrete reason for this: **the names do not agree with one another.** That four separate names are given for the same three verses suggests that these names are answers given after the event to the question "who could have been meant?". The reports themselves are also mostly *mursal*, that is, with the Companion link omitted.

In the tradition of legal theory there is a settled measure for cases of this kind: *al-ʿibratu bi-ʿumūmi'l-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣi'l-sabab* — the ruling goes by the generality of the wording, not by the particularity of the occasion. The sūra does not say "Abū Jahl"; it says "the one who repels the orphan". Whoever bears that attribute, the verse is speaking of him.

## The basmala

The word-by-word analysis of the basmala was carried out in Sūrat al-Fātiḥa; I do not repeat it here. There is only one point particular to this sūra: the first thing a text that opens with the names al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm does is to give the description of mercilessness. The person described throughout the sūra is the exact opposite of the two names in the heading. Mercy gives without return; the man the sūra describes, far from giving without return, will not even lend.

## 107/1 — أَرَءَيْتَ ٱلَّذِى يُكَذِّبُ بِٱلدِّينِ

*Araʾayta'lladhī yukadhdhibu bi'l-dīn*
"Have you seen the one who denies the religion?"

### The words

**أَرَءَيْتَ (*araʾayta*)** — Two pieces: the interrogative hamza (أ) + *raʾayta* (you saw). The root of the word is **ر أ ي**: to see.

But the "seeing" here is not seeing with the eye. In Arabic *ruʾya* is of two kinds: *ruʾya baṣariyya* (with the eye) and *ruʾya qalbiyya* (to know, to grasp, to notice). The second is what is meant here. The English question "Have you seen that man?" likewise mostly has nothing to do with seeing with the eye; it means "are you aware of the situation". Arabic has passed through the same place.

The lexicographers say that this pattern has become frozen in Arabic and turned almost into a single word: in most places *araʾayta* is used in the sense of **"tell me, what do you say, just look at this"**. This pattern occurs many times in the Qurʾān, and almost every time something startling, shaking or thought-provoking follows it. That is, this word is a **warning sign**: attention, I am now going to say something you do not expect.

**The function of the interrogative pattern.** The verse could have framed a plain sentence: "The one who denies the religion is this." It does not. It asks a question. The difference is this:

- A plain sentence gives information; the addressee is in the position of a receiver, he listens and passes on.
- A question draws the addressee into the matter. The mind that hears the question, without waiting for the answer, begins of itself to look for one. Before the sūra even reaches the second verse the reader has already begun to think "who could it be?".

This is a known technique in rhetoric and one of the instruments the Qurʾān uses most often. The question does not **make the answer accepted, it makes it found.** Information that is made accepted is forgotten; information that is found stays.

One further subtlety: the question is directed at **a singular, masculine addressee** — "you". The first addressee is the Prophet. But in Arabic there is an expression the lexicographers use for general addresses of this kind: *khiṭāb li-kulli man yaṣluḥu lahū* — "an address to everyone who is fit for it". That is, the sentence is framed as though it were said to a single person and left open to everyone. This is the way of speaking not to a crowd but to each person one by one.

**A question left unanswered.** In terms of Arabic grammar *raʾayta* is a verb that requires two objects ("I saw so-and-so in such a state"). Here the first object is present (*alladhī yukadhdhibu bi'l-dīn*), the second is not. That is, the sentence is technically incomplete: "Have you seen the one who denies the religion … ?" What will fill the gap?

The classical grammarians give two answers: either the second object has been elided and the *fa-dhālika'lladhī* that follows takes its place, or *araʾayta* has here turned entirely into the sense of "tell me" and does not look for an object. The result is the same on either view: **the gap is deliberate.** The verse leaves the question hanging in the air and defers the answer to the next verse. That momentary gap between the two verses is where the whole effect of the sūra is built.

**يُكَذِّبُ (*yukadhdhibu*)** — Root **ك ذ ب**: falsehood. In the *tafʿīl* form (*kadhdhaba*): "to count as false, to give the lie to, to call a thing a lie."

The difference here matters. *Kadhaba* (Form I) means "he lied" — the one who performs the act is the liar. *Kadhdhaba*, however, means "he called another's true word a lie" — the one who performs the act may not even be a liar; what he does is to reject what is true.

That is, *takdhīb* is not a silent denial. Not believing inwardly is *kufr*; bringing that out and saying "this is a lie" is *takdhīb*. *Takdhīb* is an active stance, a taking up of a position.

The verb is in the **imperfect** (*muḍāriʿ*). In Arabic the imperfect conveys continuity and renewal. Not "he denied it once" but "he goes on denying it". This is not an event but a state.

**بِ (*bāʾ*)** — The verb *kadhdhaba* takes its object with this letter: *kadhdhaba bi-*. It is rendered in English simply as "denies", but the letter adds a sense of adhesion: the act of denying touches its object, makes contact with it. What is rejected is not an idea standing at a distance but a thing taken hold of and shoved away.

### الدِّين — what does "dīn" mean here?

The most critical choice of word in the sūra is here.

Root **د ي ن**. This root has several interconnected senses in Arabic, and all of them turn around a single centre: **requital.**

- **دَيْن (*dayn*)** — a debt. The paying back of a thing given.
- **دَانَ لَهُ (*dāna lahū*)** — he submitted to him, he obeyed him. He did not leave the command without a response.
- **دَانَهُ (*dānahū*)** — he called him to account, he gave him what he deserved. An Arab proverb gathers this into a single sentence: *kamā tadīnu tudān* — "as you requite, so shall you be requited."
- **دِين (*dīn*)** — a way, a law, a system that is served.

If one looks closely the same skeleton is in all of them: **a thing given and its coming back.** In a debt the property comes back, in obedience the command finds its response, in the reckoning the deed finds its requital. Even when the word means "religion" in Arabic it carries this skeleton along with it: religion is not a system of belief without return but a bond in which a relation of debt and credit has been established.

**Which of them, then, in this verse?** There are three views:

| View | Proof | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **The Day of Reckoning / the day of requital** | The parallel usages in the Qurʾān point this way. In *al-Muṭaffifīn 83/11* the same structure is written out plainly: "those who deny **the Day of Judgement**". In *al-Infiṭār 82/9*, immediately after "you deny the religion" is said, the angels who write down the deeds are named. In *al-Tīn 95/7*, after "denying the religion" comes "Is God not the best of judges?" In all three the context is the reckoning. | The strongest view. |
| **Religion, that is, Islam / religious truth as a whole** | This is the word's most widespread use. Moreover the continuation of the sūra takes as its subject not the world to come only, but service and morals as well. | Possible, but the parallels within the Qurʾān push it into second place. |
| **Both together** | It is not rare in the Qurʾān for a word to be used so as to carry both senses; the more so when the root gives rise to both. | Though it looks like an evasion, it can be defended. |

**The preference and its ground.** The first view — the day of reckoning and requital — looks the stronger. Its ground is not only the parallel verses but the sūra's own logic:

The sūra points to the man's **deeds** as its proof. A person's repelling the orphan shows not "that he rejects Islam" but **"that he does not believe he will see the requital of what he does"**. If no one is going to call him to account when he repels an orphan, if nothing is entered into any record when he looks past a poor man — the behaviour turns out exactly so. The behaviour the sūra points to is precisely the behaviour of the belief that *there is no requital*.

That is, when you read "dīn" as the day of reckoning the chain of logic between the verses builds itself: *if there is no requital → there is no reckoning → there is no price for what is done to the weak → the orphan is repelled.* On the other reading this chain remains looser.

This preference is not binding. And in practice the difference is small, because by the logic of the root the one who rejects the reckoning has rejected the religion as well: a command with no requital is not a command.

### Context

The sūra's place in the muṣḥaf is striking. Sūrat Quraysh, immediately before it, ends thus: *"Who has fed them against hunger and secured them against fear…"* (Quraysh 106/4). The favour counted out to Quraysh is **being fed**. And immediately after it comes al-Māʿūn: it describes those who will not go near feeding.

Connections of this kind between sūras (*munāsabāt al-suwar*) are a separate field of exegesis. It is right to pass them on not as a proof but as a possibility of reading — the arrangement of the muṣḥaf and the order of revelation are different, and the contribution of the exegete who frames the connection is part of the matter. But the contrast here is too plain to miss: to a community that has been fed, the portrait of one who does not feed is shown.

### What this says to the present

The question the verse asks is, in its present-day form, this: *How do you tell what a person believes?*

The widespread answer: from what he says, from how he defines himself, from which group he stands in. The sūra will reject this answer in the second verse. But already in the first verse it does something: it asks the question not as **"who does not believe?"** but as **"can you see the one who does not believe?"** That is, the matter is not to make a list but to acquire an eye.

## 107/2 — فَذَٰلِكَ ٱلَّذِى يَدُعُّ ٱلْيَتِيمَ

*Fa-dhālika'lladhī yaduʿʿu'l-yatīm*
"That is the one who repels the orphan."

### The backbone of the sūra: why is the answer a behaviour?

The preceding verse asked the question: have you seen the one who denies the religion? Now the answer comes. And one must attend to **what the answer is not.**

The answer could have been these:
- "He is the one who denies God."
- "He is the one who does not believe in the Prophet."
- "He is the one who says there is no world to come."

Every one of these would have been true and none of them would have been surprising. The sūra says none of them. What it says is: **the one who repels the orphan.**

This is the backbone of the sūra and it must be well grasped. What is done here is not to *support* creed with morals; it is to make morals the **proof** of creed. The equation the sūra frames is this:

> What a person believes is measured by what he does in the presence of someone weak.

One has to think about why this is so. Because belief cannot show itself so long as it is not tested. It costs a person nothing to say "there is a day of reckoning" — a word demands no price. But if there is before you a child with no one to defend him, and if shoving him about has **no worldly cost to you at all**, then what you do at that moment is the cleanest measure of what you believe. Because the only thing holding you there is a belief about an unseen reckoning. Nothing else is holding you.

That is, the sūra is not choosing an arbitrary example. It chooses **the case in which belief can be tested on its own**: the moment when there is no worldly sanction whatever.

The Qurʾān frames this link elsewhere too. *Al-Muṭaffifīn 83/12*, describing the one who denies the reckoning: *"And none denies it but every aggressor, every sinner."* Note the direction of the sentence: immorality is shown not as the *consequence* of denial but as its **cause**. First the way of life, then belief conforms to it.

The most comprehensive example is *al-Baqara 2/177*: it begins, "Righteousness is not that you turn your faces to the east and the west"; then it enumerates the articles of faith, and immediately after that it enumerates the giving of wealth to kin, to orphans, to the poor. The two are within a single sentence, in the same list. Not two separate headings.

### The words

**فَ (*fāʾ*)** — The letter of the answer. It announces that the response to the question is coming. In English it is aptly rendered with the presentative "that is the one"; but the *fāʾ* at the same time conveys **succession and connection without a break**. That is, there is no gap between the question and the answer: no sooner is the question asked than the answer sticks to it.

**ذَٰلِكَ (*dhālika*)** — The demonstrative of distance: "that one, there he is". In Arabic *hādhā* is used for what is near, *dhālika* for what is far.

Why a demonstrative of distance? Two explanations are given in classical exegesis and both fit the text:

1. **Belittling and distancing (*taḥqīr*):** The speaker pushes the person he is speaking of away from himself. What we do in English when we say "that man there".
2. **Visibility:** So evident that he can be pointed at even from a distance. That is, "this type is so familiar that you can point him out from far off".

**الَّذِي (*alladhī*) + a verbal clause** — In Arabic the structure "he is the one who does such-and-such" (*huwa'lladhī yafʿalu*) conveys **ḥaṣr**, that is, restriction. It does not say "he is someone who repels the orphan"; it says **"the one who repels the orphan — that is he"**. The description is stuck fast to the person. This man may well have other qualities — he is rich, he is respected, he is a good talker. The verse casts them all aside and makes a single attribute his identity.

**يَدُعُّ (*yaduʿʿu*)** — One of the harshest words in the sūra. Root **د ع ع**, a doubled (*muḍāʿaf*) verb.

Its meaning: **to shove violently, to drive off with a rough motion, to seize by the scruff of the neck and throw out.** Not a polite "no"; a physical repulsion. There is a harshness even in the sound of the word: the doubled ʿayn is pronounced with a constriction that comes from the throat.

The root's other use in the Qurʾān settles the matter. *Al-Ṭūr 52/13*: *"On that day they will be thrust into the fire of Hell with a thrusting"* — the same root, both as verb and as reinforcing verbal noun (*yudaʿʿūna… daʿʿā*). That is, whatever verb the Qurʾān uses to describe people's being flung into Hell, it describes the treatment given to the orphan with the same verb.

This is not chance; it looks like a deliberate pairing. The one who repels the orphan is in fact giving him the treatment of the Day of Resurrection. And that treatment will one day come back to himself.

The verb is again in the **imperfect**: continuous, in the manner of a habit. Not someone who lost his temper once and shoved; this is the normal state of the relation he has with the orphan.

**الْيَتِيم (*al-yatīm*)** — Root **ي ت م**. The original sense of the root is *infirād*: being left single, aloneness, being parted from one's fellow.

This sense appears plainly in the word's other uses: *durratun yatīma* — "an orphan pearl", that is, a pearl with no like and no fellow, standing alone. There is no pity here; there is singleness. At the centre of the word there is **not helplessness but aloneness**. What defines an orphan is not his being poor — a rich orphan is an orphan too — but **his having no one behind him**.

A fine distinction the lexicographers record: in human beings orphanhood comes with the death of **the father**, in animals with the loss of **the mother**. The reason is clear: it is the mother that feeds the animal's young, whereas in that social order it is the father who protects and provides for a human child. That is, the word itself says that what is lost is **protection and livelihood**.

In jurisprudence orphanhood ends with maturity; a report to the effect that "there is no orphanhood after maturity" is transmitted on this point. That is, orphanhood is not a lifelong attribute but the name of **a period of defencelessness**.

**The definite article (al-).** Not "an orphan" but "the orphan". In Arabic this conveys the genus: whoever there is that is an orphan. Not a particular child but a category.

### Historical background: being an orphan in seventh-century Mecca

To understand the weight of this verse one has to know how "protection" worked in that period.

There was no state in Mecca. Police, courts, prison, a force of order — none of these existed. The administration around the Kaʿba was a loose structure carried on by the leading men of the tribes in a consultative manner, and it did not serve the function of protecting the individual.

What, then, protected a person? **The men behind him.** A person's safety depended on the existence of people who would call to account anyone who harmed him. The blood feud, primitive though it looks, was in fact a mechanism of deterrence: if it was clear what would befall anyone who touched you, you were not touched.

In this system **a child whose father had died and who had not yet come of age fell entirely outside the mechanism.** He was not of an age to seek his own right; and the primary person to seek it on his behalf had died. An uncle or some other relative would become his guardian — but there was no institution to supervise that guardianship. The guardian managed the child's property and rendered an account of it to no one.

This is exactly why the Qurʾān speaks with unaccustomed harshness on the matter of an orphan's property. *Al-Nisāʾ 4/10*: *"Those who consume the property of orphans unjustly consume only fire into their bellies."* This harshness is the filling with a divine threat of the place the law has left empty. In a field with no sanction, the only sanction is believing in the reckoning.

And precisely for this reason Sūrat al-Māʿūn chooses the orphan. The orphan is the living examination paper of the question "do you believe in the reckoning?".

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

This verse must be read alongside Sūrat al-Ḍuḥā. Al-Ḍuḥā addresses the Prophet: *"Did He not find you an orphan and give you shelter?"* (al-Ḍuḥā 93/6), and then the command comes: *"So do not oppress the orphan."* (al-Ḍuḥā 93/9).

Now go back to the first verse of al-Māʿūn: *"Have you seen?"* This question is being asked **of one who was himself an orphan**. The one addressed knows the treatment that is being described. That the question is asked as "have you seen" takes on, in this light, a wholly different weight.

The verb used in al-Ḍuḥā is *lā taqhar* (do not oppress, do not crush); in al-Māʿūn it is *yaduʿʿu* (he shoves about). One is a command, the other a description; one says what ought to be done, the other what is done.

And there is also *al-Fajr 89/17*: *"No indeed! Rather, you do not honour the orphan."* The threshold here is higher still: not merely not repelling him but **honouring** him is asked.

## 107/3 — وَلَا يَحُضُّ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ ٱلْمِسْكِينِ

*Wa lā yaḥuḍḍu ʿalā ṭaʿāmi'l-miskīn*
"And does not urge the feeding of the needy."

### Note where the threshold has been brought down to

The whole force of this verse lies in **what it does not say**.

The verse could have said the following:
- *"He devours the property of the needy"* — the heaviest accusation.
- *"He does not feed the needy"* — the expected accusation.
- *"He does not give to the needy"* — the reasonable accusation.

It says none of them. What it says is: **he does not urge others to feed.**

That is, the accusation is not "you do not give" but **"you do not even want it to be given"**. The threshold has been drawn down as far as it will go. Why?

Because urging **costs nothing at all.** To give, you must have property; to urge, you need have nothing. Even a poor person can call others to feed someone. Your word, your look, a sentence you might say at a table — these are free.

The meaning of this is: the sūra catches not the man's hand but his **intention**. A person who does not give can say "I had no means". But for a person who does not even urge there is no such excuse. If he does not urge, the matter is not means but **not wanting**. Deep down he finds the feeding of the needy unnecessary.

There is a second layer of meaning too: urging **takes the matter out of the individual and makes it social.** The alms one person gives feeds one person; a widespread conviction that it ought to be given changes the system. The verse asks for something beyond individual acts of help: that the feeding of the needy should be **a normal, expected, spoken-of thing in society**. The person who does not do this is not merely subtracting one meal; he is obstructing the establishing of a norm.

This is an accusation the sūra directs at silence. *Not doing evil is not counted as enough; not being the voice of good is entered into the record too.*

### The words

**لَا يَحُضُّ (*lā yaḥuḍḍu*)** — Root **ح ض ض**. This too is a doubled verb, just like *yaduʿʿu* in the preceding verse. There is a link between the two verses framed through sound: *yaduʿʿu* / *yaḥuḍḍu*. Both are on the same pattern, both end with a hard doubled consonant. The ear notices that the two sentences are two faces of the same man before it has even worked out the meaning.

The meaning of the verb *ḥaḍḍ*: **to urge insistently, to incite, to try to move someone to act.** There is a compulsion in it, a prodding. Not a gentle piece of advice.

The verb is negated and in the imperfect: he does not do it continually. He has not simply forgotten once; this is his settled state.

**عَلَىٰ (*ʿalā*)** — "Upon". The verb *ḥaḍḍ* takes its object with this letter: to push towards something, to direct towards that side. The letter shows that the urging is directed and has a target.

**طَعَام (*ṭaʿām*)** — Root **ط ع م**: to taste, to eat. *Ṭaʿām* can mean both "food" (the substance) and "feeding" (the act, the verbal noun). This double possibility will determine how the construction is to be read.

**الْمِسْكِين (*al-miskīn*)** — Root **س ك ن**: *sukūn*, immobility, stillness, settledness.

*Miskīn*, by the structure of the word, means **"the person whom poverty has left motionless"**. Close to what we mean in English by "the destitute", but more technical: the one who cannot stir. Poverty has wedged him into such a place that he has become unable to move.

This is a very exact description of poverty. Poverty is not merely "having little money"; it is **the absence of options.** What the poor person really loses is the capacity to move: he cannot change his job, cannot move house, cannot wait, cannot take a risk, cannot afford to fall ill. The word describes this paralysis.

**The difference between *faqīr* and *miskīn*.** The two are counted separately in the Qurʾān (*al-Tawba 9/60*, the heads of expenditure of the alms-tax), so they are not the same thing. But on which of the two is the more needy, the schools of law give opinions in opposite directions: according to the Ḥanafīs the *miskīn* is the more needy, according to the Shāfiʿīs and Ḥanbalīs the *faqīr*. This is a technical question of jurisprudence to do with the distribution of the alms-tax; no side is taken here, I content myself with transmitting it.

An interesting datum from within the Qurʾān: in *al-Kahf 18/79* people who work at sea and **own a boat** are spoken of as "*masākīn*" (the needy). That is, the *miskīn* is not necessarily one who has nothing at all; he is the one who cannot stand on his feet with what he has, the fragile one. In present-day terms: he has property but no security.

### طَعَامِ الْمِسْكِين — "the food of the needy"

The most heavily loaded construction of the sūra is here. Two readings are possible:

**The first reading — a verbal-noun construction:** *Ṭaʿām* is here in the sense of "feeding". The construction then means "the feeding of the needy". Simple and fluent.

**The second reading — a construction of possession:** *Ṭaʿām* is in the sense of "food" (the substance), and the construction conveys ownership: **"the food that belongs to the needy."**

The second reading, once accepted, changes the understanding of property from the ground up. Because then the verse would be saying: the food given is already **not the giver's but the needy person's.** The one who gives is not bestowing a favour; he is **handing over another's property to its owner.**

This reading has very strong support from within the Qurʾān:

- *Al-Dhāriyāt 51/19*: *"And in their wealth there was a **right** for the one who asks and for the one who is deprived."*
- *Al-Maʿārij 70/24-25*: *"And in whose wealth there is **a determined right** for the one who asks and the one who is deprived."*

In both verses the word used is **a right** — not a favour, not a bounty, not a gift. And that it is called "determined" (*maʿlūm*) shows that this is not an arbitrary amount but a defined share.

The consequences of this are serious:

1. **The superiority of the giver disappears.** A man who pays his debt expects no gratitude. This is why the Qurʾān counts the throwing of charity in the face (*menn*) as something that annuls the charity (*al-Baqara 2/264*).
2. **The shame of the receiver disappears.** One who takes what is his by right has not held out his hand.
3. **Not giving becomes not "failing to be generous" but "withholding a right".** One passes from a moral deficiency to a legal violation.

The classical exegetes mention both readings and most prefer the first (the verbal-noun) reading; the second is also entirely valid linguistically and is the ordinary sense of the construction. In my judgement the two readings do not exclude one another: to feed the needy is to hand over what belongs to him. This is a preference and it is not binding.

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

The expression of this verse occurs almost word for word in two further places in the Qurʾān:

- *Al-Ḥāqqa 69/33-34*, where the person in Hell is described: *"For he did not believe in God the Most High **and did not urge the feeding of the needy.**"* Two clauses, joined by "and". Faith and urging, side by side in the same list. The whole claim of the sūra rests on that conjunction.
- *Al-Fajr 89/18*: *"And you do not urge one another to the feeding of the needy."* Here the verb is on the *taḥāḍḍūna* pattern — the *mufāʿala/tafāʿul* form, which conveys reciprocity. That is, not merely "urge others" but "urge one another". The social dimension is expressed openly.

And the most comprehensive is *al-Muddaththir 74/42-46*. Those in Hell are asked: *"What brought you into Saqar?"* The answer is four items:

1. *"We were not among those who prayed."*
2. *"And we did not feed the needy."*
3. *"And we used to plunge into vain talk along with those who plunged."*
4. *"And we used to deny the Day of Judgement."*

Note this list: **prayer, feeding the needy, denying the Day of Judgement.** The three main elements of Sūrat al-Māʿūn are together, in the reverse order, in al-Muddaththir. The two texts say the same thing; one as a confession from the mouths of those in Hell, the other as a diagnosis made while still in this world.

## 107/4 — فَوَيْلٌ لِّلْمُصَلِّينَ

*Fa-waylun li'l-muṣallīn*
"So woe to those who pray…"

### The breaking point of the sūra

Here the sūra turns and displaces the reader.

The first three verses described the one who denies the religion. The reader was safe while listening to it: the one described is somebody else, he is outside, he is not me. In the fourth verse the target changes and **those who pray** is said.

The violence of this change must not be missed. Had the sūra said "woe to the disbelievers" nothing would have happened. Nor would anything have happened had it said "to the hypocrites" — the hypocrite is a bad man already, no one looks for himself there. But when **muṣallīn** — those who pray — is said, the sentence comes straight down upon the listener. Because the listener does pray.

The technique here is this: the sūra first sets up a "them", puts the reader at ease, and then draws the line over to the reader's own side. This is not a text that points outward; it is **a mirror.**

**The letter *fāʾ*** joins the two halves to one another. That is, the "woe" sentence is not an independent addition but the continuation and the consequence of what precedes. The conjunction signals from the very outset that the two groups in fact meet on the same matter.

### وَيْل — wayl

**The meaning of the word.** *Wayl* is a word in Arabic conveying destruction, torment, severe evil and ruin. It is rendered into English as "woe", but that rendering stays weak; "woe" conveys pity, whereas *wayl* conveys **imprecation and sentence**. A closer equivalent: "damnation on him", "let him perish".

The word's opposite occurs in the Qurʾān: *ṭūbā* (blessedness be his). *Wayl* is the exact contrary of *ṭūbā*.

**In the reports** it is said that *wayl* is the name of a valley or of a pit in Hell. This is not a piece of information coming from the dictionary sense of the word but an explanation resting on exegetical reports, and their chains are disputed. The word's real function in the language is not to be a place-name but to be **a declaration of ruin**.

**A grammatical point.** *Waylun* is an indefinite (*nakira*) word and it is the subject at the head of the sentence. Arabic grammar does not normally look kindly on beginning a sentence with an indefinite word; the reason this is allowed here is that the sentence carries the sense of **a prayer or imprecation**.

But the indefiniteness itself carries meaning too. The indefiniteness here is for **magnification and for striking dread** (*tafkhīm*, *tahwīl*): "such a *wayl* as has no description". Had it been definite, a known torment would have been spoken of; left indefinite, its limit is not drawn but left open.

**The other instances of *wayl* in the Qurʾān.** It is instructive to look at whom this word is directed. *Al-Muṭaffifīn 83/1*: *"Woe to those who give short measure and short weight!"* — that is, to people who in trade give little and take much. If you see *wayl* at the head of a verse, what you have before you is most often either denial or **the shortchanging of another's right**. The *wayl* in al-Māʿūn falls exactly into this second category.

### الْمُصَلِّين — those who pray

**The word.** *Muṣallī* means "one who performs the prayer"; an active participle from the *tafʿīl* form. Root **ص ل و**.

There are several views on the original sense of the root. The widespread one is **supplication** — *ṣalāt* in Arabic is supplication. Another explanation attaches it to the word *ṣalawān*: the two bones at the lower end of the back, in the region of the tailbone. On this view the word comes from the movement of bending in the bowing.

A usage transmitted by the lexicographers stands here particularly well: in horse races **the horse that comes in second is called *muṣallī*** — because its head comes close to the *ṣalawān* of the horse in front of it. That is, *muṣallī*, in the word's most concrete state, means **"the one who comes right behind, who follows, who goes on the heels of another"**.

This image tells what the prayer is better than a definition does: the one who prays is a person who has accepted going after someone who goes in front. I pass this on as a point about language; it carries no value as a ruling, but it shows the picture behind the word.

**The definite article and the critical point.** The verse does not say *"li-muṣallīn"* (to some who pray), it says *"li'l-muṣallīn"* (to those who pray). Definite. But which ones?

The answer is in the next verse. And because this is **the most misunderstood place in the sūra**, it must be said plainly:

> The verse does not end here. The fourth verse is not a completed sentence without the adjectival clause in the fifth.

Grammatically *alladhīna hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn* is an **adjectival (*naʿt*)** clause; it qualifies *muṣallīn*, it does not set up a group separate from them. If the qualifier is removed the sentence says something entirely different — and wrong: woe to the prayer itself.

For this reason to quote this verse on its own is to corrupt the text. The sūra passes judgement not on the prayer but on **a particular state of prayer**.

And there is this too: why does the verse, instead of saying "the disbelievers", say by a roundabout route "those who pray"? Because what is targeted is **contenting oneself with the prayer itself**. A person counts his praying as an identity document, a document of acquittal. The sūra invalidates this document: the presence of the service does not excuse that service's producing no result.

## 107/5 — ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ عَن صَلَاتِهِمْ سَاهُونَ

*Alladhīna hum ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn*
"Those who are heedless of their prayer."

### Everything a single letter changes

This verse is much worked over in the classical commentaries, and the reason is a single letter: **عَن (*ʿan*)**.

Had the verse said *"fī ṣalātihim sāhūn"* — "those who go astray **in** their prayer" — the meaning would have been entirely different. Let us compare them thus:

| Structure | Its meaning | Whom it takes in |
|---|---|---|
| **فِي صَلَاتِهِمْ** (*fī* — within) | Distraction *inside* the prayer: the mind slipping, forgetting how many cycles one has prayed, thinking of something else instead of the supplication. | Almost everyone. A consequence of being human. |
| **عَنْ صَلَاتِهِمْ** (*ʿan* — away from) | Moving away *from the prayer itself*: not caring about it, letting its time run out, being indifferent to whether one prays or not. | Those who have a prayer but have no prayer. |

Why is the *fī* reading impossible? For two reasons:

**First, the letter's own meaning.** In Arabic the basic function of the letter *ʿan* is *mujāwaza*: parting from a thing, moving away from it, leaving it behind. *Fī*, on the other hand, conveys being inside. The two point in opposite directions. The verse positions the heedlessness not *inside* the prayer but *towards* the prayer. That is, the heedlessness is not distraction within a prayer that is performed; it is **the attitude taken towards the thing called prayer.**

**Second, the rulings of the religion itself.** To go astray within the prayer is not an offence in Islam; on the contrary, a remedy has been laid down for it: the prostration of forgetfulness. A thing cannot both be tied to a rule for its forgiveness and be threatened with "*wayl*". That would be the text contradicting itself.

This distinction is widely worked over in the exegetical tradition and is a point the lexicographers dwell on. I do not ascribe any particular sentence to any exegete; but the view itself is commonly accepted in the classical sources.

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān: the same pattern, a different letter

The cleanest proof of this reading is in the Qurʾān itself. In Sūrat al-Maʿārij **all but the identical grammatical structure** is used, but the letter changes and it turns into praise:

- *Al-Maʿārij 70/22-23*: *"Except those who pray (*muṣallīn*); those who are **constant** in their prayer."* — *alladhīna hum **ʿalā** ṣalātihim dāʾimūn*
- *Al-Maʿārij 70/34*: *"And those who **guard** their prayer."* — *alladhīna hum **ʿalā** ṣalātihim yuḥāfiẓūn*

Set the structure side by side:

| | al-Māʿūn 107/5 | al-Maʿārij 70/23 |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | *alladhīna hum* | *alladhīna hum* |
| Letter | **عَن** (moving away) | **عَلَىٰ** (standing upon) |
| Construction | *ṣalātihim* | *ṣalātihim* |
| Quality | *sāhūn* (heedless) | *dāʾimūn* (constant) |

The two verses use the same pattern and the only difference is in the letter. One **stands upon** the prayer (*ʿalā* — on top of it, fixed, without parting from it), the other **moves away** from the prayer (*ʿan*). This contrast within the Qurʾān itself is the strongest indication that *ʿan* cannot here mean "within".

Moreover, in the same sūra (al-Maʿārij 70/24-25) "in their wealth there is a determined right" comes immediately afterwards. That is, those praised in al-Maʿārij are exactly the contrary of those blamed in al-Māʿūn: the one who guards his prayer and acknowledges the right of the needy in his wealth. The two sūras can be read as the negative of one another.

### The words

**هُمْ (*hum*)** — Here there is a pronoun written separately which is not grammatically required. *Alladhīna ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn* could have been said; the meaning would not have been damaged. The addition of *hum* is for **specification and emphasis**: "those, exactly those". The pronoun nails the description to the person.

**سَاهُونَ (*sāhūn*)** — Root **س ه و**. Its meaning: distraction, the slipping of attention elsewhere, the mind's not being there.

The distinction the lexicographers draw between *sahw* and *nisyān* (forgetting) is useful here:

- **Nisyān**: the information leaves the mind entirely. If the person wants to remember he cannot.
- **Sahw**: the information is in the mind, but attention has slipped away from it. Once reminded, the person remembers at once.

This distinction fixes where the accusation falls. The person the sūra describes does **not** fail to know the prayer. He knows. He knows that the prayer exists, that its time has come, what it is. He simply **does not care.** Not a lack of knowledge but a lack of interest. This is why there is no ground for excuse.

**A nominal sentence and an active participle.** *Sāhūn* is not a verb but an active participle; and the sentence is a nominal sentence. In Arabic this conveys **fixity and continuity**. That is, not "they are sometimes heedless" but "being heedless is their state". Not a passing condition but a settled attribute.

### Whom does this description take in?

In the classical commentaries the states falling within the scope of this verse are enumerated thus: those who let the prayer's time run out, those indifferent to whether they pray or not, those who while praying have no prayer in their mind at all, those who pray when they see people and leave it off when they are alone (this last item is tied to the next verse).

A balance has to be struck here, and the classical exegetes have struck it scrupulously: the verse does not mean the ordinary believer whose mind wanders in his prayer. Wandering of mind belongs to the human constitution and its remedy lies within the service itself. The verse's target is the one who has turned the prayer into **an operation that touches no part of his life**.

Indeed the Qurʾān says what the prayer ought to do: *"The prayer surely restrains from indecency and from wrong."* (*al-ʿAnkabūt 29/45*). This verse is a measure as much as it is a promise: a prayer that does not restrain has fallen outside the description. The fifth verse of al-Māʿūn is precisely the negative face of this measure.

## 107/6 — ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ يُرَآءُونَ

*Alladhīna hum yurāʾūn*
"Those who make a show of it."

### The word

**يُرَآءُونَ (*yurāʾūna*)** — Root **ر أ ي**: to see. The words *riyāʾ* and *murāʾāt* come from here too.

**The pattern: *mufāʿala*.** This verb is in the *fāʿala* (*mufāʿala*) form, and this form's proper function in Arabic is **reciprocity**: *qātala* (he fought with another), *sāraʿa* (he raced with), *ʿāmala* (he dealt in trade with). It requires two parties.

This explains exactly what ostentation is. Ostentation is not a sin for one person; **it is a transaction.** It has two parties:

- One party **shows**: he displays his service.
- The other party **sees and pays for it**: approval, standing, trust, position.

That is, the ostentatious man makes a payment with his service and receives goods in return. The transaction is complete; the price has been paid up front. For this reason nothing remains owing to him in the world to come — he has taken his wage.

And in this transaction **God is not one of the parties.** The contract has been made between the one who serves and the spectator. The prayer is being performed, but the one it addresses has changed.

**The point about the root: the sūra's turn.** The sūra had begun with this word: **أَرَأَيْتَ** — "have you seen?" The same root: **ر أ ي**.

At the sūra's opening there was a call to see: *you, see.* At its end there is an act of showing: *they show.* The two ends of the same root. The sūra opens by calling to "seeing" and closes by describing "those who live in order to be seen".

This is a real verbal link framed within the sūra; not forced, but a symmetry that stands in the text's own weave.

**Its object is not stated.** The verb does not say "to whom they make a show". In Sūrat al-Nisāʾ this gap is filled: *"When they rise for the prayer they rise sluggishly, **making a show to people**, and they remember God but little."* (*al-Nisāʾ 4/142*) — there *yurāʾūna'l-nās* is said, the object is explicit.

In al-Māʿūn the dropping of the object widens the scope: not to a particular audience but to whoever is looking. In today's language: whoever the spectator may be.

**A difference of tense.** In the preceding verse there was *sāhūn* (an active participle, a fixed state); here there is *yurāʾūna* (a verb, imperfect). In Arabic this difference is meaningful: **heedlessness is a settled condition, whereas ostentation is an act performed anew each time.** Heedlessness stands on the ground; ostentation is played out upon it again and again. I present this distinction as a reading of the grammar; similar points are mentioned in the classical sources.

### Why is ostentation named together with the prayer in particular?

Because the prayer is a **visible** act of service. Fasting is not visible — no one knows that it has not been kept. The intention in the heart is not visible. But the prayer is done with the body, its time is fixed, its place is fixed, it is performed in congregation. Its visibility makes it the act of service most open to ostentation.

This is not a defect of the service but a trial of it. Everything that is visible brings with it the possibility of being displayed.

The Qurʾān frames the same mechanism for charity as well: *"…like one who spends his wealth to be seen by people…"* (*al-Baqara 2/264*). That is, both service and charity are open to the same corruption. What they have in common is being visible.

There are reports in the corpus of hadith about the danger of ostentation; in a report transmitted in the *Musnad* of Aḥmad b. Ḥanbal it is stated that the Prophet named the thing he feared most for his community "the lesser *shirk*" and explained that this was ostentation. I do not quote the exact wording of the report here; there is discussion about its soundness and its variants, but transmissions to this effect are present in the sources.

### A connection with social science: ostentatious piety

Ostentation is the element of the sūra that looks most "modern", and it is a social mechanism worth thinking about.

For ostentation to be able to exist, **a precondition** is required: that piety be socially rewarded. No one makes a show with something nobody values. A behaviour is worth imitating only if it brings standing.

The historical consequence of this is: **in Mecca there was little ground for ostentation.** In Mecca being a Muslim brought not standing but loss — exclusion, boycott, torture. No one could put on airs by praying. Ostentation is a phenomenon of the **Medinan** environment, in which Islam had become dominant in society and piety had turned into social capital.

This is the strongest argument of the view that counts the second half of the sūra Medinan; and, as will be noticed, this is not a transmitted report but a sociological reasoning. It cannot be counted certain — but neither is it unimportant.

There is a concept in the social sciences close to this: **the costly signal.** A group verifies membership only through behaviours that are hard to imitate and that carry a price; by this means those who merely look like insiders are separated from those genuinely bound to it. The trouble is that the mechanism can also work in reverse: when the signal's price falls, or when the signal begins to bring a reward in itself, the behaviour breaks away from its own purpose and becomes merely a **badge**.

What Sūrat al-Māʿūn does is exactly to reject this badge. And its method of rejecting is very practical: it does not argue about the badge, it **asks after the behaviour behind the badge.** The next verse is that very question.

## 107/7 — وَيَمْنَعُونَ ٱلْمَاعُونَ

*Wa yamnaʿūna'l-māʿūn*
"And they withhold the māʿūn."

### يَمْنَعُونَ — to withhold

Root **م ن ع**: to obstruct, to hold back, not to give, to withhold.

The choice of word matters. There is a difference between *not giving* and *withholding*: **manʿ** is not giving a thing that has been asked for. That is, there is a request in the case. If no one has asked, there is no "withholding". So in the picture someone has come to the door, has asked for something, and has been turned away.

The verb is again in the imperfect: habit, continuity.

### ٱلْمَاعُون — a word that occurs once in the Qurʾān

This word occurs **only here** in the Qurʾān. Nowhere else. This is the main cause of the disagreement over its meaning: there is no possibility of comparison within the Qurʾān itself.

**Its root.** Two paths are taken:

1. The root **م ع ن**. From this root *maʿn* means "a little thing, a small thing, an insignificant amount". On this view *māʿūn* becomes **"a thing so small that no one would withhold it"**. The word itself carries within it how shameful the not giving of it is.
2. The root **ع و ن**. From this root *maʿūna* means "help, support". On this view *māʿūn* is **"the thing given by way of help"**.

Both derivations have been defended in the classical sources, and both bring the word's semantic field to the same place: a small but necessary help.

**The views on its meaning:**

| View | Ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **The alms-tax** | The root's sense of "help"; and also the context: the mentioning of the alms-tax after the prayer is a usual pairing in the Qurʾān. This view occurs in reports ascribed to ʿAlī, Ibn ʿUmar, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and Qatāda. | Strong, but there is a problem: the institutionalising of the alms-tax with fixed rates belongs to the Medinan period. If the sūra is counted Meccan this reading is strained. Moreover the expression *"they withheld the māʿūn"* is a strangely vague word for an institutional obligation such as the alms-tax. |
| **The everyday things neighbours borrow from one another** — pots and pans, a bucket, an axe, a needle, salt, fire | In a report transmitted from Ibn Masʿūd it is stated that the Companions understood *māʿūn* as "the lending of the bucket and the cooking pot". It also fits the word's sense of "a small thing" exactly. | The most widely defended view. The dictionary sense of the word and the logic of the sūra support it. |
| **Water** | Some words to do with water in Arabic (such as *maʿīn*) are close in sound; water was the thing whose withholding was counted most shameful. | Weak. The derivation is disputed, and *māʿūn* and *maʿīn* are different words. It can be recorded as a subordinate possibility. |
| **General: every kind of small benefit and support** | The word occurs once in the Qurʾān and **is left unexplained.** Had the Qurʾān wished to define a word it would have defined it. The vagueness may be deliberate. | In my judgement the most apt reading. It does not exclude the others; it takes them all in. |

**The preference and its ground.** I prefer the last view: *māʿūn* is the whole of the small helps that go unnamed — borrowed goods, the alms-tax, water, a neighbourly service, all are included in it. The ground:

The whole structure of the sūra is built on **bringing the threshold down step by step.** In the third verse not even "feeding" was asked for, only "urging". In the last verse what is asked is smaller still: **not even that it be given, but that it be lent.** That is, a thing he will get back. The sūra descends a staircase that grows ever smaller and stops on the lowest step: an object of no value that will not leave his hand, that will come back. And the man will not even give that.

On this reading the vagueness of the word is not a deficiency but the technique itself. *Māʿūn* is not defined, because **every place, every age, every person has its own māʿūn.**

### The architecture of the sūra: from a cosmic claim to a cooking pot

Now look at the sūra as a whole. The scale runs thus:

1. **The Day of Reckoning** — the largest claim in the universe.
2. **The orphan** — an unprotected human being.
3. **The food of the needy** — one meal.
4. **The prayer** — five moments of the day.
5. **Ostentation** — a glance.
6. **Al-Māʿūn** — a bucket, a cooking pot.

The sūra begins at the highest point and descends to the lowest, and there it stops. The last sentence is a matter of pots and pans.

This descent carries the sūra's real claim: **great claims are tested in small things.** Whether a person believes in the resurrection or not is known not from his talk about the resurrection but from whether he gives his neighbour his bucket. The difference of scale is deliberate; by tying the greatest question to the smallest object, the sūra shows where religion lives.

And the last verse is the knot that joins the two halves of the sūra. The man in the first half did not feed the needy; the man in the second half does not give his bucket. One denies the religion, the other prays. **Their hands do the same thing.** The whole argument of the sūra is gathered into this pairing: if the indicator of religion is the hand, then the one who rejects with his tongue and the one who rejects with his hand stand in the same place.

### The structure of sound

All the verses of the sūra end with the same sound: *dīn, yatīm, miskīn, muṣallīn, sāhūn, yurāʾūn, māʿūn.* A long vowel followed by a nasal *nūn* or *mīm*. A rhyme unbroken across seven verses.

This is a shared feature of the short Meccan sūras, and its function is memorability, rhythm and oral transmission. But here it has an additional effect: the unchanging sound makes the sharp change of subject in the middle of the sūra (the passage from the one who denies the religion to the one who prays) **inaudible**. The ear hears that the flow is not interrupted, while the mind notices that the target has changed. This tension increases the shock of the fourth verse.

## The sūra as a whole

### Historical background: the economy of lending

To understand what the last verse says, one has to know what borrowing and lending meant in that society.

In seventh-century Arabia a household did not own its own copy of every tool it needed. A large cooking pot, a millstone for grinding flour, a bucket and rope for drawing water from the well, an axe, a knife for slaughtering an animal — these were expensive, durable and seldom-used implements. The neighbourhood used them **by sharing.**

This was not charity; **it was infrastructure.** What the electricity grid is today, the lending network was then. And it was reciprocal: whoever took your bucket today would give his own axe tomorrow. The system worked like an insurance — everyone could reach everything without anyone having to own everything.

In this frame **not giving the bucket** is not miserliness but **withdrawing from the system.** It is to benefit from the network and contribute nothing to it. The work done in anthropology on reciprocity (from Mauss's classic study of the gift onward) shows the social as well as the economic function of networks of this kind: what is given is not an object but a bond. Whoever refuses the bond is cutting himself off from the community.

That the sūra sets this **beside the prayer** is meaningful for this reason. The prayer too is a bond — the vertical one. Al-Māʿūn too is a bond — the horizontal one. The sūra says that the person who frames the vertical bond and cuts the horizontal one has not framed the first either.

### The orphan and the needy: why these two?

The sūra chose the orphan and the *miskīn* as its examples. This choice is not random. What the two have in common is this: **they cannot make a return.**

- The orphan is a child who has no father to thank you. Doing him a kindness puts no one in your debt.
- The *miskīn* is by definition the person who cannot move. It is not possible for him to send any work your way or to repay you one day.

That is, both are people the kindness done to whom has **no worldly return.** Everything done for them is without recompense. And exactly for this reason both are the reverse of ostentation: ostentation is done by taking a return, whereas the kindness done to these two is done without taking one.

This is why the sūra chooses these two. If you want to see what a person really believes, you look at what he does in a situation that will **gain him nothing.**

### What it does not say: two misreadings

That the sūra is as sharp as this leaves it open to abuse in two directions. Both must be plainly ruled out.

**The first misreading: "The prayer is unnecessary, what matters is morals."** The sūra does not say this. Had it said it, it would have targeted not those who pray but the prayer itself. Whereas what is targeted is a state of prayer **restricted by the qualifier in the fifth verse.** Moreover, in al-Muddaththir 74/43 the first confession of those in Hell is "we were not among those who prayed" — the absence of the prayer is also a deficiency that goes into the record. The sūra does not set service against morals; it says **that service cannot remain without morals.** These two are very different claims.

**The second misreading: using the sūra to take the measure of others.** The manoeuvre the sūra performs in the fourth verse is exactly meant to prevent this. The text first puts the reader in a safe place and then takes him out of it. To pick up these verses and say "that man too falls under the description in al-Māʿūn" is to turn the sūra's own work upside down. The sūra is an instrument of diagnosis, and a diagnosis is made on oneself first.

And one further point which must be noted per the method (`STYLE.md`): the sūra describes no group, no class, no section of society. It describes **attributes.** Whoever bears that attribute, the verse is speaking of him; whatever his identity, his affiliation, his outward appearance.

### What this says to the present

**One. The measure of belief is the hand.** The sūra's main thesis stands in the same place today: what a person believes shows itself not in how he defines himself but in **what he does in the presence of someone who will gain him nothing.** This measure is all the more meaningful in an age in which religious identity can be displayed very easily and very cheaply — because the more the markers of identity multiply, the more they lose their value as a measure.

**Two. Staying silent is not innocence.** The third verse closes off the defence "I did not do it, but I did not oppose it either". Not doing evil is not counted sufficient by the sūra's measure; not being the voice of good is written down too. Its present-day counterpart is plain: not taking direct part in injustice does not produce an excuse for saying nothing about it. Speaking is free, and because it is free it has no excuse.

**Three. The limit of ownership.** The construction "the food of the needy" says that not all of what we possess is ours. This is an idea beyond taxation: within the property in hand there is a share belonging to another that has never been touched. To give this share is not generosity but delivery; not to give it is not miserliness but withholding. This distinction changes how help is given too — because the person who hands over a right expects no gratitude, has no photograph taken, does not remind anyone of it.

**Four. The vanished māʿūn.** Life today has all but liquidated borrowing and lending. Every household owns its own copy of every tool; no one needs anyone else's bucket. This looks like a return of prosperity and in one respect it is. But lending was not merely a transfer of goods — **it was an occasion for contact.** It was a pretext for the door to be knocked on. When al-māʿūn disappeared, the bond framed along with it disappeared too.

The last verse of the sūra comes to the present with this question: *What is there in your hand that would cost you nothing when given to someone, and are you giving it?* This does not have to be money. Time, attention, an introduction, a piece of information, a signature, a reference, an answer. These may be the māʿūn of the age.

**Five. Does the service have a result?** This is the sūra's most uncomfortable question. If the prayer is being performed something must have changed — because the prayer exists in order to restrain from wrong (*al-ʿAnkabūt 29/45*). If nothing changes, the question to be asked is not "am I praying?" but "what is the thing I am performing good for?".

The sūra asks this question with a threat: *wayl*. But the one the threat addresses is the person who counts himself exempt from asking this question. The person who goes on asking it has already begun to step outside the description.
