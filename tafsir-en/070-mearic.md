# 70. Sūrat al-Maʿārij

Forty-four verses. It takes its name from a construct in the third verse: **ذِى ٱلْمَعَارِجِ.**

The sūra opens with a question — but the one asking is not someone who wants an answer. He is mocking. And the whole sūra is the unfolding of the answer given to that mockery.

## What the sūra does

The structure of the sūra rests on a **question and answer**, but the answer does not come from where it is expected.

The question is: *"When?"* That is: where is this punishment you threaten us with?

The expected answer would be a **date**. The sūra gives no date. Instead it does three things:

**First (1-7): it changes the measure of time.** It does not say when the punishment will come; it questions the understanding of time on which the question rests. *"They see it as far off; We see it as near."*

**Second (8-35): it describes that day and the one who has prepared for it.** The scene is set: a melted sky, mountains like carded wool, friends who see one another but cannot ask, a criminal who wants to ransom himself with everything. Then a **diagnosis**: man was created *halūʿ*. And then an **exception**: a eight-item list of those who fall outside that diagnosis.

**Third (36-44): it turns back to those who asked.** People craning their necks to look, gathering in scattered groups from right and left. And a hard answer.

What holds the sūra together is the root in its name: **ع-ر-ج** — to ascend, to climb step by step. The sūra tells of an ascent and of a waiting. And the name of the distance between the two is **patience** — the single command given in the fifth verse of the sūra.

## The hidden spine of the sūra: the root س-أ-ل

In this sūra one root occurs **three times**, and each time it names an entirely different person. Recording this at the outset makes the sūra easier to read:

| Verse | Word | Who | What he does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **70/1** | **سَأَلَ سَآئِلٌ** | The mocker | **He asks** — without wanting an answer |
| **70/10** | **لَا يَسْـَٔلُ حَمِيمٌ حَمِيمًا** | The friend | **He cannot ask** — he would want to |
| **70/25** | **لِّلسَّآئِلِ** | The poor man | **He asks** — and he has a right |

**Three askers in one sūra: the one who mocks, the one who cannot ask, and the one who has a claim.**

In Arabic the root **س-أ-ل** carries both "to ask" and "to request"; *suʾāl* is a question, *sāʾil* is both the one who asks and the one who begs. The two senses stand in one root because both perform the same movement: **turning towards another and expecting something from him.**

And the sūra shows three states of that movement: one who asks expecting nothing in return, one who cannot ask because he will get nothing in return, and one to whom the return is due.

I record this as my own reading. The presence of the three occurrences and the two senses of the root are data that can be verified.

## Meccan or Medinan

There is something close to unanimity that it is Meccan, and the content supports this: the punishment made an object of mockery, the command to the Prophet to be patient, the scene of the resurrection, and a direct depiction of opposition of the sort in *"they crane their necks towards you"* (36).

**One qualification is needed:** the expression in the twenty-fourth verse, *"in their wealth there is a known right"*, seems to point to the Medinan period when it is read as alms (*zakāt*). This is the same matter discussed for the word *māʿūn* in the section on al-Māʿūn, and the caution recorded there holds here too: **the institutionalisation of alms at fixed rates belongs to the Medinan period; but the idea that the poor have a right in one's wealth is present in Meccan sūras as well.** I shall return to this matter when I reach the twenty-fourth verse.

Since the basmala was treated in the section on al-Fātiḥa, it is not taken up again here; the verse numbers are counted without it.

## 70/1-3 — سَأَلَ سَآئِلٌۢ بِعَذَابٍ وَاقِعٍ · لِّلْكَٰفِرِينَ لَيْسَ لَهُۥ دَافِعٌ · مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ ذِى ٱلْمَعَارِجِ

*Saʾala sāʾilun bi-ʿadhābin wāqiʿ · Li'l-kāfirīna laysa lahū dāfiʿ · Mina'llāhi dhi'l-maʿārij*
"An asker asked about a punishment that will fall — for the disbelievers, and none can ward it off. It comes from God, the Lord of the ways of ascent."

### سَأَلَ سَآئِلٌ — "an asker asked"

**The sentence is itself a structure of repetition:** the verb and its subject come from the same root (*saʾala sāʾilun*). In Arabic this is called *ishtiqāq*, and it conveys emphasis.

And note: the subject is **indefinite** — *sāʾilun*, "an asker". He is not named, and we are not told who he is.

This is a stance the sūra establishes from the outset: the one who asks is not made a person. As recorded in the sections on al-Qāriʿa and al-Māʿūn, **indefiniteness conveys either magnification or generalisation.** Here generalisation seems the better fit: the question is not one man's question but the question of an attitude.

### The particle بِـ and the sense of the verb

**The most disputed point in the sentence is that the verb *saʾala* comes with *bāʾ*.** In Arabic *saʾala* normally comes with the particle *ʿan* (*saʾala ʿanhu* — he asked about it) or takes two objects directly. Its coming with *bāʾ* requires explanation.

**There are three readings:**

| Reading | Explanation | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **بِ = عَنْ** | In Arabic *bāʾ* is used in place of *ʿan* with certain verbs. There is an instance of this in the Qurʾān: *"**Ask one who is informed** about Him"* (al-Furqān 25/59 — *fa's'al bihī khabīrā*) | "An asker **asked about** the punishment" — a request for information, or mockery |
| **بِ = a demand** | *Saʾala bi-* means "he demanded, he called for" | "One who demanded **called for** the punishment to come" — a challenge |
| **The reading سَالَ (without hamza)** | Read without the hamza, the verb comes from the root **س-ي-ل**: to flow. *"A valley flowed"* | A depiction of the punishment coming like a flood |

**The third reading is a matter of variant readings.** That it was read without the hamza is recorded in the sources. **Since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which imām, I give no names.**

**Between the first two readings there is a practical difference**, and that difference sets the tone of the verse:

- On the first reading the question is **mockery**: "so where is this punishment?"
- On the second the question is a **challenge**: "bring it on and let us see."

**Both are attitudes the Qurʾān records.** And both come out in the same place: the question is not asked in order to get an answer.

### The disagreement: who is the asker?

Several views are transmitted on this in the classical sources, and **all of them are of the nature of an inference**, since the verse gives no name.

| View | Explanation | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **A mocking mushrik** | Someone challenging by demanding that the punishment come | The reading closest to the wording of the verse; and the Qurʾān records this attitude elsewhere |
| **A particular person** | Names are mentioned in the reports | The names do not agree with one another; **I give no name** |
| **A believer asking** | Someone asking, "for whom is this punishment?" | The qualification *li'l-kāfirīn* in the second verse makes this reading possible |

**The strongest indication within the Qurʾān is this**, and most of the reports transmitted rest on it:

*"And when they said: 'O God! If this is the truth from You, then rain down stones upon us from the sky, or bring us a painful punishment.'"* (al-Anfāl 8/32)

This verse records exactly the attitude al-Maʿārij 70/1 describes: **demanding** the punishment itself. And it supports the "demand" reading of the *bāʾ*.

**But I do not claim that the verse of al-Anfāl is the occasion of revelation of this sūra.** What I record is that the two verses describe the same attitude. The personal names given in the reports I do not transmit, **for the same reason set down in the sections on al-Māʿūn and al-Humaza**: the names do not agree with one another, and this suggests that they are answers given after the event to the question "who was meant?"

And the measure of the tradition of legal theory applies here too: *al-ʿibratu bi-ʿumūmi'l-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣi's-sabab* — the ruling follows the generality of the wording.

### عَذَابٍ وَاقِعٍ — a punishment that will fall

**Root: و-ق-ع.** To fall, to occur, to come to pass. The concrete sense of the root is **falling**.

**And this word links up with the fifteenth verse of the preceding sūra:**

| | **al-Ḥāqqa 69/15** | **al-Maʿārij 70/1** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *وَقَعَتِ ٱلْوَاقِعَةُ* | *بِعَذَابٍ **وَاقِعٍ*** |
| Form | **Perfect** — "it occurred" | **Active participle** — "it will occur, it is occurring" |
| Who says it | The narrator — conveying certainty | Said about **the mocker** |

**Al-Ḥāqqa announced in the perfect tense that the event had occurred; al-Maʿārij uses the same root as an active participle.**

And the difference is meaningful. In al-Ḥāqqa the scene had been set: the event had happened. In al-Maʿārij there is something that has not yet happened but **is happening**. The active participle conveys continuity and nearness.

So in the very first verse of the sūra, the answer to the question stands **inside the word**: the man asks "when?"; the verse names the punishment "that which is occurring".

I record this as my own reading. The root and the patterns of the two words are data that can be verified.

And this too should be recorded: *ٱلْوَاقِعَة* is the name of the 56th sūra of the Qurʾān and one of the names of the resurrection. In the table of names for the resurrection in the section on al-Qāriʿa, the aspect it was noted to bring forward was recorded thus: **the inevitability of its occurring.**

### لِّلْكَٰفِرِينَ لَيْسَ لَهُۥ دَافِعٌ

**This verse is the first answer to the question** — and the answer is not what the question asked.

The man asked **"when?"**. The verse answers **"to whom"** and **"it cannot be warded off"**.

**That is, the sūra passes over the time part of the question and gives two other pieces of information: the addressee and the inevitability.**

This is not an evasion but a **reframing**. Inside the question there is a hidden assumption: "if it is not coming, it will never come." The verse takes aim at that assumption.

**لِّلْكَٰفِرِينَ** — the lām conveys **entitlement** (*istiḥqāq*): "**for** the disbelievers", that is, reserved for them. The definite article is there too: not a particular group, but everyone who bears that attribute.

**دَافِع — Root: د-ف-ع.** *Dafaʿa* — to push, to drive away, to ward off. *Mudāfaʿa* and *dafʿ* are from the same root. **دَافِع** is the active participle: **the one who would ward it off.**

And the structure *laysa lahū dāfiʿ* does something like the "closing both doors" method recorded in the section on al-Ṭāriq — but in a single word: no one can ward off the punishment. Neither he himself nor anyone else.

### مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ ذِى ٱلْمَعَارِجِ

**The third verse gives the source of the answer** — and the name of the sūra stands here.

The particle **مِّنَ** conveys a point of origin: the punishment comes **from there**.

### ٱلْمَعَارِج — the ways of ascent

**Root: ع-ر-ج.** The primary sense of the root is **to rise, to go up; to climb by a sloping path.**

Its derivatives show the field of the root:

- **عَرَجَ** — he went up. *"Then it **ascends** to Him in a day"* (al-Sajda 32/5); *"a ladder **into** the sky"* (al-Anʿām 6/35 — *sullaman fi's-samāʾ*).
- **مِعْرَاج** — a ladder, a means of going up. Its plural is **مَعَارِج**.
- **مَعَارِج** — occurs once more in the Qurʾān, and there in its concrete sense: *"…and for their houses roofs of silver and **stairways** by which to go up"* (al-Zukhruf 43/33).
- **أَعْرَج** — lame. An interesting branch of the root: a lame person, having one leg shorter, moves **up and down** as he walks; his step is not level but sloped.

**Note the last item.** At the core of the root there is **unevenness, slope**. A ladder is sloped; a ramp is sloped; the gait of a lame man is sloped. In this Arabic root, to ascend is **not to go straight**: to go up by changing direction, step by step.

I transmit this explanation as a relation drawn by the lexicographers; I do not present it as a settled etymological ruling.

**And this explains the word's function in this sūra.** Al-Zukhruf 43/33 gives the concrete state of the word: the stairway by which one goes up to a house. So *maʿārij* says that there is a **road** between sky and earth — and that road is not a distance crossed in one step but a road with **steps**.

### ذِى ٱلْمَعَارِج — a title

**This construct occurs only here in the Qurʾān.**

And the form of the construct is worth noting: titles built with **ذُو** (owner, possessor) are few in the Qurʾān, and each is chosen for a particular context:

| Title | Where | Context |
|---|---|---|
| **ذِى ٱلْعَرْش** | al-Takwīr 81/20 and elsewhere | **A chain of authority** — treated in the section on al-Takwīr |
| **ذِى ٱلْمَعَارِج** | al-Maʿārij 70/3 | **The ways of ascent** |
| **ذِى ٱلْجَلَٰلِ وَٱلْإِكْرَامِ** | al-Raḥmān 55/27, 78 | Majesty and honour |
| **ذُو ٱلْفَضْلِ ٱلْعَظِيمِ** | al-Baqara 2/105 and elsewhere | Bounty |

The principle recorded in the section on al-Takwīr works here too: **the title is chosen to suit the subject.** There the subject was a chain of authority, and "Lord of the Throne" was chosen. Here the subject is **distance and time**; "Lord of the ways of ascent" is chosen.

And the next verse will explain this: we shall be told how long the ways take.

**One further reading.** Some exegetes explain *maʿārij* as "degrees, ranks": the degrees of elevation God grants. This reading too is possible in the language, and it rests on the sense of "height" in the word. Both appear in the classical sources; I do not impose a preference, since the next verse already opens out the first reading (road/ladder).

### The word order of the three verses

The three verses are one sentence, and its parts come in order:

1. **The event:** an asker asked — about a punishment that will fall.
2. **The addressee and its character:** for the disbelievers; there is none to ward it off.
3. **The source:** from God, the Lord of the ways of ascent.

And note: the sentence is built **backwards**. First the question, then the character of the punishment, and last of all its source.

So the sūra takes the question and gives the answer **step by step** — exactly in the manner its name announces.

I record this as an observation about the word order; the relation I draw between the name and the structure is a reading.

## 70/4 — تَعْرُجُ ٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةُ وَٱلرُّوحُ إِلَيْهِ فِى يَوْمٍ كَانَ مِقْدَارُهُۥ خَمْسِينَ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ

*Taʿruju'l-malāʾikatu wa'r-rūḥu ilayhi fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhū khamsīna alfa sana*
"The angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a day whose measure is fifty thousand years."

### تَعْرُجُ — they ascend

The verb is from the same root as *maʿārij* in the preceding verse. So the sūra first names the road (3), then shows those who travel it (4).

The verb is **imperfect**: it conveys continuity and renewal. Not a single event but a **continuing** movement.

### ٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةُ وَٱلرُّوحُ — the angels and the Spirit

**This pair occurs in several places in the Qurʾān**, and its best-known occurrence is in Sūrat al-Qadr:

| | **al-Qadr 97/4** | **al-Maʿārij 70/4** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *تَنَزَّلُ **ٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةُ وَٱلرُّوحُ** فِيهَا* | *تَعْرُجُ **ٱلْمَلَٰٓئِكَةُ وَٱلرُّوحُ** إِلَيْهِ* |
| Verb | **تَنَزَّلُ** — they descend | **تَعْرُجُ** — they ascend |
| Direction | **Downward** | **Upward** |
| When | *Laylat al-Qadr* — its measure **better than a thousand months** | A day whose measure is **fifty thousand years** |
| What is brought / taken | *مِن كُلِّ أَمْرٍ* — every command | (not specified) |

**The same two subjects, two opposite verbs, two different measures.**

This verse was treated in the section on al-Qadr; I do not repeat it here. But the picture the two sūras build together should be recorded: **the Qurʾān says that the same beings both descend and ascend.** That is, the movement between sky and earth is **two-way**; and the measure of both directions has been given.

And both measures are measures of **time**, but they work in opposite directions: in al-Qadr a single night is counted as longer than a thousand months (a short span, a great value); in al-Maʿārij a single day is counted as fifty thousand years (a long span, a great distance).

I record this as a reading drawn from the comparison of the two sūras. The wordings of the two verses are data that can be verified.

**Who is ٱلرُّوح?** It is disputed, and this disagreement was recorded in the section on al-Qadr as well. The classical views: Gabriel; a great being distinct from the angels; or a class of angels. **I express no preference**; the Qurʾān gives no definition.

The grammatical datum that must be recorded is this: *wa'r-rūḥ* comes by **conjunction**, that is, it is mentioned **separately** from the angels. In Arabic, singling out one member of a class for separate mention (*ʿaṭf al-khāṣṣ ʿalā'l-ʿāmm*) is done in order to **bring that member forward**. So the Spirit, even if not outside the angels, occupies a position that calls for separate mention.

### إِلَيْهِ — "to Him"

The pronoun refers to God. And the particle *ilā* conveys a **destination**.

One qualification is needed, and it is commonly set down in the classical sources: this expression does not convey **place**. The ascent describes the movement of the angels; the place arrived at is not a place where God is but **the centre of command and decree**. The distinction between *tafwīḍ* and *taʾwīl* recorded in the section on al-Fajr 89/22 (*"your Lord comes"*) holds here too, and I do not repeat that discussion. The shared principle is al-Shūrā 42/11: *"There is nothing like Him."*

### فِى يَوْمٍ كَانَ مِقْدَارُهُۥ خَمْسِينَ أَلْفَ سَنَةٍ

**This is the most discussed expression in the sūra.**

**مِقْدَار — Root: ق-د-ر.** This root was treated in detail in the section on **al-Fajr 89/16** (three families of meaning: power/capacity, measure/determination, straitening) and was also taken up in the section on **Sūrat al-Qadr**. I do not repeat it.

*Miqdār* is on the *mifʿāl* pattern and conveys **an instrument of measurement or the quantity measured**. So the verse does not say "that day is fifty thousand years"; it says **"the measure of that day is fifty thousand years"**. It passes through the act of measuring.

### Fifty thousand years: the classical explanations

**Let us first set out the other measures within the Qurʾān**, because the matter is discussed not on this verse alone but in a threefold comparison:

| Verse | Expression | Context |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Sajda 32/5** | *"Then it ascends to Him in a day whose measure is **a thousand years** of what you count"* | The command descending from sky to earth and returning |
| **al-Ḥajj 22/47** | *"A day with your Lord is **like a thousand years** of what you count"* | The punishment being demanded in haste |
| **al-Maʿārij 70/4** | *"…in a day whose measure is **fifty thousand years**"* | The ascent of the angels |

**The principal explanations of the classical exegetes concerning this trio:**

| Explanation | Account |
|---|---|
| **Different days** | The "day" in al-Sajda and the "day" in al-Maʿārij are not the same day. Al-Sajda speaks of the cycle by which the command descends and returns, al-Maʿārij of the day of resurrection. Different events, different measures |
| **Different stations of the same day** | The day of resurrection has stages called *mawqif*; each stage has its own measure. The whole must not be confused with a part |
| **Different distances of ascent** | The angels come from different levels; each ascent has its own distance and therefore its own measure |
| **The number conveys difficulty, not duration** | In Arabic large numbers often convey not an exact count but **magnitude and weight**. "Fifty thousand years" says how unbearable the day will be |
| **It is not the same for everyone** | The day is experienced differently according to a person's state; for the believer it is shortened. Reports to this effect are transmitted |

**One qualification about the last item:** reports to this effect do exist in the sources and are widely transmitted. **I do not give the wording of the report or its source here**, because I cannot rule with certainty on its soundness or its variants. I confine myself to recording that the idea is found in the sources.

**The fourth explanation is, in terms of language, the reading that requires the fewest assumptions**, and it is supported by the Qurʾān's own usage. As recorded in the section on al-Ḥāqqa, when the Qurʾān says *"even if you ask forgiveness for them seventy times"* (al-Tawba 9/80) it is not giving an exact count. In Arabic certain large numbers convey **multitude**.

**But I do not impose a preference.** None of the explanations runs against the wording of the text, and I can see no decisive evidence requiring a choice among them.

### A limit: claims from modern physics

**Per the method (`STYLE.md`) I state this plainly: I do not proceed from this verse to the theory of relativity, to the relativity of time, or to any claim of modern physics.**

Such connections are frequently drawn in the popular literature. The reason for not drawing them is this:

**One.** The verse states not a proposition of physics but a **measure**. The expression "a day being equivalent to fifty thousand years" is an ordinary formula used in Arabic and in many other languages to convey the weight of an experience of time. To read that formula as a physical equation is to take the text outside its own language.

**Two.** The principle set down in `STYLE.md` is this: *"No modern piece of knowledge is forced onto a verse."* And in the section on al-Ṭāriq, claims about embryology and astronomy were rejected on the same ground; in the section on al-Fajr the same qualification was set down for claims about archaeology.

**Three.** When such a link is made, the truth of the text becomes tied to the fate of the link. As recorded in the section on al-Fajr: *"to tie the truth of a text to an excavation report is to tie the text to the fate of that report."* The same holds for a theory of physics.

**What the verse says is plain and is already enough:** man's measure of time and the measure of time of this event are not the same. That is the subject of the next three verses.

### The work the verse does in the sūra

And now the real question: **why is this verse here?**

In the first verse someone asked "when?". The fourth verse gives a time — but not a time of the kind he asked for.

The man wanted a **date**. The verse gave a **unit of measurement**.

And this overturns the assumption on which the question rested: the man was calculating with his own measure of time ("we have waited this long, and it has not come"). The verse says that the unit in which the calculation is made is **wrong**.

I record this as my own reading. Its ground is that the question in the first verse is a question about time and that the fourth verse gives a measure of time.

## 70/5 — فَٱصْبِرْ صَبْرًا جَمِيلًا

*Fa'ṣbir ṣabran jamīlā*
"So be patient with a beautiful patience."

### فَ — a consequence

The **فَ** at the head ties the four verses to this command: *"so."* That is, this command is the **consequence** of the measure of fifty thousand years.

The chain of reasoning is this: the punishment is coming (1-2), its source is known (3), the measure of time is other than yours (4) — **so wait.**

### The two commands of the sūra

In this sūra there are **two commands** addressed to the one spoken to (the Prophet), and the two stand at the two ends of the sūra:

| Verse | Command | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **70/5** | **فَٱصْبِرْ** | Be patient |
| **70/42** | **فَذَرْهُمْ** | Leave them |

And both begin with **فَ**; both come as the conclusion of a piece of reasoning.

**The two commands together describe a stance:** be patient and let go. That is, neither hurry nor force.

I record this as an observation about the structure; the position of the two commands can be counted in the text.

### صَبْر — patience

**Root: ص-ب-ر.** This root was treated in the section on **al-Baqara 2/45**. I do not repeat the findings there; in summary:

- The primary sense of the root is **to hold, to restrain, to confine**. To halt something in a place by force.
- *"This differs from 'enduring'. Enduring is **passive** — you bear what befalls you. Patience is **active**: to hold the self, not to let it go where it wants to go."*
- For this reason patience is asked for not only in affliction but **in worship and in refraining from sin** as well; in all of them the work done is the same: holding.

**What is to be added here is the adjective.**

### جَمِيلًا — "beautiful"

**Root: ج-م-ل.** *Jamāl* — beauty. *Jamīl* — beautiful.

**And the use of this adjective in the Qurʾān is worth noting.** The word qualifies four different actions in four separate places, and all four belong to the same field:

| Verse | Expression | What is qualified |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Maʿārij 70/5** | *صَبْرًا جَمِيلًا* | **Patience** |
| **al-Ḥijr 15/85** | *فَٱصْفَحِ ٱلصَّفْحَ ٱلْجَمِيلَ* | **Pardon / turning away** |
| **al-Muzzammil 73/10** | *وَٱهْجُرْهُمْ هَجْرًا جَمِيلًا* | **Parting / keeping apart** |
| **al-Aḥzāb 33/28** | *وَأُسَرِّحْكُنَّ سَرَاحًا جَمِيلًا* | **Release / divorce** |

**What the four have in common is this: all four are responses given in the face of a hurt.**

Patience is in the face of a hardship; *ṣafḥ* in the face of a fault; *hajr* in the face of ill-treatment; *sarāḥ* at the ending of a relationship.

And in all four the adjective *jamīl* is added. That is, the Qurʾān asks for a distinct quality in behaviour at the moment of being hurt.

**This is a pattern of usage that can be verified**; the wordings of the four verses are in the text. The interpretation that the pattern constitutes an order is my own.

### What does "beautiful patience" mean?

The most widespread explanation in the classical sources is this: **patience in which there is no complaint**; a person's not pouring out his trouble to people.

This explanation is widely transmitted in the tradition. **I do not ascribe it to a particular person**, but the view itself is commonly accepted in the classical sources.

**And within the Qurʾān there is an indication that limits this explanation** — one that guards against misunderstanding it.

The expression *ṣabr jamīl* occurs once more in the Qurʾān, from the mouth of Jacob:

*"From now on, what falls to me is **a beautiful patience**."* (Yūsuf 12/18 and 12/83)

And in the same sūra the same man says:

*"I complain of my grief and my sorrow **to God alone**."* (Yūsuf 12/86)

**Read together, the two verses draw the boundary of "beautiful patience":** Jacob says his patience is *jamīl*, and in the same sūra he voices his grief. So *ṣabr jamīl* is not the absence of feeling pain, nor never speaking at all.

The difference lies in **to whom** it is spoken. Jacob presents his pain to God. The forbidden form of complaint is the form directed at people and seeking a fellow sufferer.

**Let us record:** the word **بَثّ** in Yūsuf 12/86 was already mentioned in the section on al-Qāriʿa when the root ب-ث-ث was treated — what was recorded there was: *"grief that cannot be held inside and spills out… the spreading outward of something that has accumulated within."*

So the Qurʾān does not forbid pouring out what has accumulated within; it fixes its **direction**.

I record this reading as my own. The wordings of the three verses and the analysis of the root in the section on al-Qāriʿa are data that can be verified.

### To whom the command is addressed

The command is to a singular masculine addressee: *iṣbir*. The first addressee is the Prophet.

But the measure recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn holds here too: *khiṭāb li-kulli man yaṣluḥu lahū* — "an address to everyone fit to receive it". The sentence is framed as though spoken to one person and left open to all.

### And the link with the diagnosis of the sūra

**Now I want to draw attention to something, and it is one of the most striking internal links in the sūra.**

In the fifth verse the sūra commands *patience*. And between the nineteenth and twenty-first verses it delivers its diagnosis of man:

*"Man was created **halūʿ**: when evil touches him, **جَزُوع** (wailing); when good touches him, **مَنُوع** (withholding)."*

**جَزُوع** (*jazūʿ*) is in Arabic the exact opposite of **صَبُور** (*ṣabūr*). *Jazaʿ* is the collapse, the wailing, the intolerance that is the contrary of patience. The Qurʾān places the two side by side plainly in one place:

*"Whether we wail or are patient, it is all the same for us."* (Ibrāhīm 14/21 — *a-jaziʿnā am ṣabarnā*)

**So the sūra gives the remedy in the fifth verse before it names the illness in the nineteenth.**

I record this as my own reading. The opposition of the two words is beyond dispute in Arabic, and Ibrāhīm 14/21 confirms it within the Qurʾān; the conclusion I draw from the order of the two verses within the sūra is my own.

## 70/6-7 — إِنَّهُمْ يَرَوْنَهُۥ بَعِيدًا · وَنَرَىٰهُ قَرِيبًا

*Innahum yarawnahū baʿīdā · Wa narāhu qarībā*
"They see it as far off; We see it as near."

### The symmetry of the two verses

**One of the most tightly built oppositions in the Qurʾān, and in fewer than ten words.**

| | **Verse 6** | **Verse 7** |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | **هُمْ** — they | **نَحْنُ** (implicit in the verb) — We |
| Verb | *يَرَوْنَ* — they see | *نَرَىٰ* — We see |
| Object | **هُ** — it | **هُ** — it |
| Verdict | **بَعِيدًا** — far | **قَرِيبًا** — near |

The same verb, the same object, two different subjects, two opposite verdicts. The only thing that changes is **who is looking.**

### The choice of the verb

**And here is the real point.** The verse does not say *"they are mistaken"*. Nor does it say *"it is near"*.

It says **"We see it as near"**.

That is, the correction is made not at the level of fact but at the level of **seeing**. Both sides "see"; the measure is who does the seeing.

This has a consequence worth recording: the verse implies that farness and nearness are **not absolute properties**. Whether a thing is far or near depends on the position of the one looking.

And the right position is specified: **We.**

I record this as my own reading. Its ground is that the same verb is used in both verses — the verse could have used another verb (to know, to suppose), and did not.

### بَعِيد — far

**Root: ب-ع-د.** To be far. And in Arabic this word carries one further sense besides **distance**: **improbability, remoteness from reason.**

English has the same metaphor: "a remote possibility", "that looks like a long shot".

The Qurʾān uses this second sense plainly:

- *"'When we are dead and have become dust? That is a **far-fetched** return.'"* (Qāf 50/3) — *rajʿun baʿīd*, "a return that will not happen".
- *"How can they **attain** it from a place so far off?"* (Sabaʾ 34/52)
- *"They see it as **far off**."* (this verse)

**So the *baʿīd* of the verse is not merely "far off in time"; it may also mean "impossible, out of the question".**

And this joins with the question in the first verse: when the man asked "when?", he really meant "never". The sixth verse names that supposition.

**Both readings occur in the classical sources**, and they do not exclude one another: seeing it as far in time, seeing it as far in probability. In my judgement the two work together; **this is not binding.**

### قَرِيب — near

**Root: ق-ر-ب.** To be near. *Qurba*, *taqarrub* and *aqribāʾ* are from the same root.

**The Qurʾān announces the nearness of the Hour in many places:**

- *"The Hour has **drawn near**, and the moon has split."* (al-Qamar 54/1)
- *"The reckoning of mankind has **drawn near**, while they turn away in heedlessness."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/1)
- *"And perhaps it is **near**."* (al-Aḥzāb 33/63; al-Shūrā 42/17)

**Al-Shūrā 42/17 is particularly worth noting**, because there the formula *mā yudrīka* is used: *"What will make you know? Perhaps the Hour is **near**."* By the rule recorded in the sections on al-Humaza and al-Qāriʿa, the *muḍāriʿ* form announces that no information will follow it — and indeed no date is given there either.

So even when the Qurʾān says the Hour is near, it **gives no date**. Two pieces of information are kept apart: nearness is announced, the time is not.

### What does "near" mean?

**And here an explanation is needed**, because the expression is open to misunderstanding.

The sūra spoke of a measure of fifty thousand years (4), and then says "near" (7). Do the two contradict each other?

No — and the reason they do not lies in the fourth verse itself. The fourth verse had already said that **the measure is different**. The seventh verse gives the consequence of that difference: what is far by your measure is near by the other measure.

So the fourth and seventh verses are parts of a single argument:

1. **The measure is different** (4).
2. **Therefore the perception of distance is different too** (6-7).

I record this as a reading of the structure; the order of the four verses is in the text.

**And there is this as well.** A thing's being "near" depends as much on **certainty** as on distance. Something certain to come is near however far away it is — because the interval does not remove it, it only defers it.

The Qurʾān frames this idea plainly elsewhere: *"On the day they see it, it will be as though they had tarried [in the world] but an evening or a morning."* (al-Nāziʿāt 79/46)

That is, elapsed time shrinks once it has elapsed. The waiting is long, the past is short.

I record this as a reading.

## 70/8-9 — يَوْمَ تَكُونُ ٱلسَّمَآءُ كَٱلْمُهْلِ · وَتَكُونُ ٱلْجِبَالُ كَٱلْعِهْنِ

*Yawma takūnu's-samāʾu ka'l-muhl · Wa takūnu'l-jibālu ka'l-ʿihn*
"On the day the sky becomes like molten metal, and the mountains become like wool."

### Two similes

Here the sūra opens the scene, and it opens it with two similes. This structure was present in Sūrat al-Qāriʿa too, and was treated in detail there.

**This verse was already mentioned in the section on al-Qāriʿa**, where the following was recorded: *"Al-Maʿārij 70/9 and al-Qāriʿa 101/5 are almost identical: 'and the mountains become like wool.' Al-Qāriʿa adds a single word: ٱلْمَنفُوش."*

And there *ʿihn* and *manfūsh* were analysed in detail:

- **عِهْن** — wool; according to the majority, specifically **dyed wool.** Some lexicographers say there is no qualification of colour; the disagreement was recorded.
- **ٱلْمَنفُوش** — carded, teased apart; root ن-ف-ش. When raw wool is carded the fibres separate, the volume grows, the weight stays the same, the density falls.
- The conclusion recorded in the section on al-Qāriʿa: *"Al-Maʿārij 70/9 was content with the first stage. Al-Qāriʿa adds the second… the sūra reduces the weight of the mountain to nothing in two steps."*

**I do not repeat these.** What is to be added here is al-Maʿārij's own pairing — because al-Qāriʿa's pairing was a different one.

### The pairings of the two sūras

| | **al-Qāriʿa 101/4-5** | **al-Maʿārij 70/8-9** |
|---|---|---|
| First simile | **People** → scattered moths | **The sky** → molten metal |
| Second simile | **Mountains** → carded wool | **Mountains** → wool |
| Logic of the pairing | **The lightest living thing ↔ the heaviest body** | **The highest ↔ the most solid** |
| Shared conclusion | Both are scattered | Both **lose their solidity** |

**Al-Qāriʿa's pairing was the living and the lifeless; al-Maʿārij's is the sky and the earth.**

And the direction of the two similes is **opposite** — this is peculiar to al-Maʿārij and worth recording:

- **The sky** becomes *muhl*: from solid to **liquid**. Melting is a dissolution downward; what melts flows, and sinks.
- **The mountains** become *ʿihn*: from solid to **fluffed**. Wool is air; it lifts, it flies.

**So both bodies lose their firmness, but in two different directions:** one flows, the other flies.

I record this as my own reading. The physical properties of the two substances (molten metal flows, carded wool flies) are beyond dispute; the conclusion I draw from the opposition of the two directions is my own.

### ٱلْمُهْل — molten metal

**Root: م-ه-ل.** And the root's primary sense in Arabic seems not to be at work here, so attention is needed.

**مَهْل** — to act slowly, not to hurry. *Muhla* (granting a term), *imhāl* and *tamahhul* are from the same root. In the Qurʾān: *"Grant the disbelievers a **respite**"* (al-Ṭāriq 86/17 — *amhilhum*).

**ٱلْمُهْل**, on the other hand, is described in the classical sources thus: **molten metal**, melted copper or lead; and according to some lexicographers **the dregs of olive oil**, a thick, dark liquid.

**The link between the two senses:** molten metal **flows heavily**. Water flows fast; molten metal flows slowly and heavily. The "acting slowly" in the root *mahl* and the "heavily flowing liquid" of *muhl* meet in the same idea.

I transmit this explanation; I issue no ruling on the historical development of the root. The sense at work in the verse is, according to the majority of the lexicographers, **molten metal**.

**Its other occurrences in the Qurʾān make the field of the word clear:**

- *"…they will be given relief with water like **molten metal** that scalds their faces."* (al-Kahf 18/29)
- *"[The zaqqūm] boils in the bellies like **molten metal**."* (al-Dukhān 44/45)

In both, *muhl* is a **substance of punishment**: a liquid drunk or poured.

**And here the sky is likened to it.**

That is, the verse takes the substance of the punishment and gives it to **the sky**. The thing above you turns into the thing given to drink in the Fire.

I record this as my own reading; the wordings of the three verses can be verified.

### A comparison with al-Raḥmān

The sky melting and changing colour is told once more in the Qurʾān:

*"When the sky is split open and becomes **a red rose like molten oil**…"* (al-Raḥmān 55/37)

The word **ٱلدِّهَان** here likewise conveys a melted, thick substance, and belongs to the same field as *muhl*. The two verses give the same image with two different substances.

In the section on al-Ḥāqqa a table was given of the states of the sky at the resurrection (*kushiṭat*, *infaṭarat*, *tushaqqaq*, *ṭuwā*, *wāhiya*, *wardatan ka'd-dihān*). Al-Maʿārij adds one more item to this list: **ka'l-muhl.**

And the measure recorded in the section on al-Ḥāqqa works here too: the sky's chief attribute in the Qurʾān is **firmness** (*shidād*, *maḥfūẓ*, *lā fuṭūr*). The scenes of the resurrection tell of the loss of that firmness. Al-Ḥāqqa said *wāhiya* (its fabric slackened); al-Maʿārij says *ka'l-muhl* (melted).

**Two neighbouring sūras describe the same loss in two different states: one a slackening, the other a melting.**

I record this as my own reading.

### The word order of the two verses

And a grammatical observation: the two verses are **on exactly the same pattern.**

> *wa takūnu* + definite noun + *ka* + definite noun

In the section on al-Qāriʿa it was recorded that the two similes there were matched on the pattern *"ka + definite noun + definite passive participle"*. Al-Maʿārij's pattern is plainer: there is no passive participle.

And its verse-endings are accordingly: *al-muhl / al-ʿihn* — short, single-syllable closes. Al-Qāriʿa's *al-mabthūth / al-manfūsh* were longer and denser.

So al-Maʿārij passes through the same scene **faster**. The reason may be plain: the subject of al-Maʿārij is not the landscape but the **human relations** in the middle of the landscape. Indeed the next verse goes straight there.

I record this as my own reading.

## 70/10-11 — وَلَا يَسْـَٔلُ حَمِيمٌ حَمِيمًا · يُبَصَّرُونَهُمْ

*Wa lā yasʾalu ḥamīmun ḥamīmā · Yubaṣṣarūnahum*
"No friend will ask after a friend. Yet they will be made to see one another."

### The scene changes

The eighth and ninth verses were a cosmic landscape: the sky, the mountains. The tenth verse descends all at once to a space between **two people**.

And this transition shows what the sūra means to do. The scene of the resurrection is scenery here; the real subject is **what is left between people** in that scenery.

### حَمِيمٌ حَمِيمًا — the same word twice

**Root: ح-م-م.** This word was treated in detail in the section on **al-Ḥāqqa 69/35**: the two branches of meaning in the root (boiling water / close friend), the "heat" link drawn by the lexicographers, and the fact that *ḥamīm* means "one whose concern is warm". I do not repeat it.

**And there a comparison with this verse was made:**

| | **al-Ḥāqqa 69/35** | **al-Maʿārij 70/10** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *فَلَيْسَ لَهُ … حَمِيمٌ* | *وَلَا يَسْـَٔلُ حَمِيمٌ حَمِيمًا* |
| What it says | He **has no friend** | The friend of one who **has** a friend **is of no use** |
| About whom | A particular person | **Everyone** |

**The two sūras give two stages.** In al-Ḥāqqa one man had no friend at all. In al-Maʿārij there are friends — and they are of no use.

**The second is the heavier.** For friendlessness is a lack; having a friend who is of no use is a **collapse**. In the first, something was missing; in the second, something present does not work.

I record this as my own reading. The wordings of the two verses are data that can be verified.

### The twofold repetition of the word

**The verse does not use a pronoun.** It could have said *"a friend will not ask after him"*; it does not. **The word is repeated twice:** *ḥamīmun ḥamīmā.*

As recorded in the sections on al-Qāriʿa and al-Ḥāqqa, in Arabic the repetition of a noun where a pronoun would serve is an act of **emphasis**.

Here the function of the emphasis may be this — I record it as my own reading: **the word is given to both sides.** That is, both ends of the relation are *ḥamīm*; one does not ask, and neither does the other. Reciprocity is shown by the repetition of the word.

And when the "warm" sense of *ḥamīm* is borne in mind, a scene is set in which nothing passes between two warmths.

### لَا يَسْـَٔلُ — "will not ask"

**And the choice of the verb is critical.**

The verse does not say *"will not help"*, does not say *"will not save"*, does not say *"will not recognise"*. It says **"will not ask"**.

Asking is the **smallest** contact between people. Not an act of help but an enquiry after someone's state. It costs nothing.

That is, the verse picks the weakest link of relationship and says that even that has been broken. It is the very method recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn: *"lowering the threshold step by step."* Al-Māʿūn in its last verse asked for a bucket; al-Maʿārij here asks for a question.

I record this as my own reading. The finding in the section on al-Māʿūn is taken from there.

**Two readings of the verb.** It is transmitted in the classical sources that the verb is read both in the **active** (*yasʾalu* — "will not ask") and in the **passive** (*yusʾalu* — "will not be asked"). **Since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which imām, I give no names.**

The two readings differ in sense but come out in the same place:
- **Active:** a friend does not ask after a friend — no one wonders about anyone.
- **Passive:** a friend is not asked [about] his friend — no one is held responsible for anyone, no one carries another's burden.

The second reading accords with the Qurʾānic principle that *"no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another"* (al-Anʿām 6/164 and elsewhere).

### The second occurrence of the root س-أ-ل

**And here the hidden spine of the sūra is at work.**

In the first verse there was a *sāʾil*: he was asking and did not want an answer.
In the tenth verse there is a *ḥamīm*: he cannot ask, and he would want to.

**Between the two scenes there is a reversal:** in the world he asked questions but did not care about the answer; on that day the answer is everything, but he cannot ask.

I record this as my own reading. The two occurrences of the root can be counted in the text.

### يُبَصَّرُونَهُمْ — "they will be made to see one another"

**Root: ب-ص-ر.** To see. And the verb here is **two-layered**: it is both in the *tafʿīl* form (to cause to see) and **passive** (*yubaṣṣarūna* — they are made to see).

That is: *"they are shown"*, *"they are subjected to being shown."*

**And this makes the tenth verse harder still.**

Had the sentence run thus, it would have been intelligible: "a friend does not ask after a friend, because he cannot see him." They have been lost in the crowd; they cannot recognise one another.

The verse says exactly the opposite: **they do see.** Indeed they are shown — that is, the seeing is brought about not by chance but by an act.

So the scene is this: **they recognise one another, they look at one another, and they do not ask.**

This is a picture the Qurʾān sets up several times in its scenes of the resurrection, and it takes its clearest form in Sūrat ʿAbasa. I shall come to it shortly.

**The arrangement of the pronouns.** *Yubaṣṣarūna-hum* — "they are shown to them". Two plural pronouns. The verse moves from the singular (one friend) to the plural (all of them): the tenth verse described one relationship, the eleventh describes all relationships.

**One link.** The root ب-ص-ر also occurred in the thirty-eighth verse of the preceding sūra: *"by what you see and what you do not see"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/38-39). There seeing was the **line dividing an oath**. Here seeing is **a faculty that is of no use.**

Two neighbouring sūras use the same root for two different tasks. I record this as an observation.

## 70/11-14 — يَوَدُّ ٱلْمُجْرِمُ لَوْ يَفْتَدِى مِنْ عَذَابِ يَوْمِئِذٍۭ بِبَنِيهِ · وَصَٰحِبَتِهِۦ وَأَخِيهِ · وَفَصِيلَتِهِ ٱلَّتِى تُـْٔوِيهِ · وَمَن فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ جَمِيعًا ثُمَّ يُنجِيهِ

*Yawaddu'l-mujrimu law yaftadī min ʿadhābi yawmiʾidhin bi-banīh · Wa ṣāḥibatihī wa akhīh · Wa faṣīlatihi'llatī tuʾwīh · Wa man fi'l-arḍi jamīʿan thumma yunjīh*
"The criminal will wish that he might ransom himself from the punishment of that day with his sons, his wife, his brother, the kin who sheltered him, and all who are on the earth — so that then it might save him."

### The harshest scene in the sūra

**And the force of this scene emerges when it is compared with a list set up in another sūra of the Qurʾān.**

In Sūrat ʿAbasa the same day and the same people are described, but with an entirely different verb:

*"On that day a man will flee from his brother, his mother, his father, his wife and his children. On that day each one of them will have concern enough for himself."* (ʿAbasa 80/34-37)

In the section on ʿAbasa this list was treated in detail and **three logics** were set out: difficulty of severance, exhaustiveness, and chronology. The table framed there was this: brother (lateral bond) → parents (upward) → wife (horizontal, chosen) → children (downward); and it was recorded that the order runs *"from the easiest to break to the hardest"*.

**In the section on ʿAbasa this list of al-Maʿārij was not mentioned at all.** I make the comparison here.

### The two lists side by side

| | **ʿAbasa 80/34-36** | **al-Maʿārij 70/11-14** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **يَفِرُّ** — he flees | **يَفْتَدِى** — he ransoms |
| Position of the persons | **Fled from** | **Offered** |
| Order | Brother → mother → father → wife → children | **Children → wife → brother → kin → those on the earth** |
| Direction of the order | **From far to near** — easiest to break to hardest | **From near to far** — from the most precious to the widest |
| End of the list | Children — **the nearest** | All who are on the earth — **the widest** |
| What happens | Bonds **dissolve** | Bonds are **turned into currency** |

**And the two lists run in exactly opposite directions.**

ʿAbasa moves inward: it begins with the brother and arrives at the children. The conclusion recorded in the section on ʿAbasa was this: *"The measure of the terror is the last item of the list"* — that is, the moment the bond hardest to break is broken.

Al-Maʿārij moves outward: it begins with the children and arrives at the whole earth. And here too the measure of the terror stands at the **head** of the list: the offer opens with **the children**.

**The two sūras traverse the same web of bonds from two different directions.**

### The opposition itself

**But the real point is not the direction.**

In ʿAbasa these people are **fled from**. In al-Maʿārij the same people are **offered as ransom**.

And between these two verbs there is a great difference:

- **To flee** is to leave someone behind. It does him no harm; it simply takes no interest in him. As recorded in the section on ʿAbasa, this is also the explanation the verse itself gives: *"each one of them will have concern enough for himself."* That is, the flight arises not from selfishness but from **lack of capacity**.
- **To ransom** is to **hand someone over.** To present him as a price. And what is presented as a price passes to the other party.

**So al-Maʿārij is one step beyond ʿAbasa.** In ʿAbasa the bonds were dissolving; in al-Maʿārij the bonds are **used**.

I record this as my own reading. The senses of the two verbs and the contents of the two lists are data that can be verified; the interpretation of the opposition is my own.

### And one further detail

The second logic recorded in the section on ʿAbasa was **exhaustiveness**: *"Every axis of kinship a person can belong to has been counted. No one is left outside. So this is not a sample list but a census."*

**Al-Maʿārij makes the same census and then goes beyond it.**

ʿAbasa ended with four axes: lateral, upward, horizontal-chosen, downward. Al-Maʿārij counts three axes (downward: sons; horizontal-chosen: wife; lateral: brother), then widens to the **kin**, then to **the whole earth**.

So al-Maʿārij's list does not end with kinship; it takes in **the whole of humanity**.

And there is a reason for this: a ransom is a price that **must be sufficient**. A list of those fled from may have a limit (as many as your relatives); the limit of a ransom list is set by the size of the debt. The man raises his offer, raises it again, and begins to offer things that are not his to give.

I record this as my own reading.

### يَوَدُّ — he wishes

**Root: و-د-د.** *Wudd* — love, affection. *Wadūd* is one of the names of God; *mawadda* is the bond of love.

**And the choice of the verb is bitter.**

*Yawaddu* means "he wishes" — but at the centre of the root there is **love**. In Arabic *wadda* is to want something **with affection**; not an ordinary demand.

So the verse, using a verb from the root of love, describes the wish to hand over those one loves.

I record this as an observation about the word. The sense of the root is set down in the dictionaries; I do not claim that the verse intends this contrast — *wadda* is an ordinary verb for "to want" in Arabic and is often used in this sense in the Qurʾān (*"The disbelievers wish that…"*, al-Nisāʾ 4/102; al-Baqara 2/109).

**ٱلْمُجْرِم — Root: ج-ر-م.** *Jarm* — to cut, to sever; *jarīma* (crime) comes from here. The lexicographers' explanation: a crime is the act that **severs** a person from the community. *Mujrim* is the active participle: one who commits a crime.

And note: the verse does not say *"disbeliever"*, does not say *"wrongdoer"*. **مُجْرِم** — criminal. That is, a legal category is chosen; the scene is a courtroom scene.

### يَفْتَدِى — to ransom

**Root: ف-د-ي.** *Fidya* — the price given to redeem a captive or an obligation. **ٱفْتَدَىٰ** (*iftiʿāl*) — to give a price in order to redeem oneself.

**And the word was the name of a concrete institution in that society.**

A man taken captive in war was redeemed by a price paid by his tribe. *Fidya* was not a metaphor; it was a working practice. The Qurʾān records it: *"Then release them, either as a favour or for a **ransom**"* (Muḥammad 47/4).

So the verse reminds the one addressed of **a transaction he knows**: the redeeming of a captive.

**And it reverses the logic of the transaction.** Normally the ransom is paid by **someone else** — the captive's family, his tribe. Here the captive himself tries to pay, and what he wants to pay is **his family**.

That is, those who would pay the ransom become the ransom itself.

I record this as my own reading. The workings of the institution of ransom are a historical datum; the interpretation of its reversal is my own.

**The Qurʾān rejects this offer elsewhere too:**

- *"If they had all that is on the earth and as much again with it, they would **offer it as ransom** to escape the evil punishment of the Day of Resurrection."* (al-Zumar 39/47)
- *"From none of those who disbelieve and die as disbelievers will an earth-full of gold be accepted as **ransom**."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/91)
- *"On that day no **ransom** will be taken from you or from those who disbelieved."* (al-Ḥadīd 57/15)

**Al-Zumar 39/47 is particularly close**, because there too the expression *"all that is on the earth"* occurs — the same as al-Maʿārij 70/14.

### فَصِيلَة — kin

**The word occurs only here in the Qurʾān.**

**Root: ف-ص-ل.** *Faṣala* — to separate, to cut apart, to open a space between. *Faṣl* (separation), *tafṣīl* (setting out in detail), *fāṣila* (that which separates — the name given to verse-endings as well), *fiṣāl* (weaning) are from the same root.

**فَصِيلَة**, as the lexicographers describe it, is a person's nearest group of kin; the branch that **splits off** from the tribe.

**So the word itself conveys a separation.** *Faṣīla* is the small group broken off from the great trunk — and it is a person's nearest.

And this yields a nicety of wording: **the group formed by being broken off is now offered up to be broken off from the man.** I record this as a nicety; I do not claim the verse intends it, for *faṣīla* is an ordinary Arabic word in this sense.

**The word's relative in the Qurʾān:** *"Warn your nearest kin"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/214 — *ʿashīrataka'l-aqrabīn*) — a different word is used there. Al-Maʿārij's *faṣīla* conveys a narrower circle.

### ٱلَّتِى تُـْٔوِيهِ — "that sheltered him"

**Root: أ-و-ي.** *Awā* — to take shelter, to lodge. **آوَىٰ** (*ifʿāl*) — to shelter, to give a place of refuge. *Maʾwā* — a place of refuge, a shelter.

**And this root occurs in a verse treated in this commentary:**

*"Did He not find you **an orphan** and **shelter** you?"* (al-Ḍuḥā 93/6 — *a-lam yajidka yatīman fa-**āwā***)

This verse was treated in the section on al-Ḍuḥā; I do not repeat it.

**But the two verses must be set side by side:**

| | **al-Ḍuḥā 93/6** | **al-Maʿārij 70/13** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **فَـَٔاوَىٰ** — He sheltered | **تُـْٔوِيهِ** — it shelters |
| Who shelters | **God** | **The kin** |
| What happens | It is counted as a favour | **It is offered as ransom** |

So the same verb names, in one sūra, a favour that is counted, and in the other a bond one wishes to sacrifice.

**And the presence of the relative clause is critical.** The verse does not merely say *"his kin"*; it says **"the kin who sheltered him"**. That is, the clause recalls **what that group is for**: it sheltered you.

And the man offers it up.

I record this as my own reading. The shared root and the presence of the relative clause are data that can be verified.

### وَمَن فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ جَمِيعًا ثُمَّ يُنجِيهِ

**The last item of the list, and the conclusion.**

**وَمَن فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ جَمِيعًا** — "and all who are on the earth." The offer passes out of kinship into humanity.

And this shows the **madness** of the offer: the man is offering things he does not own. The people of the earth are not his; he cannot give them.

So the offer is no longer a bargain but a **thrashing about**.

**ثُمَّ يُنجِيهِ** — "so that then it might save him."

**ثُمَّ** here puts a distance between. That is: were he to give all this, only **after that** might he be saved. Deliverance does not come directly after the list; it is far off.

**يُنجِى — Root: ن-ج-و.** *Najā* — to be saved; **أَنْجَىٰ** — to save. *Najāt* and *nājiya* are from the same root.

**And who is the subject of the verb?** Two readings: the ransom (may what is given save him), or the act of ransoming. Both come out in the same place.

**The sentence is left incomplete.** The verse does not say "he will be saved"; it says "so that it might save him" — a **wish**. And whether the wish is fulfilled is not said.

But the next verse gives the answer, and gives it in a single word: **كَلَّا.**

### The scene as a whole

Let us read the four verses together:

> The criminal will wish that he might ransom himself from the punishment with **his sons**, **his wife**, **his brother**, **the kin who sheltered him** and **all who are on the earth** — so that then he might be saved.

**The sentence lengthens, and as it lengthens the offer grows.** This is the sūra's technique of word order: the four verses are a single sentence, and each verse adds one item to the list.

And the verse-ending of the four verses is the same as well: *banīh, akhīh, tuʾwīh, yunjīh* — all ending in **-īh**.

**The verse-ending of ʿAbasa's list of flight was the same:** *akhīh, abīh, banīh, yughnīh* (80/34-37). This sound was recorded in the section on ʿAbasa.

**Two sūras tell of the same subject with the same sound.** I record this as an observation; the verse-endings of the two lists can be counted in the text. I do not claim that the shared sound is deliberate — in Arabic, words ending in the first-person and third-person pronouns naturally give this sound.

## 70/15-16 — كَلَّآ ۖ إِنَّهَا لَظَىٰ · نَزَّاعَةً لِّلشَّوَىٰ

*Kallā innahā laẓā · Nazzāʿatan li'sh-shawā*
"No! It is a flame; it strips away the skin."

### كَلَّا — the cutting-off of the offer

**The function of this particle was treated in the sections on al-Takāthur 102/3, al-Humaza 104/4 and al-Fajr 89/17**: *radd* (rejection) and *zajr* (rebuke). I do not repeat it.

The distinction recorded in the section on al-Fajr is useful here: there two instances of *kallā* were compared — one cut off an **inference**, the other a **way of life**.

**The *kallā* here cuts off an offer.**

For four verses the man bargained: let me give this, let me give that, let me give everything. And *kallā* closes the bargaining.

And note how it closes it: the answer is not "no, it will not be accepted." The answer is **a name.** *"It is a flame."*

That is, there is no other party to the bargain. The man was making an offer to an interlocutor; the verse shows him who the interlocutor is not: facing him is not a bargaining table but **a fire**.

I record this as my own reading. Its ground is that the sentence following *kallā* is not a refusal but a naming.

### لَظَىٰ — flame

**Root: ل-ظ-ي.** *Laẓā* — flame, the tongue of fire. **تَلَظَّىٰ** — to blaze, to burn in flames.

**And the verbal form was treated in this commentary:**

*"I have warned you of a **blazing** fire."* (al-Layl 92/14 — *nāran **talaẓẓā***)

This verse was taken up in the section on al-Layl; I do not repeat it.

**Let us set the two verses side by side:**

| | **al-Layl 92/14** | **al-Maʿārij 70/15** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *نَارًا تَلَظَّىٰ* | *إِنَّهَا لَظَىٰ* |
| Class of the word | **Verb** — "it blazes" | **Proper name** — "Laẓā" |
| Indefinite/definite | *nāran* indefinite — "a fire" | *laẓā* **diptote** — a proper name |

**What in al-Layl is an attribute becomes in al-Maʿārij a name.**

And the grammar confirms this: *laẓā* here takes no tanwīn and is **diptote**, that is, it is treated as a proper name. In the section on al-Qāriʿa the names of Hell were listed, and *laẓā* was among them.

So the word follows a course within the Qurʾān: first the **work** the fire does (al-Layl), then the **name** of the fire (al-Maʿārij).

I record this as my own reading. The wordings of the two verses and their grammatical status are data that can be verified.

**إِنَّهَا — to what does the pronoun refer?** It is feminine. The most widespread reading: *al-nār* (the fire) is meant implicitly; or it is a pronoun standing in for the *ʿadhāb* of verse 11. It makes no difference to the sense.

### نَزَّاعَةً لِّلشَّوَىٰ

**The two densest words in the sūra.**

### نَزَّاعَة — that which tears away

**Root: ن-ز-ع.** *Nazaʿa* — to pull, to pull out, to tear away. The concrete sense of the root is **to pull with force**: to tear something from where it is.

Its occurrences in the Qurʾān show the field of the root:

- *"The wind was **tearing people away** as though they were uprooted palm trunks."* (al-Qamar 54/20 — *tanziʿu'n-nās*)
- *"We **tore out** whatever rancour was in their breasts."* (al-Ḥijr 15/47; al-Aʿrāf 7/43)
- *"You **take away** sovereignty from whom You will."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/26)
- *"He was **stripping off** their garments."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/27 — of Satan)
- **تَنَازُع** — to quarrel, to fall into dispute. Two parties pulling one thing from two ends. *"Do not **quarrel** among yourselves"* (al-Anfāl 8/46).

**Al-Qamar 54/20 is particularly worth noting** — and the reason lies in the preceding sūra.

In the section on al-Ḥāqqa the following was recorded: the image of *"hollow palm trunks"* in al-Ḥāqqa 69/7 appears in al-Qamar 54/20 as *"uprooted palm trunks"*, and the verb used there is **تَنزِعُ**.

**So the root ن-ز-ع names, in al-Qamar, the wind that tore the people of ʿĀd from their places; in al-Maʿārij it names the work of the fire.**

Two neighbouring sūras — al-Ḥāqqa and al-Maʿārij — stand at the two ends of the same root: one describes the result of that wind (trunks lying flat), the other gives the same verb to the fire.

I record this as my own reading. The wordings of the three verses and the shared root are data that can be verified.

**The pattern: فَعَّالَة.** This is the **intensive** pattern in Arabic: one who does the act much, who does it as though it were his trade. *Kadhdhāb* (a great liar), *ghaffār* (much forgiving), *ḥammāla* (treated in al-Masad 111/4).

So *nazzāʿa* is not "that which tears away" but **"that which keeps tearing away"**. The act is repeated.

**Grammar.** The word has been read in the **accusative** (*nazzāʿatan*) and the grammarians generally take it as a **circumstantial qualifier** (*ḥāl*). A **nominative** reading (*nazzāʿatun*) is also transmitted; in that case it is an adjective of *laẓā* or a second predicate. The existence of the difference is set down in the sources; **since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which imām, I give no names.** It makes no great difference to the sense.

### ٱلشَّوَىٰ — the extremities of the body

**The word occurs only here in the Qurʾān**, and there is disagreement over its meaning.

**Root: ش-و-ي.** *Shawā* — to roast, to cook over fire (*shiwāʾ*, a roast). And from here another branch of meaning arises.

**The meanings the lexicographers give:**

| Meaning | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The scalp, the skin of the head** | *Shawāt* is the skin of the head; its plural is *shawā* |
| **The extremities of the body** | Hands, feet, arms — the **non-fatal** parts of the body |
| **Skin, the outer surface** | The outer layer of the body |

**The second meaning deserves attention**, because it rests on an established usage in Arabic.

In Arabic the **مَقَاتِل** (*maqātil*) are the **fatal** points of the body: the heart, the neck, the temple. The places outside these are called **شَوَى**. And there is an idiom in the language: **أَشْوَاهُ** — "he struck him but could not strike a fatal spot."

So *shawā* is the name of the places that **do not kill when struck**.

**And this gives the layer that must be read into the verse.**

The fire tears away not the fatal points of the body but its **non-fatal** parts. That is, the act does not bring life to an end.

And this links up with the twenty-seventh verse of the preceding sūra. The man in al-Ḥāqqa had said: *"Would that it had been the finisher!"* (69/27) — *al-qāḍiya*, "that which finishes".

**Al-Maʿārij explains at the level of the word why that wish is not granted: the places torn away are places that do not finish.**

I record this as my own reading, and I set down one qualification: there is disagreement over the meaning of *shawā*, and the reading "non-fatal limbs" is not the only reading. Nor do I claim that the verse intends the link between the two sūras. What I record is that the Arabic idiom *ashwāhu* is set down in the dictionaries and that the senses of the two verses complete one another.

**There are verses within the Qurʾān that support this idea:**

- *"Whenever their skins are burnt through, We shall exchange them for other skins, so that they may taste the punishment."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/56)
- *"Death is not decreed for them, that they might die, nor is any of their punishment lightened."* (Fāṭir 35/36)
- *"Then he will neither die there nor live."* (al-Aʿlā 87/13)

**Al-Aʿlā 87/13** was treated in this commentary. All three verses say the same thing: the act is an act that does not reach its conclusion.

### The work the two words do together

*Nazzāʿa* is on the intensive pattern: a **repeated** act. *Shawā* is the non-fatal places: an act that **does not conclude**.

**Together the two describe an unending cycle:** it is torn away, it does not end; it is torn away again.

And a measure recorded in the section on al-Humaza works here in reverse. There, of the verb *ʿaddada* (he kept counting), this was written: *"The repetition lies not in the nature of the act but in the act's not working. If a process yields a result, there is no need to repeat it."*

The same measure holds here — but this time the cause of the repetition is not the person but the kind of act.

I record this as my own reading.

## 70/17-18 — تَدْعُوا۟ مَنْ أَدْبَرَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ · وَجَمَعَ فَأَوْعَىٰ

*Tadʿū man adbara wa tawallā · Wa jamaʿa fa-awʿā*
"It calls the one who turned his back and turned away; who gathered and hoarded."

### تَدْعُوا۟ — the fire calls

**And this is one of the most startling verbs in the sūra.**

The fire is depicted not as an object but as a **calling** subject. *Tadʿū* — "it calls, it summons."

**The reversal is here.** In the world there was a messenger who called, and the man called turned his back. On that day the fire calls, and the one called is the same man.

And the verb is from the same root: **د-ع-و.** The Qurʾān names the Prophet's work with this root: *"one who **calls** to God"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/33 — *daʿā ila'llāh*); *"God's **caller**"* (al-Aḥqāf 46/31 — *dāʿiya'llāh*).

**So two calls, one root.** The man did not answer the first call; he does not have to answer the second — because the second call **takes him away.**

I record this as my own reading. The two uses of the root are plain in the Qurʾān.

**A note on the reading.** Some exegetes take the verb *tadʿū* as figurative: not "it calls" but "it draws to itself", "it merits". Others hold to its plain sense. I do not enter this discussion; on either reading the verse's work within the sūra is the same: to say **whom the fire takes.**

### Two pairs of verbs

The verse sets up two groups with four verbs, and the groups are separated by **conjunctions**:

| Group | Verbs | Conjunction | Field |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First** | *أَدْبَرَ* + *تَوَلَّىٰ* | **وَ** — coordinating | **Attitude** — refusing to turn towards |
| **Second** | *جَمَعَ* + *أَوْعَىٰ* | **فَ** — sequence | **Act** — the accumulation of wealth |

And the link between the two groups is **وَ** as well.

**So the verse counts two offences: an attitude and a behaviour.** And this is the same as the structure of the thirty-third and thirty-fourth verses of the preceding sūra:

| | **al-Ḥāqqa 69/33-34** | **al-Maʿārij 70/17-18** |
|---|---|---|
| First item | *كَانَ لَا يُؤْمِنُ* — he did not believe | *أَدْبَرَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ* — he turned his back |
| Second item | *لَا يَحُضُّ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ ٱلْمِسْكِينِ* — he did not urge | *جَمَعَ فَأَوْعَىٰ* — he gathered, he put away |
| Shared structure | **Belief + wealth** | **Belief + wealth** |

**Two neighbouring sūras set the same measure in two verses each.** In al-Ḥāqqa, not believing and not urging others to give; in al-Maʿārij, turning away and gathering and hiding.

And the difference is this: **al-Ḥāqqa counts with negative verbs, al-Maʿārij with positive ones.** Al-Ḥāqqa says *"he did not do"*; al-Maʿārij says *"he did"*. The same man, from two different angles.

I record this as my own reading. The structures of the two verses are in the text.

### أَدْبَرَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ

**أَدْبَرَ — Root: د-ب-ر.** *Dubur* — the back, the rear. **أَدْبَرَ** (*ifʿāl*) — he turned his back, he turned around. From the same root, *tadbīr* (thinking through the **end** of a matter), *dābir* (what remains behind), *adbār* (backs).

**تَوَلَّىٰ — Root: و-ل-ي.** *Walī* — one who is near, a friend, a protector; *walāya*. **تَوَلَّىٰ** (*tafaʿʿul*) carries two senses: **he turned away** and **he took charge, he undertook.** An interesting contronymy: the same verb can convey both turning towards and turning away. Context decides.

**Why are the two verbs side by side?** They are close in meaning but not identical:

- **أَدْبَرَ** — a movement of the body: turning one's back. **Physical.**
- **تَوَلَّىٰ** — a change of orientation: breaking off interest, turning in another direction. **A matter of will.**

So one conveys the movement, the other the decision.

**This pair is established in the Qurʾān, and it has occurred several times in this commentary:**

- *"Then he **turned his back** and grew proud."* (al-Muddaththir 74/23 — *adbara wa'stakbara*)
- *"But he **denied and turned away**."* (al-Qiyāma 75/32; al-ʿAlaq 96/13 — *kadhdhaba wa tawallā*)
- *"None will enter it but the most wretched, who denies and **turns away**."* (al-Layl 92/16)

**Al-Layl 92/16 is particularly close**, because there too the one who will enter the fire is described and the same verb is used.

### وَجَمَعَ فَأَوْعَىٰ — "he gathered and put it in a vessel"

**The two densest words in the sūra, and they have been mentioned earlier in this commentary.**

In the section on **al-Humaza 104/2** this verse was already used, and there the following was recorded:

> *"He gathered and hid it in a vessel."* (al-Maʿārij 70/18) — "A sentence of two words, and **of the same structure as al-Humaza 104/2: a verb of gathering followed by a verb of keeping.** There too what is told is **not what is done with the wealth but what is not done.**"

And in the section on **al-Inshiqāq 84/23**, when the root و-ع-ي was treated, this verse was given as the example of **أَوْعَىٰ**.

**I repeat neither.** There are three things to add here.

### One — the difference from al-Humaza

Let me open out the comparison framed in the section on al-Humaza a little, since it was recorded there that the two verses are **of the same structure** but their differences were not taken up:

| | **al-Humaza 104/2** | **al-Maʿārij 70/18** |
|---|---|---|
| First verb | **جَمَعَ** — he gathered | **جَمَعَ** — he gathered |
| Second verb | **عَدَّدَهُ** — he kept counting | **أَوْعَىٰ** — he put it in a vessel |
| Pattern of the second verb | *tafʿīl* — conveys **repetition** | *ifʿāl* — conveys **completion** |
| Kind of act | **Looking** — the number is visible | **Keeping** — the number is invisible |
| Object | **مَالًا** — specified | **Not specified** |

**The most telling row is the second.**

What was recorded in the section on al-Humaza was this: *"ʿaddada is not 'he counted' but 'he kept counting'… it is acquired once and counted continually."* And: *"if you are counting something over and over, you are not using it."*

**In al-Maʿārij there is no repetition.** *Awʿā* is in the *ifʿāl* form and conveys a completed act: **he put it in a vessel and closed the lid.**

So the man in al-Humaza **opens** his wealth: he counts it, looks at it, counts it again. The man in al-Maʿārij **closes** his wealth: he puts it in a vessel and leaves it there.

**Two different relationships:** one is in constant contact with his wealth, the other has taken his wealth out of sight. But both meet in the same place — the wealth is **not used**.

I record this as my own reading. The functions of the two patterns are recognised in Arabic; the interpretation of the comparison is my own.

### Two — the object left unsaid

**And this is the most important feature of the verse.**

Al-Humaza says *"مَالًا"*: he gathered wealth. Al-Maʿārij **says nothing at all.** Both verbs are without object: *jamaʿa fa-awʿā* — "he gathered, he put it in a vessel."

In Arabic the dropping of the object is called *ḥadhf al-mafʿūl*, and one of its functions is **generalisation**: when a verb loses its object it opens onto every object.

This technique has been recorded twice before in this commentary:

- In the section on **al-Māʿūn 107/6**: *"The verb does not say 'to whom they make a display'… In al-Māʿūn the dropping of the object widens the scope: not to a particular audience, but to whoever is looking."*
- In the section on **al-Shams 91/11**: *"when the object is dropped the verb becomes general."*

**The same technique is at work here.** The verse does not say "he gathered wealth"; it says **"he gathered"**.

And the consequence is this: **it does not matter what was gathered.** It may be wealth, it may be knowledge, it may be opportunity, it may be relationships, it may be standing. The verb waits for its object, and the reader supplies his own.

I record this as my own reading. The function of the technique is recognised in Arabic and has been recorded twice in this commentary.

### Three — the root و-ع-ي and three vessels

**And now the link that joins three sections of this commentary.**

A table was framed in the section on al-Ḥāqqa, and it is completed here:

| Verse | Expression | The vessel | What is put in it | Its value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Ḥāqqa 69/12** | *أُذُنٌ وَٰعِيَةٌ* | **The ear** | The reminder | **Praise** |
| **al-Inshiqāq 84/23** | *بِمَا يُوعُونَ* | Not specified (the breast) | What they hide | **Neutral → threat** |
| **al-Maʿārij 70/18** | *جَمَعَ فَأَوْعَىٰ* | Not specified (the storehouse) | What he gathered | **Blame** |

**Three verses, one root, three vessels.**

And the finding recorded in the section on al-Inshiqāq explains all three at once: *"the vessel is not empty, it is only not full of the right thing. It is not that a person takes nothing in; he takes something else in."*

**The root is in itself neither positive nor negative.** *Waʿy* is "to take within". Its value depends on what is taken in.

And the measure recorded for *mabthūth* in the section on al-Qāriʿa holds here too: *"the word is not in itself negative… whether scatteredness is good or bad depends on what is scattered."*

**And two neighbouring sūras use this root at the two ends:** in al-Ḥāqqa the ear takes in the reminder; in al-Maʿārij the hand takes in the wealth. Both are **vessels**, and both were **filled**.

**I record this as my own reading.** The shared root of the three verses is a linguistic datum beyond dispute; the interpretation that the three form a weave, and that they stand at two ends in two neighbouring sūras, is my own.

### What this says to the present

The object having been left unsaid opens the verse directly onto the present.

*"He gathered and put it in a vessel"* — a sentence without an object. And in the life of today, the things gathered and put in vessels have gone well beyond wealth: content saved but never watched, books bought but never read, opportunity accumulated but never used, a network of relations built but never worked.

What they have in common is this: **all of them are inside a vessel, and none of them is in circulation.**

The verse's measure is not having; it is **the lid being shut.** And, as recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn, the question is: *"what do you hold that would cost you nothing to give to someone, and are you giving it?"*

---

## 70/19-21 — إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا · إِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلشَّرُّ جَزُوعًا · وَإِذَا مَسَّهُ ٱلْخَيْرُ مَنُوعًا

*Inna'l-insāna khuliqa halūʿā · Idhā massahu'sh-sharru jazūʿā · Wa idhā massahu'l-khayru manūʿā*
"Man was created **halūʿ**: wailing when evil touches him, withholding when good touches him."

### The sūra's diagnosis

**Here the sūra stops and delivers a diagnosis.**

For eighteen verses there was something being told: a question, an answer, a scene, an offer, a fire. Now the text draws back and describes **man**.

And this is the very movement Sūrat al-Fajr makes. What was recorded in the section on al-Fajr was: *"The historical section ends here, and the text puts a single subject in the place of the peoples: **al-insān**."*

**Al-Maʿārij makes the same movement:** the scene of the resurrection ends and a single subject is left.

**ٱلْإِنسَٰن — the definite article.** This was treated in the section on al-Fajr and I do not repeat it. In summary: the article conveys **comprehensiveness** (*istighrāq*), taking in the whole of the kind. And: *"when the name of a species is used in the singular, the verdict does not descend on individuals one by one but is written into the definition of the species… this is not a majority statistic; it is the description of the default state, which holds unless the contrary is shown."*

**And the qualification "unless the contrary is shown" becomes critical here** — because in the twenty-second verse an exception will come.

### هَلُوع — the key word of the sūra

**The word occurs only here in the Qurʾān.** And this is both the cause of the disagreement over its meaning and the resolution of it.

**Root: ه-ل-ع.** The meanings the lexicographers give: **fierce greed; intolerance; rapacity; inner restlessness; impatience.**

In the classical dictionaries *halaʿ* is described as "the fiercest greed" and "the fiercest *jazaʿ*" (wailing). That is, the word is a **combination of two states**: one who is never sated in acquiring, and who cannot bear it in losing.

**Pattern: فَعُول.** This is the **intensive** pattern in Arabic: one who does the act much, who is in that state habitually. *Ṣabūr* (much enduring), *shakūr* (much thankful), *ghafūr*, *ʿaẓūm*.

**And now note what the verse does.**

### The verse defines its own word

**The Qurʾān uses a word and then immediately defines it** — and this is not something one meets often in the Qurʾān.

The twentieth and twenty-first verses are the definition of *halūʿ*:

> **هَلُوع** = *idhā massahu'sh-sharru* **جَزُوع** + *wa idhā massahu'l-khayru* **مَنُوع**

And the two words used in the definition are **on the same pattern: فَعُول.**

| Word | Pattern | Root | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| **هَلُوع** | فَعُول | ه-ل-ع | **The general state** |
| **جَزُوع** | فَعُول | ج-ز-ع | When evil touches |
| **مَنُوع** | فَعُول | م-ن-ع | When good touches |

**Three words, one pattern.** And the verse-ending is the same too: *halūʿā / jazūʿā / manūʿā.*

So in defining a word the sūra makes the definition with two words **from the same pattern**. The definition carries the form of what is defined.

I record this as an observation about form; the metre and verse-endings of the three words are data that can be verified.

**A finding recorded in the classical sources:** there is no need to dispute the meaning of *halūʿ*, because **the verse has explained it itself.** This view is widely transmitted in the sources. I record it; I do not ascribe it to a particular person.

### The Qurʾān's diagnoses of man

**And this verse is one of the Qurʾān's "man is thus" sentences.** Read together, these sentences yield a striking pattern:

| Verse | Expression | Word | Pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Maʿārij 70/19** | *خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا* | **هَلُوع** — greedy and intolerant | **فَعُول** |
| **al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/6** | *لِرَبِّهِۦ لَكَنُودٌ* | **كَنُود** — ungrateful / barren | **فَعُول** |
| **al-Isrāʾ 17/11** | *وَكَانَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ عَجُولًا* | **عَجُول** — hasty | **فَعُول** |
| **al-Isrāʾ 17/100** | *وَكَانَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ قَتُورًا* | **قَتُور** — niggardly | **فَعُول** |
| **al-Aḥzāb 33/72** | *إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ ظَلُومًا جَهُولًا* | **ظَلُوم جَهُول** | **فَعُول** |
| **al-Nisāʾ 4/28** | *وَخُلِقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ ضَعِيفًا* | **ضَعِيف** | *faʿīl* |
| **al-Anbiyāʾ 21/37** | *خُلِقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ مِنْ عَجَلٍ* | **عَجَل** — haste | a noun |
| **al-Kahf 18/54** | *أَكْثَرَ شَىْءٍ جَدَلًا* | **جَدَل** — disputation | a noun |

**Most of the diagnoses in the list are on the pattern فَعُول.**

And this is no coincidence: *faʿūl* is the intensive pattern, that is, it conveys **a settled state**. The Qurʾān names man's faults not as "he sometimes does this" but as **"one who is thus"**.

I record this as an observation about patterns; the metres of the words are data that can be verified. The interpretation that the pattern constitutes an order is my own.

**In the section on al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/6** *kanūd* was treated, and there its link with the sense of "soil that yields no crop" was framed. The two diagnoses complete one another: *kanūd* names the one who does not give, *halūʿ* names both the one who wants to take and the one who does not give.

### خُلِقَ — "was created"

**And the most serious question here lies in this verb.**

The verb is **passive**: *khuliqa* — "he was created". That is, this state is not something man acquired later; it is something present **in his creation**.

**Is this an excuse?**

The question is serious and has been debated in the classical sources. Two main approaches:

| Approach | Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **A description of creation, not a declaration of defect** | *Halūʿ* is a tendency present in man's **constitution**; and a tendency is the subject of a testing. The Qurʾān also says man was created weak (al-Nisāʾ 4/28) and does not offer this as an excuse | The **exception** in the twenty-second verse: "except those who pray". If there is an exception, the state can be overcome |
| **Censure** | The expression censures the state in which man mostly settles | The tone of blame in the *faʿūl* pattern |

**The first approach is closer to the text, and its ground lies within the text: three verses later the exception comes.**

If a state cannot be overcome, there can be no one excepted from it. The twenty-second verse marks off a group by saying *"illa'l-muṣallīn"*; so being *halūʿ* is **not a fate but a starting point.**

I state this as a preference; **it is not binding.** But the presence of the exception is beyond dispute in the text.

**And this accords with the framework set up in Sūrat al-Shams.** In al-Shams 91/8 it is said of the soul that *"its wickedness and its godwariness were inspired into it"* — that is, both directions were given. This was treated in the section on al-Shams. Al-Maʿārij says the same thing from another angle: the constitution is given, the choice is not.

### مَسَّهُ — "when it touches"

**Root: م-س-س.** *Massa* — to touch, to graze. And the choice of the verb is meaningful.

The verse does not say *"when it befalls him"*, does not say *"when it strikes him"*. It says **"when it touches him"**.

**Touch is the lightest form of contact.** Not a blow but a graze.

So the verse shows the **disproportion** of the reaction: the lightest contact produces the fiercest response. A light evil touches him and the man collapses; a light good touches him and the man closes up.

I record this as my own reading. The sense of the verb is set down in the dictionaries.

**And the verb is the same in both verses:** *massahu'sh-sharr*, *massahu'l-khayr*. The only thing that changes is what touches; the manner of the touching is the same.

**The Qurʾān uses this formula elsewhere too:**

- *"When a hardship **touches** man he calls upon Us; then when We grant him a favour from Us he says, 'this was given me only because of my knowledge.'"* (al-Zumar 39/49)
- *"When We grant man a favour he turns away and draws aside; but when an evil **touches** him he prays at length."* (Fuṣṣilat 41/51)
- *"If We let him taste a mercy from Us, he says, 'this is my due.'"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/50)

**Fuṣṣilat 41/50-51 is particularly close** and counts the same two situations as al-Maʿārij.

### جَزُوع — wailing

**Root: ج-ز-ع.** *Jazaʿ* — the opposite of patience: intolerance, collapse, lamentation.

On the concrete sense of the root the lexicographers record this: *jazaʿa* is to cut something **through the middle**, to break it in two; *jizʿ* is a part of something, a cut portion. And from here arises the sense of "to break, to snap under strain".

I transmit this explanation; I do not present it as a settled etymological ruling. But the sense at work in the verse is beyond dispute, and the Qurʾān itself uses it as **the contrary of patience**:

*"Whether we **wail** or are **patient**, it is all the same for us."* (Ibrāhīm 14/21)

**And the link with the fifth verse.** In the fifth verse the sūra had said *"be patient with a beautiful patience"*. Now it describes man as *jazūʿ* — the exact contrary of patience.

This link was recorded above; I do not repeat it. The only thing to record is that **the sūra gave the remedy before the illness.**

### مَنُوع — withholding

**Root: م-ن-ع.** *Manaʿa* — to prevent, to hold back, to refuse, to withhold.

**And this root occurs in the last verse of Sūrat al-Māʿūn:**

*"And they **withhold** the māʿūn."* (al-Māʿūn 107/7 — *wa **yamnaʿūna**'l-māʿūn*)

This verb was treated in the section on al-Māʿūn, and there the following was recorded: *"There is a difference between not giving and withholding: **manʿ** is not giving something that has been asked for. That is, there is a request. If no one asked, there is no 'manʿ'."*

**Let us set the two verses side by side:**

| | **al-Māʿūn 107/7** | **al-Maʿārij 70/21** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **يَمْنَعُونَ** — a verb, imperfect | **مَنُوع** — an adjective, intensive |
| What it conveys | **Behaviour** — they withhold | **Character** — of a withholding constitution |
| Its object | *al-māʿūn* — specified | **Not specified** |
| Position | The **conclusion** of the sūra | The **diagnosis** of the sūra |

**Al-Māʿūn names the act; al-Maʿārij names the constitution the act comes out of.**

So the two sūras are two links of one chain: al-Maʿārij shows the character, al-Māʿūn the behaviour.

I record this as my own reading. The shared root and the patterns of the two words are data that can be verified.

### The link with al-Fajr: a wrong inference and a wrong reaction

**And now the most fruitful external link of this sūra.**

Sūrat al-Fajr, in verses 15-16, takes up exactly the same two situations — abundance and straitness — and records man's error there. This was treated in detail in the section on al-Fajr, and the following table was framed:

| | First state (al-Fajr 89/15) | Second state (al-Fajr 89/16) |
|---|---|---|
| Verb of testing | *ibtalāhu* | *ibtalāhu* (**the same verb**) |
| Content | *fa-akramahū wa naʿʿamahū* — abundance | *fa-qadara ʿalayhi rizqah* — straitness |
| Man's **inference** | *"rabbī akraman"* — my Lord has honoured me | *"rabbī ahānan"* — my Lord has humiliated me |

And the conclusion recorded in the section on al-Fajr: *"**Man uses the right word and draws the wrong inference.**"* And: *"Man's error is not unbelief… The error concerns **what the thing given means**."*

**Now let us put al-Maʿārij into the same table:**

| | **al-Fajr 89/15-16** | **al-Maʿārij 70/20-21** |
|---|---|---|
| The two situations | **Abundance / straitness** | **Good / evil** |
| Shared verb | *ٱبْتَلَىٰ* — He tested | *مَسَّ* — it touched |
| What man produces | **A sentence** — "My Lord has…" | **A reaction** — wailing / withholding |
| Where the error lies | **Interpretation** — reading the meaning of the event | **Behaviour** — the response given to the event |
| Form | *fa-yaqūlu* — **he says** | *jazūʿā / manūʿā* — **a state** |

**The two sūras take the same two situations and record two different errors: al-Fajr the wrong inference, al-Maʿārij the wrong reaction.**

And this means the two sūras complete one another:

- **Al-Fajr:** when abundance comes he says *"I deserved it"*; when straitness comes he says *"I have been humiliated"*. The error is **in the mind.**
- **Al-Maʿārij:** when good touches him he **holds on**; when evil touches him he **collapses**. The error is **in the behaviour.**

**And the two errors require one another.** This was recorded plainly in the section on al-Fajr: *"One who counts abundance an 'honouring' is bound to count straitness a 'humiliation'."* What al-Maʿārij adds to this is: one who counts abundance his own due does not **share** it (because what is deserved is not shared), and one who counts straitness a humiliation cannot **bear** it (because humiliation is unbearable).

**So the two behaviours al-Maʿārij describes are the natural consequence of the two inferences al-Fajr describes.**

I record this as my own reading. The verses of the two sūras and the findings in the section on al-Fajr are data that can be verified; the interpretation that the two errors require one another is my own.

### The inversion

**I want to draw attention to one more thing.**

The two reactions in the definition of *halūʿ* are **the exact opposite of what they should be:**

| Situation | What is expected | What *halūʿ* does |
|---|---|---|
| **Evil touches** | Patience — holding | **Jazaʿ** — collapsing |
| **Good touches** | Spending — letting go | **Manʿ** — holding |

**And note: *halūʿ* goes the wrong way in both situations.**

In hardship, where he should hold, he lets go (he is not patient); in abundance, where he should let go, he holds (he does not give).

**So the definition of *halūʿ* is this: in both situations, using his hand the wrong way round.**

And this fits exactly when joined with the sense of the root of patience. What was recorded in the section on al-Baqara 2/45 was: *ṣabr*, by its root, is **to hold**. Patience is holding, spending is letting go. *Halūʿ* lets go where he should hold, and holds where he should let go.

I record this as my own reading. The senses of the roots are data that can be verified; the table of inversion is my own.

## 70/22-23 — إِلَّا ٱلْمُصَلِّينَ · ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ عَلَىٰ صَلَاتِهِمْ دَآئِمُونَ

*Illa'l-muṣallīn · Alladhīna hum ʿalā ṣalātihim dāʾimūn*
"Except those who pray — those who are constant in their prayer."

### The exception

**إِلَّا** is the particle of exception. And the type of the exception here settles the question of the diagnosis.

The grammarians distinguish **connected** (*muttaṣil*) and **disconnected** (*munqaṭiʿ*) exception:

- **Connected:** what is excepted is in fact within that kind. "The people came, except Zayd" — Zayd too is a person.
- **Disconnected:** what is excepted is not of that kind.

**The exception here is connected**, and its being accepted as such yields an important consequence: **those who pray were also created *halūʿ*.**

That is, the sūra does not mark off a different kind of human being. It says that some among people of the same constitution have **come out** of that constitution.

And this turns the thirteen-verse list into **a way out**: the list counts the ways of coming out of being *halūʿ*.

I record this as a reading; that the exception is connected is widely accepted in grammar.

### ٱلْمُصَلِّين — those who pray

**The word was treated in detail in the section on al-Māʿūn 107/4.** I do not repeat it; in summary:

- *Muṣallī* is "one who performs the *ṣalāt*"; an active participle of the *tafʿīl* form. Root **ص-ل-و.**
- There are various views on the primary sense of the root; the widespread one is **supplication**. Another explanation ties it to the word *ṣalawān* (the two bones at the base of the back) and says it comes from the movement of bending in the bow.
- And the linguistic point transmitted there: *"in horse races the horse that comes second is called *muṣallī* — because its head comes close to the *ṣalawān* of the horse in front of it… **muṣallī**, in the most concrete state of the word, means 'the one coming right behind, following, going in another's track'."*

**One qualification.** In the preceding sūra (al-Ḥāqqa 69/31) the verb *صَلُّوهُ* occurred, and that is from the root **ص-ل-ي**: to cast into the fire. **The two roots are distinct**, and this distinction was recorded plainly in the section on al-Aʿlā 87/12-15. **I draw no meaning from this similarity of sound.**

### The same word, two sūras, two verdicts

**And now the link this sūra makes with al-Māʿūn.**

In the section on al-Māʿūn the following was recorded, and stressed plainly because it is the most misunderstood point of that sūra: the *wayl* in al-Māʿūn 107/4 is directed not at those who pray but at **a state of prayer qualified by the attribute in the fifth verse.**

And there the two sūras were compared:

| | **al-Māʿūn 107/5** | **al-Maʿārij 70/23** |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | *alladhīna hum* | *alladhīna hum* |
| Particle | **عَن** (moving away) | **عَلَىٰ** (resting upon) |
| Construct | *ṣalātihim* | *ṣalātihim* |
| Attribute | *sāhūn* (heedless) | *dāʾimūn* (constant) |

The conclusion recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn was this: *"The two verses use the same pattern, and the only difference is the particle… This opposition within the Qurʾān itself is the strongest indication that *ʿan* cannot mean 'within' here."*

**I do not repeat this. But I can complete the table** — because the Qurʾān uses this pattern with **a third particle** as well.

### The third particle

In Sūrat al-Muʾminūn the same structure occurs with a third particle:

*"Those who are humble in their prayer."* (al-Muʾminūn 23/2 — *alladhīna hum **fī** ṣalātihim khāshiʿūn*)

**Now let us set the three verses together:**

| | **al-Māʿūn 107/5** | **al-Muʾminūn 23/2** | **al-Maʿārij 70/23** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pattern | *alladhīna hum* | *alladhīna hum* | *alladhīna hum* |
| Particle | **عَنْ** — from, away from | **فِى** — within | **عَلَىٰ** — upon |
| Attribute | *sāhūn* — heedless | *khāshiʿūn* — humble | *dāʾimūn* — constant |
| What it looks at | **Distance from prayer** | **The inside of prayer** | **Attachment to prayer** |
| Verdict | **Blame** | **Praise** | **Praise** |

**Three particles, three positions.**

In the table framed in the section on al-Māʿūn there were two particles, and this was recorded there: *"Had the verse said 'fī ṣalātihim sāhūn' — 'heedless within their prayer' — the sense would have been entirely different."*

**And al-Muʾminūn 23/2 frames exactly that sentence** — but with *khāshiʿūn*, not *sāhūn*. That is, the Qurʾān uses the particle *fī* too, and when it does, it speaks of the state **inside** the prayer.

**The picture that emerges when the three verses are read together is this:** prayer can be assessed from three distinct places — the **distance** from it (*ʿan*), its **interior** (*fī*), and **attachment** to it (*ʿalā*). The Qurʾān names all three with distinct particles.

I record this as my own reading. The wordings of the three verses are data that can be verified; the two-particle table from the section on al-Māʿūn is taken from there and the third link is added here.

### دَآئِمُون — those who are constant

**Root: د-و-م.** *Dāma* — it lasted, it continued, it became permanent. *Dawām*, *mudāwama* and *dāʾiman* are from the same root.

**The concrete usage of the word is interesting and gives rise to two readings.** In Arabic **ٱلْمَاءُ ٱلدَّائِم** means *standing water*, water that does not flow. So the root carries the sense of **"remaining motionless"** as much as of "continuing".

**And this yields two readings of the verse:**

| Reading | Which aspect of the root | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuity** | To continue | They never abandon the prayer; they do not let its times slip |
| **Stillness** | To be still | They are calm in their prayer; they do not hurry, they do not fidget |

**Both have been defended in the classical sources.** And the two do not conflict; one concerns time, the other state.

In my judgement both fit the text and there is no evidence requiring a choice between them. **I do not impose a preference.**

**But the second reading gains something when compared with the last item of the list** — because the list will close with prayer, and there **a different word** will be used.

### Its grammar: a nominal sentence

**دَآئِمُون** is an **active participle**, not a verb. And the sentence is a nominal sentence.

This distinction was recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn: *"In Arabic this conveys fixity and continuity. That is, not 'they are sometimes heedless' but 'being heedless is their state.' Not a passing condition but a settled attribute."*

The same measure works here in the positive direction: *dāʾimūn* is not a passing state but **a settled attribute.**

And this will set up a contrast with the last item of the list. I shall return to it when its turn comes.

### هُمْ — the pronoun of emphasis

As recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn, the *hum* here is not grammatically necessary: it could have been *alladhīna ʿalā ṣalātihim dāʾimūn*. Its addition is for **specification and emphasis**: "these, precisely these."

**And this pronoun will be repeated six times in the list.** Between verses 22 and 34 of the sūra the pattern **ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ** occurs in six places (23, 27, 29, 32, 33, 34); in two places the pronoun is **dropped** (24 and 26).

This distribution is worth noting, and I shall return to it when its turn comes: the two items where the pronoun drops are items of **two different kinds** within the list.

## 70/24-25 — وَٱلَّذِينَ فِىٓ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ حَقٌّ مَّعْلُومٌ · لِّلسَّآئِلِ وَٱلْمَحْرُومِ

*Wa'lladhīna fī amwālihim ḥaqqun maʿlūm · Li's-sāʾili wa'l-maḥrūm*
"And those in whose wealth there is a known right — for the one who asks and for the one who is deprived."

### This verse was treated in this commentary

**In the section on al-Māʿūn 107/3**, when the two readings of the construct *"the food of the poor"* were discussed, **the ownership reading was grounded on this verse.** What was recorded there:

- *al-Dhāriyāt 51/19*: *"In their wealth there was a **right** for the one who asks and the one who is deprived."*
- *al-Maʿārij 70/24-25*: *"In their wealth there is a **known right** for the one who asks and the one who is deprived."*
- And the conclusion recorded: *"The word used in both verses is **right** — not favour, not benefaction, not gift. And its being called 'known' (maʿlūm) shows that this is not an arbitrary amount but a defined share."*
- And three consequences were listed: *"The superiority of the giver disappears… The shame of the receiver disappears… Not giving becomes not 'failing to be generous' but 'withholding a right'."*

**I do not repeat these.** Three things are to be added here: the qualification *maʿlūm*, the difference between the two recipients, and the dropping of the pronoun.

### مَّعْلُوم — "known"

**Root: ع-ل-م.** To know. *Maʿlūm* is the passive participle: **known.**

**And here is the difference from al-Dhāriyāt 51/19.** Al-Dhāriyāt says only *ḥaqq*; al-Maʿārij says *ḥaqqun **maʿlūm***. Al-Maʿārij adds a qualification: the right is a **known** right.

**What does "known" mean?** There are two readings:

| Reading | Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed in amount** | A share whose rate has been determined, like alms | *Maʿlūm* conveys definiteness; an indefinite right would not be "known" |
| **Determined by the person himself** | He has set aside a portion of his wealth and bound it to an order; this goes beyond obligatory alms | The sūra's being counted as Meccan; the institutionalisation of alms belonging to Medina |

**And here we meet the very difficulty recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn.**

In the section on al-Māʿūn this qualification was set down for the reading of *māʿūn* as alms: *"the institutionalisation of alms at fixed rates belongs to the Medinan period. If the sūra is counted Meccan, this reading is strained."*

The same difficulty is present here. And the second reading removes it: **if a person has determined his own share, no institutional rate is needed.**

**I find the second reading stronger, and my ground lies in the sūra's own logic:**

The list counts the ways of coming out of man's *halūʿ* state. And the second half of *halūʿ* is **مَنُوع**: withholding. So this item of the list ought to be the antidote to withholding.

An obligatory tax does not cure withholding — a person pays the tax and goes on withholding. But if there is a share in his wealth that **he himself has set aside** and that he **knows**, that is a change of disposition.

**This preference is not binding.** And its practical difference is limited, since both readings arrive at the same idea: within one's wealth there is a **determined** portion belonging to someone else.

### فِىٓ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ — "in their wealth"

**Note the particle.** The verse does not say *"they give of their wealth"*, nor does it say *"they set aside a portion of their wealth"*. It says **"in their wealth there is…"**.

The particle **فِى** conveys containment. That is, the right is not an obligation coming from **outside** the wealth; it is something present **within** the wealth.

And this supports at the level of grammar the ownership reading framed in the section on al-Māʿūn: the right is **not added to the wealth afterwards**; it is already there.

**And the sentence is a nominal sentence**, with no verb. That is, no act is announced but a **state**. What is said is not what the person does but **what kind of wealth his wealth is**.

I record this as an observation about the word order.

### لِّلسَّآئِلِ وَٱلْمَحْرُومِ — two recipients

**And this is the subtlest point of the verse.**

**ٱلسَّآئِل — Root: س-أ-ل.** The one who asks, the one who begs. The same word as the *sāʾil* of the sūra's first verse.

**ٱلْمَحْرُوم — Root: ح-ر-م.** *Ḥarama* — to forbid, to prohibit, to deprive. *Ḥarām*, *ḥurma*, *al-Ḥaram* (the inviolable precinct) and *iḥrām* are from the same root. **مَحْرُوم** is the passive participle: **deprived, cut off from his share.**

**In the classical sources there are two main views on who the *maḥrūm* is:**

| View | Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The poor man who does not ask** | He is in need but does not hold out his hand; out of self-restraint he does not ask | *"The ignorant would think them well off because of their restraint"* (al-Baqara 2/273) |
| **One whose property has gone** | His crop has withered, his wealth has been destroyed; his share has been cut off by a disaster | *"No, we have been **deprived**"* (al-Qalam 68/27) — the men whose garden was burnt use this word of themselves; also al-Wāqiʿa 56/67 |

**Al-Qalam 68/27 is a particularly strong indication**, because there the word is used **in exactly this sense** and from the mouths of the people themselves: the owners of the garden, having lost their produce, say *"we are maḥrūm"*.

**The two views do not exclude one another** and both are transmitted in the classical sources. What they share is this: the *maḥrūm* is the **invisible** needy person.

### Why two separate words?

**And this is the real question.**

The verse could have used a single word: *li'l-fuqarāʾ* (for the poor), *li'l-masākīn* (for the destitute). It does not. It uses **two words**, and the difference between them is **visibility**:

| | **ٱلسَّآئِل** | **ٱلْمَحْرُوم** |
|---|---|---|
| What he does | **He asks** | **He does not ask** |
| His visibility | **Visible** — he comes to the door | **Invisible** — he has to be found |
| How his right arises | By his request | **Without a request** |

**And the second row settles the whole matter.**

If what is given were a **favour**, the trigger of the favour would be the **request**: someone asks, you give. No obligation would arise concerning one who does not ask — because a favour is awakened by asking.

**But if what is given is a *right*, no request is needed.** A debt is a debt even if the creditor does not remind you of it. And the verse shows this **by putting someone who does not ask into the list.**

**So the presence of the word *maḥrūm* is the proof of the word *ḥaqq*.**

In the section on al-Māʿūn the ownership reading was grounded on the words *ḥaqq* and *maʿlūm*. What I add here is this: the third ground of the reading is **the second recipient.**

I record this as my own reading. The senses of the two words and their standing side by side in the verse are data that can be verified.

### Al-Māʿūn's *miskīn* and al-Maʿārij's *maḥrūm*

And setting the poverty-words of the two sūras side by side completes the picture:

| Word | Sūra | Root | What it brings forward |
|---|---|---|---|
| **مِسْكِين** | al-Māʿūn 107/3 | س-ك-ن — stillness | **Immobility** — poverty's paralysing effect |
| **مَحْرُوم** | al-Maʿārij 70/25 | ح-ر-م — to withhold | **Being cut off** — the blocking of a share |
| **سَآئِل** | al-Maʿārij 70/25 | س-أ-ل — to ask | **Request** — being voiced |

What was recorded for *miskīn* in the section on al-Māʿūn was: *"Poverty is not merely 'having little money'; it is **the absence of options.**"*

*Maḥrūm* adds a layer to this: poverty is **the cutting off of a share**. And the passive pattern of the word says so — *maḥrūm* is not one who has deprived himself; he is **one who has been deprived.**

I record this as an observation about the word; the pattern's being passive is a grammatical datum.

### The dropping of the pronoun

**And let us return to the grammatical detail recorded above.**

In the list the pattern *ٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ* occurs six times. **In two places the *hum* is dropped:**

- **Verse 24:** *وَٱلَّذِينَ فِىٓ أَمْوَٰلِهِمْ حَقٌّ مَّعْلُومٌ*
- **Verse 26:** *وَٱلَّذِينَ يُصَدِّقُونَ بِيَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ*

**The reason is grammatical and beyond dispute:** *hum* is a pronoun of emphasis and stands between the subject of a sentence and its predicate. In the twenty-fourth verse the subject of the sentence is *ḥaqqun* (not the persons), and in the twenty-sixth the predicate is a **verb** (*yuṣaddiqūna*). In neither case is there room for *hum*.

**This is a grammatical necessity; I draw no inference of meaning from it.** But the consequence is worth recording: the two items differ **in structure** from the other items of the list — one rests on a **state** (the character of the wealth), the other on an **act** (affirming). The remaining six items are **adjectival** clauses.

## 70/26-28 — وَٱلَّذِينَ يُصَدِّقُونَ بِيَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ · وَٱلَّذِينَ هُم مِّنْ عَذَابِ رَبِّهِم مُّشْفِقُونَ · إِنَّ عَذَابَ رَبِّهِمْ غَيْرُ مَأْمُونٍ

*Wa'lladhīna yuṣaddiqūna bi-yawmi'd-dīn · Wa'lladhīna hum min ʿadhābi rabbihim mushfiqūn · Inna ʿadhāba rabbihim ghayru maʾmūn*
"And those who affirm the day of reckoning. And those who go in dread of their Lord's punishment — for of their Lord's punishment none can be secure."

### يُصَدِّقُونَ بِيَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ — the exact negative of al-Māʿūn

**And this is the cleanest opposition between the two sūras.**

Sūrat al-Māʿūn opened with this sentence:

*"Have you seen the one who denies the religion?"* (al-Māʿūn 107/1 — *alladhī **يُكَذِّبُ بِٱلدِّينِ***)

Al-Maʿārij here says:

*"And those who affirm the day of reckoning."* (70/26 — *alladhīna **يُصَدِّقُونَ بِيَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ***)

**Let us set the two sentences side by side:**

| | **al-Māʿūn 107/1** | **al-Maʿārij 70/26** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **يُكَذِّبُ** — he denies | **يُصَدِّقُونَ** — he affirms |
| Form | *tafʿīl* | *tafʿīl* |
| Particle | **بِ** | **بِ** |
| Object | **ٱلدِّين** | **يَوْم ٱلدِّين** |
| Tense | **Imperfect** — continuing | **Imperfect** — continuing |

**The same form, the same particle, the same object, the opposite verb.**

In the section on al-Māʿūn the following was recorded of these two sūras: *"Those praised in al-Maʿārij are exactly the contrary of those blamed in al-Māʿūn… the two sūras can be read as negatives of one another."*

**This verse is the clearest evidence for that finding**, and it had not yet been framed in the section on al-Māʿūn. There the comparison was made by way of the verses on prayer; here it is made by way of the **opening sentence**.

### يُصَدِّقُ — to affirm

**Root: ص-د-ق.** *Ṣidq* — truthfulness, a statement's matching reality. **صَدَّقَ** (*tafʿīl*) — "he called it true, he affirmed it."

The distinction recorded for *kadhdhaba* in the section on al-Māʿūn works here exactly in reverse:

> *Kadhaba* (Form I) means "he lied" — the one performing the act is the liar. *Kadhdhaba* means "he called another's true word a lie".

**In the same way:** *ṣadaqa* is "he spoke truly" (the one performing the act is truthful); ***ṣaddaqa*** is **"he called another's word true"**.

**So *taṣdīq* is not your own truthfulness; it is the response you give to a report.**

And the measure recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn holds here too: *"Denial is an active stance, a taking of a position."* So is affirmation. Neither is a silent acceptance or refusal; both are **a taking of a position.**

**And from the same root comes *ṣadaqa* (the wealth given).** The link the lexicographers draw: alms is what **shows the truthfulness** of a person's faith — the act that confirms the word.

I transmit this explanation; I do not present it as a settled etymological ruling. But it should be recorded that it accords with the ordering of the list: **the twenty-fourth verse gives a right in wealth, the twenty-sixth affirmation.** The root relation binds the two items to one another.

### بِيَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ — the day of reckoning

**The word ٱلدِّين was treated in detail in the section on al-Māʿūn 107/1**: the root **د-ي-ن**, at whose centre stands **requital** (*dayn* a debt, *dāna lahū* he submitted, *dānahu* he called to account, *dīn* a way/law). And there three views were discussed and the reading *"the day of reckoning / the day of requital"* was preferred. I do not repeat it.

**And note: in al-Māʿūn there was a reading that had to be preferred; in al-Maʿārij no preference is needed** — because the verse adds the word **يَوْم**: *yawmu'd-dīn*, "the day of requital."

So al-Maʿārij closes for itself the place al-Māʿūn left open.

I record this as an observation; the wordings of the two verses are in the text.

### مُّشْفِقُون — those who go in dread

**Root: ش-ف-ق.** And this root yields one of the finest words for feeling in the Qurʾān.

**The two branches of the root:**

- **إِشْفَاق** — the state in which fear is mixed with tenderness: **anxiety** that something bad may happen to something.
- **شَفَق** — **twilight**, the redness left on the horizon after sunset.

**The second branch was treated in this commentary.** In the section on al-Inshiqāq 84/16 *al-shafaq* was taken up and the disagreement over redness/whiteness was recorded; I do not repeat it.

**And the link between the two branches explains the sense of the word.**

The explanation the lexicographers give is this: *shafaq* is neither full day nor full night — it is **a mixture of two states**. And so is *ishfāq*: neither pure fear nor pure love; **a mixture of the two.**

That is, beneath the root lies the idea of **mixture, an intermediate state, impurity.**

I transmit this explanation; I do not present it as a settled etymological ruling. But it clarifies the sense at work in the verse.

**The difference between *ishfāq* and *khawf* (fear):**

| | **خَوْف** (*khawf*) | **إِشْفَاق** (*ishfāq*) |
|---|---|---|
| Directed against | A threat | A **loss** |
| Its content | Pure fear | Fear + **love/concern** |
| Example | Fearing an enemy | Being anxious for one's child |

**The closest English word is "dread" in the older sense, or "apprehension"**; "fear" comes out too hard.

**And this changes what the verse says.** The verse does not say *"they fear the punishment"*. It says *"they go in dread of the punishment"* — that is, a fear with a **concern**, a **bond**, inside it.

Indeed what is feared is named accordingly: *ʿadhābi **rabbihim*** — "**their Lord's** punishment." As recorded in the section on al-Fajr, the word *Rabb* carries the weight of nurture and care. The authority feared is not a foreign authority.

**The Qurʾān's use of *ishfāq* confirms this:**

- *"They tremble in **awe** of Him."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/28 — of the angels)
- *"Those who go in **dread** of their Lord's punishment…"* (al-Maʿārij 70/27, and its parallel al-Muʾminūn 23/57)
- *"You will see the wrongdoers **in dread** because of what they have earned."* (al-Shūrā 42/22)
- *"We offered the trust to the heavens, the earth and the mountains, and they **shrank** from bearing it."* (al-Aḥzāb 33/72)

**Al-Anbiyāʾ 21/28 is particularly worth noting**, because there *ishfāq* is ascribed to **the angels**. The angels do not rebel; their fear is not the fear of a crime. So *ishfāq* is not a fear that results from a fault — it is a state that comes with **one's position.**

I record this as an observation; the wording of the verse is in the text.

### إِنَّ عَذَابَ رَبِّهِمْ غَيْرُ مَأْمُونٍ

**And the twenty-eighth verse is the ground of the twenty-seventh.**

This is the only sentence of **grounds** in the list. In a list of thirteen verses no item is explained — that this item is explained is worth noting.

**مَأْمُون — Root: أ-م-ن.** And this is one of the finest word-choices of the list.

The root was treated in detail in the section on **al-Baqara 2/3**: at its centre stand **trust and security**; *amān*, *amāna*, *amīn*, *al-Muʾmin* (the giver of security). And what was recorded there: *"faith is not a one-way acceptance but a bond of mutual trust."*

**مَأْمُون** is the passive participle: **that of which one can be secure, that which is trusted.** *Ghayru maʾmūn* — "that of which one cannot be secure."

**And now read the verse together with the root.**

The people counted in the list are **muʾmin** — that is, they are named with a word from the root أ-م-ن: those who make a bond of trust.

And what is said about them is this: there is one thing they **cannot be secure of** — with a word from the same root.

**So faith is a bond of trust made towards something of which one cannot be secure.**

I record this as my own reading. The shared root is a linguistic datum beyond dispute; the conclusion I draw from it is my own.

**And this establishes a theological balance.** The Qurʾān rejects both the believer's being secure from punishment and his despairing of mercy:

- *"Did they then feel **secure** from God's plan? None feels secure from God's plan but a people who are lost."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/99)
- *"Do not **despair** of God's mercy."* (al-Zumar 39/53)

**Both doors are left open.** And the *ishfāq* of the twenty-seventh verse is exactly the state between the two doors: neither security nor despair — **dread.**

And the sense of "mixture" in the root of the word carries this: *ishfāq* is the place where two states mix; like *shafaq*, neither day nor night.

I record this as my own reading. The two branches of the root and the wordings of the two verses are data that can be verified.

## 70/29-31 — وَٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ لِفُرُوجِهِمْ حَٰفِظُونَ · إِلَّا عَلَىٰٓ أَزْوَٰجِهِمْ أَوْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَٰنُهُمْ فَإِنَّهُمْ غَيْرُ مَلُومِينَ · فَمَنِ ٱبْتَغَىٰ وَرَآءَ ذَٰلِكَ فَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْعَادُونَ

*Wa'lladhīna hum li-furūjihim ḥāfiẓūn · Illā ʿalā azwājihim aw mā malakat aymānuhum fa-innahum ghayru malūmīn · Fa-mani'btaghā warāʾa dhālika fa-ulāʾika humu'l-ʿādūn*
"And those who guard their chastity — save with their wives and those their right hands possess; for these they are not blamed. Whoever seeks beyond that, they are the ones who transgress."

### An exact parallel with al-Muʾminūn

**These three verses are almost word for word the same as al-Muʾminūn 23/5-7.**

This is a rare thing in the Qurʾān: the same three verses repeated in two sūras. And the repeated block stands in the middle of this list.

**The two lists must be seen together**, because the parallel is not confined to three verses.

| | **al-Muʾminūn 23/1-11** | **al-Maʿārij 70/22-35** |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | *قَدْ أَفْلَحَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ* — they have prospered | *إِلَّا ٱلْمُصَلِّينَ* — an exception |
| Item 1 | **Humility** in prayer (*fī ṣalātihim*) | **Constancy** in prayer (*ʿalā ṣalātihim*) |
| Item 2 | Turning from idle talk (*laghw*) | **A known right in wealth** |
| Item 3 | **Alms** | **Affirming the day of reckoning** |
| Item 4 | **Chastity** (3 verses) | **Dread of the punishment** |
| Item 5 | Trusts and covenants | **Chastity** (3 verses) |
| Item 6 | **Guarding** their prayers (*ʿalā ṣalawātihim yuḥāfiẓūn*) | Trusts and covenants |
| Item 7 | — | **Upholding their testimonies** |
| Item 8 | — | **Guarding** the prayer (*ʿalā ṣalātihim yuḥāfiẓūn*) |
| Closing | *أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْوَٰرِثُونَ* | *أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ فِى جَنَّٰتٍ مُّكْرَمُونَ* |

**Three things stand out.**

**One. Both lists open with prayer and close with prayer.** In al-Muʾminūn the opening is *"they are humble in their prayer"* and the close *"they guard their prayers"*; in al-Maʿārij the opening is *"they are constant in their prayer"* and the close *"they guard their prayer"*.

**And the last item of both is the same verb: يُحَافِظُونَ.** And both use the same particle: *ʿalā*.

**Two. Al-Muʾminūn says *صَلَوَاتِهِمْ* (plural), al-Maʿārij *صَلَاتِهِمْ* (singular).** A small but real difference. The plural looks at the **individual** prayers; the singular takes the prayer as an **institution**.

**Three. Al-Maʿārij's list has two extra items:** *affirming the day of reckoning* (26) and *upholding testimonies* (33). And there is one item present in al-Muʾminūn and absent from al-Maʿārij: *turning away from idle talk.*

**There may be a reason for these differences, and I record it as my own reading:** al-Maʿārij's list is framed as **the antidote to a diagnosis.** The sūra defined man as *halūʿ*: one who does not think of the reckoning, who holds on and who cannot bear. The two items added to the list look directly at that diagnosis — *affirmation* closes the matter of the reckoning, *testimony* the matter of another's right.

Al-Muʾminūn's list, by contrast, is not the antidote to a diagnosis; it is a **description of deliverance** (*qad aflaḥa*). Its content may therefore have been arranged differently.

This is a reading; the verses of the two sūras are data that can be verified.

### فُرُوج and حَٰفِظُون

**فُرُوج — Root: ف-ر-ج.** *Faraja* — to open, to part; *furja*, the gap or opening between two things. *Faraj*, the relief of a hardship, an easing. **فَرْج** — an opening on the body; the word is a veiled expression.

**The word is itself a veil:** no direct naming is done; "opening" is said instead. The Qurʾān's language on this subject is generally of this kind.

**حَٰفِظُون — Root: ح-ف-ظ.** To guard, to preserve, to watch over. *Ḥāfiẓ*, *ḥifẓ* and *muḥāfaẓa* are from the same root.

**And this root occurs twice in the list:**

| Verse | Word | Pattern | What is guarded |
|---|---|---|---|
| **70/29** | **حَٰفِظُونَ** | *fāʿil* — active participle | **Chastity** |
| **70/34** | **يُحَافِظُونَ** | *mufāʿala* — a verb | **Prayer** |

**The same root, at the two ends of the list.** And this is the list's second frame — alongside prayer.

**And the patterns differ.** *Ḥāfiẓūn* is a simple active participle; *yuḥāfiẓūna* is of the **mufāʿala** form, and that form conveys reciprocity or **sustained effort**. The function of the mufāʿala form was treated in the section on al-Māʿūn.

So guarding chastity is **a state**, guarding prayer **an effort**.

I record this as an observation about patterns; the metres of the two words are data that can be verified.

### The exception and a legal question

**إِلَّا عَلَىٰٓ أَزْوَٰجِهِمْ أَوْ مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَٰنُهُمْ**

**This expression is a matter of jurisprudence, and per the method (`STYLE.md`) no legal ruling is issued here.** In the classical legal literature there is a broad discussion of the scope of the expression *"mā malakat aymānuhum"*, its historical application and its present-day equivalent; that discussion is not the subject of this section and is referred to the works of those qualified in it.

**The linguistic data that must be recorded are these:**

- **أَزْوَٰج — Root: ز-و-ج.** *Zawj* is **each member** of a pair. In Arabic *zawj* is used of both man and woman; the word means "spouse" and by itself conveys no gender.
- **أَيْمَٰن — Root: ي-م-ن.** Right hands. *Mā malakat aymānuhum* — "what their right hands possess"; it is an established construct for ownership in Arabic.
- **غَيْرُ مَلُومِينَ — Root: ل-و-م.** *Lawm* — to blame. *Malūm* — one who is blamed. The expression is not a **ruling** but the **lifting of a blame**: "for these they are not blamed."

### فَمَنِ ٱبْتَغَىٰ وَرَآءَ ذَٰلِكَ

**ٱبْتَغَىٰ — Root: ب-غ-ي.** *Ibtighāʾ* (the *iftiʿāl* form) — to seek, to want, to pursue. From the same root comes **بَغْي** — to transgress, to act wrongfully. The link between the two senses: wanting excessively leads to crossing a limit.

**وَرَآء — Root: و-ر-ي.** Behind, beyond. This word was treated in the section on **al-Inshiqāq 84/10** (*warāʾa ẓahrihī*) and there its link with **al-Burūj 85/20** was framed.

Here its sense is **"beyond"**: outside the limit set.

**ٱلْعَادُون — Root: ع-د-و.** *ʿAdā* — to overstep a limit, to encroach, to be hostile. *ʿAduww* (enemy), *ʿudwān* (aggression) and *iʿtidāʾ* are from the same root. **عَادُون** is the plural of the active participle: **those who transgress.**

**And this word links up with the diagnosis of the sūra.**

The sūra defined man as *halūʿ*, and *halūʿ* is by definition **without measure**: greed that is never sated, endurance that does not hold. And here, in the middle of the list, the name of measurelessness appears: *al-ʿādūn*.

So the list, while counting the contrary of being *halūʿ*, also names once the crossing of a limit.

I record this as my own reading. The senses of the two words are data that can be verified.

**And the word's closest occurrence in the Qurʾān is on the same subject:**

*"You leave the wives your Lord created for you. Indeed you are a people who **transgress**."* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/166 — *bal antum qawmun **ʿādūn***)

The same word, in the same field. And the root's sense of "crossing a limit" appears also in the verb *iʿtidāʾ* from the same root: *"You know those among you who **violated** the Sabbath"* (al-Baqara 2/65).

## 70/32-33 — وَٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ لِأَمَٰنَٰتِهِمْ وَعَهْدِهِمْ رَٰعُونَ · وَٱلَّذِينَ هُم بِشَهَٰدَٰتِهِمْ قَآئِمُونَ

*Wa'lladhīna hum li-amānātihim wa ʿahdihim rāʿūn · Wa'lladhīna hum bi-shahādātihim qāʾimūn*
"And those who tend their trusts and their covenant. And those who uphold their testimonies."

### أَمَٰنَٰت — trusts

**Root: أ-م-ن.** The same root as *maʾmūn* in the twenty-eighth verse.

**And this shows the inner weave of the list:** in the twenty-eighth verse the punishment was said not to be *maʾmūn*; in the thirty-second the person is said to tend his *amānāt*. **The same root, in two directions:** one names what cannot be trusted, the other what is given in trust.

In the section on al-Takwīr 81/21, when *amīn* was treated, the following was recorded: *"a trust is what is in a person's hands but **does not belong to him** and **must be handed over as it is**."*

**And this description links up with the twenty-fourth verse.** There was a *"known right"* in the wealth — that is, a portion in one's hands that does not belong to one. This was treated in the section on al-Māʿūn as the ownership reading.

**Two items say the same idea in two fields:** in wealth there is another's right (24), what is in your hand in trust is not yours (32).

I record this as my own reading. The description of the root is taken from the section on al-Takwīr; the link between the two items is my own.

**It comes in the plural:** *amānāt*. That is, not a single trust but **kinds** of trust. In the classical sources the scope is kept broad: obligations towards God, obligations towards people, secrets, property, offices.

The Qurʾān itself supports this breadth: *"God commands you to render trusts to those to whom they are due"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/58) — plural there too.

### عَهْد — covenant

**Root: ع-ه-د.** *ʿAhd* — to give one's word, to make a compact; and **to keep, to watch over, to visit often.** *Muʿāhada* (a treaty), *taʿahhud* (undertaking) and *ʿahd* (an era, an age) are from the same root.

**An interesting branch of the root:** *taʿahhada'sh-shayʾa* — "he looked to a thing often, he did not neglect it." So beneath the root lies **continuous attention**.

And this explains why the word for covenant comes from this root: to give one's word is not something done once and left; it is a bond that **has to be checked on continually.**

I transmit this explanation; I do not present it as a settled etymological ruling.

**It comes in the singular:** *ʿahd*. Trusts plural, covenant singular.

The classical explanation: *ʿahd* here is a generic noun and takes in all covenants. Another explanation: what is meant is the **fundamental covenant** man gives his Lord, and that is one.

Both are possible; I do not impose a preference.

### رَٰعُون — those who tend

**Root: ر-ع-ي.** And here the concrete sense of the root settles the matter.

**رَعَى** — to pasture an animal, to herd it. **رَاعٍ** — a **shepherd**. **رَعِيَّة** — the flock that is herded; and from here the sense "a people, subjects" arises.

So *rāʿin*, in the most concrete state of the word, is **a shepherd**.

**And shepherding is a particular kind of guarding.** The shepherd does not shut the flock up somewhere; he walks with it, pastures it, lets it spread out but never takes his eye off it. Not guarding but **tending.**

So the verse does not say *ḥāfiẓūn* (guardians) of the trust — three verses earlier that word was used for chastity. It says **رَٰعُونَ**: those who tend.

**The difference between the two words:**

| | **حَافِظ** | **رَاعٍ** |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete field | Keeping, storing | **Shepherding** |
| The act | Holding a thing in place | **Moving along with** a thing and not losing sight of it |
| Its object | May be fixed | **Moving** |

And trusts and covenants are **moving** things: they are used, they circulate, they are tested over time. Locking a trust in a safe may be to guard it, but it is not to do what the trust requires.

I record this as my own reading. The concrete sense of the root is set down in the dictionaries; the difference I draw between the two words is my own.

**A linguistic note.** The word *rāʿinā* is the subject of a prohibition in the Qurʾān (al-Baqara 2/104): this word, from the same root, was ordered not to be used because it carried an improper association in another language. That verse was not reached in the section on al-Baqara; here I record only the shared root.

### بِشَهَٰدَٰتِهِمْ قَآئِمُون — those who uphold their testimonies

**And this is the most striking item of the list** — because it is the only **public** item in it.

The other items of the list concern the person himself or his property: prayer, a right in his wealth, affirmation, dread, chastity, trust. **Testimony, by contrast, is where another human being's right is settled.**

And there the one who speaks is not himself a party; he is a **third person.**

**شَهَٰدَٰت — Root: ش-ه-د.** *Shahida* — to be present, to witness. *Shāhid* (a witness), *shahīd*, *mashhad* and *mushāhada* are from the same root.

**At the centre of the root is "being present".** A witness is the person who was **there**. This is why testimony differs from knowledge: knowledge can be transmitted, testimony cannot.

**It comes in the plural:** *shahādāt*. And this is rare in the Qurʾān; the word is generally used in the singular. The plural counts **each single instance** of testimony separately: every testimony is a separate obligation.

**قَآئِمُون — Root: ق-و-م.** To stand, to rise up, to hold a matter upright. This root was treated in the section on **al-Baqara 2/3** by way of *iqāma*, and there the following was recorded: *"discharging closes a debt, upholding keeps a thing standing."*

**And the verb comes here with *bāʾ*:** *qāʾimūn **bi**-shahādātihim* — "standing with their testimonies."

In Arabic the construction *qāma bi-* means **to take on a task, to carry it out, to bear its responsibility.** So testimony is a load that is carried.

**The Qurʾān's verses on testimony show how heavy this item is:**

- *"O you who believe! Be **upholders** of justice, **witnesses** for God, even against yourselves, or your parents and kin."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/135)
- *"Do not **conceal** testimony. Whoever conceals it, his heart is sinful."* (al-Baqara 2/283)
- *"**Uphold** testimony for God."* (al-Ṭalāq 65/2)

**Al-Nisāʾ 4/135 is particularly worth noting**, because it describes the hardest form of testimony: even against oneself.

**And this links up with the ransom scene of the sūra.**

In the eleventh through the fourteenth verses the criminal was offering his **kin** as ransom: his sons, his wife, his brother, his kin. That is, he was **sacrificing** the bond of kinship for his own advantage.

In al-Nisāʾ 4/135 the exact opposite is asked: setting the bond of kinship aside **for the sake of the truth.**

**The two scenes take up the same bonds and tell of giving them up on two different grounds:** one to save oneself, the other to speak the truth.

I record this as my own reading. The wordings of the two verses are data that can be verified; the comparison is my own.

### The place of testimony in the list

**The position of the item is meaningful too:** after the trusts, before the prayer.

That is, the list runs thus: what concerns oneself (prayer, wealth, affirmation, dread, chastity) → what concerns another (trust, covenant, testimony) → and prayer again.

**The middle of the list looks inward, its end outward; and prayer stands at the two ends.**

I record this as a reading of the structure; the order of the items is in the text.

## 70/34-35 — وَٱلَّذِينَ هُمْ عَلَىٰ صَلَاتِهِمْ يُحَافِظُونَ · أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ فِى جَنَّٰتٍ مُّكْرَمُونَ

*Wa'lladhīna hum ʿalā ṣalātihim yuḥāfiẓūn · Ulāʾika fī jannātin mukramūn*
"And those who guard their prayer. It is they who will be honoured in gardens."

### The list closes with prayer

**And now we can see the frame of the list.**

| | **Opening — 70/23** | **Closing — 70/34** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *عَلَىٰ صَلَاتِهِمْ **دَآئِمُونَ*** | *عَلَىٰ صَلَاتِهِمْ **يُحَافِظُونَ*** |
| Particle | **عَلَىٰ** | **عَلَىٰ** |
| Construct | *ṣalātihim* | *ṣalātihim* |
| Class of the word | **A noun** — active participle | **A verb** — imperfect |
| Root | د-و-م — constancy | ح-ف-ظ — guarding |
| What it conveys | **A fixed state** | **A renewed effort** |

**A list of thirteen verses opens and closes with the same particle and the same construct. Two things change: the root and the class of the word.**

**And both changes are meaningful.**

**One — the change of root: from constancy to guarding.**

*Dāʾimūn* announces that the prayer **continues**: it is not abandoned.
*Yuḥāfiẓūn* announces that the prayer is **guarded**: it is not spoiled.

The two are different things. Something may be continuing and yet be spoiled; the person Sūrat al-Māʿūn describes is exactly this — he has a prayer but he is heedless of his prayer.

**So the list puts the two separate tests of prayer at its two ends: not to abandon and not to spoil.**

**Two — the change of class: from noun to verb.**

This distinction was recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn, and there it worked in the opposite direction:

> *"In the preceding verse there was *sāhūn* (an active participle, a fixed state); here there is *yurāʾūna* (a verb, imperfect). In Arabic this difference is meaningful: **heedlessness is a settled condition, while showing off is an act performed anew each time.**"*

**The same measure works here in the positive direction:**

- **دَآئِمُونَ** — a noun: attachment to prayer is **a settled state.**
- **يُحَافِظُونَ** — a verb: guarding the prayer is **a work done anew each time.**

So the list says: attachment is established once and stands; guarding is repeated at every prayer.

**And this accords with the whole logic of the list.** The list counts the ways out of a diagnosis (*halūʿ*). A change of constitution requires both **settling** and **continual effort**. The sūra puts both on the list and names both with prayer.

I record this as my own reading. The patterns and roots of the two words are data that can be verified; the distinction from the section on al-Māʿūn is taken from there.

### Three sūras, three sentences on prayer

**And now the table begun in the section on al-Māʿūn can be completed.**

In the section on al-Māʿūn two verses were compared. Above I added al-Muʾminūn 23/2. Now the fourth link comes as well:

| Verse | Particle | Attribute | Pattern | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Māʿūn 107/5** | **عَنْ** | *sāhūn* — heedless | A noun | **Blame** |
| **al-Muʾminūn 23/2** | **فِى** | *khāshiʿūn* — humble | A noun | Praise |
| **al-Maʿārij 70/23** | **عَلَىٰ** | *dāʾimūn* — constant | A noun | Praise |
| **al-Maʿārij 70/34** | **عَلَىٰ** | *yuḥāfiẓūn* — guardians | **A verb** | Praise |
| **al-Muʾminūn 23/9** | **عَلَىٰ** | *yuḥāfiẓūn* — guardians | **A verb** | Praise |

**Five verses, one pattern (*alladhīna hum* + particle + *ṣalāt* + attribute), three different particles.**

And note: **both sūras close with the same verb** — *ʿalā ṣalātihim/ṣalawātihim yuḥāfiẓūn.* Al-Maʿārij in the singular (*ṣalātihim*), al-Muʾminūn in the plural (*ṣalawātihim*).

I record this as a textual datum that can be verified; the wordings of the five verses are in the text. The interpretation that the table constitutes an order is my own.

### أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ فِى جَنَّٰتٍ مُّكْرَمُونَ

**The conclusion of the list.**

**أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ** — the far demonstrative in the plural: "it is they." As recorded for *dhālika* in the section on al-Māʿūn, in Arabic the far demonstrative can be used both for belittling and for **exalting**; here it is the second: their position is distinct enough to be pointed at from a distance.

**فِى جَنَّٰتٍ** — plural and **indefinite**: "in gardens", "in certain gardens". The indefiniteness here conveys magnification.

And the particle again is **فِى**: enclosure. As recorded for *fī ʿīshatin rāḍiya* in the section on al-Qāriʿa: *"The person does not possess the life; he is inside the life. Not possession but enclosure."*

**مُّكْرَمُون — Root: ك-ر-م.** **A passive participle: "those honoured, those given hospitality."**

**And its being passive is critical.**

The verse does not say *"those who are **karīm** in the gardens"* — that would be an attribute of the persons themselves. It says **"those who are honoured"**: the honouring is something **done to** them.

And this links directly with the matter treated in Sūrat al-Fajr.

In al-Fajr 89/15 man said: *"My Lord has **honoured** me"* — and the conclusion recorded in the section on al-Fajr was: *"Man reads wealth as a statement of worth… The error concerns what the thing given means."*

**Al-Maʿārij shows where the real honouring lies.**

| | **al-Fajr 89/15** | **al-Maʿārij 70/35** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *فَأَكْرَمَهُۥ … رَبِّىٓ أَكْرَمَنِ* | *فِى جَنَّٰتٍ **مُّكْرَمُونَ*** |
| Where | **In the world** — wealth | **In the gardens** |
| Who says it | **Man himself** — an inference | **The narrator** — a verdict |
| What happens | Abundance is taken for honouring | Honouring is **given** |

And the link recorded in the section on al-Ḥāqqa is added here too: in al-Fajr 89/17 it was said *"you do not **honour** the orphan"*. So the same root in three places: the honouring expected (al-Fajr 15), the honouring not given (al-Fajr 17), the honouring granted (al-Maʿārij 35).

I record this as my own reading. The shared root of the three verses is a datum that can be verified.

### The list as a whole

**Thirteen verses, six items, two frames of prayer.** Its table is this:

| # | Verse | Item | Field |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Frame** | 23 | **Constancy** in prayer | Worship |
| 1 | 24-25 | A **known right** in wealth | Wealth |
| 2 | 26 | **Affirming** the day of reckoning | Belief |
| 3 | 27-28 | **Dread** of the punishment | Inner state |
| 4 | 29-31 | **Chastity** | Body |
| 5 | 32 | **Trust and covenant** | Another's right |
| 6 | 33 | **Testimony** | Public |
| **Frame** | 34 | **Guarding** the prayer | Worship |

**And the arrangement of the list carries a logic** — I record it as my own reading:

The list goes **from outside inward, then from inside outward.** It opens with prayer (outer), moves to wealth (outer), descends to belief (inner), descends to the inner state (innermost), rises to the body, rises to trust (another), rises to testimony (everyone), and closes with prayer (outer).

So the list traces a curve of **descent and ascent**, with *ishfāq* (the inner state) at its centre.

This is a reading; the order of the items is in the text.

**And the list's relation to the diagnosis of *halūʿ*:**

| Half of *halūʿ* | Its antidote in the list |
|---|---|
| **مَنُوع** — withholding when good touches | **A known right in wealth** (24-25), **trust** (32) |
| **جَزُوع** — wailing when evil touches | **Constancy in prayer** (23), **dread of the punishment** (27) |

**And note:** the antidote to *jazūʿ* is not patience but **prayer** and **dread.** That is, the sūra treats intolerance not by saying "be patient" outright but by **building the support on which patience rests.**

In the fifth verse it had said *"be patient with a beautiful patience"*. The list shows how that command is made possible: a regular bond (prayer) and a right fear (*ishfāq*).

I record this as my own reading.

## 70/36-37 — فَمَالِ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ قِبَلَكَ مُهْطِعِينَ · عَنِ ٱلْيَمِينِ وَعَنِ ٱلشِّمَالِ عِزِينَ

*Fa-māli'lladhīna kafarū qibalaka muhṭiʿīn · ʿAni'l-yamīni wa ʿani'sh-shimāli ʿizīn*
"What is the matter with those who disbelieve, that they come craning their necks towards you — from the right and from the left, in scattered groups?"

### The sūra turns back to those who asked

**And this is the beginning of the sūra's third section.**

In the first verse there was someone asking a question. Now the sūra turns to him — and to those like him. The thirty-five verses in between were the answer to the question.

**And note: the sūra opened with a question, and now answers with a question.**

| | **70/1** | **70/36** |
|---|---|---|
| Who asks | **They** | **The text** |
| The question | *"When is the punishment?"* | *"What is the matter with these people?"* |
| Is an answer expected | **No** — mockery | **No** — reproach |

**Neither question wants an answer.** One is mockery, the other reproach. The sūra takes the form of the addressee's question and gives it back to him.

I record this as my own reading. The positions of the two questions are in the text.

### فَمَالِ — "what is the matter?"

There is something noteworthy in the orthography: **مَا** and **لِ** are written separately (*mā li-*). It is one of the peculiarities of the orthography of the codex; it does not affect the sense.

The pattern **فَمَا لِـ** conveys in Arabic **astonishment, reproach and rebuke**: "What is the matter with these people?", "What is wrong with them that they do this?"

The closest English rendering: *"What is wrong with them?"*

This pattern occurs in several places in the Qurʾān and is always in the same tone: *"What is the matter with them, that they turn away from the reminder?"* (al-Muddaththir 74/49).

### قِبَلَكَ — "towards you"

**Root: ق-ب-ل.** *Qabila* — to meet, to accept. **قِبَل** — the front of a thing, its facing side. *Qibla* (the direction turned to), *muqābil* (opposite), *istiqbāl* (reception, and "the future") are from the same root.

**قِبَلَكَ** — "towards your side", "facing you."

So the people are coming **towards** the Prophet. They are not fleeing; they are drawing near.

**And this is the strangeness of the scene.** Those who deny gather around the man they deny.

### مُهْطِعِين — running with outstretched necks

**Root: ه-ط-ع.** *Haṭaʿa* — to stretch out the neck, to fix one's eyes; and **to go quickly with the head raised.**

The meanings the lexicographers give gather into two branches:

| Meaning | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **To fix one's gaze** | Looking with the neck stretched out, without blinking |
| **To run fast** | Running towards a target with the head up |

**Both describe the same bodily movement:** the neck extending forward. The one who looks stretches his neck, and so does the one who runs.

**Its other occurrences in the Qurʾān show both branches:**

- *"They **run** with their heads raised; their gaze does not return to them."* (Ibrāhīm 14/43 — *muhṭiʿīna muqniʿī ruʾūsihim*)
- *"With their eyes cast down, they will come out of the graves **hastening** to the caller."* (al-Qamar 54/8 — *muhṭiʿīna ila'd-dāʿ*)

**Both verses are in a scene of the resurrection.** That is, in the Qurʾān the word generally describes the people running **on that day**.

**And al-Maʿārij uses it in this world.**

So the verse describes those craning their necks around the Prophet today by the movement of those who will run on that day. **The same bodily movement, in two scenes.**

And the sūra will repeat this plainly in the forty-third verse: *"They come out of the graves in haste."*

I record this as my own reading. The wordings of the three verses are data that can be verified.

**The character of the gaze.** The verse does not say "they listen", does not say "they ask". It describes a **bodily movement**: the stretching of the neck.

And this movement conveys not attention **itself** but the appearance of attention. They look but do not take in; they draw near but do not join.

I record this as a reading.

### عَنِ ٱلْيَمِينِ وَعَنِ ٱلشِّمَالِ — from the right and from the left

**Both directions are mentioned**, and this conveys **encirclement**: from every side.

**And these two words were the names of two destinies in the preceding sūra.**

In al-Ḥāqqa 69/19 the book was given **بِيَمِينِهِ** (in his right hand); in 69/25 **بِشِمَالِهِ** (in his left). This opposition was treated in the section on al-Ḥāqqa.

**Al-Maʿārij uses the same two words — but as directions, not destinies.**

| | **al-Ḥāqqa 69/19, 25** | **al-Maʿārij 70/37** |
|---|---|---|
| *yamīn* / *shimāl* | **Two destinies** — the saved and the lost | **Two directions** — from the right and from the left |
| What it conveys | **Division** | **Encirclement** |

**Two neighbouring sūras use the same pair for two different tasks:** in al-Ḥāqqa it divides, in al-Maʿārij it gathers.

I record this as my own reading. The occurrences of the words in the two sūras are data that can be verified. I claim no wordplay; *yamīn* and *shimāl* are ordinary words for direction in Arabic.

### عِزِين — scattered groups

**The word occurs only here in the Qurʾān.**

**عِزِين** is the plural of *ʿizatun* and means **separate companies, scattered clusters**: groups standing not together but in clumps.

**There are two views on its root:**

| Root | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **ع-ز-و / ع-ز-ي** | *ʿAzā ilā* — to **attribute** to a lineage or a group, to count as belonging. On this view *ʿiza* is a small company whose members attribute themselves to one another |
| **ع-ز-ز** | A reading that ties it to the sense of separating, dividing |

**The first origin is the more widespread** and adds an interesting layer to the sense of the word.

**For the verb *ʿazā* is "belonging": being attached to a group.** And *ʿizīn* is that belonging **multiplied**: everyone has a group, but there is no common group.

**So the word conveys both joining and dispersal at once:** the people have come together — but **in clumps.**

I record this as an observation about the word. The disagreement over the origin is set down in the sources; the layer of meaning I draw from the first origin is my own and is **not offered as a settled etymological ruling.**

**And this completes the scene:** people have gathered around the Prophet, they have stretched out their necks, they look from right and left — but **they are not one community.** They stand in clusters.

**A grammatical note.** The word *ʿizīn* is declined like a **sound masculine plural** (with *-īn*), but its singular is *ʿiza* and it is feminine. Some words in Arabic are declined this way (*sinīn* — years, singular *sana*). The final *nūn* of the word compensates for a dropped letter. This also makes the word fit the verse-ending.

### What this scene says to the present

These two verses are among the rare verses that describe the listener, and they give an image worth recording for today.

**The scene is this:** people come, draw near, look, crane their necks — and stand scattered. There is interest, there is no participation. There is proximity, there is no bond.

And in the thirty-eighth verse this interest will turn into an expectation: *"Does each one of them hope to be admitted to a garden?"* That is, watching is taken for joining.

I record this as a reading; the content of the two verses and their link with the next verse are in the text.

## 70/38-39 — أَيَطْمَعُ كُلُّ ٱمْرِئٍ مِّنْهُمْ أَن يُدْخَلَ جَنَّةَ نَعِيمٍ · كَلَّآ ۖ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَٰهُم مِّمَّا يَعْلَمُونَ

*A-yaṭmaʿu kullu'mriʾin minhum an yudkhala jannata naʿīm · Kallā innā khalaqnāhum mimmā yaʿlamūn*
"Does each one of them covet being admitted to a garden of bliss? No! We created them from what they know."

### أَيَطْمَعُ — does he covet?

**Root: ط-م-ع.** *Ṭamiʿa* — to want greedily, to set one's eye on, to carry an expectation that has no ground.

**And in Arabic there is a difference between *ṭamaʿ* and *rajāʾ* (hope):**

| | **رَجَاء** (*rajāʾ*) | **طَمَع** (*ṭamaʿ*) |
|---|---|---|
| What it rests on | A cause, a promise | **Nothing** |
| Its tone | Neutral or positive | **Generally negative** — greed |

*Ṭamaʿ* is to expect to obtain without any reason for obtaining.

**And the word sits exactly right in this verse:** the people hope to enter the Garden; but for thirty-five verses the sūra had been counting who will enter the Garden — a list of eight items. These people are not on that list.

**So the question is: on what ground?**

And the question is not answered; it is cut off by *kallā*.

**The Qurʾān uses *ṭamaʿ* in a positive context too** — so the word is not bad in itself: *"We **hope** our Lord will forgive us our faults"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/51); *"Call upon Him in fear and **hope**"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/56). The value of the word depends on the ground of what is hoped for.

I record this because the verse does not forbid *ṭamaʿ*; it rejects the **groundless** kind.

### كُلُّ ٱمْرِئٍ مِّنْهُمْ — "each one"

**The verse speaks not collectively but one by one:** "**each one** of them."

The preceding verse had shown them in **clusters** (*ʿizīn*). Now the clusters too break up: **each one separately.**

And this prepares the next step: the list was an individual list; the reckoning too is individual.

**ٱمْرِئ — Root: م-ر-أ.** A person, a man. *Al-marʾ* was treated in the section on ʿAbasa 80/34, and there its kinship of root with *murūʾa* (manliness, virtue) was recorded as a nicety; I do not repeat it.

**And there is a link between the two verses:** in ʿAbasa 80/34 *al-marʾ* was fleeing; here *imruʾ* is coveting. The same word, in two different scenes.

### أَن يُدْخَلَ — passive

**The verb's being passive is critical.**

The verse does not say *"to **enter** the Garden"*. It says **"to be admitted to the Garden"**: *yudkhala*, passive.

That is, entering is not something the person does; it is something done to him. He does not pass through the gate himself; he **is taken in.**

**And this accords with the last sentence of the list:** *"It is they who will be **honoured** in gardens"* (70/35) — passive there too: *mukramūn.*

**Two passive verbs, about two different groups:** one is honoured, the other covets being admitted. **The same passivity, one realised and one merely hoped for.**

I record this as my own reading. The patterns of the two verbs are data that can be verified.

**جَنَّةَ نَعِيمٍ** — "a garden of bliss". It comes indefinite (*naʿīm*, undetermined). **Root: ن-ع-م.** *Niʿma*, *naʿīm*, *nāʿim* — softness, abundance, ease.

And in the section on al-Takāthur *naʿīm* was treated (*"then on that day you will be asked about the bliss"*, 102/8) and the views on the scope of the bliss were given there in a table. I do not repeat it.

### كَلَّا — the second time

**Kallā occurs twice in the sūra:**

| Verse | What it cuts off |
|---|---|
| **70/15** | **The offer of ransom** — in the hereafter |
| **70/39** | **The expectation of the Garden** — in this world |

**Both cut off an expectation.** One the expectation of escape, the other the expectation of gain.

And both are **groundless** expectations: he had nothing to give as ransom, he has no ground for entering the Garden.

I record this as my own reading. The two occurrences can be counted in the text.

### إِنَّا خَلَقْنَٰهُم مِّمَّا يَعْلَمُونَ — "We created them from what they know"

**The shortest and hardest answer in the sūra.**

**The expression is compressed** and has two readings:

| Reading | Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The substance of creation** | "We created them from that thing whose nature they know" — that is, from a simple fluid. They have no origin to boast of | The Qurʾān names this substance plainly elsewhere |
| **Being created is not enough** | "We created them — and creating is not admitting to the Garden." Having been brought into being does not entail having a claim | What *kallā* cuts off being a **claim of right** |

**The first reading is the more widespread in the classical sources** and has strong support within the Qurʾān:

- *"Did We not create you from a despised fluid?"* (al-Mursalāt 77/20)
- *"Then He made his progeny from an extract of a base fluid."* (al-Sajda 32/8)
- *"Let man look at what he was created from!"* (al-Ṭāriq 86/5)

**Al-Ṭāriq 86/5** was treated in this commentary and what that looking does was taken up there; I do not repeat it.

**The second reading is defensible too** and fits the context: the question carried a **claim of right** ("why should I not enter?"), and the answer cuts off the ground of that claim.

**In my judgement the two do not exclude one another** and together give a single idea: **there is no superiority in one's origin.** I do not impose a preference.

### مِّمَّا يَعْلَمُونَ — "from what they know"

**And here is the finest point in the expression.**

The verse does not **name** the substance. It does not say *"We created them from a fluid"*. It says **"from what they know"**.

That is, the knowledge is referred back to **the addressee**: you already know.

**And this does two things:**

**One — it says without saying.** The word is not mentioned, but everyone knows. This is a choice of decorum and is often found in the Qurʾān's language on this subject.

**Two — it uses the addressee's own knowledge against him.** The same technique was recorded in the section on al-Takwīr 81/22: *"the verse uses the strongest evidence in the addressee's hands — his own observation — against him."*

There the evidence was *"your companion"*; here it is *"what you know"*.

I record this as my own reading. The finding in the section on al-Takwīr is taken from there.

### Two sentences of "creation"

**And the root خ-ل-ق occurs twice in the sūra**, and both are sentences of diagnosis:

| Verse | Expression | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| **70/19** | *إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ **خُلِقَ** هَلُوعًا* | Man's **disposition** |
| **70/39** | *إِنَّا **خَلَقْنَٰهُم** مِّمَّا يَعْلَمُونَ* | Man's **substance** |

**Both sentences begin with the same particle (*inna*) and use the same root.**

And together they complete a picture: **his disposition is intolerant, his substance worthless.**

And note: the first is **passive** (*khuliqa* — "he was created"), the second **active** (*khalaqnāhum* — "We created them"). That is, when the diagnosis is delivered the agent is concealed; when the claim is rejected the agent is declared.

I record this as my own reading. The patterns of the two verses are data that can be verified.

## 70/40-41 — فَلَآ أُقْسِمُ بِرَبِّ ٱلْمَشَٰرِقِ وَٱلْمَغَٰرِبِ إِنَّا لَقَٰدِرُونَ · عَلَىٰٓ أَن نُّبَدِّلَ خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمَسْبُوقِينَ

*Fa-lā uqsimu bi-rabbi'l-mashāriqi wa'l-maghāribi innā la-qādirūn · ʿAlā an nubaddila khayran minhum wa mā naḥnu bi-masbūqīn*
"No! I swear by the Lord of the risings and the settings that We are surely able to replace them with better than them — and We are not to be outrun."

### فَلَآ أُقْسِمُ — the same formula as in the neighbouring sūra

This formula was set out in four views in a table in the section on **al-Balad 90/1** and was also taken up in the section on **al-Ḥāqqa 69/38**. I do not repeat it.

**And in the section on al-Ḥāqqa the two sūras were compared:**

| | **al-Ḥāqqa 69/38-39** | **al-Maʿārij 70/40** |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | *فَلَآ أُقْسِمُ* | *فَلَا أُقْسِمُ* |
| Sworn by | **بِمَا تُبْصِرُونَ وَمَا لَا تُبْصِرُونَ** | **بِرَبِّ ٱلْمَشَٰرِقِ وَٱلْمَغَٰرِبِ** |
| The answer to the oath | *إِنَّهُۥ لَقَوْلُ رَسُولٍ كَرِيمٍ* — **the source of the text** | *إِنَّا لَقَٰدِرُونَ* — **the scope of the power** |

**Two neighbouring sūras defend two different things with the same formula:** one the truth of a word, the other the sufficiency of a power.

And the **subject** of each oath suits its function:

- Al-Ḥāqqa defends the source of a **word**, and swears by the distinction between **what is seen and what is not seen** — because the source of a word is not seen.
- Al-Maʿārij defends the scope of a **power**, and swears by **the risings and the settings** — because power is measured by scope.

I record this as my own reading. The subjects of the two oaths are in the text.

### ٱلْمَشَٰرِقِ وَٱلْمَغَٰرِبِ — the risings and the settings

**And here there is a striking linguistic datum.**

The Qurʾān uses these two words in **three different numbers**:

| Number | Verse | Expression |
|---|---|---|
| **Singular** | al-Baqara 2/115, 2/142 and elsewhere | *ٱلْمَشْرِقُ وَٱلْمَغْرِبُ* |
| **Dual** | al-Raḥmān 55/17 | *رَبُّ ٱلْمَشْرِقَيْنِ وَرَبُّ ٱلْمَغْرِبَيْنِ* |
| **Plural (one side)** | al-Ṣāffāt 37/5 | *وَرَبُّ ٱلْمَشَٰرِقِ* |
| **Plural (both sides)** | **al-Maʿārij 70/40** | *رَبِّ ٱلْمَشَٰرِقِ وَٱلْمَغَٰرِبِ* |

**This verse is the place where both come in the plural.**

**The classical exegetes' explanation of this trio is as follows**, and it rests on observation:

- **Singular:** east and west as directions; two basic bearings.
- **Dual:** the two extreme points of sunrise and sunset in the summer and winter months. This is an observable fact: the sun rises and sets at different points of the horizon at different times of the year.
- **Plural:** the point of rising shifts slightly on **every day** of the year; hence the points of rising are many in number.

**For a community living in the desert and reading the horizon like a calendar, this explanation is an ordinary observation.** To find one's way and to reckon the seasons one needs to know that the sun's point of rising shifts across the year; and that knowledge was present in that society.

**One qualification: I do not offer this as a claim of scientific miracle.** By the principle set down in `STYLE.md`, such links are made only if they genuinely exist and can be made without strain. What is described here is not a modern discovery; it is **a fact visible to the naked eye and known at that time.** The only thing I record is that the Qurʾān used the singular, dual and plural forms separately — and this is a textual datum that can be verified.

**And the function of the oath:** the title *"Lord of the risings and the settings"* conveys **regular recurrence**. The sun rises every day, sets every day, and its point shifts a little every day.

So what is sworn by is **an order that is renewed every day and never falters.** And the answer to the oath is a power of **replacement**.

I record this as my own reading. The fit between the oath and its answer is a reading.

### أَن نُّبَدِّلَ خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ

**Root: ب-د-ل.** *Badala* — to change, to put another in place of. **بَدَّلَ** (*tafʿīl*) — to exchange one thing for another. *Badal*, *tabdīl* and *istibdāl* are from the same root.

**And this threat occurs in several places in the Qurʾān:**

- *"If you turn away, He will **bring** another people in your place; then they will not be like you."* (Muḥammad 47/38)
- *"O you who believe! Whoever of you turns back from his religion, God will **bring** a people whom…"* (al-Māʾida 5/54)
- *"If He wills, He can remove you and **bring** others in your place."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/133; Ibrāhīm 14/19-20; Fāṭir 35/16-17)

**The shared idea is this:** the call does not depend on its addressee. The addressee may change; the call continues.

**And this links up with the fifth verse of the sūra.** There it had said *"be patient"*. Patience requires accepting that the outcome is not in one's own hands. The fortieth verse gives the ground of that acceptance: **the outcome is not in your hands anyway; and if need be, others will be brought.**

I record this as my own reading. The link between the two verses is a reading.

**خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ** — "better than them". And note: the verse does not say "others"; it says **"better than them"**. So the change is not a mere substitution but an **improvement.**

### وَمَا نَحْنُ بِمَسْبُوقِينَ

**Root: س-ب-ق.** *Sabaqa* — to pass, to get ahead, to leave behind. *Sābiq* (the one in front), *sibāq* (a race) and *musābaqa* are from the same root.

**مَسْبُوق** is the passive participle: **one who is passed, outrun, left behind in a race.**

*Mā naḥnu bi-masbūqīn* — "we are not to be outrun." And the *bāʾ* is redundant; it conveys emphasis. The same note was set down for the *mā … bi-* structure in the section on al-Takwīr 81/22: **not "is not" but "is by no means".**

**There are two readings of the expression's sense:**

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| **The impossibility of escape** | No one can **escape** Us; no one can get ahead of Us |
| **Not falling short** | We do not **fall behind** in this matter; We cannot be prevented from doing what We will |

The Qurʾān's own usage supports the first reading: *"Do those who do evil suppose they will **outrun** Us?"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/4 — *an yasbiqūnā*).

**Both occur in the sources and come out in the same place.** I do not impose a preference.

**And the word comes from the field of racing.** In a race, to be "passed" is to fall behind. The verse takes this image and rejects it.

And this links up with the first verse of the sūra: the man was taking the **delay** of the punishment as evidence. The forty-first verse gives the answer: **delay is not falling behind.**

I record this as my own reading.

## 70/42 — فَذَرْهُمْ يَخُوضُوا۟ وَيَلْعَبُوا۟ حَتَّىٰ يُلَٰقُوا۟ يَوْمَهُمُ ٱلَّذِى يُوعَدُونَ

*Fa-dharhum yakhūḍū wa yalʿabū ḥattā yulāqū yawmahumu'lladhī yūʿadūn*
"So leave them to plunge and to play until they meet the day they are promised."

### The sūra's second command

In the fifth verse it had said *"be patient"*; here it says *"leave"*. The sūra's two commands, at its two ends.

**And the two commands together describe a stance:** hold yourself in (patience), do not interfere with them (leaving).

**فَذَرْ — Root: و-ذ-ر.** *Wadhara* — to leave, to abandon. The verb has a curious feature: in Arabic **its perfect and its verbal noun are not used**; only the imperative and the imperfect forms are in service. The missing places are filled by the verb *taraka*.

**This command occurs in several places in the Qurʾān, and always in the same context:**

- *"**Leave** them to eat and to enjoy themselves, and let hope distract them."* (al-Ḥijr 15/3)
- *"**Leave** Me and the deniers."* (al-Muzzammil 73/11)
- *"**Leave** those who have taken their religion as play and amusement."* (al-Anʿām 6/70)

**And this command is not a giving up.** The Qurʾān at the same time commands the conveying of the message. The sense of the command is this: **stop forcing**, stop taking the outcome on yourself.

The measure recorded in this commentary in the sections on al-Ghāshiya 88/22 and al-Fajr 89/14 holds here too: *"the watch-post belongs not to the messenger but to the Lord"*; *"your task is to convey."*

### يَخُوضُوا۟ — let them plunge

**Root: خ-و-ض.** And the concrete sense of the root sets the image.

**خَاضَ ٱلْمَاءَ** — **he entered the water, he walked in the water**, he sank into mud. This is the primary sense of the root: to enter a liquid and walk in it.

**And from here the metaphor arises:** *khāḍa fi'l-ḥadīth* — he plunged into talk; especially **empty, groundless, irresponsible** talk.

**The logic of the metaphor:** one who walks in water does not see where he sets his foot; the bottom is murky, he does not know what he is treading on. So it is with one who plunges into empty talk: he does not see where he is arriving.

I transmit this explanation; the concrete sense of the root is set down in the dictionaries, and the logic of the metaphor is an explanation of the kind the lexicographers give.

**And this word occurs in a verse mentioned in the section on al-Māʿūn.**

In the section on al-Māʿūn al-Muddaththir 74/42-46 was transmitted: the four-item confession of those in the Fire. The third item was this:

*"We used to **plunge into vain talk with those who plunged**."* (al-Muddaththir 74/45 — *wa kunnā **nakhūḍu** maʿa'l-**khāʾiḍīn***)

**So al-Maʿārij 70/42 describes what is confessed in al-Muddaththir 74/45.**

And note a detail in the sentence in al-Muddaththir — *"**with** those who plunged"*. That is, the act is not done alone; it is a **collective** business.

And so it is in al-Maʿārij: *"yakhūḍū"* is plural, and two verses earlier it had said *ʿizīn* (scattered groups). **People gathered in clumps, plunging together.**

I record this as my own reading. The wordings of the two verses and the transmission in the section on al-Māʿūn are data that can be verified.

**The root's other occurrences:** *"You plunged as they **plunged**"* (al-Tawba 9/69); *"When you see those who **talk idly** about Our signs, turn away from them"* (al-Anʿām 6/68).

### وَيَلْعَبُوا۟ — let them play

**Root: ل-ع-ب.** *Laʿiba* — to play. *Luʿba* (a game), *malʿab* (a playing-ground).

**And there is a definition of this word in the Qurʾān:** play is work **that has no result**. The Qurʾān qualifies the life of this world with this word in several places:

- *"The life of this world is only **play** and amusement."* (al-Anʿām 6/32; al-ʿAnkabūt 29/64; Muḥammad 47/36; al-Ḥadīd 57/20)

**Why are the two verbs side by side?**

| Verb | What it looks at |
|---|---|
| **يَخُوضُوا۟** | **Speech** — empty talk |
| **يَلْعَبُوا۟** | **Action** — an occupation with no result |

**So one describes their tongues, the other their hands.** Together the two take in a whole life: what is said and what is done.

I record this as my own reading.

**And one more thing:** both verbs are in the jussive as **the response to a command** (*yakhūḍū*, *yalʿabū*). That is, grammatically the structure *"leave them so that they may plunge"* is built. This is not permission; it is **the statement of a consequence.**

The verse does not say *"allow them to plunge"*; it says *"if you leave them, they plunge"*.

### حَتَّىٰ يُلَٰقُوا۟ — "until they meet"

**Root: ل-ق-ي.** To meet, to encounter. **مُلَاقَاة** (*mufāʿala*) — a mutual meeting.

This pattern was treated in the section on **al-Ḥāqqa 69/20** (*mulāqin ḥisābiyah*) and was also taken up in the section on **al-Inshiqāq 84/6**. I do not repeat it; in summary: the mufāʿala form conveys reciprocity, that is, a person comes **face to face** with his reckoning.

**حَتَّىٰ** is a particle of **limit**: "until". That is, the plunging and the playing **come to an end** at a point.

**And this sets the tone of the whole verse.** The command is *"leave"*, but the leaving is not without term; it has a limit.

So the verse says: this state is temporary; its end point is fixed.

### يَوْمَهُمُ ٱلَّذِى يُوعَدُونَ — "their day which they are promised"

**And the possessive construction here is noteworthy.**

The verse does not say *"that day"*. It says **"their day"**: *yawma-**hum***.

That is, the day is **attributed** to them. As though the day were their property.

**There are two readings, and both are possible:**

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| **The day appointed for them** | That day is theirs; it has been set aside for them |
| **The day they themselves prepared** | By what they did they have built their own day |

The second is a reading and goes further; **I do not present it as certain.** But the presence of the possessive construction is in the text.

**يُوعَدُونَ — Root: و-ع-د.** *Waʿd* — to promise. Passive: *"which they are promised"*.

And the verb is **imperfect**: the promise continues, it is renewed.

## 70/43-44 — يَوْمَ يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ ٱلْأَجْدَاثِ سِرَاعًا كَأَنَّهُمْ إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ يُوفِضُونَ · خَٰشِعَةً أَبْصَٰرُهُمْ تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّةٌ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْيَوْمُ ٱلَّذِى كَانُوا۟ يُوعَدُونَ

*Yawma yakhrujūna mina'l-ajdāthi sirāʿan ka-annahum ilā nuṣubin yūfiḍūn · Khāshiʿatan abṣāruhum tarhaquhum dhillah; dhālika'l-yawmu'lladhī kānū yūʿadūn*
"The day they come out of the graves in haste, as though they were racing towards a standing stone. Their eyes cast down, humiliation covering them. That is the day they were promised."

### ٱلْأَجْدَاث — the graves

**Root: ج-د-ث.** *Jadath* — a grave, a tomb. Its plural is **أَجْدَاث**.

Arabic has several words for a grave: *qabr*, *jadath*, *ramz*, *ḍarīḥ*. *Jadath* brings forward the **pit** aspect of a grave.

**The word occurs in three places in the Qurʾān**, and all three are in the same scene:

- *"With their eyes cast down, they will come out of the **graves** as though they were scattered locusts."* (al-Qamar 54/7)
- *"And suddenly they are hastening from their **graves** to their Lord."* (Yāsīn 36/51)
- **al-Maʿārij 70/43.**

### An exact closeness to al-Qamar 54/7

**And this verse is strikingly close to al-Qamar 54/7.**

| | **al-Qamar 54/7** | **al-Maʿārij 70/43-44** |
|---|---|---|
| Eyes | *خُشَّعًا أَبْصَٰرُهُمْ* | *خَٰشِعَةً أَبْصَٰرُهُمْ* |
| Coming out | *يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ ٱلْأَجْدَاثِ* | *يَخْرُجُونَ مِنَ ٱلْأَجْدَاثِ* |
| Simile | *كَأَنَّهُمْ جَرَادٌ مُّنتَشِرٌ* — **scattered locusts** | *كَأَنَّهُمْ إِلَىٰ نُصُبٍ يُوفِضُونَ* — **as though racing to a standing stone** |

**Both verses carry the same three elements:** cast-down eyes, coming out of the graves, and a simile. The only thing that changes is **the simile.**

**And now we can complete a comparison framed earlier in this commentary.**

In the section on al-Qāriʿa, al-Qamar 54/7 was compared with al-Qāriʿa 101/4, and the following was recorded:

> *"A **swarm of locusts** goes in a common direction. It has a direction, it has an order; what is fearsome about it is its numbers and its destructiveness. A **swarm of moths** has no direction. Each goes somewhere else. Al-Qāriʿa chose the second. The sūra's emphasis is not on the crowd but on **the loss of direction.**"*

**Al-Maʿārij adds a third simile** — and that simile says something different from both:

| Sūra | Simile | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Qāriʿa 101/4** | Scattered **moths** | **No direction** — each one elsewhere |
| **al-Qamar 54/7** | Spread-out **locusts** | **A common direction** — as a swarm |
| **al-Maʿārij 70/43** | Those racing to a **standing stone** | **A direction — but the wrong one** |

**Three similes, three different relations to direction: no direction, a direction, a wrong direction.**

And the third is the harshest, because in the first two there was a loss or a crowd; here there is **a target**, and what the target is is said in the next word.

I record this as my own reading. The wordings of the three verses are data that can be verified; the two-sided comparison in the section on al-Qāriʿa is taken from there and the third link is added here.

### نُصُب — a standing stone

**And the whole weight of the simile lies in this word.**

**Root: ن-ص-ب.** *Naṣaba* — to set up, to erect, to raise upright. **نُصُب** — a thing set up; and in Arabic especially **a standing stone, an idol, a stone on which sacrifice is offered.**

**The word's best-known use in the Qurʾān is this:**

*"O you who believe! Wine, gambling, **standing stones** and divining arrows are an abomination of Satan's work."* (al-Māʾida 5/90 — *wa'l-**anṣāb***)

And: *"[Forbidden to you is] what has been slaughtered on **standing stones**."* (al-Māʾida 5/3)

**So *nuṣub* is the standing stone of pre-Islamic Arabia around which people gathered, at which sacrifice was offered, around which circuits were made.**

**There are two readings, and both occur in the sources:**

| Reading | Sense | Image |
|---|---|---|
| **Idol / standing stone** | An object of worship gathered around | They run as they used to run to their idols |
| **A goal / banner / marker post** | The point to be reached in a race, a planted standard | They run as men run towards a goal |

**The first reading makes a stronger link with the content of the sūra**, and the ground is this:

Forty-five verses earlier the sūra had described the deniers as **gathered around** the Prophet: *"craning their necks towards you, from the right and from the left, in clumps."*

And now the same people come out of their graves as though racing to a **standing stone.**

**The two scenes share the same bodily movement:** gathering and running. In the world they ran to one place, on that day they run to another — and the movement is the same.

And one detail: the verb in the thirty-sixth verse was **مُهْطِعِين**, and that word is used in a scene of the resurrection in al-Qamar 54/8 as well: *"hastening to the caller"*. So the choice of word already linked the two scenes.

**I record this as my own reading, and I set down one qualification:** there is disagreement over the meaning of *nuṣub*, and the "goal/banner" reading is also valid. Nor do I claim that the verse intends the link between the two scenes. What I record is that the two verses describe the same bodily movement.

**A note on the variant readings.** It is transmitted that the word is also read as **نَصْب** (*naṣb*); on that reading the sense approaches the verbal noun "the setting up". **Since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which imām, I give no names.** It makes no great difference to the sense.

### يُوفِضُون — they race

**Root: و-ف-ض.** *Awfaḍa* — to hurry, to run fast. **The word occurs only here in the Qurʾān.**

The lexicographers describe *īfāḍ* as "a fast and flurried gait"; there is **haste** and a **disorder** in it.

And the verse had already used the word **سِرَاعًا** (in haste). So speed is announced twice: once with a circumstantial word, once with a verb.

**سِرَاع — Root: س-ر-ع.** The plural of *sarīʿ* (fast). It occurs in the Qurʾān in the construct *sarīʿu'l-ḥisāb* (swift in reckoning) as well.

### خَٰشِعَةً أَبْصَٰرُهُمْ — cast-down eyes

**Root: خ-ش-ع.** This root was treated in the section on **al-Baqara 2/45**, where the following was recorded:

> *"Root: **to soften, to be lowered, to bend.** … Khushūʿ is the breaking of hardness, the softening of the shell, becoming able to take in what comes."*

And **Fuṣṣilat 41/39** was given as evidence: *"You see the earth barren (**khāshiʿa**); when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells."*

I do not repeat it.

**What is to be added here is this: the same root occurs in two different places with two opposite values** — one in this sūra, the other in al-Muʾminūn, which is al-Maʿārij's parallel:

| Verse | Expression | Who | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Muʾminūn 23/2** | *فِى صَلَاتِهِمْ **خَٰشِعُونَ*** | The believers — in prayer | **Praise** |
| **al-Maʿārij 70/44** | ***خَٰشِعَةً** أَبْصَٰرُهُمْ* | The deniers — on that day | **Blame** |

**The same root, in two opposite places.**

And the difference is this: the *khushūʿ* in al-Muʾminūn is **chosen** — a person bows in prayer. The *khushūʿ* in al-Maʿārij is **compelled** — the eyes fall on that day.

So the picture the Qurʾān draws is this: **bowing is either chosen or undergone.** The measure recorded for the scales in the section on al-Qāriʿa and for seeing in the section on al-Takāthur works here for bowing.

I record this as my own reading. The wordings of the two verses and the shared root are data that can be verified.

**And the link with al-Ghāshiya 88/2:** *"Some faces on that day will be **cast down**"* (*wujūhun yawmaʾidhin **khāshiʿa***). This verse was treated in the section on al-Ghāshiya; there what was cast down was **faces**, here it is **eyes.**

**Grammar.** *Khāshiʿatan* is in the accusative and is a **circumstantial qualifier** (the state of *yakhrujūna* in verse 43). The reading **مَرْفُوع** is also transmitted. **I give no imām's name.**

### تَرْهَقُهُمْ ذِلَّة — humiliation covers them

**تَرْهَقُ — Root: ر-ه-ق.** *Rahiqa* — to overtake and cover, to envelop, to settle upon. *Irhāq* — to compel, to load with more than one can bear.

**And this verb occurs in a verse treated in this commentary:**

*"[On that day some faces] will be **covered by a darkness**."* (ʿAbasa 80/41 — *tarhaquhā qatara*)

This verse was treated in the section on ʿAbasa; I do not repeat it.

**Let us set the two verses side by side:**

| | **ʿAbasa 80/41** | **al-Maʿārij 70/44** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *تَرْهَقُهَا* | *تَرْهَقُهُمْ* |
| What covers | **قَتَرَة** — darkness, soot | **ذِلَّة** — humiliation |
| What is covered | **Faces** | **Persons** |

**The same verb, two different coverings.** In ʿAbasa a physical darkness, in al-Maʿārij a condition.

**And the choice of the verb is meaningful:** *rahiqa* conveys something that comes from outside and **covers over** a thing. That is, humiliation is not a feeling that arises from within a person; it is something **put on** him.

I record this as an observation about the word.

**ذِلَّة — Root: ذ-ل-ل.** *Dhull* — lowness, submission, loss of standing. Its opposite is *ʿizza*.

**And the root has an interesting side:** the same root gives both the negative *dhilla* and the positive *dhalūl* (docile, submissive — especially of a mount). *"He made the earth **submissive** to you"* (al-Mulk 67/15 — *dhalūlā*).

So the root is not negative in itself: it conveys **submission**. Its value depends on to whom and how one submits.

I record this as an observation about the word; the two uses of the root are plain in the Qurʾān.

### ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْيَوْمُ ٱلَّذِى كَانُوا۟ يُوعَدُونَ

**The last sentence of the sūra. And it is a repetition.**

The forty-second verse ended thus: *"…until they meet their day which they are promised"* (*yawmahumu'lladhī **yūʿadūn***).

The forty-fourth verse repeats the same expression — **with one difference:**

| | **70/42** | **70/44** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *يَوْمَهُمُ ٱلَّذِى **يُوعَدُونَ*** | *ٱلْيَوْمُ ٱلَّذِى **كَانُوا۟ يُوعَدُونَ*** |
| Verb | **Imperfect** — they are being promised | **كَانَ + imperfect** — they **were being** promised |
| Time | **Now** — the promise continues | **Past** — the promise is closed |
| Demonstrative | *yawma-hum* — their day | *dhālika'l-yawm* — **that is the day** |

**Between the two verses, across a span of two verses, the time changes.**

In the forty-second verse the promise **was continuing**; in the forty-fourth it **had continued**. That is, between two sentences the sūra moves the reader over to the side on which the promise has been fulfilled.

And the demonstrative changes as well: first *"their day"*, then *"that is the day."* The far demonstrative makes the event visible to the eye.

I record this as my own reading. The tenses of the two verses are data that can be verified.

### Why does the sūra end here?

The sūra had opened with a question: **"When?"**

And its last sentence is: **"That is the day."**

**The question and the answer stand at the two ends of the sūra.** And the answer is the whole of the forty-three verses in between: the source of the punishment, the difference of measure, the scene itself, the diagnosis, the list, and the final scene.

So the sūra answers the question not **by giving a date** but **by showing how the one who asked it will meet that day.**

I record this as my own reading. The opening and closing sentences of the sūra are in the text.

### The single argument of the sūra

If the whole of the sūra had to be reduced to a single sentence, it would be this — I record it as my own reading:

> **The man who asks "when?" is not really asking a question about time. He is asking a question about constitution: why can I not bear this waiting?**

The steps of the sūra show this:

**First step (1-7): the measure of the question is wrong.** The man calculates with his own measure of time. The verse announces that the measure is other, and says that the distinction "far/near" changes **with the one who looks.**

**Second step (8-18): the answer to the question is a scene.** And at the centre of the scene there is a human relationship: friends who cannot ask, a family offered as ransom. That is, the punishment is given less as a depiction of a penalty than as a depiction of **solitude.**

**Third step (19-21): the real matter is named.** The problem is not the delay of the punishment; it is **the constitution of man.** A being who is *halūʿ* cannot wait in any case — because a constitution that wails and withholds cannot bear a deferred requital.

**Fourth step (22-35): the way out.** And the way out is not an argument but an **order**: nine items, with prayer at both ends.

**Fifth step (36-44): the return to the question.** Those who asked are described — and the description is made with a bodily movement: craning the neck, running, standing scattered. Then the same movement is repeated in the scene of the resurrection.

**So what the sūra does is this: it moves from the one who asks to the question itself, from there to the constitution of the one who asks, from there to the treatment of that constitution, and at the end back to the one who asks.**

**The two sūras do two different things with the same vocabulary.**

Al-Ḥāqqa's subject is **occurrence**: a claim comes true. This is why al-Ḥāqqa looks to the past (five examples of destruction) and defends the source of the text.

Al-Maʿārij's subject is **waiting**: the occurrence has not yet taken place, and this has been made a matter of objection. This is why al-Maʿārij takes time, perception and the power to bear as its subject.

**And the two sūras are joined to one another by a question:** al-Ḥāqqa ends by saying that what will happen is certain (*ḥaqqu'l-yaqīn*). Al-Maʿārij begins with the question *"so when?"*

I record this as my own reading; the contents of the two sūras are in the text.

## What this says to the present

**One. The kind of the question.**

The sūra's most practical lesson lies in its opening: a question does not always ask what it says it asks.

The man who asked *"when?"* was not expecting a date; he was **refusing to wait.** And the sūra answers him not by giving a date but by showing why waiting comes hard.

This is a distinction that still works today: an objection often comes from somewhere other than the ground it puts forward. The sūra's method is to attend not to the ground but to the **source.**

**Two. *Halūʿ* today.**

The sūra's diagnosis is two sentences: **wailing when evil touches, withholding when good touches.**

And what the two reactions have in common is this: both are a sudden response to **contact**. The verb is *massa* — a touch. That is, the reaction is triggered not by the size of the event but by **its having touched.**

The life of today has multiplied this contact: every day more news, more comparison, more small losses and gains touching us. For a constitution that is *halūʿ*, this means a constant oscillation — a collapse or a closing at every touch.

What the sūra offers is not a piece of advice but an **order**: a list of nine items with prayer at its two ends. That is, **rhythm** is proposed as the antidote to reaction.

**Three. Wrong inference and wrong reaction.**

The matter treated in the section on al-Fajr is completed here: al-Fajr recorded the **misreading** of abundance and straitness; al-Maʿārij records the **wrong response** to both.

And the two feed one another. One who reads abundance as "I deserved it" does not share it; one who reads straitness as "I have been humiliated" cannot bear it.

The practical consequence is this: what a person does in abundance and in straitness depends on **how he names** those two states. Without a change in the naming, the behaviour does not change.

**Four. "He gathered and put it in a vessel."**

These two words of the sūra, having no object, open directly onto the present.

And the difference from al-Humaza is worth noting: the man in al-Humaza **counts** his wealth (he looks at it, he attends to it); the man in al-Maʿārij **closes** it away (he takes it out of sight). The second is quieter and harder to notice.

Its equivalent today is everything that is held and never enters circulation. The measure is not ownership; it is **whether the lid is opened.**

**Five. The ransom list.**

Verses eleven through fourteen set up a scene in which a man offers up his nearest for the sake of a deliverance. And this scene is heavier than the scene of flight in ʿAbasa: there the bonds were dissolving, here they are **used.**

What this says to the present is not confined to the scene of the resurrection. What a person is able to sacrifice shows what he really values — and this becomes visible in moments of pressure. The sūra shows that moment in its most extreme form.

**Six. Testimony.**

The least discussed item of the list is the thirty-third verse, and it is the list's only **public** item: to speak the truth in a matter in which one is not a party.

And the presence of this item on the list widens the sūra's measure: after a person's prayer, his wealth, his chastity and his trusts have been counted, **the settling of rights among other people** is counted as well.

Its equivalent today is the question of how an event is recounted: to say what you know without subtracting from it, without taking sides, even if it harms you or those close to you. This is the threshold al-Nisāʾ 4/135 sets.

**Seven. Watching and joining.**

Verses thirty-six through thirty-eight set up a picture together: people come, look, crane their necks, stand in scattered clumps — and *"each one of them hopes to be admitted to a garden."*

That is, **interest is taken for participation.**

This may be the point at which the sūra looks most directly at the present. To stand near something, to watch it, to talk about it, to gather around it — none of these is joining it. And the sūra has drawn the difference between the two with a list of thirty-five verses: the list counts what the one who joins does.

**Eight. "Leave them."**

The command of the forty-second verse is, for today, a sentence about limits: not taking the outcome on oneself.

As recorded in the sections on al-Ghāshiya and al-Fajr, the watch-post does not belong to the messenger. Al-Maʿārij says this with a command, and frames the command with a command to **patience**: at the two ends of the sūra stand *be patient* and *leave*.

Together the two describe a single stance: **hold yourself in, do not force the one before you.** And both issue from the same acceptance — the outcome is not in your hands.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

Per the method (`STYLE.md`), I list plainly the places where, throughout the text, certain language was deliberately not used.

- Three readings were given for **the coming of *saʾala* with *bāʾ* in the first verse** (question / demand / *sāla* without hamza); no preference was imposed. The reading without hamza is a variant reading, and **no imām's name was given.**
- **Who the asker was** is disputed. Al-Anfāl 8/32 was transmitted as an indication, but **it was not claimed that that verse is the occasion of revelation of this sūra.** The **personal names** occurring in the reports **were not given**; the ground is the same as in the sections on al-Māʿūn and al-Humaza.
- Two readings were given for **the meaning of ٱلْمَعَارِج** (ways of ascent / degrees); no preference was imposed.
- **Who ٱلرُّوح is** is disputed; the views were listed, **no preference was expressed.**
- **Five classical explanations of "fifty thousand years" were given in a table and no preference was imposed.** It was recorded that reports to the effect that the day will be shortened for the believer are found in the sources, but **their wording and source were not given**, because no ruling could be issued about their soundness.

- It was recorded that **the expression إِلَيْهِ does not convey place**; the *tafwīḍ*/*taʾwīl* distinction of the section on al-Fajr was preserved and not repeated.
- **The explanation of "beautiful patience" as "patience without complaint"** is widespread in the classical sources; **it was not ascribed to a particular person.** The boundary drawn with Yūsuf 12/86 is **my own reading.**
- Two readings were given for **whether بَعِيد means "far in time" or "out of the question"**; the judgement that the two work together was expressed, and it was recorded that **it is not binding.**
- The view of the majority of the lexicographers was transmitted regarding **what ٱلْمُهْل is** (molten metal / the dregs of oil); **no definite ruling was issued.** Nor was any ruling issued on whether the two senses of the root م-ه-ل come from a single root.

- For **the meaning of هَلُوع** the verse's own definition was taken as the basis; it was recorded that this approach is widespread in the sources, and **it was not ascribed to a particular person.**
- Two approaches were given regarding **whether خُلِقَ هَلُوعًا is an excuse**; the first was preferred on the ground of the existence of the exception, and it was recorded that **it is not binding.**
- Two readings were given for **the meaning of دَآئِمُون** (continuity / stillness); **no preference was imposed.**
- **What حَقٌّ مَّعْلُوم is** (alms / a share the person himself determines) is disputed; the second reading was found stronger on the ground of the sūra's logic, and it was recorded that **it is not binding and that the practical difference is limited.** The Meccan/Medinan caution of the section on al-Māʿūn was preserved.

- **The scope of the expression مَا مَلَكَتْ أَيْمَٰنُهُمْ and its present-day equivalent are a matter of jurisprudence; per the method (`STYLE.md`) no ruling was issued** and the discussion was referred to the works of those qualified in it. Only the linguistic data were recorded.
- Two views were given about **the origin of عِزِين**; the layer of meaning drawn from the first origin is **my own reading** and was not presented as a settled etymological ruling.
- Two readings were given for **the meaning of مِّمَّا يَعْلَمُونَ** (the substance of creation / the insufficiency of having been created); it was recorded that they do not exclude one another, and **no preference was imposed.**
- The classical explanation of **the plural form of ٱلْمَشَٰرِق وَٱلْمَغَٰرِب** was transmitted, and it was stated plainly that **this was not offered as a claim of scientific miracle**: what is described is not a modern discovery but a fact visible to the naked eye and known at that time.
- Two readings were given for **the meaning of مَسْبُوقِين**, and it was recorded that they come out in the same place.
- **The meaning of نُصُب** (idol / goal-banner) is disputed; the first reading was preferred on the ground of a link within the sūra, and **a qualification was set down.**
- The second reading of **the construction يَوْمَهُمُ** ("the day they themselves prepared") **was offered as a reading and not given as certain.**
- **The explanations of the roots**: ع-ر-ج (ascent / lameness), م-ه-ل (acting slowly / molten metal), ح-م-م (boiling water / friend), ش-ف-ق (twilight / dread), ج-ز-ع (to cut / wailing), ع-ه-د (word / checking on), ص-د-ق (truthfulness / alms), ر-ع-ي (shepherding / tending), خ-و-ض (walking in water / plunging into empty talk), ذ-ل-ل (humiliation / docility), ف-ص-ل (to separate / nearest kin) — all of these **were transmitted as relations drawn by the lexicographers**, and not presented as settled etymological rulings.
- **ص-ل-ي and ص-ل-و are distinct roots**; **no meaning was drawn** from the similarity of sound between *ṣallūh* in al-Ḥāqqa 69/31 and *muṣallīn* in this sūra. The qualification of the section on al-Aʿlā 87/12-15 was preserved.
