# 73. Sūrat al-Muzzammil

Twenty verses. It is counted Meccan; but there is disagreement about the last verse (20), and some scholars hold it to be Medinan. This disagreement will be taken up separately in the section on verse 20.

The name of the sūra comes from its first word: **al-Muzzammil** — "the one wrapped in his covering". The name is taken in the form in which it occurs in the text, and from the single place where it occurs.

This sūra requires being read together with **al-Muddaththir**, which follows it. Both open with the same pattern, both address the same person in the same state, and both begin with the same command. This twinning will be set out in detail in a separate section (at the end of the sūra and in `074-muddessir.md`); but what must be known from the outset is this: **there are two places in the Qurʾān where the Prophet is addressed not by his name and not by the name of his office, but by the bodily state he was in at that moment — and the two are the first verses of these two sūras.**

## The skeleton of the sūra

| Section | Verses | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **The call and the command of the night** | 1-4 | Rise from the covering; divide the night by a measure; recite the Qurʾān with *tartīl* |
| **The ground** | 5-7 | A heavy word is coming; the night is firmer, the day a long swim |
| **Commands of turning** | 8-9 | Mention the name, cut yourself off and turn, take Him as trustee |
| **The stance towards the addressees** | 10-11 | Be patient, part beautifully, leave the deniers to Me |
| **The recompense** | 12-14 | Fetters, flame, a morsel that sticks in the throat, the shaken earth |
| **The historical proof** | 15-16 | We sent you a messenger — as We sent one to Pharaoh |
| **Warning and close** | 17-19 | The day that turns children grey; this is a reminder |
| **The lightening** | 20 | The ruling is readjusted to the state of the human being |

## The structure of the sound

The verse-ending of this sūra is one of the most monotonous sound structures in the Qurʾān — and one whose monotony is meaningful.

From the first verse to the nineteenth, almost every verse ends on the same sound: the long **-īlā** (or a close on a sound very near to it).

*al-muzzammil, qalīlā, qalīlā, tartīlā, thaqīlā, qīlā, ṭawīlā, tabtīlā, wakīlā, jamīlā, qalīlā, jaḥīmā, alīmā, mahīlā, rasūlā, wabīlā, shībā, mafʿūlā, sabīlā.*

Nineteen verses, a single sound. The few verses that come in between (*jaḥīmā*, *alīmā*, *shībā*) stay within the same flow on the long **-ā**.

Then the twentieth verse comes and the sound breaks entirely: long, close to prose, and ending with *ghafūrun raḥīm*.

**This break coincides exactly with the place where the content breaks.** The single-breathed, measured, insistent rhythm built up across nineteen verses is let go in the verse where the ruling is lightened. The text speaks with measure while it is speaking of measure; when it says the measure is being loosened, it loosens its rhythm too.

I record this as an observation about the sound, not as a theological claim. But it is an observable order.

## The historical frame

The background of this sūra is bound up with two separate matters, and both were treated earlier:

- **The scene of the first revelation** — Ḥirāʾ, the command *iqraʾ*, the pressing three times, the Prophet's return trembling, Khadīja and Waraqa b. Nawfal — was transmitted in `096-alak.md`, and its state of soundness and its variants were discussed there. I do not repeat it here.
- **The *fatra*** (the pause in revelation) — was treated in `093-duha.md`. As recorded there: the existence of the pause is widely accepted; the figures that circulate about its length (three days, twelve days, forty days) contradict one another and none of them is certain.

**On the order of revelation.** In the widespread lists al-Muzzammil is placed among the early positions. As recorded in `096-alak.md`, the first five verses of al-ʿAlaq are counted absolutely first; al-Muddaththir is transmitted as the first sūra to come down after the *fatra*. The sources do not agree on the exact place of al-Muzzammil relative to these two. **The lists of the order of revelation are themselves a matter of scholarly judgement (*ijtihād*) and are not fixed by mass transmission** — this note was set down in `093-duha.md` and holds here as well. I therefore do not place al-Muzzammil at any particular position.

**The relation of the report "cover me" to the sūra.** In the scene transmitted in `096-alak.md` it is recorded that the Prophet returned home and wanted to be covered. Some exegetes attach the first verses of both al-Muzzammil and al-Muddaththir to that moment. Care is needed here, and I am obliged to draw a distinction:

**The existence of the report is one matter; the wording of the verse is another.** The verse does not narrate an event; it sets up an address. And the address itself — as will be seen shortly — works without needing that scene. I do not reject the report; nor do I say that the verse narrates it. What is certain is this: the sūra calls its addressee while he is covered.

The basmala is not taken up again here, since it was treated in earlier sūras; the verse numbers are counted without the basmala.

## 73/1 — يَا أَيُّهَا ٱلْمُزَّمِّلُ

*Yā ayyuha'l-muzzammil*
"O you wrapped in your covering!"

### The root زمل — and its second sense, which is easily missed

**Root: ز-م-ل.** The field of meaning the lexicons give runs along two branches, and the second branch is at least as important for this verse as the first.

**First branch — to wrap oneself, to cover oneself.**

- **تَزَمَّلَ (*tazammala*)** — he wrapped himself in a covering, he wound a cloth about himself.
- **زِمْل / زُمْلَة (*ziml* / *zumla*)** — a wrapped bundle, a pack, a load bound together.

At the centre of the root there is a movement: **to wrap something up and gather it together.** The person who wraps himself in a covering gathers himself up too; the cloth wraps the body, the body's outlines blur, the person becomes a bundle.

**Second branch — to share a load, to be a travelling companion.**

- **زَمِيل (*zamīl*)** — a travelling companion; in particular **one who shares the same mount**, one of the two who ride the same camel, one in front and one behind.
- **زَامِلَة (*zāmila*)** — a beast of burden; a camel set aside for carrying loads.
- **مُزَامَلَة (*muzāmala*)** — companionship on the road, the carrying of a load in common.

In Turkish, the borrowed **zâmil**, and the *zemîl* that is still alive in some dialects, come from this branch.

**What is the link between the two branches?** The explanation the lexicographers record is this: in both there is **a binding together, a winding around one another**. When a bundle is tied, the things inside it are wrapped against each other; when two people mount one camel, the two lean on each other and carry the load in common. The root describes two things becoming a single load.

I do not present this relation as a settled judgement in etymology; it is a link the lexicographers make. But it needs recording, because it works in the place where the verse stands.

### What the second sense does here

Let us now go back to the verse.

The addressee is called as one who **covers himself**. And two verses later **a load** will be given to him: *"We shall lay upon you a heavy word"* (73/5).

The same root carries both "the one who wraps himself" and "the sharer of a load". And the sūra is about exactly this: someone who has wrapped himself coming under a load.

**I record this as an inference, as my own reading.** The verse does not say that it is using this second sense, and most of the classical commentaries proceed on the first. But the second branch of the root is recorded in the lexicons, and its overlap with the content of the sūra is striking: the man wrapped in his covering is about to be made the sharer of a load.

And there is a difference: a bundle is wrapped around itself, a *zamīl* leans on another. The first is a closing-in, the second a partnership. The sūra takes its addressee out of the first and moves him into the second.

### The structure of the word: what is it originally?

**The word is originally *al-mutazammil*.**

The active participle of Form V: **مُتَزَمِّل** (*mutazammil*). Its meaning: one who wraps himself, who performs the covering himself.

The basic function of this form (Form V) is **the reflexive sense (*muṭāwaʿa*)** and **the person's performing the act upon himself**: *tanazzala* (he came down), *takabbara* (he made himself great), *tadhakkara* (he remembered). The covering, then, is not something done from outside; it is something the addressee did himself.

Then comes **assimilation (*idghām*)**: the sound **ت** (*tāʾ*) turns into the **ز** (*zāy*) that follows it, and the two *zāy*s merge and take a *shadda*.

**مُتَزَمِّل → مُزَّمِّل**

This is a widespread and regular sound change in Arabic: the *tāʾ* at the head of Form V, when the letter after it is close to it in sound, turns into that letter and merges with it. There are examples of similar mergers in the Qurʾān (*iddāraʾtum* — al-Baqara 2/72; *iththāqaltum* — al-Tawba 9/38). And the nearest example — **the first word of the next sūra**: *al-mutadaththir → al-muddaththir*.

**And here is the fine point.** The two sūras open with two words that have undergone the same sound change. In both the *tāʾ* drops and merges into the following letter; in both the word shortens and tightens.

I record this as an observation about the sound. But this much can be said: assimilation is two letters **entering one another and becoming one letter** — and both words carry the sense of "wrapping, going inside". This overlap between the structure of the word and its meaning is striking; I write it as an observation, not as a claim.

### The address itself: not by his name, but by his state

This is the single most important point in the sūra, and it is usually passed over quickly.

The Qurʾān uses particular patterns when it addresses the Prophet:

| Pattern | Where | What it addresses |
|---|---|---|
| **يَا أَيُّهَا ٱلنَّبِىُّ** | al-Anfāl 8/64-65, al-Aḥzāb 33/1, 33/28, 33/45, al-Taḥrīm 66/1, 66/9 etc. | The office — the attribute of prophet |
| **يَا أَيُّهَا ٱلرَّسُولُ** | al-Māʾida 5/41, 5/67 | The office — the attribute of messenger |
| **يَا أَيُّهَا ٱلْمُزَّمِّلُ** | al-Muzzammil 73/1 | **The state — the bodily condition of that moment** |
| **يَا أَيُّهَا ٱلْمُدَّثِّرُ** | al-Muddaththir 74/1 | **The state — the bodily condition of that moment** |

The first two patterns call a **status**: you are the messenger, you are the prophet. The last two call a **posture**: you are the one who is wrapped up right now.

And this changes the distance of the address entirely.

**To address a person by the name of his office raises him; to address him by his state comes close to him.** This is the difference between "O teacher" and "you there, standing at the door". The first is institutional, the second personal — and the second implies that the one calling **sees** the one addressed.

That is, the verse knows the addressee's physical condition at that moment and calls him out of that condition. This is the source of the closeness that both sūras set up.

**And one more observation.** These two addresses stand at the head of two sūras that are placed among the early positions in the lists of the order of revelation. That is, before the attributes of office are yet in place; before the addressee has begun to be called "prophet" or "messenger". I do not present this ordering as a settled chronology — it was recorded above that the lists are a matter of scholarly judgement. But this much can be said: **the address uses the state before the attribute.**

### The covering itself is not censured

A misreading must be closed off from the start.

The verse does not scold the addressee for having covered himself. There is no particle of blame in the sentence, no question, no *kallā*. There is only a call — and after the call a command will come.

In the classical commentaries this address is said to convey **kindness** and **gentleness**; it is read as a person being taken from the state he is in and called to a new work. This reading fits the text.

And one thing more: **covering oneself is the first thing a person does when he is cold or shaken.** The verse does not count that reflex a fault. It counts it a starting point.

### Word order: why definite?

**ٱلْمُزَّمِّلُ** comes with the article *al-* at its head, that is, definite.

In Arabic the pattern *yā ayyuha'l-…* requires a definite noun in any case; in that respect there is no surprise. But it produces another effect: **definiteness reduces the addressee to one.**

Not "O someone who is covered", but "O (that known) covered one". It is assumed that there is no other covered person present, or that the one meant is known.

This raises the closeness of the address one degree further: the one who calls knows whom he is speaking of and has no need to say it.

## 73/2-4 — قُمِ ٱلَّيْلَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا / نِّصْفَهُۥٓ أَوِ ٱنقُصْ مِنْهُ قَلِيلًا / أَوْ زِدْ عَلَيْهِ وَرَتِّلِ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ تَرْتِيلًا

*Qumi'l-layla illā qalīlā / Niṣfahū awi'nquṣ minhu qalīlā / Aw zid ʿalayhi wa rattili'l-Qurʾāna tartīlā*

"Stand the night — save a little of it. Half of it; or take a little from that. Or add to it. And recite the Qurʾān with *tartīl*."

The three verses are the working-out of a single command; they need to be taken together.

### قُمِ — rise

**Root: ق-و-م.** The concrete sense of this root and its family of derivatives were treated in `001-fatiha.md` (*mustaqīm*) and in `002-bakara.md` (*iqāmat al-ṣalāt*); I do not repeat it here. The core recorded there was this: the root conveys both **straightness** and **standing upright**, and *iqāma* is not "to perform and finish" but "to keep standing".

Here the verb is in its barest form: **قُمْ** — imperative, second person singular. "Get up on your feet."

And the concreteness of the word does particular work in this verse. The addressee is someone **lying down** — wrapped in his covering. The first command given to him is not an article of belief, not a prayer, not an act of remembrance; it is **a bodily movement**. To pass from the horizontal to the vertical.

It was recorded in `096-alak.md` that the first revelation contained no "command to worship". Here a bodily command comes for the first time — and the first command is still not the name of an act of worship: it does not say "pray", it says "rise".

**And the same verb will be repeated in the second verse of the next sūra:** *qum fa-andhir* — "Rise and warn." The two sūras begin with the same command; but one points to the night, the other to people. This is one of the firmest linguistic data for the link between the two sūras, and it will be taken up separately in the comparison section at the end of the sūra.

### ٱلَّيْلَ — the night as object

The point of word order is here.

It could have said *qum fi'l-layl* ("rise in the night"). It could have said *qum mina'l-layl* ("rise during part of the night") — and indeed al-Isrāʾ 17/79 says *wa mina'l-layli fa-tahajjad bih*.

Here, though, **ٱلَّيْلَ** stands directly **in the accusative**, that is, like the object of the verb.

The lexicographers explain this as an **adverbial** (*ẓarf* of time, "through the night"). This is what is correct grammatically, and it is not disputed.

But the **effect** this word order leaves is a separate matter: the night ceases to be a vessel inside which a work is done and stands instead like **a material worked upon**. English expressions such as "to see the night through" or "to get through the night" set up a similar picture — time is handled like an object.

I record this effect not as a grammatical judgement but as the impression the word order leaves on the reader.

### إِلَّا قَلِيلًا — the position of the exception

No sooner is the command given than an exception comes: **"save a little of it."**

This is a striking order. First it says "stand the night" — a command that looks absolute, without limit. Then immediately after it a limit is set.

**And the direction of the limit announces the whole sūra.** In the twentieth verse the ruling will be lightened once more. That is, the sūra makes its first lightening even while it is giving its first command. The command is never given without limit.

An observation — as my own reading: **the exception comes in the same breath as the command.** A heavy demand is not put first and then loosened by negotiation; the limit is set inside the demand itself.

### The working-out of the measure

The third and fourth verses open up the phrase "save a little of it" in the second verse:

| Option | Text | How much |
|---|---|---|
| The basic measure | نِصْفَهُۥ | Half the night |
| Deviation downward | أَوِ ٱنقُصْ مِنْهُ قَلِيلًا | A little less than half |
| Deviation upward | أَوْ زِدْ عَلَيْهِ | More than half |

**And the word order here is worth pausing over.**

The verse does not give a figure. It gives a range — and **both ends of the range are left open**. "Half" is set as a centre, then deviation in both directions is left free.

More important still: **the restriction placed on decreasing is not placed on increasing.**

- *inquṣ minhu **qalīlā*** — "take **a little** from it". The extent of the decrease is limited.
- *zid ʿalayhi* — "add to it". The extent is not stated.

So there is a floor in the downward direction and no ceiling in the upward.

I record this as my own reading: **the measure is a range whose lower bound is protected but whose upper bound is left open.** This says something about the structure of the demand — a minimum ground is asked for, a maximum ceiling is not set.

### Why the night?

The verse will give the ground in the sixth verse (*inna nāshiʾata'l-layli hiya ashaddu waṭʾan*), and it will be treated in that section. But a preliminary note is needed here.

The command of the night is settled in the early texts of the Qurʾān, and it does not stand alone:

- *"And in part of the night prostrate to Him; and glorify Him through the long night"* (al-Insān 76/26).
- *"And in part of the night, wake and pray with it — as something additional for you"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/79).
- *"Their sides forsake their beds; they call upon their Lord in fear and in hope"* (al-Sajda 32/16).

The common point: none of these texts presents the night as an ordeal. The night is presented as the time in which the work **can be done**.

### The root رتل — the setting of the teeth

The most beautiful word in the sūra, and a place that needs pausing over separately.

**Root: ر-ت-ل.** The concrete sense the lexicons give is unexpectedly specific:

**رَتَل (*ratal*)** — the setting of teeth that is **even, evenly spaced, not crowded against one another**. An Arab describing fine teeth says *thaghrun ratilun* or *ratlu'l-asnān*: teeth in an even row, the gaps equal, none riding over another.

That is, at the centre of the root there is not **a tooth** but **a row of teeth**. Not the beauty of the individual teeth but **the order between them.**

From here the word widens:

- **رَتَّلَ (*rattala*)** — to set something out at even intervals, to arrange it in sequence.
- **تَرْتِيل (*tartīl*)** — the verbal noun of that act: spaced, measured, ordered arrangement.
- In modern Arabic **رَتْل (*ratl*)** — a convoy, a column advancing in file. The same idea: one after another, at regular intervals.

### What does *tartīl* come to mean?

Now the concrete sense of the root can be carried into the verse.

For the recitation to be "with *tartīl*" means **for the recitation to be like a row of teeth**. And this comparison says three things at once:

**1. The words will not be crowded against one another.** There are gaps between teeth; without them there would be no teeth but a single bone. In the recitation too there will be space between the words.

**2. The gaps will be equal and regular.** Not random space but measured space. To hurry in one place and drag in another is not an even setting.

**3. Each unit will be seen separately.** In an evenly set row of teeth each tooth is picked out one by one. In a text recited with *tartīl* too each word is heard one by one.

**And a fourth thing, which the comparison does not say but implies:** teeth are set in a row for a **function** — for chewing. A disordered setting spoils the function. I write this last item as my own inference; the lexicons do not explain the root by way of function.

### The cognate accusative: تَرْتِيلًا

It could have said *wa rattili'l-Qurʾān* and left it there. It did not; the verbal noun is repeated: **تَرْتِيلًا**.

This is what is called in Arabic the **cognate accusative** (*mafʿūl muṭlaq*), and its function is **emphasis**: to bring the verbal noun of a verb after the verb is to say "truly, fully, as it should be".

The sūra will use this technique once more: *wa tabattal ilayhi **tabtīlā*** (73/8), *wa mahhadtu lahū **tamhīdā*** (at 74/14 — in the other sūra), *fa-akhadhnāhu **akhdhan wabīlā*** (73/16).

But there is an added effect here, and it is easily missed: **the cognate accusative adds another syllable to the sentence and lengthens it.** That is, the very sentence that gives the command "recite with *tartīl*" slows down by taking on one more word.

I record this as an observation, not as a claim.

### The Qurʾān's own account of it: al-Furqān 25/32

The Qurʾān itself uses this word once more, and the context is explanatory:

*"Those who disbelieve said: should the Qurʾān not have been sent down upon him all at once, in a single stretch? Thus — that We might make your heart firm with it (We sent it down so), and We set it apart **with tartīl***" (al-Furqān 25/32).

Here *tartīl* is ascribed not to **the recitation** but to **the sending down**: the text was sent down piece by piece, at intervals.

**And when the two usages are put together a whole emerges:** the text came down at intervals, and it is to be recited at intervals. The manner of its descent and the manner of its recitation are named with the same word.

I record this as an inference. That the two verses use the same root is beyond dispute; the link made between them is interpretation.

### Are *tartīl* and *tajwīd* the same thing?

A distinction has to be drawn, because the two words are often confused.

**Tajwīd** is a **body of rules** systematised in later periods: the points of articulation of the letters, the measures of prolongation, the rules of joining and merging. It is a branch of learning and a technical discipline.

**Tartīl**, by contrast, is the word that occurs in the verse and conveys a **quality**: spaced, measured, unhurried recitation.

The two do not contradict each other; *tajwīd* is a method developed afterwards for how *tartīl* is to be realised. But **the verse does not command *tajwīd***; it commands *tartīl*. To draw this distinction is not to diminish *tajwīd*; it is to say correctly what the verse says.

And one more note: in the classical commentaries it is often said that *tartīl* has to do not only with the ordering of sound but also with **following the meaning** — the purpose of slow reading being that one can dwell on what is read. This explanation does not run against the text; but the wording of the verse does not say it directly, and the concrete sense of the root is setting and interval.

### The order of the command: first the time, then the manner

The arrangement of the three verses carries a logic:

1. **Rise** — bodily condition.
2. **Divide the night** — measure of time.
3. **Recite with *tartīl*** — the quality of the work.

That is, first the person is put on his feet, then time is set aside for him, then he is told how the work to be done in that time is to be done.

**And note: "how much you are to recite" is not said.** What is measured is the time; what is qualified is the recitation. Quantity never comes up at all.

This reading of the sequence is my inference; the text does not offer such a justification.

## 73/5 — إِنَّا سَنُلْقِى عَلَيْكَ قَوْلًا ثَقِيلًا

*Innā sanulqī ʿalayka qawlan thaqīlā*
"Surely We shall lay upon you a heavy word."

The central sentence of the sūra. The ground of the commands in the second and fourth verses is given here: rising in the night is not an end but **a preparation**.

### إِنَّا — emphasis and majesty

The sentence opens with **inna** and the pronoun is **nā** (we). *The nūn of majesty.*

This is the first place in the sūra where the speaker names himself. The first four verses were in the imperative; who was giving the command had not been said. In the fifth verse the subject appears.

**And the order is meaningful:** first the command, then the one who gives it introducing himself. The addressee already knows who is speaking; the sentence names the subject not to give information but to give an **undertaking**.

### سَنُلْقِى — the future tense and "to lay down"

**سَ (*sīn*)** — the particle conveying the near future. "Soon, shortly."

That is, the heavy word **has not yet come.** The command first, the load after. The preparation is asked for before the load.

This opens the door to one of the most practical observations in the sūra, and it will be treated in the section "What this says to the present": **capacity is built before the need arises.**

**The verb: أَلْقَى (*alqā*), from the root ل-ق-ي, in Form IV.**

The concrete sense of the root is **to meet, to come upon** (*liqāʾ* — meeting, encounter; *mulāqāt*). In Form IV it becomes transitive: **to put something down somewhere, to throw, to place, to let fall.**

Its uses in the Qurʾān show the weight of the root:

- *"Throw down your staff"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/117; al-Naml 27/10; al-Qaṣaṣ 28/31) — *alqi ʿaṣāk*. A releasing from the hand.
- *"The magicians were cast down in prostration"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/120) — *ulqiya'l-saḥaratu sājidīn*. Passive: they did not fall of themselves, they were made to fall.
- *"Cast him into the bottom of the well"* (Yūsuf 12/10) — *alqūhu fī ghayābati'l-jubb*.
- Uses like *"then We **laid** a word upon Moses"* occur for revelation as well: *"Surely the Qurʾān is being **conveyed** to you from One wise and knowing"* (al-Naml 27/6) — there it is *tulaqqā*, the Form V pattern of the same root.

**The fine point in the choice of the word:** it could have said "We shall send down" (*nunazzilu*) — the Qurʾān uses this verb a great deal. It could have said "We shall reveal" (*nūḥī*). It could have said "We shall make you recite" (*sanuqriʾuka*, as at al-Aʿlā 87/6).

**It did not; it said "We shall lay down / We shall place".**

And the difference is this: *nuzūl* conveys movement downward from a height; *ilqāʾ* conveys **a load being set down in a place**. In the first the emphasis is on the height of the source, in the second on **the place where the load is set**.

This is completed when it joins with the next particle.

### عَلَيْكَ — "upon"

It could have said *ilayka* ("to you"). The Qurʾān uses this particle for revelation too: *"what We have sent down to you"* — *mā anzalnā **ilayka***.

Here there is **عَلَيْكَ**: "**upon** you."

The particle **عَلَى** conveys being over and **weight**. It is the idea carried by English expressions like "it weighs on him", "it is on his back".

So the three elements of the sentence all work in the same direction:

| Element | What it conveys |
|---|---|
| **سَنُلْقِى** | Something being **set down** in a place |
| **عَلَيْكَ** | The place it is set: **upon the addressee** |
| **ثَقِيلًا** | The quality of what is set: **heavy** |

The three together build a single picture: **a load being set on a back.**

And the sense of *zamīl* (the travelling companion who shares a load), treated in the first verse of this sūra, comes to mind here once again. I state again that I record this link as my own reading.

### ثَقِيل — heavy

**Root: ث-ق-ل.** The concrete sense of this root, its derivatives (*thiql*, *athqāl*, *mithqāl*, *thaqalān*, *muthqal*) and its distribution in the Qurʾān were treated in `099-zilzal.md`; its use as the result of a weighing (*thaqulat mawāzīnuh*) was taken up in `101-karia.md`, and there a table set out how the two sūras divide the root between them. I do not repeat it here.

The frame recorded there was this: the root conveys both **physical weight** and **moral burden**; *mithqāl* is an instrument of measure; and in the matter of the scales, weight is measured as a **result**.

**The difference here is in what the weight is ascribed to.**

In al-Zilzāl and al-Qāriʿa the weight belonged to **deeds** — what was weighed was what the human being had done. Here the weight belongs to **the revelation itself**. Not something weighed but something **carried**.

So the same root stands in three separate positions:

| Place | What is heavy | Function |
|---|---|---|
| al-Zilzāl 99/2, 7-8 | The burdens of the earth / the weight of an atom | The unit of measure |
| al-Qāriʿa 101/6 | The pan of the scales | The result of the measure |
| **al-Muzzammil 73/5** | **The word itself** | **The load carried** |

This third position is one of the most striking uses of the root in the Qurʾān: **a word is described as an object that has weight.**

### In what respect is it "heavy"? — the disagreement

Several explanations are given in the classical commentaries. None of them is stated openly in the text.

| View | What it means | Ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The bodily weight of the descent** | That the Prophet was physically strained at the moment revelation came | In the reports about the moment of revelation, states such as sweating, growing heavy, and the mount sinking down are transmitted | The verse describes not an event but the quality of the word; and the reports come not as exegesis of this verse but as separate reports |
| **The weight of the responsibility** | The burden of conveying, of being opposed, of enduring | The continuation of the sūra (verses 10-11) describes exactly this burden | The verse says "word"; whether it is the word itself or the task it brings remains unclear |
| **The weight of the content** | The difficulty of carrying the commands, the prohibitions, the rulings | al-Ḥashr 59/21: *"Had We sent this Qurʾān down upon a mountain, you would have seen it humbled and split apart out of fear of God"* | That verse conveys the weight by way of a mountain, not as a burden a human being carries |
| **The weight of the value** | "Heavy" here means precious, serious, not to be taken lightly | In Arabic *thaqīl* is also used in the sense of "serious, important" | The particles *ilqāʾ* and *ʿalā* in the verse strengthen the picture of a physical load; had it meant only "precious", this word order would not be expected |

**These do not exclude one another** and in the classical sources they are often given together. I do not impose a preference.

**What can be drawn from the text itself.** Two things can be said:

1. The weight is described as **the effect of the word upon the addressee** — not as the content of the word. The particle *ʿalayka* shows this.
2. The place where the verse stands implies that the weight is **carriable**. Because immediately before it there is a command to prepare. Preparation is not asked for a load that cannot be carried.

The second observation is my inference.

### Context: the link between the preparation and the load

This verse explains the three verses before it retrospectively.

There is no *fāʾ* or other particle of cause; the link is made by the two sentences standing side by side. But the link is strong and is made almost unanimously in the classical commentaries: **rising in the night is in order to be able to carry the heavy word.**

And the logic here is one of the most concrete places in the sūra:

- If a load is to be carried, the one who is to carry it must first **grow strong**.
- The growing strong is done not after the load comes but **before**.
- And **one single thing** is shown as the way to grow strong: rising in the night and reciting the text with measure.

This third item is striking. The verse does not propose an austerity, an ordeal, a discipline of solitude as the way to grow strong. It proposes **a regular and slow contact with the text**.

I record this as an inference; the text does not make this justification itself, it is drawn from the two commands standing side by side.

## 73/6 — إِنَّ نَاشِئَةَ ٱلَّيْلِ هِىَ أَشَدُّ وَطْـًٔا وَأَقْوَمُ قِيلًا

*Inna nāshiʾata'l-layli hiya ashaddu waṭʾan wa aqwamu qīlā*
"Surely the rising of the night is stronger in tread and more upright in speech."

The densest verse in the sūra and the hardest to translate. There are three key words and all three must be taken separately.

### نَاشِئَة — the rising of the night

**Root: ن-ش-أ.** Its concrete sense: **to be born, to grow up, to come forth, to rise.**

Derivatives:

- **نَشَأَ (*nashaʾa*)** — he grew, he was raised, he came forth.
- **نَشْأَة (*nashʾa*)** — coming into being, creation. In the Qurʾān: *"You have known the first bringing into being; should you not then reflect?"* (al-Wāqiʿa 56/62) — *al-nashʾata'l-ūlā*. And *"then God will bring about the last bringing into being"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/20) — *al-nashʾata'l-ākhira*.
- **أَنشَأَ (*anshaʾa*)** — he brought into being, he built. *"It is He who brought gardens into being"* (al-Anʿām 6/141).
- **نَاشِئ (*nāshiʾ*)** — one who is growing up; a youth.

In Turkish the borrowed **"neşet etmek"** ("to arise out of somewhere") and **"inşa"** ("construction") come from this same root.

The word **نَاشِئَة**, in the form in which it occurs in this verse, is unique in the Qurʾān. And what exactly it means has been disputed.

### What does نَاشِئَة mean? — the disagreement

| View | What it means | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The rising of the night itself** | The act of rising from sleep; the one who rises | The root conveys "to come forth, to straighten up"; an active participle in the feminine can also be ascribed to the act |
| **The hours of the night** | The advancing, "rising" hours of the night | The root's sense of "coming forth afterwards"; the parts of the night come in sequence |
| **The night prayer itself** | The prayer performed at night | The context: rising in the night was commanded in the preceding verses |
| **What is recited at the rising of the night** | The word uttered at that hour | The link with *qīlā* (speech) later in the verse |

**These are not far from one another** and in practice they come to the same place: rising from sleep during part of the night and making use of that time.

I do not impose a preference. But this needs recording: **the root of the word is "to be born", and this is a strangely apt word for a person rising from sleep.** The straightening up of a lying body is like something coming up out of the ground. This observation is my own reading; the lexicons do not explain the root by way of sleep.

### وَطْء — to tread

**Root: و-ط-أ.** Its concrete sense: **to tread with the foot, to set the foot upon, to trample.**

Derivatives:

- **مَوْطِئ (*mawṭiʾ*)** — a place trodden by the foot. In the Qurʾān: *"…nor do they tread a place that enrages the disbelievers…"* (al-Tawba 9/120) — *mawṭiʾan yaghīẓu'l-kuffār*.
- **وِطَاء / مُوَاطَأَة (*wiṭāʾ* / *muwāṭaʾa*)** — coinciding with one another, agreeing, matching. In the Qurʾān: *"…so as to make the number match…"* (al-Tawba 9/37) — *li-yuwāṭiʾū ʿiddata mā ḥarrama'llāh*.
- **وَطِيء (*waṭīʾ*)** — level ground, ground fit to tread on.

**The centre of the word is in the contact of the foot with the ground.** And the quality of that contact matters — is it firm, is it slippery, is it balanced?

### What does "stronger in tread" mean?

*Ashaddu waṭʾan* — "more severe / stronger with respect to tread."

It is hard to render into English, because English has no such idiom. But the picture is clear: **the foot setting down firmly on the ground.**

Several explanations are given in the classical commentaries, and all of them come out of this picture:

**1. The firmness of the agreement.** Heart and tongue, what is heard and what is said, sit exactly upon one another. This explanation rests on the root's sense of *muwāṭaʾa* (coinciding, matching) — and, as will be seen below, it is supported by a variant reading as well.

**2. The weight of the difficulty.** Rising at night comes hard to the self; "tread" here conveys the hardship. This explanation too comes out of the root: to tread on something is to bring weight down on it.

**3. The force of the effect.** The mark the work leaves on a person is deeper — like a foot that leaves a print where it treads.

All three occur in the classical sources and they do not exclude one another. I state no preference.

**But I want to record one observation, as my own reading.** The measure the word chooses is **contact** — the foot's relation to the ground. That a work is "firm" is measured here not by how much of it is done but by **where it treads**. This fits the sūra's general stance on measure: not quantity but seating.

### The variant reading: وَطْئًا / وِطَاءً

A variant reading is transmitted for this word, and it **genuinely changes the meaning**:

| Reading | Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **وَطْئًا** (*waṭʾan*) | *faʿl* — verbal noun | To tread, treading |
| **وِطَاءً** (*wiṭāʾan*) | *fiʿāl* — verbal noun of Form III | Coinciding, matching, agreeing |

With the second reading the meaning becomes: *"…stronger with respect to agreement"* — that is, heart and tongue, intention and speech, sitting more exactly upon one another.

**The two readings do not contradict each other; they complete each other.** The first conveys treading on the ground, the second inward agreement — and both come out of the idea of "sitting exactly" that the same root carries.

**I do not give a definite list of which reading belongs to which imām.** In the widely printed muṣḥaf (the transmission of Ḥafṣ) **وَطْئًا** is read.

### أَقْوَمُ — more upright, more correct

**Root: ق-و-م.** As recorded above (73/2), this root was treated in `001-fatiha.md` and `002-bakara.md`: it conveys both **straightness** and **standing upright**.

**أَقْوَم** is the elative pattern: "more standing", "more upright", "more correct", "more to the point".

This word occurs in several places in the Qurʾān and in all of them it means "most sound, most firm":

- *"Surely this Qurʾān guides to that which is most upright"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/9) — *yahdī lillatī hiya aqwam*.
- *"That is more equitable in the sight of God, and firmer for testimony…"* (al-Baqara 2/282) — *aqwamu li'l-shahāda*.

### The two measures of the verse

The verse declares the rising of the night superior in two respects, and the two measures complete each other:

| Measure | Word | What it looks at | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tread** | أَشَدُّ وَطْئًا | The act's seating on the ground | **Downward** — the foot, the ground |
| **Speech** | أَقْوَمُ قِيلًا | The uprightness, the correctness of the word | **Upward** — the tongue, straightening up |

And both words can be read at once bodily and inwardly: *waṭʾ* is both a real tread of the foot and an agreement; *aqwam* is both a real uprightness and a correctness.

**This shows the sūra's choice of language.** It describes inward states with bodily words: to tread, to stand upright, to rise, to wrap oneself, the back, the throat. As recorded in `094-insirah.md`, this is a widespread method of the Qurʾān — and, as was also noted there, a general property of language.

### قِيلًا — speech

**Root: ق-و-ل.** *Qīl*, like *qawl*, is a verbal noun, and it means "the word that is spoken".

There is a point of word order here: **in the fifth verse *qawlan thaqīlā* (a heavy word), in the sixth verse *aqwamu qīlā* (more upright in speech).** The same root, in two consecutive verses.

That is: a word that is heavy is coming; and the time is shown at which that word will be spoken in the most upright way. **The word that comes and the word that is spoken are named with the same root.**

I record this as an observation; I do not claim it is a deliberate move of the text.

### Why the night? — an observation that is not forced

The verse does not explain why the night is more suitable; it only states that it is. But there are a few observable things, and I write them not as a claim of "scientific miracle" but as the record of an experience everyone knows:

- The night is the time when external stimulus is least. No sound, no movement, no visual distraction.
- The night is the time when no one is watching. The work done cannot be displayed.
- The night is time a person gives out of his sleep. That is, it is time with a price.

Can all three of these be drawn from what the verse says? No. **These are my own observations**, not the ground the verse gives. The verse gives no ground; it gives a measure.

And I know that in modern sleep and attention research there is a literature on the cognitive effects of night wakefulness; but the findings of that literature are disputed and vary from person to person. **I do not attach the verse to it.** The rule in `STYLE.md` that "no hunting for scientific miracles is done" exists precisely to preserve this limit.

## 73/7 — إِنَّ لَكَ فِى ٱلنَّهَارِ سَبْحًا طَوِيلًا

*Inna laka fi'l-nahāri sabḥan ṭawīlā*
"Surely in the day you have a long swim."

The most pictorial sentence in the sūra, and the complement of the verse before it.

### The root سبح

**Root: س-ب-ح.** The concrete sense of the root — **to swim in water or in air, to flow onward, to move swiftly through a medium** — and its link with *tasbīḥ* were treated in `110-nasr.md`. The note recorded there holds here too: the link between swimming and *tanzīh* is a relation the lexicographers make, not a settled judgement in etymology.

And the evidence from within the Qurʾān given there showed that the concrete sense of the root is preserved: *"Each swims in an orbit"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/33; Yāsīn 36/40).

**Here the word is entirely in its concrete sense** and has nothing to do with *tasbīḥ*: **سَبْح** — swimming, flowing onward.

This is one of the most concrete uses of the root in the Qurʾān, and it is striking for exactly that reason.

### "A long swim" — the force of the comparison

In describing the day, the verse sets up a picture of **water**.

And to see what the comparison says, one has to look at what it does not say. The verse does not say:

- "In the day you have much work." — that would be a statement of quantity.
- "In the day you are busy." — that would be a statement of state.
- "In the day you have no time." — that would be a statement of absence.

**It says: in the day you have a long swim.**

Swimming says three things at once:

**1. It requires continuous movement.** Whoever stands still in water sinks. When the swimmer stops he not only ceases to advance, he loses the possibility of staying up. The work of the day is like this too: when it is let go, it does not stay where it was.

**2. The medium carries and drags the person.** Water is not under the swimmer's control. There is current, there is wave, there is direction. While the person tries to go where he wants, the medium takes him somewhere else.

**3. There is no ground.** The swimmer treads on nothing. And this stands in direct contrast with the word of the preceding verse.

### The contrast between verses 6 and 7

This is the finest point of word order in the sūra, and without it both verses remain incomplete.

| | **73/6 — night** | **73/7 — day** |
|---|---|---|
| Key word | **وَطْء** — to tread | **سَبْح** — to swim |
| Ground | Present: the foot treads the earth | Absent: water gives no support |
| Movement | Can stop | Cannot stop |
| Direction | The person determines it | The medium drags |
| Quality | **أَشَدُّ** — stronger | **طَوِيلًا** — long |
| Kind of measure | **Firmness** | **Duration** |

**Two words describe two separate relations of the human being to ground:** one the ground that is trodden, the other the medium that cannot be trodden.

And the last row is especially important: the night is qualified by **quality**, the day by **duration**. The measure of the night is how firm it is; the measure of the day is how long it lasts.

I record this reading as my own inference. The concrete senses of the two roots are beyond dispute; the contrast drawn between them is interpretation.

### Is the verse an excuse or an observation?

There are two readings in the classical commentaries:

**1. The reading as ground.** "You are busy in the day, so rise at night." That is, the verse supports the command of the night: you have no time in the day, you have it at night.

**2. The reading as easing.** "You are already occupied at length in the day; the whole of the night is not being asked for." That is, the verse supports the exception *illā qalīlā* in the second verse.

**Both fit the text and they do not exclude each other.** But the second looks more consistent with the general flow of the sūra — a flow that constantly sets limits and constantly lightens. This is a preference, and it is not binding.

**And a third reading is possible too, which I record as my own reading:** the verse may also be a warning. To describe the day as a "swim" is not neutral — the swimmer is dragged. That is, the sentence may also be saying that the day catches a person up in its current. On this reading the command of the night is not an excuse but an **antidote**: the one being dragged is given ground to set his foot on.

The text says none of this openly.

### Why "long"?

**طَوِيل** — long. Its root is ط-و-ل.

The day is not in fact longer than the night; for most of the year the two are close to each other. So "long" here does not convey a measure of hours.

**It conveys the length of the occupation.** How long a day lasts changes according to what is done inside it — this is an experience everyone knows.

It sets up an interesting contrast with the principle recorded in `097-kadr.md`: there it was said that "the value of a stretch of time is measured not by its length but by its content". Here the same principle works in the opposite direction: the **length** of a stretch of time is also measured by its content. I make this link as my own reading.

## 73/8 — وَٱذْكُرِ ٱسْمَ رَبِّكَ وَتَبَتَّلْ إِلَيْهِ تَبْتِيلًا

*Wa'dhkuri'sma rabbika wa tabattal ilayhi tabtīlā*
"Mention the name of your Lord; and cut yourself off and turn to Him, with a full cutting off."

### وَٱذْكُرِ ٱسْمَ رَبِّكَ — to mention the name

**The root ذ-ك-ر** was treated in `094-insirah.md`: it carries two fields of meaning — **to remember** and **to mention / to utter** — and the two are bound to each other.

What is striking here is the object of the mentioning: **اسْمَ رَبِّكَ** — "the **name** of your Lord".

It could have said *udhkur rabbaka* ("mention your Lord"). It did not; **name** has come in between.

This word order is settled in the Qurʾān and is repeated in several places:

- *"And mention the name of your Lord"* (al-Insān 76/25).
- *"Glorify the name of your Lord, the Most High"* (al-Aʿlā 87/1; al-Wāqiʿa 56/74, 56/96, al-Ḥāqqa 69/52).
- *"Recite in the name of your Lord"* (al-ʿAlaq 96/1) — treated in `096-alak.md`.
- *"Mention the name of your Lord and cut yourself off from everything and turn to Him"* — this verse.

**In the classical commentaries two explanations are given of the function of the word "name" here:**

1. **It is counted redundant** and the meaning becomes "mention your Lord". This usage is known in Arabic.
2. **It is functional**: what is mentioned is not the Essence itself but the side of it the servant can reach. A human being can mention God only by His names.

The second explanation fits the stance treated in `112-ihlas.md`: drawing a limit instead of describing. But I do not impose a preference; the first explanation is legitimate on linguistic grounds too.

### The root بتل — to cut

**Root: ب-ت-ل.** Its concrete sense: **to cut, to separate, to break something off from what it is attached to.**

The primary usage the lexicons give is agricultural: **بَتَلَ النَّخْلَة** — to separate a palm shoot from the parent tree. The shoots that come up at the foot of a date palm are cut away from the root stock and planted separately. *Batl* is exactly this operation.

Derivatives:

- **بَتُول (*Batūl*)** — one who has been cut off, separated. The woman's name **Betül**, alive in Turkish, comes from here. In the tradition this attribute has been used of Mary (Maryam) and of Fāṭima; I do not go into the details of these ascriptions or into which report they rest on.
- **صَدَقَةٌ بَتْلَة** — a gift that is final, with no going back on it.

**At the centre of the root there is a breaking off** — and this breaking off does not mean that what is broken off disappears, but that it **gains a separate existence**. The palm shoot does not die when it is cut from the parent trunk; it becomes a tree of its own.

I want to record this detail, because it explains the use of the word here.

### تَبَتَّلْ — cut yourself off and turn

The verb is in **Form V (تَفَعَّلَ)**: **تَبَتَّلَ** — to cut oneself off, to break away.

This is the form of *mutazammil* in the first verse: the act performed by the person upon himself. That is, the one who does the cutting and the one who is cut are the same person.

**And the word order says something here:**

The verb *tabattal* by itself means "cut, break away" — it conveys separation from somewhere. But after it comes **إِلَيْهِ** (towards Him).

So the sentence becomes: **"Be cut off towards Him."**

In Arabic this is a usage the lexicographers have noted: the verb of cutting normally requires the particle *ʿan* (to be cut off *from* something). Here there is *ilā* (towards something).

**This is the finest point in the verse.** The breaking off is set up not as a moving away but as a **turning towards**. The bond that is cut turns into another bond.

It was recorded in `094-insirah.md` that the root *رغب* changes direction according to its particle (*raghiba fī* = to want, *raghiba ʿan* = to turn away). Something similar happens here but by a different route: the verb's own meaning conveys separation, the particle conveys turning towards, and the two are joined.

### تَبْتِيلًا — the difference of pattern

There is a grammatical point here, and it is treated particularly in the classical sources.

The verb is **تَبَتَّلَ** (Form V). The verbal noun of this form should have been **تَبَتُّل** (*tabattul*).

But in the verse **تَبْتِيلًا** (*tabtīl*) comes — and this is the verbal noun of **تَفْعِيل** (Form II), that is, the verbal noun of the verb **بَتَّلَ**.

So the verb from one form, the verbal noun from another.

**The explanation for this is given in the classical sources as follows:** Form II conveys **transitivity** and **intensity**. Form V, by contrast, is reflexive. When the two are brought together this is what results: the verb remains a work the person does upon himself (Form V), but the verbal noun adds an **intensity and deliberateness** to that work (Form II).

And its coming as a cognate accusative already conveys emphasis — like *tartīlā* in the fourth verse.

**The upshot:** "cut yourself off — and make this cutting complete." A cutting left half done is not a cutting; it is a bond not cut but merely thinned.

I record this as my own reading; the classical sources record the difference of pattern, but the conclusion I draw from it is interpretation.

### Is this monasticism?

A misreading must be closed off, because the word is open to it.

*Tabattul* became a technical term in the Sufi literature of later periods, and it was at times used in the sense of **withdrawing from the world altogether**, not marrying, retiring into seclusion.

**The verse does not say this.** For three reasons:

**First:** the verse itself specifies the **direction** of the cutting off (*ilayhi*), not its object. What one is to be cut off from is not said.

**Second:** the last verse of this same sūra **counts** setting out for trade and going to war as excuses — that is, it records worldly occupation not as a fault but as a reality.

**Third:** the Qurʾān records monasticism separately and openly: *"As for the monasticism they invented — We did not prescribe it for them"* (al-Ḥadīd 57/27).

*Tabattul*, therefore, is not the cutting of relations but **the making of the turning single**. The next verse will confirm this.

## 73/9 — رَّبُّ ٱلْمَشْرِقِ وَٱلْمَغْرِبِ لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ فَٱتَّخِذْهُ وَكِيلًا

*Rabbu'l-mashriqi wa'l-maghribi lā ilāha illā huwa fa'ttakhidhhu wakīlā*
"The Lord of the east and the west — there is no god but He. So take Him as trustee."

### ٱلْمَشْرِق وَٱلْمَغْرِب — east and west

**مَشْرِق** is from the root ش-ر-ق; **مَغْرِب** is from the root غ-ر-ب. Both are on the pattern **مَفْعِل**, and in Arabic this pattern forms a **noun of place**: the place of rising, the place of setting.

- **شَرَقَ (*sharaqa*)** — it rose (of the sun). *Sharq*, *ishrāq*, *mushrik* (this last word is from a different root — ش-ر-ك — and should not be confused with it).
- **غَرَبَ (*gharaba*)** — it set, it went far off. *Gharb*, *ghurba* (the state of being far away), *gharīb* (one who comes from far off, a stranger).

**In Turkish the borrowed words "gurbet" and "garip" come from this root** — and the link between the two is direct: the place where the sun sets is the direction of distance.

### Why east and west?

The sentence opens with a construct, and the attribute chosen is surprising: the Lord is introduced as the Lord **of the east and the west**.

It could have said "the Lord of the heavens and the earth". It could have said "the Lord of all the worlds". It did not.

**And the choice has to do with the context.** What has the sūra spoken of so far?

- The night (2-4)
- The comparison of night and day (6-7)

**That is, the sūra has been speaking of divisions of time from the start.** And what determines night and day is exactly rising and setting.

The sentence does this: it shows the addressee **the owner** of that stretch of time whose measure he is commanded to set. The one asked to divide the night is reminded who divides the night.

I record this link as my own reading; the text does not make this justification.

### The east-west construct in the Qurʾān

This construct occurs in the Qurʾān in three separate numbers, and all three are meaningful:

| Pattern | Where | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| **Singular** — *al-mashriq wa'l-maghrib* | al-Baqara 2/115, 2/142, 2/177, 2/258; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/28; **al-Muzzammil 73/9** | Two directions; the whole of the directions |
| **Dual** — *al-mashriqayn wa'l-maghribayn* | al-Raḥmān 55/17 | Two risings, two settings |
| **Plural** — *al-mashāriq wa'l-maghārib* | al-Maʿārij 70/40; also *rabbu'l-mashāriq* — al-Ṣāffāt 37/5 | A great number of points of rising and setting |

**The dual and plural readings are explained in the classical sources as follows:** the sun rises and sets each day from a different point on the horizon; over the course of the year these points shift. The dual is read as pointing to the extremes of summer and winter; the plural to all the points in between.

**This explanation rests on an observable fact** — the shifting of the sun's point of rising along the horizon over the year is something that can be followed with the naked eye and has been followed in many cultures throughout history. I do not present it as a "scientific miracle"; for a community that sees the horizon every day in the desert it is an observation requiring no special knowledge.

What I record here is simpler: **that the Qurʾān uses the same construct in three separate numbers shows that the number was not chosen at random.**

### لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ — the sentence that comes in between

A sentence has come in between the construct and the command: *lā ilāha illā hū.*

This is one of the most repeated sentences in the Qurʾān, and its structure is the same as the stance treated in `112-ihlas.md`: first the denial, then the exception.

**And its function here is to prepare the next command.** The command will be: "take Him as trustee." To take someone as trustee is a verb of **choice** — it is chosen. The sentence that comes in between states that this choice has no alternative.

So the sequence is: **introduction → making single → command.** Before the command, the ground of the command is laid.

### The root وكل — trustee

**Root: و-ك-ل.** Its concrete sense: **to refer a matter to another, to leave it to him, to trust him and hand it over.**

Derivatives:

- **وَكِيل (*wakīl*)** — the one to whom a matter is left, who is set to do the work. On the *faʿīl* pattern; it can carry both the active sense (the one who does the work) and the passive (the one to whom it is referred).
- **تَوَكُّل (*tawakkul*)** — leaving a matter, referring it. Form V — again a work the person does himself.
- **تَوْكِيل (*tawkīl*)** — appointing an agent. The legal *vekâlet* ("power of attorney") in Turkish comes from this branch.

**It is important to see the concrete ground of the word:** *wakīl* is a commercial and legal word. It is the person someone leaves in his place in a matter at which he cannot himself be present. In a trading city like Mecca this word was one everyone knew, an everyday word.

### فَٱتَّخِذْهُ — "take"

The verb is **اتَّخَذَ** (*ittakhadha*), from the root أ-خ-ذ, in Form VIII. Its meaning: **to take for oneself, to choose for oneself, to adopt.**

Form VIII, as recorded in `090-beled.md`, conveys that the act is performed **by the person's own effort and taken upon himself**.

**That is, taking a trustee is not something given; it is something done.** The person chooses.

And this says something about the sūra's view of the human will: rising in the night was commanded, being cut off was commanded, now taking a trustee is commanded. **All three are works the addressee is to do.**

### What *tawakkul* does not mean

A note has to be set down, because this word has narrowed.

In Turkish, the borrowed word *tevekkül* is most often paired with **inaction**: letting go, not interfering, leaving things to take their course.

**The ground of the word in Arabic does not support this.** When a merchant leaves his business to a person he has appointed as agent, he has not abandoned the business; he has left the business **to someone**. Agency is not the disappearance of the work but the binding of responsibility to one person.

And the place where this verse stands confirms it: **the sentence stands in the middle of four commands.** Before it, rising in the night and being cut off were commanded; after it, patience and parting will be commanded. That is, the command "take Him as trustee" is placed not in a gap where no work is done but in the middle of the commands.

I record this reading as my own inference. But this too must be added: in the classical literature of theology and Sufism there are detailed discussions of the degrees of *tawakkul*, and they do not all converge in one direction. I do not enter that discussion here; I confine myself to recording the linguistic ground of the word.

## 73/10 — وَٱصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَٱهْجُرْهُمْ هَجْرًا جَمِيلًا

*Wa'ṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūna wa'hjurhum hajran jamīlā*
"Be patient with what they say; and part from them with a beautiful parting."

Third persons appear in the sūra for the first time: *yaqūlūna* — "they say." Who they are is not said; they will be qualified in the next verse.

### وَٱصْبِرْ — be patient

**Root: ص-ب-ر.** The concrete sense of this root was treated in `002-bakara.md` at 2/45, and the core recorded there was this: the root's primary sense is **to hold, to restrain, to confine**; patience is not a passive enduring but **an active holding**. I do not repeat it here.

What is to be added here is the **object** of the patience: *ʿalā mā yaqūlūn* — "with what they say".

And this is a striking narrowing.

The verse does not say "be patient with them". Nor does it say "be patient with what they do". **It says "be patient with what they say".**

That is, the subject of the patience is **speech**. And this fits the period in which the sūra stands: in the early Meccan period the main instrument of opposition was **speech** far more than physical pressure — the labels *majnūn*, *shāʿir*, *sāḥir*, *kāhin*. These attributes and their functions were treated in `094-insirah.md`: *"classifying the speaker instead of discussing what is said."*

**And there is a link between the weight of the word and the object of the patience.** In the fifth verse the addressee was told that a "heavy word" would be laid upon him. In the tenth verse he is asked to be patient with the words spoken to him. There are two kinds of speech in the sūra: the carried and the endured.

I record this as an observation.

### The root هجر

**Root: ه-ج-ر.** Its concrete sense: **to part, to leave, to move away from a place or a person.**

Derivatives:

- **هِجْرَة (*hijra*)** — migration from one place to another. The historical sense comes from here.
- **مُهَاجِر (*muhājir*)** — one who migrates.
- **هَجْر (*hajr*)** — leaving, parting, cutting off relations with a person.
- **هُجْر (*hujr*)** — ugly, foul speech. In the Qurʾān: *"…in arrogance towards it, talking nonsense by night"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/67) — *sāmiran tahjurūn*.

**The last derivative is striking:** the same root carries both "to leave" and "to speak foully". The explanation the lexicographers give: foul speech is speech in which reason and decency have been **left behind**.

### Two kinds of *hijra*

The use of the root in the Qurʾān runs in two separate directions, and the distinction needs to be seen:

- **Leaving a place** — migrating from somewhere. The name of the event of the Hijra.
- **Leaving a relation** — moving away from a person or a stance.

This verse is in the second sense. And **in the next sūra the same root will be used once more, with a third object:** *wa'l-rujza fa'hjur* (74/5) — "leave the filth." There what is left is neither a place nor a person.

**So three separate *hajr*s:** a place, a person, a stance. The two sūras use this root with two different objects, and this is one of the most concrete linguistic data for the link between them. It will be taken up again in the comparison section at the end of the sūra.

### جَمِيل — "beautiful"

**Root: ج-م-ل.** Beauty, pleasingness, fittingness.

And the distribution of this word in the Qurʾān shows an order worth pausing over.

**جَمِيل, as the adjective of a verbal noun, occurs in the Qurʾān in the following places:**

| Verse | Construct | What it qualifies |
|---|---|---|
| Yūsuf 12/18, 12/83 | *ṣabrun jamīl* | Patience |
| al-Maʿārij 70/5 | *ṣabran jamīlā* | Patience |
| al-Ḥijr 15/85 | *al-ṣafḥu'l-jamīl* | Pardon, turning away |
| al-Aḥzāb 33/28, 33/49 | *sarāḥan jamīlā* | Divorce, release |
| **al-Muzzammil 73/10** | ***hajran jamīlā*** | **Parting** |

**And the common point is striking:** in the Qurʾān the attribute *jamīl* almost without exception qualifies **a stance taken in the face of a harm or a parting**. Patience, pardon, divorce, leaving.

That is, the word "beautiful" here is not an aesthetic quality; it is a measure for **how a relation is to be ended**.

I record this distribution as an observation. I have given the list from memory; I make no claim to a complete survey, but the places listed are correct.

### What does "a beautiful parting" mean?

The explanations given in the classical commentaries are close to one another:

- **To part without reproach, without retaliating.** The parting itself is not turned into an instrument of punishment.
- **To part without holding a grudge.** The relation is cut, enmity is not set up.
- **To part quietly.** The parting makes no noise.

**What can be drawn from the text itself.** Two observations:

1. The command has **two parts** and the two parts run in opposite directions: *be patient* (stay, hold) and *part* (go). Their being given together shows that the parting is not a flight — the person parts because he can endure, not because he cannot.
2. The adjective is placed not on the parting but on **the manner of the parting**. *Hajran* is the verbal noun, *jamīlā* its adjective. That is, the matter is not whether to part or not, but how the parting is to be done.

The second observation is my inference.

### A distinction: this is not a command to fight

A note has to be set down.

This verse does not give a command to struggle, to resist, to retaliate. **Both verbs are inward-turned:** to hold and to move away.

And the continuation of the sūra confirms this: in the eleventh verse the work of settling accounts will openly be taken from the addressee and handed over to another — *wa dharnī* ("leave me alone with them").

That is, the stance the sūra sets up is this: **be patient with the speech, withdraw from the relation, leave the reckoning to Me.** The three together make up a single attitude.

In what period this stance held, and to what extent it was qualified by which verses, is among the disputed subjects of the literature of law and of the Prophet's life. **I do not enter that discussion here and I draw no ruling from it;** I confine myself to recording what the wording of the verse says.

## 73/11 — وَذَرْنِى وَٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ أُو۟لِى ٱلنَّعْمَةِ وَمَهِّلْهُمْ قَلِيلًا

*Wa dharnī wa'l-mukadhdhibīna ūli'l-naʿmati wa mahhilhum qalīlā*
"Leave Me and the deniers — those people of ease — alone together. And give them a little respite."

### ذَرْنِى — "leave Me"

**Root: و-ذ-ر.** An interesting verb: its perfect is not used, only the imperfect and the imperative. Its meaning: **to leave, to abandon, to leave to itself.**

**وَذَرْنِى** — "leave Me."

And the word order here is a particular pattern in Arabic: **وَ + مفعول معه** (*mafʿūl maʿah* — "the object of accompaniment"). *Dharnī wa'l-mukadhdhibīn* — "leave me (alone) with the deniers."

So the meaning is not "leave me, and leave them too"; it is **"leave me alone with them"**. It is the idea carried by the English "you stay out of it, I will deal with him".

**This pattern occurs in several places in the Qurʾān and in all of them it does the same work:** taking the reckoning from the addressee and handing it to the speaker. The eleventh verse of the next sūra will open with the same word: *dharnī wa man khalaqtu waḥīdā*.

**The eleventh verse of both sūras begins with the same verb.** I record this overlap as an observation; I build no claim whatever on the verse numbers coinciding — the division of verses in the muṣḥaf is a separate matter, and to draw a ruling from numerical overlaps of this kind is exactly the sort of operation `STYLE.md` forbids.

### The direction of the command: the work is handed over

In the preceding verse two commands were given to the addressee: be patient, part. Now the third comes: **do not get involved.**

And this means a load being taken off the addressee. Answering back, calling to account, obtaining a result — none of it belongs to him.

This was the work done by al-Inshirāḥ 94/2 (*"We took your burden off you"*), treated in `094-insirah.md`. Here there is a similar lightening, but by a different route: the load is not taken off, **the task is handed over.**

### ٱلْمُكَذِّبِين — the deniers

**Root: ك-ذ-ب.** In Form II, **كَذَّبَ** — to call a lie, to count as false.

The difference of form matters:

- **كَذَبَ (*kadhaba*)** — to tell a lie. The person's own word is false.
- **كَذَّبَ (*kadhdhaba*)** — to call a lie, to count as false. It is the rejection of another's word.

The verse uses the second. **That is, what is charged is not lying but counting the word that has come as a lie.**

And since the active participle comes in the plural (*al-mukadhdhibīn*), this is an **attribute** — not the name of a group. As recorded in `STYLE.md`: it is the attribute the verse describes; whoever carries that attribute is included in it.

### أُو۟لِى ٱلنَّعْمَة — there is a difference of emphasis here

**This construct needs careful attention, because renderings usually give it as "those who possess favours", and this pulls the word to the wrong side.**

**Root: ن-ع-م.** This root produces different words according to its vowelling, and their meanings diverge:

| Word | Vowel | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **نِعْمَة** (*niʿma*) | *kasra* | Favour, gift, bounty — the thing given |
| **نَعْمَة** (*naʿma*) | *fatḥa* | **Ease, living in plenty, comfort** — the state lived in |
| **نُعْمَى** (*nuʿmā*) | *ḍamma* | Kindness, bounty |

**What occurs in the verse is نَعْمَة with *fatḥa*.** That is, not "those to whom favours have been given" but **"those who live in ease", "those who are comfortably off."**

The emphasis shifts from what is given to **the standard of living that is lived**.

The same word occurs once more in the Qurʾān in this sense: *"…and an ease in which they took delight"* (al-Dukhān 44/27) — *wa naʿmatin kānū fīhā fākihīn*. In the enumeration of what the house of Pharaoh left behind.

**This changes the semantic weight of the word entirely.** The verse qualifies the opposition not by a category of belief but by a **condition of life**.

### The class side of the opposition

This is one of the most striking observations in the sūra, and it does not stand alone in the Qurʾān.

In describing the deniers the verse adds an attribute — and the attribute it chooses is not doctrinal but **economic**.

The Qurʾān makes this link in many places:

- *"Never did We send a warner to a town but that those of its people who lived in ease (*mutrafūhā*) said: 'We reject what you have been sent with'"* (Sabaʾ 34/34).
- *"When We wish to destroy a town, We command those of its people who live in ease (*mutrafīhā*)…"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/16).
- *"The chiefs of those who disbelieved among the people of Noah (Nūḥ) said: We see you only as a human being like ourselves, and we see that those who follow you are the lowest among us (*arādhilunā*)"* (Hūd 11/27).
- *"How many a generation We destroyed before them — they were finer in possessions and in appearance"* (Maryam 19/74).

**The common structure is this:** the resistance comes from the part of society that has the most to lose.

And the logic of this is not forced: the proposal of a new order runs against the interest of whoever benefits most from the existing one. This is recorded in the social sciences too, as an observable social regularity.

**But I am obliged to set down a note here, because otherwise the verse is misread.**

The same note was set down in `089-fecr.md`, and the balance struck there must be kept here as well: plenty is not itself declared a crime. As recorded there — "it was given to me" is correct, "what is given is good" is correct; what is rejected is the counting of material condition as evidence for a verdict. And the warning added there holds here too: **to see a rich man and say "there is the man in the verse" is precisely the operation the text forbids.**

What the verse says is narrower and more exact: **in this verse the act of denial and the state of ease are recorded as joined in the same people.** The verse lays down no rule (it does not say "people of ease deny"); it describes the composition of a particular opposition.

The mechanism treated in `104-humeze.md` completes this description: there the link was made between wealth's "ceasing to be a means and becoming an end" and the illusion of permanence produced by counting. And the link made in `089-fecr.md` looks here too: the person who counts his own plenty as divine approval resists a proposal that would invalidate that approval.

The inference joining these three is mine; the verses do not build this chain themselves.

### وَمَهِّلْهُمْ قَلِيلًا — "give them a little respite"

**Root: م-ه-ل.** Its concrete sense: **not to hurry, to act slowly, to grant time.**

- **مَهْل / مُهْلَة (*mahl* / *muhla*)** — a term, the granting of time.
- **مَهَّلَ (*mahhala*)** — Form II: to give a term.
- **أَمْهَلَ (*amhala*)** — Form IV: the same meaning.
- **عَلَى مَهْلٍ** — slowly, without haste.

**The two forms are not in the same verse here, but they are used side by side in the Qurʾān:** *"Give the disbelievers respite; grant them a little time"* (al-Ṭāriq 86/17) — *fa-mahhili'l-kāfirīna amhilhum ruwaydā*.

That verse is the last verse of Sūrat al-Ṭāriq and was treated in `086-tarik.md`. **The overlap between the two verses is almost exact:** the same root, the same form, the same command, the same note of "a little".

### قَلِيلًا — for the third time

This word occurs in the sūra for the third time, and this is one of its finest weavings:

| Place | Expression | A little of what |
|---|---|---|
| 73/2 | *illā **qalīlā*** | The part of the night to be slept |
| 73/3 | *inquṣ minhu **qalīlā*** | The amount to be taken off the measure |
| **73/11** | *wa mahhilhum **qalīlā*** | **The term granted to the deniers** |

**The same word, three separate measures.** The first two divide the addressee's night; the third measures the other side's time.

And the "little" in the third serves as a consolation: the person asked to be patient and to withdraw is told that this is not without term.

**One more observation, as my own reading:** the word "little" is used here not for the addressee but for the other side — yet it is said for the addressee to **hear**. Something is being said to the one who is patient about the term of his patience.

## 73/12-13 — إِنَّ لَدَيْنَآ أَنكَالًا وَجَحِيمًا / وَطَعَامًا ذَا غُصَّةٍ وَعَذَابًا أَلِيمًا

*Inna ladaynā ankālan wa jaḥīmā / Wa ṭaʿāman dhā ghuṣṣatin wa ʿadhāban alīmā*
"Surely with Us there are fetters and a blazing fire; and a food that sticks in the throat, and a painful punishment."

The recompense of the reckoning handed over in the preceding verse is enumerated here. Four things, in two verses.

### أَنكَال — fetters

**Root: ن-ك-ل.** Its singular is **نِكْل** (*nikl*): **a heavy iron ring put on the ankle or the neck, a fetter, a shackle.**

And the family of derivatives shows what the word does:

- **نَكَل عَنْ (*nakala ʿan*)** — he held back, he shrank, he took fright and gave up.
- **نَكَال (*nakāl*)** — **a punishment given to serve as a warning.** In the Qurʾān: *"We made it a warning punishment (*nakālan*) for those before it and those after it"* (al-Baqara 2/66); *"…as a warning punishment from God"* (al-Māʾida 5/38); *"So God seized him with the punishment of the hereafter and of the first life (*nakāla'l-ākhirati wa'l-ūlā*)"* (al-Nāziʿāt 79/25).
- **تَنْكِيل (*tankīl*)** — deterring, making an example of. The Turkish *tenkil* ("suppression by making an example") comes from this root.

**At the centre of the root there is one idea: prevention.** The fetter prevents the body; the warning punishment prevents others. The word names a "stopping" that is at once physical and social.

**And the point of word order here:** the verse uses the **plural and indefinite** (*ankālan*). No number is stated, no definiteness is given. That the indefinite in Arabic can convey **magnification** was treated in the sections `112-ihlas.md` and `103-asr.md`; that reading is possible here too.

### جَحِيم — the blazing fire

**Root: ج-ح-م.** Its concrete sense: **the flaring up of fire, the intensifying of heat.** *Jaḥmatu'l-nār* — the fiercest part of a fire, its hottest place.

The word is settled in the Qurʾān as one of the names of hell.

### طَعَامًا ذَا غُصَّةٍ — food that sticks in the throat

The most concrete and most uncomfortable image in the sūra.

**Root: غ-ص-ص.** **غُصَّة** (*ghuṣṣa*): **a morsel that catches in the throat and cannot be swallowed.** And from here the figurative sense: a distress that settles inside and does not pass.

*Ghaṣṣa bi'l-māʾ* — he choked on water, that is, it stuck as he was drinking.

**English has exact equivalents: "to stick in one's throat", "it will not go down".** And the Turkish *gussa* ("grief") has come over from this same root.

### The force of the image

The image of punishment the verse chooses is worth pausing over, because it is unusual.

What is widespread in descriptions of hell is **burning**: fire, flame, boiling water. That is here too (*jaḥīm*).

But something entirely different comes in between: **a food that cannot be eaten.**

And the property of this image is this: **it does not cause pain, it does not complete itself.** A burning fire does a work; a morsel stuck in the throat does nothing — it neither goes down nor comes up. It stays suspended.

The Qurʾān sets up this kind of "non-completion" in other places too:

- *"He tries to gulp it down but can hardly swallow it"* (Ibrāhīm 14/17).
- *"No sentence of death is passed on them that they might die, nor is their punishment lightened for them"* (Fāṭir 35/36).

**The common structure: a being-caught-in-between that does not end.**

I record this as my own reading: the verse describes the punishment not only as an intensity but also as a **staying suspended**.

### The ascent in the word order

The four elements come in this sequence:

| Order | Element | What it does | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **أَنكَال** — fetter | Stops movement | Outside the body |
| 2 | **جَحِيم** — flame | Burns | On the body |
| 3 | **طَعَام ذُو غُصَّة** — the morsel that sticks | Neither goes down nor comes up | **Inside** the body |
| 4 | **عَذَاب أَلِيم** — painful punishment | A general name | Everywhere |

**The first three advance from the outside inward.** First movement is bound, then the skin, then the throat. The fourth is a kind of collecting heading: a punishment left unnamed, qualified only as "causing pain".

This reading of the sequence is my inference; the text claims no such order.

### Context: why here?

In the eleventh verse the reckoning was handed over. In the twelfth and thirteenth verses it is shown what the reckoning that was handed over is.

**And this explains why the sentence "leave Me alone with them" is a consolation.** The work taken from the addressee does not go into a void; it is stated that there is a recompense for it.

The word order supports this too: **إِنَّ لَدَيْنَا** — "surely **with Us**". The particle *ladā* conveys nearness and being present. That is, the recompense is not something to be produced later; **it stands ready.**

## 73/14 — يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ ٱلْأَرْضُ وَٱلْجِبَالُ وَكَانَتِ ٱلْجِبَالُ كَثِيبًا مَّهِيلًا

*Yawma tarjufu'l-arḍu wa'l-jibālu wa kānati'l-jibālu kathīban mahīlā*
"On the day the earth and the mountains shake; and the mountains become a heap of sand that pours away."

### تَرْجُفُ — to shake

**Root: ر-ج-ف.** Its concrete sense: **to be shaken violently, to tremble, to be moved from its place.**

- **رَجْفَة (*rajfa*)** — a shaking, an earthquake. In the Qurʾān it is used of the peoples that were destroyed: *"So the shaking seized them"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/78, 7/91, 7/155; al-ʿAnkabūt 29/37).
- **رَاجِفَة (*rājifa*)** — that which shakes; used at al-Nāziʿāt 79/6 of the resurrection.
- **إِرْجَاف (*irjāf*)** — unsettling people by spreading baseless news. In the Qurʾān: *"…and those who spread baseless news in the city (*al-murjifūn*)…"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/60).

**The last derivative is striking:** the same root names both the shaking of the earth and the shaking of a society. Rumour and baseless news are what set a community trembling.

### كَثِيب مَّهِيل — a heap of sand that pours away

The most concrete and most local image in the sūra.

**كَثِيب (*kathīb*)** — from the root **ك-ث-ب**: a sand hill, a dune, a heaped-up mass of sand. The sense of the root is "to gather, to be piled together".

**مَّهِيل (*mahīl*)** — from the root **ه-ي-ل**, a passive participle: **that which pours away and scatters when it is trodden on or drawn out from beneath.**

*Hāla'l-ramla* — he poured the sand out, made it run. *Ahāla* — he made it run, made it slide.

**What the word describes is a behaviour everyone who lives in the desert knows:** when a handful is taken from the foot of a dune, the sand above runs down to fill the hollow. The heap does not hold; at every intervention it slides.

That is, the verse describes not the disappearance of the mountain but **its losing the property of holding together**. The mountain is still there — but it is now a heap, and when it is touched it runs.

### The fate of the mountains — the images the Qurʾān gives

The Qurʾān describes the state of the mountains at the resurrection with more than one image, and these form a series:

| Verse | Expression | Image |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Muzzammil 73/14** | *kathīban mahīlā* | A running heap of sand |
| al-Qāriʿa 101/5 | *ka'l-ʿihni'l-manfūsh* | Carded wool |
| al-Wāqiʿa 56/5-6 | *bussat bassā / habāʾan munbaththā* | Dust crumbled and scattered |
| Ṭāhā 20/105 | *yansifuhā rabbī nasfā* | Being winnowed and dispersed |
| al-Nabaʾ 78/20 | *fa-kānat sarābā* | A mirage |
| Alongside al-Muzzammil: al-Maʿārij 70/9 | *ka'l-ʿihn* | Wool |

**And the series shows an ascent:** heap of sand → wool → dust → winnowing → mirage. At each step the matter grows a little lighter, and at the end the appearance remains and the matter does not.

The comparison with *manfūsh* (carded wool) was treated in `101-karia.md`; *sarāb* was taken up in `078-nebe.md`. The link added here is the **heaviest end** of the series: sand is still matter, still heavy — it simply no longer holds.

**And the choice of the comparison is meaningful.** In the Arab imagination the mountain is the symbol of fixity and endurance; the Qurʾān qualifies mountains as *awtād* (pegs, al-Nabaʾ 78/7). Sand, by contrast, is the least stable material of the same country.

**That is, the verse turns the most fixed thing of that country into its most unsteady thing.** It is not a distant comparison; it is a scene in which the addressee sees both every day.

### What sand and mountain have in common

An observation — as my own reading.

The difference between sand and rock is not a difference of material; it is a difference of **grain size**. Sand is broken rock.

This is why the verse does not choose a different material. It says the mountain turns into a state that is **of the same material** but no longer stands together.

And what this describes is this: destruction is not a thing's disappearing; it is **the loosening of its bonds**. The material stays, what holds it together goes.

I do not present this as a claim in geology and I do not load scientific knowledge onto the verse; that sand is broken rock is something anyone who takes sand in his hand can see. What I record is the aptness in the choice of the comparison.

### Context: why mountains?

The two preceding verses had enumerated the recompense of the deniers — and those deniers had been qualified as **"the people of ease"**.

Now what is shaken is **the mountains**.

The observation recorded in `089-fecr.md` looks here: there the three structures of three peoples (the column, the hewn rock, the peg) were set beside the individual's wealth and it was said that *"what is written on stone and what is written on wealth are the same claim to permanence"*.

The same move is made here: to those who trust in their own firmness, the day is shown on which the firmest-looking thing pours away.

I make this link as my own reading; the verse does not say it openly, but the two verses standing side by side support this reading.

## 73/15-16 — إِنَّآ أَرْسَلْنَآ إِلَيْكُمْ رَسُولًا شَـٰهِدًا عَلَيْكُمْ كَمَآ أَرْسَلْنَآ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ رَسُولًا / فَعَصَىٰ فِرْعَوْنُ ٱلرَّسُولَ فَأَخَذْنَـٰهُ أَخْذًا وَبِيلًا

*Innā arsalnā ilaykum rasūlan shāhidan ʿalaykum kamā arsalnā ilā firʿawna rasūlā / Fa-ʿaṣā firʿawnu'l-rasūla fa-akhadhnāhu akhdhan wabīlā*

"Surely We have sent to you a messenger who is a witness against you — just as We sent a messenger to Pharaoh. Pharaoh disobeyed that messenger; so We seized him with a heavy seizing."

### The turning of the address: *iltifāt*

The sharpest move of word order in the sūra is here.

For fourteen verses the address was **singular**: *qum*, *rattil*, *laka*, *ʿalayka*, *rabbika*, *iṣbir*, *uhjurhum*, *dharnī*.

In the fifteenth verse it suddenly becomes **plural**: **إِلَيْكُمْ** — "to you", **عَلَيْكُمْ** — "against you".

This is **iltifāt** (a shift in the direction of address), and here the addressee changes entirely: the one spoken to is no longer the Prophet but **those who oppose him**.

**And the place of the transition is meaningful.** In the eleventh verse it was said "leave them to Me"; in the twelfth and thirteenth verses their recompense was enumerated. Now it turns to them directly.

So the sequence is: **they were spoken of to the addressee → then they were spoken to.** In between lies the handing over of the reckoning.

### شَاهِدًا عَلَيْكُمْ — "a witness against you"

The messenger is introduced with two attributes: **sent** (*rasūl*) and **witness** (*shāhid*).

**Root: ش-ه-د.** To see, to be present, to report what one has seen. *Shahāda*, *shāhid*, *shahīd*, *mashhad*.

**And the particle matters: عَلَىٰ.** "**Against** you" — that is, someone who will testify not for you but against you.

In Arabic the distinction between *shahida li* (to testify in favour) and *shahida ʿalā* (to testify against) is clear. Here it is the second.

**This is an often-skipped side of messengership:** the messenger is not only a bringer of news but also a **keeper of record**. The stance taken towards him will be the subject of his testimony.

The Qurʾān records this function in other places as well:

- *"…and that the Messenger might be a witness against you"* (al-Baqara 2/143).
- *"How will it be when We bring from every community a witness, and bring you as a witness against these?"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/41).
- *"And We brought you as a witness against these"* (al-Naḥl 16/89).

### كَمَآ أَرْسَلْنَآ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ — the choice of the comparison

The verse sets up an analogy: **We sent to you — just as We sent to Pharaoh.**

**And the example chosen is worth pausing over.**

In the Qurʾān there are many peoples to whom a messenger was sent and who were destroyed: the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, Madyan. In the early sūras these are often named together.

Here a single example has been chosen, and what is chosen is not a people but **a person**: Pharaoh.

The reason for this is explained in several ways in the classical commentaries. What can be drawn from the text is this:

**Pharaoh is the one with the greatest worldly means among the opponents the Qurʾān describes.** State, army, wealth, legitimacy, institutional religion — all of it is gathered in him.

And four verses earlier the opponents had been qualified as **"the people of ease"**.

So the analogy does not look as if it were chosen idly: to those who have means, the end of the one with the most means is shown. **I record this link as my own reading;** the verse does not state its ground.

**And there is a second function too.** The story of Pharaoh was a story known in Mecca — a narrative circulating by way of the People of the Book and along the trade routes. That is, the example is one the addressee can verify.

### Who is "the messenger sent to Pharaoh"?

The verse gives no name: *rasūlā* — "a messenger", indefinite.

Across the Qurʾān as a whole the messenger sent to Pharaoh is Moses (Mūsā) (and Aaron (Hārūn) was sent with him). This is certain from the Qurʾān's own account.

But **the verse's not giving a name is meaningful** and it builds a structure:

| | The addressees | Pharaoh |
|---|---|---|
| The one sent | *rasūlan* (indefinite) | *rasūlā* (indefinite) |
| His name | Not given | Not given |

**There is no name on either side.** What the analogy is built on is not persons but **a situation**: a messenger came, he was opposed, and this was the outcome.

This fits the principle recorded in `STYLE.md`: the text describes attribute and situation, not identity.

### فَعَصَىٰ — he disobeyed

**Root: ع-ص-ي.** Its concrete sense is interesting: **عَصَا (*ʿaṣā*)** — a staff, a stick. And **عَصَى (*ʿaṣā*)** — he rebelled, he opposed.

The link the lexicographers make is this: *rebellion* is a **separating** from the community; and the idea at the root of **عَصَا** too is "a thing held together" — like a bundle of rods. *ʿAṣā* is fibres gathered into one; *rebellion* is a coming apart.

I do not present this link as a settled judgement in etymology; it is a relation the lexicographers record.

**And an observation, as my own reading:** the best-known object of the story of Moses is the staff. And Pharaoh's act is the verb *ʿaṣā*. That the two words are made of the same letters is striking. I record this not as a claim but as an observation about the texture of the text; I make no link between the two in respect of meaning.

### ٱلرَّسُولَ — this time definite

A point of word order.

In the fifteenth verse the messenger occurred twice as **indefinite**: *rasūlan*, *rasūlā*.

In the sixteenth verse it becomes **definite**: **ٱلرَّسُولَ** — "that messenger".

This is a settled rule in Arabic: when an indefinite has been mentioned and is then repeated, it comes definite, because it is now known. The same structure exists in English: "a man came in; the man said…"

Here it produces an added effect: **within two verses the messenger becomes a known person.** Pharaoh opposes not someone he does not know but someone he knows.

### أَخْذًا وَبِيلًا — a heavy seizing

**أَخَذَ** — to seize, to hold, to grasp. It is settled in the Qurʾān as the verb of destruction: *fa-akhadhat-humu'l-rajfa*, *fa-akhadhnāhum*.

**وَبِيل** — from the root **و-ب-ل**. And the concrete sense of this root illuminates the word:

- **وَابِل (*wābil*)** — **rain in large drops, violent rain; a downpour.** In the Qurʾān twice, one after the other: *"…like a smooth rock with soil upon it; a heavy rain (*wābil*) falls on it and leaves it bare"* (al-Baqara 2/264); and immediately after, the same word in a positive context (al-Baqara 2/265).
- **وَبِيل (*wabīl*)** — heavy, severe, grave. Also in the lexicons: hard to digest, harmful pasture or food.

**At the centre of the root there is weight and violence** — and in particular a weight that comes **from above**. A downpour is both heavy and comes down from above.

### The cognate accusative, a fourth time

*Akhdhan wabīlā* — verbal noun + adjective. Again a cognate accusative.

The sūra uses this technique four times, and all four are at the turning points of the sūra:

| Verse | Expression | What it emphasises |
|---|---|---|
| 73/4 | *rattili… **tartīlā*** | The manner of the recitation |
| 73/8 | *tabattal… **tabtīlā*** | The completeness of the turning |
| 73/10 | *uhjurhum **hajran jamīlā*** | The quality of the parting |
| 73/16 | *akhadhnāhu **akhdhan wabīlā*** | The violence of the seizing |

**And in all four the adjective or the verbal noun says *how* the act is done** — not how much of it is done. From beginning to end the sūra is concerned with quality.

This observation is my inference.

## 73/17 — فَكَيْفَ تَتَّقُونَ إِن كَفَرْتُمْ يَوْمًا يَجْعَلُ ٱلْوِلْدَٰنَ شِيبًا

*Fa-kayfa tattaqūna in kafartum yawman yajʿalu'l-wildāna shībā*
"Then how will you guard yourselves, if you disbelieve, against a day that turns children into grey-haired old men?"

### The interrogative sentence: كَيْفَ

The sentence opens with **كَيْفَ** ("how"), and this is not a question that asks for information.

It is the pattern called the **interrogative of denial**: a sentence given in the form of a question but whose answer conveys impossibility. "How will you guard yourselves?" — that is, "you cannot."

And the form of the question does one thing more: **it calls the addressee to answer.** He is expected to notice for himself that he cannot answer. It is the reverse of the technique of *taqrīr* (eliciting assent) recorded in `094-insirah.md`: there the addressee was made to say "yes", here he is silenced.

### تَتَّقُونَ — to guard oneself

**Root: و-ق-ي.** Its concrete sense is **to protect, to be a shield, to place an obstacle in front of something**.

- **وِقَايَة (*wiqāya*)** — protection, a shield.
- **تَقْوَى (*taqwā*)** — guarding oneself. It is usually rendered "fear of God"; but at the centre of the root is not **fear** but **guarding**.
- **مُتَّقِى (*muttaqī*)** — one who guards himself.

**And the choice of this root builds the logic of the verse:** *taqwā* is the work of a shield. The verse asks: what shield will you set against this day?

### إِن كَفَرْتُمْ — the condition

The particle **إِنْ**, as recorded in `094-insirah.md`, conveys **possibility**: "if it happens." Its occurrence is not certain.

That is, the verse does not say "you are disbelieving"; it says **"if you disbelieve"**.

This is a particle that leaves the door open. And two verses later the door will open wider still: *"Whoever wills takes a path to his Lord"* (73/19).

### يَوْمًا يَجْعَلُ ٱلْوِلْدَٰنَ شِيبًا

The most striking image in the sūra.

**ٱلْوِلْدَٰن** — from the root **و-ل-د**, the plural of **وَلِيد** (*walīd*): newborn, small child. From the root come *walad* (child), *wālid* (father), *wālida* (mother), *mawlid* (birth).

**شِيب** — from the root **ش-ي-ب**, the plural of **أَشْيَب** (*ashyab*) or of **شَائِب**: **grey-haired, aged.**

*Shayba* — the whitening of the hair. In the Qurʾān: *"Then it is He who gives, after strength, weakness and old age (*shayba*)"* (al-Rūm 30/54). And in the prayer of Zechariah: *"My head has flared up with grey"* (Maryam 19/4) — *wa'shtaʿala'l-raʾsu shaybā*.

### The building of the image

The verse sets two words side by side, and the two are exact opposites of each other:

- **وِلْدَان** — those at the beginning of life
- **شِيب** — those at the end of life

And between them stands the verb **يَجْعَلُ** (makes, turns).

That is, a day is described which **joins the two ends of a lifetime in a single moment.** The normal flow of time — to be born, to grow, to grow old — collapses.

**And the sign chosen is striking: hair.**

The verse does not say "it kills children", it does not say "it frightens children". **It says "it turns children grey"** — that is, a visible, bodily, measurable mark.

The whitening of the hair is the most visible record of time upon a human being. How long a person has lived is estimated by looking at it.

**The verse describes a day on which that record lies.**

I record this reading as my inference.

### Literal or figurative? — the disagreement

Two readings are given in the classical commentaries:

| View | What it means | Ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Literal** | On that day children's hair actually turns white | The wording of the verse; extraordinary events are described in the resurrection scenes in any case | Whether the category "child" will exist on that day is a separate matter |
| **Figurative** | An expression conveying the magnitude of the terror | To say of a violent event that it "turns the hair white" is a known usage in Arabic | There is no indication in the verse pointing to a figure of speech |

**I do not impose a preference.** Neither reading runs against the text, and the practical outcome is the same: what is described stands at the limit of what can be endured.

This much I can say: **in both readings what works is the same image.** Even on the figurative reading, the picture the figure builds is preserved — and the work the verse does is that picture.

### A contrast: the *wildān* of the garden

The same word occurs in another context in the Qurʾān, and the contrast is striking:

- *"Around them circulate immortal youths (*wildānun mukhalladūn*)"* (al-Wāqiʿa 56/17; al-Insān 76/19).

There *wildān* are **those who do not grow old**. Here *wildān* are **those who grow old early.**

The same word, at two extremes: the place where time does not work at all, and the place where time is gathered into a single moment.

I record this contrast as an observation.

## 73/18 — ٱلسَّمَآءُ مُنفَطِرٌۢ بِهِۦ ۚ كَانَ وَعْدُهُۥ مَفْعُولًا

*Al-samāʾu munfaṭirun bih. Kāna waʿduhū mafʿūlā*
"The heaven is split open by it. His promise has been carried out."

### The root فطر

**Root: ف-ط-ر.** The concrete sense of this root — **to split, to crack, to open a thing along its length** — and its family of derivatives (*fiṭra*, *fāṭir*, *ifṭār*) were treated in detail in `082-infitar.md`. I do not repeat it here.

The core recorded there: the root is used both for the **first splitting** (creation, *fiṭra*) and for the **last splitting** (the cracking of the heaven at the resurrection); and *ifṭār* (breaking the fast) is the same idea — the opening of what was closed.

Here the word is **مُنفَطِر** — the active participle of Form VII: that which is split, cracked open.

### The point of word order: why masculine?

**This is the most discussed grammatical question in the sūra.**

**ٱلسَّمَآء** (*samāʾ*) is **feminine** in Arabic. Its adjective would be expected to come feminine as well: *munfaṭira*.

But in the verse **مُنفَطِرٌ** comes — masculine.

Several explanations are given in the classical sources:

| Explanation | Ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Samāʾ is used as masculine too** | In Arabic the word *samāʾ* is counted masculine in some usages; there are examples in poetry | Possible on linguistic grounds; but elsewhere in the Qurʾān *samāʾ* is treated as feminine (*inshaqqati'l-samāʾ*, al-Inshiqāq 84/1) |
| **An implied word: *saqf*** | What is meant is not "heaven" but "roof"; *al-samāʾ* here stands for *saqfu'l-samāʾ* | The Qurʾān qualifies the heaven as *saqfan maḥfūẓā* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/32); possible, but it requires an ellipsis |
| **The sense of relation** | *Munfaṭir* means "possessor of splitting" (*dhātu'nfiṭār*); adjectives of relation can come masculine | A usage the lexicographers record; examples such as *ḥāʾiḍ*, *ṭāliq* are given |
| **Emphasis** | The masculine form is a deviation conveying the magnitude of the event | The weakest explanation; loading meaning onto grammatical deviations is risky |

**I do not impose a preference.** The third explanation is the one most worked on among the lexicographers; but there is no settled conclusion, and I have to state that I find the fourth weak — because to draw meaning from every grammatical deviation is exactly the kind of forcing that `STYLE.md` warns against.

### بِهِ — "by it"

To what does the pronoun refer? Two possibilities:

1. **To the day** (the *yawman* of the preceding verse): "the heaven is split on that day."
2. **To the promise**, or to the event in general: "it is split for this reason."

In the classical commentaries the first is widespread and it fits the context.

**And the particle is striking: بِ.** Not *fī* ("in") but *bi* ("with, by reason of"). That is, the heaven is split not **within** that day but **by the effect** of that day.

This is the day ceasing to be a stretch of time and becoming an **agent**. It was so in the preceding verse too: the day was a subject that made children old (*yajʿalu*).

**In both verses "the day" is not a vessel but an agent.** This observation is my own reading.

### كَانَ وَعْدُهُۥ مَفْعُولًا — the work of the past tense

The finest point of tense in the sūra is here.

The sentence is in the **perfect**: *kāna… mafʿūlā* — "it had been carried out / it has been carried out."

Yet the event described is **in the future**.

**This is a settled technique of the Qurʾān** and was treated in the resurrection sūras such as `081-tekvir.md`, `082-infitar.md`, `084-insikak.md`: to narrate a future event in the past tense conveys **certainty**. What is narrated as done and finished is what has no possibility left of not happening.

And in `094-insirah.md` the same technique was recorded in another direction: *rafaʿnā* ("We raised"), said in the past tense when no visible result was in evidence; and the *innā aʿṭaynāka* of Sūrat al-Kawthar was the same move.

**What is to be added here is the choice of word:** *mafʿūl* — a passive participle, "that which has been done". That is, the promise is named not as a word not yet fulfilled but as **a work already done**.

The distance between word and deed has been removed.

### وَعْد — promise

**Root: و-ع-د.** To give one's word. And in Arabic this root is used for both good and bad news:

- **وَعْد (*waʿd*)** — a word given, a promise (generally positive).
- **وَعِيد (*waʿīd*)** — a threat, a warning (negative).

**The two are from the same root** and the difference lies in the content of the word given, not in the word itself. In this verse *waʿd* is used, but the content is a warning.

This breadth of the word makes it possible for the verse to carry a meaning that changes with its addressee: the same promise is glad tidings for some and a threat for others. I record this reading as an observation.

## 73/19 — إِنَّ هَـٰذِهِۦ تَذْكِرَةٌ ۖ فَمَن شَآءَ ٱتَّخَذَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّهِۦ سَبِيلًا

*Inna hādhihī tadhkira. Fa-man shāʾa'ttakhadha ilā rabbihī sabīlā*
"Surely this is a reminder. So whoever wills takes a path to his Lord."

The first close of the sūra. The twentieth verse will come as a separate section; the main body of nineteen verses ends here.

### تَذْكِرَة — a reminder

**The root ذ-ك-ر** was treated in `094-insirah.md`. **تَذْكِرَة** is on the *tafʿila* pattern and means "an instrument of reminding, the thing that reminds".

**And the way the text names itself is worth pausing over.**

Heavy things have been said through the sūra: fetters, flame, a morsel stuck in the throat, mountains running away, children growing old. And at the end of all of it the text gives itself a name: **a reminder.**

That is, the text does not name itself a threat, a ruling, a proclamation.

**And the word "reminder" has a presupposition:** what is recalled is something the addressee **already knew**. No new information is being given; something forgotten or suppressed is being brought forward.

In `081-tekvir.md` the text's being defined as *dhikr* and tied to the divine will (*mashīʾa*) was treated (81/27-29) and its connection with `080-abese.md` was made. The same structure is here too, and it will be completed in the next sentence.

### فَمَن شَآءَ — "whoever wills"

**And now one of the most important moves of word order in the sūra comes.**

For nineteen verses the sūra spoke in the imperative: *qum*, *rattil*, *udhkur*, *tabattal*, *ittakhidh*, *iṣbir*, *uhjur*, *dharnī*, *mahhil*. Nine commands.

And in the last sentence the imperative is let go: **"whoever wills."**

That is, the sūra begins with a series of commands and ends with a **setting free**.

**This shows how the text positions its own proposal:** the commands were given to the addressee (the Prophet); to the general addressees a door was shown and no compulsion was placed.

### ٱتَّخَذَ — the verb that returns within the sūra

There is an inner weaving here, and it is easily missed.

This verb occurs in the sūra for the second time:

| Verse | Expression | Who takes | What is taken |
|---|---|---|---|
| 73/9 | *fa-**ittakhidhhu** wakīlā* | The addressee (by command) | God as trustee |
| 73/19 | *man shāʾa **ittakhadha** ilā rabbihī sabīlā* | Whoever wills (freely) | A path to his Lord |

**The same verb, two moods.** The first imperative, the second declarative. The first to a single person, the second to everyone.

And as recorded in `090-beled.md`, Form VIII conveys that the act is performed **by the person's own effort**. That is, in both verses the "taking" belongs to the person.

I record this weaving as my own reading.

### This sentence is repeated in the Qurʾān

A note: this verse occurs in another place in the Qurʾān **in exactly the same wording**:

*"Surely this is a reminder. So whoever wills takes a path to his Lord"* (al-Insān 76/29).

And there what follows is: *"But you will not unless God wills"* (al-Insān 76/30).

A similar pattern exists in another place too: *"…so let whoever wills take a place of return to his Lord"* (al-Nabaʾ 78/39).

**What these repetitions point to is that the pattern is settled:** reminder + willing + path. The three together, in more than one sūra.

And **the next sūra will build the same pattern once more:** *"No indeed, it is a reminder. So whoever wills remembers it"* (al-Muddaththir 74/54-55) — followed by *"But they will not remember unless God wills"* (74/56).

Both sūras close with the same structure: **tadhkira → man shāʾa → the qualification of the divine will.** This is one of the strongest data for the link between the two sūras, and it will be taken up again in the comparison section at the end of the sūra.

### The question of the will is not opened here

In al-Muzzammil there is *man shāʾa* ("whoever wills"), but not the **qualification** found at al-Muddaththir 74/56 and al-Takwīr 81/29 (*"unless God wills…"*).

That is, this sūra leaves the door open and leaves it there; it sets no limitation.

The frame of the discussion of the will was set up in `002-bakara.md` at 2/7 and applied in `081-tekvir.md` at 81/29. The stance recorded there — taking no side, and the note that *"none of the parties has held that the human being is without responsibility; the dispute is about how responsibility is constituted"* — holds here as well. I shall take the matter up at the last verse of `074-muddessir.md`, because the note is set down there.

## 73/20 — The lightening of the ruling

إِنَّ رَبَّكَ يَعْلَمُ أَنَّكَ تَقُومُ أَدْنَىٰ مِن ثُلُثَىِ ٱلَّيْلِ وَنِصْفَهُۥ وَثُلُثَهُۥ وَطَآئِفَةٌ مِّنَ ٱلَّذِينَ مَعَكَ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ يُقَدِّرُ ٱلَّيْلَ وَٱلنَّهَارَ ۚ عَلِمَ أَن لَّن تُحْصُوهُ فَتَابَ عَلَيْكُمْ ۖ فَٱقْرَءُوا۟ مَا تَيَسَّرَ مِنَ ٱلْقُرْءَانِ ۚ عَلِمَ أَن سَيَكُونُ مِنكُم مَّرْضَىٰ ۙ وَءَاخَرُونَ يَضْرِبُونَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ يَبْتَغُونَ مِن فَضْلِ ٱللَّهِ ۙ وَءَاخَرُونَ يُقَـٰتِلُونَ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ ۖ فَٱقْرَءُوا۟ مَا تَيَسَّرَ مِنْهُ ۚ وَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَأَقْرِضُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا ۚ وَمَا تُقَدِّمُوا۟ لِأَنفُسِكُم مِّنْ خَيْرٍ تَجِدُوهُ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ هُوَ خَيْرًا وَأَعْظَمَ أَجْرًا ۚ وَٱسْتَغْفِرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌۢ

"Surely your Lord knows that you stand for nearly two thirds of the night, and half of it, and a third of it — and so does a group of those who are with you. It is God who measures the night and the day. He knew that you could not count it, and He turned to you. So recite what is easy for you of the Qurʾān. He knew that there would be sick people among you; and others travelling in the land seeking of God's bounty; and others fighting in the way of God. So recite what is easy for you of it. And keep up the prayer, and give the *zakāt*, and lend God a beautiful loan. Whatever good you send ahead for yourselves, you will find it with God — better and greater in reward. And ask forgiveness of God. Surely God is forgiving, merciful."

**This is one of the longest verses in the Qurʾān** and takes up nearly half as much space as the whole rest of the sūra. The ruling built up over nineteen verses is readjusted in this single verse.

The verse has to be taken piece by piece.

### Meccan or Medinan? — the disagreement

One matter must be settled first.

| View | Ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **Medinan** | The verse contains the command of **zakāt**; the making obligatory of *zakāt* is generally dated to the Medinan period. **Fighting** is also mentioned (*yuqātilūna fī sabīli'llāh*); permission for fighting was given in Medina | The word *zakāt* in the verse occurs in Meccan sūras too in the sense of "purifying and giving" (al-Aʿlā 87/14; the same root at al-Shams 91/9) — it may not be in the technical sense of the obligation |
| **Meccan** | Its consistency with the sūra as a whole; the structure of address being the same | A sentence speaking of fighting occurring in the Meccan period requires explanation |
| **A Meccan verse revealed after an interval** | That there is a gap in time between the first nineteen verses and verse 20, but that both came down in Mecca | There is no certain information about the length of the interval |

**This disagreement is recorded in the classical sources and is not settled.** I do not impose a preference and I issue no definite ruling on the dating. This much I can say: the **function** of the verse does not change whichever dating we accept — the lightening of a ruling is what is being described.

---

### First part: "your Lord knows"

إِنَّ رَبَّكَ يَعْلَمُ أَنَّكَ تَقُومُ…

**The verse opens not with an accusation but with an observation.**

And what is observed is that the addressee **has been carrying out the command in excess**: nearly two thirds of the night, half of it, a third of it.

That is, the lightening does not come upon a failing. On the contrary: it comes after something done in excess.

**This is the most striking side of the verse, and it is usually skipped.** As the rule is loosened, what is given as the ground of the loosening is not the addressee's laxity but his **scrupulousness**.

### أَدْنَىٰ مِن ثُلُثَىِ ٱلَّيْلِ — the measures

**أَدْنَىٰ** — an elative from the root د-ن-و: "nearer", "less". The word *dunyā* is from the same root (the nearest one).

Three measures are enumerated:

| Measure | Text | How much |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *adnā min thuluthayi'l-layl* | Nearly two thirds of the night |
| 2 | *niṣfahū* | Half of it |
| 3 | *thuluthahū* | A third of it |

**And this is the range set in the second and third verses in its realised form.** There it said "half of it, a little less, a little more". Here what was actually done is enumerated: sometimes near two thirds, sometimes half, sometimes a third.

That is, the addressee has moved about at **both ends** of the range given.

### وَطَآئِفَةٌ مِّنَ ٱلَّذِينَ مَعَكَ — not alone

For the first time in the sūra it is said that there are others alongside the addressee.

**طَآئِفَة** — from the root ط-و-ف: those who move about in a circuit, a cluster. *Ṭawāf* is from the same root.

**"A group of those who are with you."**

And the word order is careful: *alladhīna maʿaka* — "those who are with you", and out of them a *ṭāʾifa* — "a part of them".

That is, those carrying out the command are **not all but some** of those around the addressee.

**This detail is important.** The verse does not draw an ideal picture; it records a real distribution. Some do it, some do not. And after this the ruling is lightened.

### وَٱللَّهُ يُقَدِّرُ ٱلَّيْلَ وَٱلنَّهَارَ — who measures

**Root: ق-د-ر.** The three branches of meaning of this root — **measure/determination**, **worth/honour**, **straitness** — were treated in detail in `097-kadr.md`; it was also taken up in `080-abese.md` in the context of *fa-qaddarahū*. I do not repeat it here.

The core recorded there: the most basic sense of the root is **to measure, to determine an amount**; and *taqdīr* is "determining by measure".

**Here the position of the sentence explains the choice of the root.**

For nineteen verses the addressee has been trying to **measure** the night: half, or a little less, or a little more. And now a sentence comes in between: **it is God who measures the night and the day.**

The function of the sentence runs in two directions:

1. **It shows the source of the measuring.** The one who sets the limits of what is divided is another.
2. **It prepares the next sentence.** The one who sets the measure also knows that the measure cannot be met.

And the observation recorded in `097-kadr.md` looks directly here: *"To know the worth of a thing is to give it the right measure… value is the result of measuring rightly."* Here the one who gives the right measure opens room for the human being to set his own measure.

---

### Second part: "He knew that you could not count it"

عَلِمَ أَن لَّن تُحْصُوهُ فَتَابَ عَلَيْكُمْ

**The turning point of the verse.**

### أَحْصَىٰ — to count with pebbles

**Root: ح-ص-ي.** And the concrete sense of this root opens the word onto something quite different.

**حَصَى (*ḥaṣā*)** — **small pebbles.** This is what stands at the centre of the root.

**أَحْصَىٰ (*aḥṣā*)**, in Form IV: **to count with pebbles, to reckon up, to calculate exactly.**

Because in an environment where written numerals were not widespread, counting was done with stones: one pebble for each unit. The word is the name of that practice.

**So *iḥṣāʾ* is not simple counting; it is counting unit by unit, without omission, with a full accounting.**

Its uses in the Qurʾān confirm this:

- *"God has **counted** what they did, and they have forgotten it"* (al-Mujādala 58/6).
- *"…and He has **encompassed** everything in number"* (al-Jinn 72/28).
- *"If you tried to count God's favour, you could not count it"* (Ibrāhīm 14/34; al-Naḥl 16/18) — *lā tuḥṣūhā*. The same negative pattern.

**And the last example looks directly at this verse:** there what cannot be counted is the favour, here it is the night. The same verb, the same negation.

### What cannot be counted?

To what does the pronoun (*-hū*) refer? Two explanations in the classical commentaries:

1. **To the night.** You cannot calculate the parts of the night exactly — there is no clock, no instrument of measurement.
2. **To the measure / to the time in general.** You cannot meet the amount commanded.

**The first explanation has directly to do with the conditions of the period and is realistic.**

Consider: how is a person to know when two thirds of the night is up? There is no clock. The length of the night changes with the season. The hour of waking cannot be predicted.

**That is, the measure commanded was a measure that could not be measured.** And this was a technical impossibility; not a laxity.

It is transmitted in the classical sources that those who tried to carry out this command, being unable to know how much of the night had passed, stood for more than was required and were strained by it. **Since I am not sure to which source I should ascribe this report, I give no name;** but the explanation fits the wording of the text: the verse does not say "you did not do it", it says **"you could not count it"**.

### فَتَابَ عَلَيْكُمْ — "He turned to you"

**This expression needs careful attention, because the usual rendering carries a misleading association.**

**Root: ت-و-ب.** Its concrete sense: **to turn, to come back, to return.**

And the verb is used in two directions:

- **تَابَ إِلَى اللَّه** — the servant turns to God. This is **repentance**.
- **تَابَ اللَّهُ عَلَىٰ…** — God turns to the servant. This is **acceptance and turning towards**.

**In the verse it is the second:** *tāba **ʿalaykum***.

That is, the expression does not mean "He forgave you" or "He accepted your repentance"; it means **"He turned towards you", "He turned to you"**. And what it means in the context is clear: **He rearranged the ruling in accordance with your condition.**

The same structure occurs in the Qurʾān in the context of Adam as well: *"…so his Lord turned to him"* (al-Baqara 2/37) — *fa-tāba **ʿalayh***. There too the verb is used with the particle *ʿalā* and in the same direction.

### But a question remains

**Why was this word chosen?**

There is no sin in view. The verse itself gives as its ground not a failing but an **impossibility**. Why then is the language of repentance used?

Several explanations are given in the classical commentaries:

| Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|
| The verb here means "he turned towards, he showed favour"; there is no association of sin | The root's primary sense is turning; with the particle *ʿalā* it conveys turning towards |
| There is a pardon for those who could not carry the command out fully | Some group may have failed to meet the command |
| The expression is there to soften the removal of the ruling | So that a person should not feel the easing as a deficiency |

**I do not impose a preference.** But I want to record this, as my own reading:

The choice of the verb emphasises **where the lightening comes from**. The ruling changes not at the human being's request but **by the movement of the one who laid it down**. The human being did not bargain, did not ask permission, did not offer an excuse. The turning came from the other side.

And this is the stance of the whole verse: the lightening is presented not as a concession but as a **turning towards**.

---

### Third part: "recite what is easy for you"

فَٱقْرَءُوا۟ مَا تَيَسَّرَ مِنَ ٱلْقُرْءَانِ

### تيسير — making easy

**Root: ي-س-ر.** This root was treated in detail in `094-insirah.md` in its contrast with *ʿusr* (and a comparison with al-Ṭalāq 65/7 was made there); in `087-ala.md` it was taken up in the pattern *nuyassiruka li'l-yusrā*, and the fine observation recorded there was this: the object of the verb is not the work but **the person** — "it is not the work that is made easy, but the person."

Here the verb is **تَيَسَّرَ**, in Form V and **intransitive**: "it came easy, it became easy."

**And it has no subject — or rather, its subject is unspecified.** *Mā tayassara* — "what becomes easy", "what comes easy to you".

This is the most flexible point in the verse: **the measure of ease is left to the person.** No amount is given, no number of pages, no length of time.

### The transformation of the command: from the fourth verse to the twentieth

This is the link that has to be made in order to grasp the sūra as a whole.

| | **73/4** | **73/20** |
|---|---|---|
| Command | *wa rattili'l-Qurʾān* | *fa'qraʾū mā tayassara mina'l-Qurʾān* |
| Mood | Imperative, **singular** | Imperative, **plural** |
| Verb | *rattil* — recite in a particular manner | *iqraʾ* — recite |
| Quality | **تَرْتِيلًا** — emphasised by a cognate accusative | None |
| Amount | Not stated | **مَا تَيَسَّرَ** — as much as comes easy |
| Addressee | The Prophet | The community |

**Four changes at once:** the addressee widened, the condition of manner fell away, the emphasis fell away, and a note of "ease" was placed on the amount.

**And note: the command itself did not fall away.** The command to recite stands. What fell away is the rigidity placed on the measure of the command.

This shows how the lightening works: **the act is preserved, the measure is made flexible.**

### The repetition of the command twice

The expression *fa'qraʾū mā tayassara* occurs **twice** in the verse:

1. "He knew that you could not count it, and He turned to you — **so recite what is easy for you of the Qurʾān.**"
2. After the three excuses are enumerated: "**so recite what is easy for you of it.**"

**That is, the lightening is tied to two separate grounds** and after each ground the same conclusion is repeated:

| Ground | Of what kind |
|---|---|
| Not being able to count the measure | **Structural** — the impossibility of measuring |
| Sickness, travel, war | **Situational** — the conditions of persons |

The second repetition does not reinforce the first; **it arrives at the same place through another door.**

---

### Fourth part: the three excuses

عَلِمَ أَن سَيَكُونُ مِنكُم مَّرْضَىٰ ۙ وَءَاخَرُونَ يَضْرِبُونَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ يَبْتَغُونَ مِن فَضْلِ ٱللَّهِ ۙ وَءَاخَرُونَ يُقَـٰتِلُونَ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ

**The most striking sentence in the verse and perhaps in the whole sūra.**

### The three groups

| Group | Text | Their condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **مَّرْضَىٰ** | The sick |
| 2 | **يَضْرِبُونَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ يَبْتَغُونَ مِن فَضْلِ ٱللَّهِ** | Those who set out for a living / for trade |
| 3 | **يُقَـٰتِلُونَ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ** | Those who fight in the way of God |

### يَضْرِبُونَ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ — to travel in the land

The primary sense of the verb **ضَرَبَ** is "to strike". But when it is joined with **فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ** it becomes a settled idiom in Arabic: **to set out, to travel, to go about for a living.**

The explanation the lexicographers give: the traveller advances **striking** his foot against the ground. The English "to pound the roads" carries the same picture.

Its uses in the Qurʾān:

- *"When you travel in the land there is no blame on you for shortening the prayer"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/101).
- *"…for the poor who have devoted themselves to the way of God and cannot travel about in the land…"* (al-Baqara 2/273) — *lā yastaṭīʿūna ḍarban fi'l-arḍ*.

### يَبْتَغُونَ مِن فَضْلِ ٱللَّهِ — seeking of God's bounty

**ابْتَغَىٰ** — from the root ب-غ-ي, in Form VIII: to seek, to ask for, to go after.

**فَضْل** — from the root ف-ض-ل: what is in excess, what is over; a gift, a bounty.

**And this construct is used in a settled way in the Qurʾān for "trade and earning":**

- *"When the prayer has been performed, disperse in the land and **seek of God's bounty**"* (al-Jumuʿa 62/10). Returning to work after the Friday prayer.
- *"There is no blame on you for seeking a bounty from your Lord"* (al-Baqara 2/198). About trading during the season of the pilgrimage.
- *"…and your sleeping by night and by day and your seeking of His bounty are among His signs"* (al-Rūm 30/23). The same construct occurs in the context of night and day at al-Isrāʾ 17/12 and al-Qaṣaṣ 28/73 as well.

**So "to seek of God's bounty" is the expression the Qurʾān uses for earning.** And note: earning is named "God's bounty". What a person earns himself is named as a gift.

### And the real matter: trade and war in the same list

**Now let us look at the structure of the sentence.**

The three groups are set out in the same sentence, with the same conjunction (*wa ākharūna… wa ākharūna*), in the same grammatical pattern. No difference of degree, no order of priority, no distinction of value **is placed** between them.

And there are two things standing side by side in the list:

- **Setting out for trade.**
- **Fighting in the way of God.**

**This is a word order that needs pausing over.**

Because in religious literature these two acts are generally treated on very different levels. One is positioned as a worldly occupation, the other as the highest sacrifice. Here the two are named **in the same list of excuses**.

**And the order is more striking still:** trade comes **first**, war **second**.

Comments have been made on this order in the classical commentaries; some tie it to those engaged in trade being far more numerous. This is a reasonable explanation.

**But there is no need to look at the order to see what the verse is doing; the list itself is enough.** All three situations are tied to the same conclusion: *fa'qraʾū mā tayassara minh.*

That is, the verse says: **among the things that prevent rising in the night, sickness, trade and war are in the same category.** All three are conditions; all three are excuses; in all three the measure is lightened.

### What this means — a careful inference

What can be drawn from this and what cannot must be separated.

**What the verse says:** all three situations are sufficient reason for the measure of worship to be lightened.

**What the verse does not say:** that trade and war are equal in value. The list makes no ranking of value; **it enumerates excuses.** That two things are in the same list of excuses does not show that they are of the same value.

**But it does show something**, and I record this as my own reading: **the pursuit of a livelihood counts, in the eyes of the verse, as a valid excuse.** That is, to say "I was engaged in trade" is a ground accepted against the measure of worship.

This says something important about how religious life is constituted. Because an alternative construction was possible, and has been built in many traditions throughout history: **a construction in which worship invalidates the rest of life.** In that construction working, earning, keeping a family — these are obstacles in the way of worship, and to the extent that they are obstacles they are faults.

The verse does not build this construction. It records work not as an obstacle but as a **condition** — like sickness, like war.

And when the command of *tabattul* (73/8) is recalled, one sees how striking this is: the same sūra says in its eighth verse "cut yourself off and turn", and in its twentieth verse counts a trading journey as an excuse. **The two together show what *tabattul* is not** — the inference recorded in the discussion of the sūra's eighth verse is confirmed here by the text itself.

### The demand adjusted to the human being

**This verse is one of the clearest examples in the Qurʾān of a principle:** the ruling is adjusted to the capacity of the one addressed.

The Qurʾān formulates this principle openly in other places:

- *"God does not burden anyone beyond what he can bear"* (al-Baqara 2/286).
- *"God wants ease for you, He does not want hardship"* (al-Baqara 2/185) — treated in `094-insirah.md`.
- *"He has laid no hardship on you in religion"* (al-Ḥajj 22/78).
- *"So guard against God as much as you are able"* (al-Taghābun 64/16).

**And the particular thing about al-Muzzammil is this:** here the principle is not declared as a rule; it is **applied**. A ruling is laid down, then readjusted within the same sūra.

That is, the text does not describe the principle — it shows it.

---

### Fifth part: four commands

وَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَأَقْرِضُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا

After the lightening, new commands come. And this shows what the lightening is not: **not the removal of the load but the change of its place.**

### أَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ

The concept of **iqāma** was treated in `002-bakara.md` at 2/3, and the distinction recorded there was this: *adāʾ* is the discharging of a debt — it is done and finished; *iqāma* is keeping something standing — it requires continuity and upkeep.

**And there is a weaving of words here:** the sūra had begun with **قُمِ** ("rise", 73/2). Now it is bound up with **أَقِيمُوا۟** ("keep standing"). The same root, at the two ends of the sūra.

| Place | Word | Who | What |
|---|---|---|---|
| 73/2 | **قُمِ** | Singular — the Prophet | The night |
| 73/20 | **أَقِيمُوا۟** | Plural — everyone | The prayer |

The first is the rising of the body, the second the keeping standing of a structure. And the first is to a single person, the second to a community.

**That is, the sūra opens with one person rising in the night and closes with a community keeping up the prayer.** I record this weaving as my own reading.

### وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ

**Root: ز-ك-و.** Its concrete sense: **to increase, to grow, to be blessed with growth**; and from here **to be purified, to be cleansed.**

It was taken up in `080-abese.md` in the context of *yazzakkā*; the root was also treated in `091-sems.md` and `092-leyl.md`.

**The link between the two senses of the root is striking:** to increase and to be purified. The explanation of the lexicographers: when a thing is cleared of its excess it grows soundly — like a pruned tree.

And *zakāt* carries both senses at once: the taking out of a part of one's wealth is named both a purifying and a making to grow.

### وَأَقْرِضُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا — "lend God a beautiful loan"

**The boldest expression in the sūra.**

**Root: ق-ر-ض.** And its concrete sense is unexpectedly sharp: **to cut, to cut with shears, to snap off.**

- **مِقْرَاض (*miqrāḍ*)** — shears.
- **قَرَضَ (*qaraḍa*)** — he cut, he bit off. It is used of a mouse: it gnawed, it cut.
- **قَرْض (*qarḍ*)** — a loan, a debt.

**What is the link?** The explanation of the lexicographers is direct: **to lend is for a person to cut a piece off his own property and set it aside.** The piece cut off will come back, but for the moment it has been cut off.

The Turkish word **"kredi"** ("credit") is from another root; but the Arabic borrowings **"karz"** and **"karz-ı hasen"** alive in Turkish are from this one.

### The boldness of the expression

**"Lend God."**

At first sight this expression is strange, and its strangeness looks deliberate. A loan is what the one in need takes. The expression sets up the relation between creditor and debtor — and on the debtor's side stands God.

The Qurʾān uses this expression in many places: al-Baqara 2/245, al-Māʾida 5/12, al-Ḥadīd 57/11, 57/18, al-Taghābun 64/17, al-Muzzammil 73/20.

**And in the classical commentaries the function of the expression is explained thus:** it is a language of **encouragement** and **guarantee**. It states that what is given is not lost, that it is entered in the record, that it will come back.

**An observation, as my own reading.** What the expression does may be to **change the addressee** of the act of giving. What is given to the poor is not counted as given to the poor; the record is entered elsewhere. And this has a consequence: **the giver expects no return, no obligation, no thanks from the receiver** — because that is not the party to whom he is creditor.

I record this reading as an inference. But it fits the observation made in `107-maun.md`: there the reason for the orphan and the poor being chosen was recorded as *"they cannot give anything in return"*.

**And in the next sūra the exact opposite attitude will be censured:** *wa lā tamnun tastakthir* — "do not give in order to get more" (74/6). The two sūras treat the two faces of the act of giving.

### حَسَنًا — "beautiful"

*Qarḍan ḥasanā* — "a beautiful loan."

In the tenth verse there was *hajran **jamīlā***. Here it is *qarḍan **ḥasanā***.

**Two different words, both meaning "beautiful".** And both specify the **quality** of an act — not the act itself.

The lexicographers record that there is a fine difference in Arabic between *jamīl* and *ḥasan* (*jamīl* looks more to appearance, *ḥasan* more to fittingness and goodness), but the difference is not absolute and in use they overlap. I draw no ruling here from the two having been chosen separately.

In the classical commentaries what a "beautiful loan" is is explained thus: given with a willing heart, without reproach, without expectation of return, from lawful property. None of this is written in the verse; it is drawn from the context and from other verses of the Qurʾān.

### Sixth part: the close

وَمَا تُقَدِّمُوا۟ لِأَنفُسِكُم مِّنْ خَيْرٍ تَجِدُوهُ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ هُوَ خَيْرًا وَأَعْظَمَ أَجْرًا ۚ وَٱسْتَغْفِرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ ۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌۢ

### تُقَدِّمُوا۟ — to send ahead

**Root: ق-د-م.** To go in front, to advance. *Qadam* (foot), *qadīm* (that which comes before), *taqdīm* (bringing forward), *muqaddima*.

**تُقَدِّمُوا۟** — Form II: to send in front, to send on ahead.

**The picture is clear:** a person sends his baggage on ahead before setting out on a journey. The verse describes good deeds like that baggage.

The Qurʾān uses this pattern in many places: *"Every soul will know what it has sent ahead and what it has left behind"* (al-Infiṭār 82/5); *"On that day the human being will be told what he sent ahead and what he left behind"* (al-Qiyāma 75/13); *"Let everyone look to what he has sent ahead for tomorrow"* (al-Ḥashr 59/18). The pattern was treated in `082-infitar.md`.

### لِأَنفُسِكُم — "for yourselves"

A point of word order.

The verse does not say "send for God's sake". **It says "send ahead for yourselves".**

The act of giving is named as a transaction in the giver's own favour. And this must be read together with the expression "lend God" in the preceding sentence: the loan is given to God, the benefit returns to the giver.

### هُوَ خَيْرًا وَأَعْظَمَ أَجْرًا

*"You will find it with God — **better and greater in reward**."*

Two elatives: *khayran* (better) and *aʿẓama ajrā* (greater in reward).

**And when it says "better", better than what?** The verse does not say. The classical explanation: better than the thing given itself. That is, what comes back is more than what was sent.

This fits the principle of multiplication treated in `002-bakara.md` at 2/261 (even if that verse falls outside the scope of this file, the pattern is known); I do not go into its details here.

### وَٱسْتَغْفِرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ — the last command of the sūra

**Root: غ-ف-ر.** Its concrete sense: **to cover, to close over, to overlay.**

- **مِغْفَر (*mighfar*)** — the iron helmet worn on the head in war. The Turkish *miğfer* is this word.
- **غُفْرَان (*ghufrān*)** — covering, forgiving.
- **اسْتَغْفَرَ (*istaghfara*)** — Form X: **to ask** for forgiveness.

**And the concrete sense of the root says something here.**

The sūra began by calling to a man who was **covered**: *al-muzzammil*. And it ends by **asking to be covered**: *istaghfirū*.

In both there is a covering. But its direction and its source differ:

| | The beginning of the sūra | The end of the sūra |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **تَزَمَّلَ** — he wrapped himself | **ٱسْتَغْفَرَ** — he asked to be covered |
| Who does it | The person himself | The person asks, another does it |
| What is covered | The body | The fault |
| Outcome | It is lifted by a command | It is asked for by a command |

**The sūra takes the person out of covering himself and brings him to asking to be covered.**

I record this reading as my inference. I claim no kinship between the two roots (ز-م-ل and غ-ف-ر) — the roots are entirely separate; the link made is conceptual and it is my own classification.

### The position of the last command

And one more observation.

The verse lightened the ruling. It made the measure flexible. It accepted the excuses. It gave new and more practicable commands.

**And as the last command it placed the asking of forgiveness.**

That is, the easing does not end with "now everything is in order". It is assumed that even the eased state cannot be lived without shortfall.

I record this as an observation; the verse does not make this justification.

### إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

The last sentence of the sūra and — as recorded above — the place where the sound structure of the sūra breaks.

The *-īlā* sound that ran through nineteen verses is let go here. The sūra ends with one of the most widespread closing patterns of the Qurʾān.

**Both attributes are in the intensive form:** *ghafūr* (the *faʿūl* pattern — much forgiving), *raḥīm* (the *faʿīl* pattern).

And the difference between *raḥmān* and *raḥīm* was treated in `001-fatiha.md`; I do not repeat it here.

## The sūra as a whole

### The flow of the argument

The sūra follows this path, step by step:

1. **The call** (1) — the addressee is named by the state he is in: covered.
2. **The command** (2-4) — rise, divide the night by a measure, recite with measure. As the command is given, a limit is set inside it.
3. **The ground** (5) — a heavy word is coming; the preparation comes before it.
4. **The ground of the ground** (6-7) — the night is ground that can be trodden, the day is a current.
5. **The turning** (8-9) — mention the name, cut yourself off and turn, take Him as trustee.
6. **The stance towards the addressees** (10-11) — be patient, part beautifully, leave the reckoning to Me.
7. **The content of the reckoning** (12-14) — the recompense of the work handed over is shown.
8. **The change of address** (15-18) — now the other side is spoken to; a historical example and the description of a day.
9. **The door** (19) — this is a reminder; whoever wills takes a path.
10. **The adjustment** (20) — the ruling is rearranged according to the state of the human being.

**And the most striking thing is the distance between the first step and the tenth.** The sūra begins by asking for most of the night; it ends by saying "recite what is easy for you".

### The sūra's adjustment of itself

This is perhaps the most original side of al-Muzzammil.

**A text loosens the ruling it has laid down, within itself.** And it does this three times:

| Place | What is done |
|---|---|
| 73/2 | An exception is added as the command is given: *illā qalīlā* |
| 73/3-4 | The measure is turned into a range: half of it, a little less, a little more |
| 73/20 | The measure is removed altogether and tied to the measure of "ease" |

That is, the lightening is not an exception that comes at the end of the sūra; it is **a manner that has been at work in the sūra from the start.**

This observation is my inference; but all three places stand openly in the text.

### The weaving of words — in a single table

The roots that return within the sūra:

| Root | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **ق-و-م** | 73/2 (*qum*), 73/6 (*aqwam*), 73/20 (*taqūmu*, *aqīmū*) | To rise → uprightness → to keep standing |
| **ق-ل-ل** | 73/2, 73/3, 73/11 | The little of the night → the little taken off the measure → the little granted to the enemy |
| **ق-و-ل** | 73/5 (*qawlan*), 73/6 (*qīlā*), 73/10 (*yaqūlūn*) | The word that comes → the word that is spoken → the words that are said |
| **أ-خ-ذ** | 73/9 (*ittakhidhhu*), 73/16 (*akhadhnāhu*, *akhdhan*), 73/19 (*ittakhadha*) | To take → to seize → to take |
| **ه-ج-ر** | 73/10 | (it will recur at al-Muddaththir 74/5) |
| **ذ-ك-ر** | 73/8 (*udhkur*), 73/19 (*tadhkira*) | The command to mention → the text's own name |
| **ي-س-ر** | 73/20 (*tayassara*, twice) | The name of the measure |
| **ق-د-ر** | 73/20 (*yuqaddiru*), 73/20 (*tuqaddimū* — a different root) | The one who measures |
| **ر-س-ل** | 73/15 (three times), 73/16 | Messengership |

### Verse-ending and sound — a closing note

As recorded above, nineteen verses advance on a single *-īlā* sound and it is let go in the twentieth verse.

An added observation: this sound is **long and open**. A vowel that does not close, that runs on. And the subject of the sūra is exactly a work that requires continuity: rising every night.

I record this as an observation about the sound; I do not claim it is a deliberate move of the text.

### The link between the two sūras

The comparison between al-Muzzammil and al-Muddaththir will be made as a whole at the end of `074-muddessir.md`. Here I only set out the concrete linguistic data of the link:

| Element | al-Muzzammil | al-Muddaththir |
|---|---|---|
| Opening pattern | *yā ayyuha'l-muzzammil* (73/1) | *yā ayyuha'l-muddaththir* (74/1) |
| Structure of the word | *al-mutazammil* → assimilation | *al-mutadaththir* → assimilation |
| First command | **قُمِ** ٱلَّيْلَ (73/2) | **قُمْ** فَأَنذِرْ (74/2) |
| The root ه-ج-ر | *uhjurhum hajran jamīlā* (73/10) | *wa'l-rujza fa'hjur* (74/5) |
| The root ص-ب-ر | *wa'ṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūn* (73/10) | *wa li-rabbika fa'ṣbir* (74/7) |
| The pattern ذ-ر-ن-ي | *wa **dharnī** wa'l-mukadhdhibīn* (73/11) | ***dharnī** wa man khalaqtu waḥīdā* (74/11) |
| Closing pattern | *inna hādhihī **tadhkira**… fa-**man shāʾa*** (73/19) | *innahū **tadhkira**… fa-**man shāʾa*** (74/54-55) |

**Seven separate overlaps.** Some of them (the opening pattern, *dharnī*, the close) are very marked; some (the return of the same roots in nearby positions) are more indirect. I do not claim that all of them are the product of a deliberate design; what I record is the overlap itself.

## What this says to the present

### First: capacity is built before the need

This is the most concrete structural observation of the sūra.

The fifth verse says *"We shall soon lay a heavy word upon you"* — future tense. And the command was given **before** it.

So the sequence is: **preparation → load.** One does not begin to prepare when the load arrives.

This has an observable counterpart, and its field is wide. When the weight of a task appears, the structure that will bear it must most often already have been built; a structure built at the moment of crisis is buried under the crisis. This is not a principle drawn from the verse; it is an observable present-day counterpart of the sequence the verse sets up.

**And the form of preparation the verse proposes is striking:** not a technique, not an exercise, not a plan. **A regular and slow reading.**

### Second: the measure is set as a range

The second and third verses do not give a figure; they give a range — and they place a restriction on the lower bound of the range and leave the upper open.

What this says to the present has to do with how goals are set.

A goal that sets a single figure has two outcomes: when the figure is met the work ends, when it is not met the work collapses. Both break continuity.

A goal set as a range, by contrast, leaves room for a capacity that changes from day to day. On a bad day the person comes down to the lower bound, on a good day he goes above — and **in neither case has he abandoned the work.**

I write this reading as my own inference; the verse does not teach a method of goal-setting.

### Third: the day as a "swim"

The comparison in the seventh verse works directly for the present.

The day is described not as the time when "there is much work" but as the time when **you are dragged along**. And these two descriptions produce different conclusions:

- If there is much work, the solution is better planning.
- If you are being dragged, the solution is not a plan but **ground**.

There is a widespread discussion today about how attention is scattered, and at the centre of the discussion is this: to look not at what a person does but at what is dragging him.

Does the verse say this? No. The verse qualifies the day as "a long swim"; the idea the comparison carries is being dragged. **I make the connection to the present as an interpretation, not as a claim of the verse.**

And what the verse sets over against it is not the correcting of the day: **the building, at another time, of ground on which the foot stands.**

### Fourth: "a beautiful parting"

The tenth verse sets a measure for how a relation is to be cut. And as was seen above, the Qurʾān uses the attribute *jamīl* almost only in situations of this kind: patience, pardon, divorce, leaving.

**What this says to the present is this:** the decision to part and the manner of parting are two separate matters.

To decide that a relation cannot be continued is one thing; what is done in ending that relation is another. And the second does not change the rightness of the first, but it changes the person.

The verse does not forbid parting. It prevents the parting from being turned into an instrument of punishment.

And a note: this is not a general rule of courtesy like "every parting should be gracious". The verse says this to someone who has been **asked to be patient** — that is, to the injured party. The measure is placed on the one who has been wronged, not on the one who holds the power. This distinction has to be recorded, because when the rule is applied to the wrong side it does harm.

### Fifth: the legitimacy of an excuse

This is the most practical side of the twentieth verse.

The verse enumerates three excuses: sickness, the journey for a livelihood, war. And it ties all three to the same conclusion, in the same list.

**What this says to the present has to do with the relation of religious life to the feeling of "sufficiency".**

That a person cannot meet the measure of worship can be read in two ways: as a fault, or as a condition. The first reading keeps the person in a constant feeling of indebtedness — and that feeling has a known consequence: giving up what could be done as well. The sentence "I can't do it properly anyway" most often ends in doing nothing at all.

The verse closes off the first reading. And note: in closing it off, it does not content itself with putting the person at ease, it **sets a practicable measure.** "Recite what is easy for you."

**And a note is obligatory:** this does not mean that the measure is left to the person. The verse does not remove the command; it makes its measure flexible. To abandon the act altogether by saying "it does not come easy to me" is not a licence the verse gives.

The distinction is here: the verse leaves the **amount** to the person, not the **act**.

### Sixth: trade being in the list of excuses

This was treated above; what it says to the present must be recorded separately.

The verse accepts the pursuit of a livelihood as an excuse — in the same sentence as sickness and war.

**What this means is that work is not constituted as a field outside religious life.** The working person is not counted as living a life "stolen" from worship.

The opposite construction — the splitting of life in two, into worship and "the rest" — cannot be built on this verse. And the observation recorded in `107-maun.md` looks in the same direction: *"The sūra does not set worship against morality; it says that worship cannot remain immoral."* Something similar holds here: the text does not set worship against work.

**But there is a limit and it must be stated:** the verse counts work as an excuse — not as a virtue. The two are not the same thing. An excuse lightens the measure; it does not take its place.

### Seventh: the ruling being loosened by the one who laid it down

A closing observation.

In the sūra there is no one asking for the lightening. No one says he is strained, no one asks for an exemption. On the contrary: the verse speaks of people carrying out the command in excess.

**And the lightening comes all the same.**

What this says to the present has to do with how a rule-maker looks at his own rule. To watch whether a rule can be applied is the work of the one who laid it down; and if it cannot be applied, to review the rule is not a weakness but the sign of a working order.

I do not draw this from the verse as a principle of administration. The verse gives no doctrine of administration. What I record is that the operation the text performs within itself has an observable structure.
