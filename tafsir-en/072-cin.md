# 72. Sūrat al-Jinn

Twenty-eight verses. On its having come down in Mecca no divergence is seen among the sources.

The sūra does something of which there are few other instances in the Qurʾān: **it gives the greater part of the narration to the mouth of a community that is not the Prophet's addressee.** Throughout the first half of the sūra the speaker is not Muḥammad, nor is it God's direct address; it is **a conversation a group of jinn held among themselves.** And this conversation was not made audible to the Prophet on the spot; it was **notified to him afterwards.**

These two layers settle the whole architecture of the sūra.

## What is the sūra doing?

**First layer (1-15): a report.** A small community of the jinn listened to the Qurʾān and went back to their own people to relate what they had heard. The sūra transmits this account as it stands. The sentences are strung one after another on the pattern `wa annahu` / `wa annā` ("and that…", "and that we…"); across thirteen verses the same conjunction is repeated. This is the rhythm of a deposition, or of a statement written out point by point: *and that… and that… and that…*

The content of the report is not merely a profession of faith. The jinn say the following: what they believed (2-3), the errors of their own community (4-5), a corrupt relationship established between them and human beings (6), a mistaken opinion they had shared (7), an experience concerning the heaven (8-9), **what they do not know** (10), the divisions among themselves (11, 14), that they cannot escape (12), what faith has gained them (13). The text, that is, is **a self-critical statement** made by a group of beings about their own condition.

**Second layer (16-28): the voice of the revelation itself.** The report ends, the speaker changes. First a rule is given (16-17: uprightness and abundance), then a ruling (18: the places of prostration belong to God), then a scene (19). Then `qul` — "say" — comes four times over and makes the Prophet declare his own position (20-25). The sūra closes with the question of to whom the unseen is opened (26-28).

**The two layers have a single common concern: the source and the limit of knowledge.** The jinn say they heard something; they say that the ways of going up to heaven and gathering knowledge have been closed; they say they do not know the unseen. In the second half the Prophet says the same thing: I can neither harm nor benefit; I do not know when what is promised will come. And the last verses lay down the ruling: only God knows the unseen, and He gives of it only to a messenger He has chosen, and only through a guarded channel.

The sūra, then, is from beginning to end about **who knows what**. This also sets in place why it speaks of the jinn: the jinn were beings counted, in pre-Islamic Arabia, as a source of hidden knowledge. The sūra makes them speak, and one of the first things they say is: *we do not know.*

## The name of the sūra

The sūra takes its name from the word **الجن** in the first verse. I have not seen a widespread record in the sources of its being called by another name; its being called "Qul ūḥiya" (its opening words) is natural enough, but the settled name is al-Jinn.

## One of the sūras that open with "Qul"

Five sūras in the Qurʾān begin with this command: **al-Jinn (72), al-Kāfirūn (109), al-Ikhlāṣ (112), al-Falaq (113), al-Nās (114).** This shared feature was taken up in the sections on al-Ikhlāṣ and al-Falaq; the finding there was this: all of these sūras **state a position**, and that the one stating the position did not take the word from himself is registered by the first word.

Sūrat al-Jinn adds something to this list. In the other four "qul" comes once, at the head. Here it comes **five times**: 1, 20, 21, 22, 25. That is, the sūra does not state a position once and pass on; it declares the position again and again, from different sides. And what is striking is this: four of these five "quls" make the Prophet say **what he does not have in his hand.**

## The frame of revelation: what we know, and from where

In this sūra too — as in Sūrat al-Fīl — a clear line has to be drawn between what the Qurʾān says and what the reports relate. For the greater part of the details circulating around this sūra are not in the text.

### What the text of the Qurʾān says

- A revelation came to the Prophet.
- The content of the revelation: **a company** (*nafar*) of the jinn listened to the Qurʾān.
- After listening they spoke among themselves and said the following (2-14).
- That is all.

**What the Qurʾān does not say:** Where the event took place is not said. When it took place is not said. The number of the jinn is not given (*nafar* conveys an indeterminate fewness). Where they came from is not said. Whether the Prophet saw them or did not is not stated plainly — but the expression *"it was revealed to me"* shows the source of the knowledge to be revelation; one does not expect "it was revealed to me" to be said of something observed on the spot. This is one of the finest points of the sūra and I shall take it up separately at the first verse.

### A second account within the Qurʾān: al-Aḥqāf 46/29-32

The Qurʾān relates the same event — or a similar one — once more:

> *"And when We turned towards you a company of the jinn, to listen to the Qurʾān. When they came into its presence they said, 'be silent and listen.' When it was finished they went back to their people as warners. They said: 'O our people! We have listened to a book sent down after Moses, confirming what came before it, guiding to the truth and to a straight road…'"* (al-Aḥqāf 46/29-31, in sense)

The difference between the two accounts is decisive for exegesis:

| | **al-Jinn 72/1-15** | **al-Aḥqāf 46/29-32** |
|---|---|---|
| Narrator | A revelation notified to the Prophet: "it was revealed to me that…" | Direct narration: "and when We turned towards you…" |
| Who is addressed | The Prophet, then everyone | The Prophet |
| To whom the jinn speak | Among themselves / to their people | To their people ("O our people") |
| The emphasis | What the jinn said **about themselves** | What the jinn said **about the book** |
| Earlier book named | None | The one sent down to Moses |

In al-Aḥqāf it is said plainly that the event was **directed**: *ṣarafnā* — We turned them. That is, the jinn's coming there is not chance. In Sūrat al-Jinn this information is absent; there it is only said that they listened.

Whether the two passages relate the same event or two separate events has been disputed in classical exegesis and has not been settled. Nor do I settle it. This much is enough to say: **the Qurʾān records the jinn's listening to the Qurʾān not once but twice, and both records yield the same result — those who listened believed, and went back to their people and told them.**

### What the reports relate

In the biographical and exegetical sources this event is related in connection with a journey the Prophet made outside Mecca. The widespread account is this: after the Prophet had gone to Ṭāʾif and returned without finding a response, while he was reciting the Qurʾān at the night prayer at a place called **Nakhla** on the Meccan road, a group of the jinn listened to him.

I transmit this **as a report**. At the level of detail the sources diverge from one another: different numbers are given for the jinn, different names are mentioned for the place they came from, and there are divergences over whether the event fell on the return from Ṭāʾif or on some other journey. None of these details is in the text of the Qurʾān, and none is of any use for exegesis — the argument of the sūra does not rest on the geography of the event.

There is a further cluster of reports: the question of whether the Prophet met with the jinn separately and whether he recited the Qurʾān to them. On this matter differing accounts are found in the hadith sources, and differing statements are transmitted among the Companions as well. Since I cannot make a classification I am sure of, I issue no ruling here; I record only this: **the first verse of Sūrat al-Jinn speaks, as regards the event related in this sūra, not of a meeting but of a notification.**

### The Meccan setting of the sūra

The historical ground of the sūra is set up by the text itself:

- **The practice of seeking refuge with the jinn existed** (verse 6) — the sūra names it in the past tense, as a known custom.
- **Knowledge of the unseen was ascribed to the jinn** (verses 8-10 speak to this).
- **Ascribing offspring to God was widespread** (verse 3) — that the angels were counted God's daughters in Mecca the Qurʾān records plainly elsewhere.
- **There was a mass of listeners gathering around the Prophet and this was causing discomfort** (verse 19).
- **The addressees were using the argument "we are more numerous"** (this is what verse 24 answers).

All of this fits the early Meccan period. Without needing the individual reports transmitted as occasions of revelation, the setting of the sūra's addressees can be read out of the text itself.

## What the jinn are — what the Qurʾān says and does not say

Everyone reading this sūra will stop at one point: what, then, are these beings? Before giving the answer to the question, one has to settle **where the answer will come from**; for on this subject the difference between what the Qurʾān says and what the culture has accumulated is greater than on almost any other.

### What the Qurʾān gives

**1. They are created, and their matter is fire.** *"And the jānn We created before, from a smokeless fire"* (al-Ḥijr 15/27). *"And He created the jānn from fire, from a smokeless flame"* (al-Raḥmān 55/15). In both verses they are named side by side with the human being's creation from earth. The jinn are therefore not the opposite of the human being but its **parallel**: two separate accountable kinds from two separate substances.

**2. They are accountable.** *"I did not create the jinn and human beings except to serve Me"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/56). That is to say they bear responsibility; and responsibility requires the possibility of choice.

**3. Among them there are believers and deniers alike.** Verses 11 and 14 of this sūra say so directly. "Jinn" does not mean an evil being; it is the name of a **kind**, not a moral attribute. This distinction has to be underlined, because in everyday English the word is most often rendered "demon", which is almost wholly negative in colouring. The Qurʾān's usage is not like this.

**4. Messengers came to them too.** *"O company of jinn and human beings! Did there not come to you messengers from among you, relating My signs to you?"* (al-Anʿām 6/130).

**5. They do not know the unseen.** Verse 10 of this sūra says so from the jinn's own mouth. The Qurʾān sets up the same ruling with a scene in the story of Solomon as well: *"Nothing indicated his death to them but a creature of the earth eating his staff… When he fell, the jinn realised that had they known the unseen they would not have remained in that humiliating torment"* (Sabaʾ 34/14).

**6. They are not seen — at least not under ordinary conditions.** *"He and his tribe see you from where you do not see them"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/27).

**7. They have been made into gods by human beings.** *"And they made the jinn partners with God"* (al-Anʿām 6/100). *"They were serving the jinn"* (Sabaʾ 34/41 — the angels say this). *"And they set up a lineage between Him and the jinn"* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/158). This is the background of verses 3 and 6 of Sūrat al-Jinn.

### The root of the word

The root **ج-ن-ن** was analysed in full in **`002-bakara.md`**, in the discussion of the word *janna* at 2/25. It was shown there that the concrete sense of the root is **to cover, to conceal**, and that the whole of its derivatives turn about invisibility: *janīn* (what is covered in the womb), *majnūn* (whose mind is covered), *mijann* (the shield one hides behind), *janna* (the garden whose trees cover the ground), *jinn*. I do not repeat that analysis here.

The one thing to be added here is the root's particular working in the word **jinn**.

The point to notice is this: the word *jinn* loads the sense of the root **not onto the being but onto perception**. That is, the word does not say "hidden", it says "unseen". The difference matters. To hide is an act, it has an agent, it requires intent. Not to be seen, however, is **a state of relation**: what one person cannot see may be visible to another. The word speaks not about the being itself but about **the distance between it and the human being**.

This completes the contrast set up in **`114-nas.md`**. It was shown there that the last two words of that sūra (*al-jinnati wa'n-nās*) set up an exact opposition: in the root of *nās* there is (on one view) visibility and familiarity, in the root of *jinn* there is coveredness. The two names the Qurʾān chose for human beings and jinn, then, name the two kinds **by their positions relative to us**: with the one we form a familiarity, the other we cannot see.

From here a simple but easily missed conclusion follows: **the word "jinn" is not a definition but a marker of a boundary.** The name itself says, "you have no data about this." To name a being "the unseen one" is to close, from the very start, the channel of knowledge about it.

### What the Qurʾān does not give

Now the other half of the list — and this half is more important for exegesis than the first.

The Qurʾān does not speak of the jinn's **shapes**. It does not speak of their **number**. It does not speak of **where they live**. It does not speak of **how they are organised**, **what they eat**, **how long they live**. It does not describe **by what routes** contact between them and human beings is made.

This silence is not chance. If a text keeps its subject this limited, the limit itself is a piece of information.

From this the following measure has to be drawn: **the accounts of the jinn from outside the Qurʾān — the amulets, the numbers, the descriptions, the classifications, the practices — are the material of the culture, not of the text.** That they take different forms in different geographies shows this too. In this text I do not enter that field — neither to transmit nor to assess; because I have no instrument to measure with and the Qurʾān does not speak on the matter.

The sūra itself is a good example of this. Twenty-odd sentences are transmitted from the mouths of the jinn, and not one of those sentences relates *what kind* of beings the jinn are. All of them are about **what they believe, what they do, and what they do not know.** Even the speaking party does not describe its own nature.

## The grammatical spine of the sūra: the "wa annahu" chain

Before moving to the verses the grammatical skeleton of the sūra has to be set up, because this skeleton affects the sense directly.

In the first verse the word commanded to the Prophet is this: *"Say: **it has been revealed to me that** a company of the jinn listened…"* In Arabic there is **أَنَّهُ** (annahu) here, and this is the element stating **what was revealed** by the verb "was revealed". That is: *what was revealed is that…*

Then the jinn's speech begins: *fa-qālū **innā** samiʿnā* — "they said: **we** have listened". The hamza here takes the *kasra* (*innā*), because in Arabic a clause coming after the verb *qāla* is direct speech and takes the *kasra*.

And from this point on, from verse 3 to verse 14, every verse opens with the same pattern: **وَأَنَّهُ** / **وَأَنَّا**. Twelve verses, with the same conjunction.

### The real question here: what do these "wa anna"s attach to?

This is the most disputed grammatical question of the sūra, and there are two main answers:

| View | Reading | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. They attach to the first *annahu*** | "It has been revealed to me that… and it has been revealed that… and that…" | All the items are parts of **the knowledge revealed to the Prophet**. The jinn's speech is transmitted as the content of the revelation. |
| **2. They attach to the jinn's speech** | "They said: we listened… and [we believed] that… and that…" | The items are parts of **the jinn's own statement**; they attach to the verb *āmannā bih* (we believed in it) in verse 2: "and we believed that…" |

Both readings have a ground, and both have been defended in the classical commentaries.

**A variant reading hangs on this dispute.** It is transmitted that in some readings these hamzas are read with the **fatḥa** (*anna*) and in others with the **kasra** (*inna*). The fatḥa reading fits the first view, the kasra reading the second: the kasra turns the clause into direct speech and leaves the items in the mouths of the jinn. Which of the imams of the readings took which road I do not write here with names, since I am not sure of it.

**There is an argument the grammarians advance, and it deserves consideration:** no divergence is transmitted over the hamzas in verses 18 and 19 being read with the fatḥa. And those two verses **cannot** be the jinn's speech: verse 18 addresses human beings, saying *"so do not call upon anyone along with God"*, and verse 19 relates from outside the crowd gathering around the Prophet. So at the least those two items are parts not of the jinn's statement but of **the revelation's own sentences** — and are therefore attached to the *annahu* of the first verse.

The reasonable conclusion drawn from this is the following, and I state it as a preference with the note that it is not binding: **part of the chain transmits the jinn's speech and part is the revelation's own sentence; and the transition between the two is somewhere between verses 15 and 16.** The text itself draws no sharp boundary. This blurring is not a defect; it is part of what the sūra does — where the jinn speak truly, their word and the word of the revelation give the same ruling, and the text is in no hurry to separate them.

### Why so much repetition?

*Wa annahu… wa annā… wa annahum… wa annā…* — twelve times. Repetition of a conjunction at this density is not ordinary in Arabic; normally after the second item the conjunction is dropped and the list flows.

The function of the repetition is this: **each item is registered separately.** This is not an ordinary narration but a **record of a statement**. The *anna* placed at the head of each sentence makes that sentence an independent item — just as an utterance is numbered. The sūra gives what the jinn said not as a story but as a **record**.

And this acquires its sense when joined with the end of the sūra. The last verse ends *"and He has counted all things by number."* A sūra ending with a sentence of counting begins with counted items.

## 72/1 — قُلْ أُوحِيَ إِلَيَّ أَنَّهُ اسْتَمَعَ نَفَرٌ مِّنَ الْجِنِّ فَقَالُوا إِنَّا سَمِعْنَا قُرْآنًا عَجَبًا

*Qul ūḥiya ilayya annahu'stamaʿa nafarun mina'l-jinni fa-qālū innā samiʿnā qurʾānan ʿajabā*

"Say: It has been revealed to me that a company of the jinn listened, and they said: 'We have listened to an astonishing recitation.'"

### قُلْ — "Say"

The first word of the sūra was treated in detail in the section on al-Ikhlāṣ. The finding there was, in brief: "qul" carves the source of the word into the text — this is not the Prophet's own word but a word given to him and commanded to be conveyed.

Here this function is more critical still. For what is to be transmitted is **an unverifiable report.** A conversation nobody heard, held by beings nobody can see, is being related. The one support of such a report is its source. "Qul" stands at the head for exactly this reason: the report rests not on the narrator's observation but on the notification that came to him.

### أُوحِيَ — "it was revealed": the work the passive voice does

The finest point of the sūra is here, and it is generally passed over.

The verb is **passive**: *ūḥiya* — it was revealed. Its agent is not stated (in the Qurʾān the agent of revelation is known in any case; the passive use conveys honouring and is widespread in the Qurʾān).

The real matter is the verb **itself**. The verse says none of the following:

- "A company of the jinn **came to me**"
- "I **saw** a company of the jinn"
- "**I noticed** a company of the jinn listening to me"

The verse says: **"It has been revealed to me that a company of the jinn listened."**

That is, the Prophet learns **afterwards** that a text he recited was listened to. He did not notice the listener while reciting; the knowledge that the event took place came to him through a separate channel.

This has three consequences:

**First:** the Prophet's source of knowledge is not an extraordinary faculty of sight but **revelation.** The sūra does not present prophethood as a kind of hidden capacity to see. Indeed the same ruling will become a general rule at the end of the sūra: *"He does not give anyone access to His unseen, except a messenger with whom He is well pleased"* (26-27). The Prophet does not know the unseen; he knows **what is notified to him** of the unseen. This sūra gives both the instance of that principle (verse 1) and its rule (26-27) together.

**Second:** this places the "extraordinariness" of the event in the right spot. What is extraordinary is not the coming of the jinn — they exist in any case. What is extraordinary is **the coming of the report.**

**Third — and this is an interpretation:** the Prophet was reciting without knowing who his listener was. When the text was being recited, its target audience had not been determined. This accords with something the Qurʾān says about itself: with its claim to be *"a warning to all the worlds"*. Who a warning reaches is not determined by the warner.

### إِلَيَّ — "to me"

A small detail: the addressee of the revelation is **singular** and in the **first person**. The sentence is not a testimony but a conveyance: *it was notified to me, I am passing it on to you.* The chain has two links, and both links are plainly shown.

### اسْتَمَعَ — "gave ear"

The root **س-م-ع**: to hear.

The verb here is not in the simple form (*samiʿa*) but in the **istifʿāl** form (*istamaʿa*). This form in Arabic generally conveys **wanting / seeking**; with the verb of hearing it conveys **intent and attention**.

The difference is this:

- **سمع (samiʿa)** — to hear. Sound reaching the ear. It requires no will; one hears without meaning to.
- **استمع (istamaʿa)** — to give ear, to listen. To stop in order to take in the sound, to turn one's attention there.

The same distinction as the English "to hear / to listen". And the distinction is set up deliberately here, because **both forms occur within the same verse**: the jinn *istamaʿa* (gave ear), then they themselves say *samiʿnā* (we heard). The first verb describes the act, the second the result.

The Qurʾān uses this form as a command too: *"When the Qurʾān is recited, give ear to it and be silent"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/204). *Istimāʿ*, then, is the manner of listening the Qurʾān asks for itself. What the jinn did is exactly what is commanded — and they did it without being commanded.

Note also: the verb **returns in verse 9** — *fa-man **yastamiʿi**'l-āna* ("whoever now gives ear"). There the same verb will relate the giving of ear to the heaven and the stealing of knowledge. The sūra uses the same root for two different kinds of listening: one permitted, one not. The first leads to guidance, the second meets a flame.

### نَفَرٌ — "a company"

The root **ن-ف-ر**: to leave one's place, to move swiftly towards a task, to take fright and flee. From the same root come *nafīr* (the call to mobilise), *istinfār* (setting in motion), *tanfīr* (frightening off, putting off). The command *"infirū"* (set out) and the patterns *"lā tanfirū"* in the Qurʾān are from this root.

**Nafar**, in the classical lexicons, conveys a small group of people — a company generally counted between three and ten. The word therefore conveys both **fewness** and **being in motion**: a small group that has risen from somewhere and come.

**No number is given.** Different numbers are transmitted in the reports; they do not agree with one another and have no counterpart in the verse. The word itself already says "few"; exact numerical information is not within the sūra's field of interest.

And there is this too: the sūra closes with the matter of **numbers** — *"whose helper is weaker and whose number fewer"* (24). And the Qurʾān records this sentence from the mouth of someone boasting of numbers in Mecca: *"I am richer than you in wealth and mightier **in company**"* (al-Kahf 18/34). The same word. The sūra opens with a *nafar* — a small group — hearing and believing, and closes with the refutation of a claim to superiority in numbers.

### فَقَالُوا — "and they said"

The **fāʾ** at the head conveys "immediately after". No interval, no stage of thinking, no moment of hesitation between the listening and the speaking is related. They listened — and they spoke.

And note: the word is not said to the Prophet. The jinn are speaking among themselves. This settles the sūra's mode of transmission: the text is not a dialogue but **a conversation overheard** — and the ear that overheard it is the ear of the revelation.

### قُرْآنًا عَجَبًا — "an astonishing recitation"

**قُرْآنًا is indefinite.** They do not say "we listened to the Qurʾān"; they say "we listened to a *qurʾān*". There are two readings of this and both are meaningful:

- The lexical sense of the word is to the fore: *qurʾān* is a verbal noun from **ق-ر-أ**, and means "reciting, what is recited". The jinn may then be saying "we listened to a recited text".
- Or the indefiniteness conveys **magnification**: "such a recitation that…" In Arabic an indefinite can do this work; it was taken up in the discussion of the *tanwīn* of *aḥad* at al-Ikhlāṣ 112/1.

The adjective supports the second reading.

**عَجَبًا — ʿajab.** The root **ع-ج-ب**: a thing's rousing a reaction in a person because it falls outside the accustomed. The usual renderings are "astonishment, wonder", but the word's load is wider: *ʿajab* is what a person **cannot place within his own measures.**

Note the pattern: *ʿajab* is a **verbal noun**, not an adjective. In Arabic the use of a verbal noun in place of an adjective conveys **intensification** — not "an astonishing recitation" but almost "a recitation that is astonishment itself". What we do in English when we say "this whole thing is a disaster."

The relatives of the root open up the horizon: *ʿajūz* — an old woman (at Hūd 11/72 Abraham's (Ibrāhīm's) wife uses it of herself, and immediately after the angels say *"do you wonder at God's affair?"*, 11/73 — the same root in two verses). *ʿUjb* — self-admiration; a person's being taken with himself. *Aʿjaba* — it pleased him.

**The choice of the word says a great deal.** The jinn do not say they **liked** the text they heard, they say they were **struck** by it. Not praise, but a diagnosis: this does not fit the categories we know.

And this is **exactly the same word** as the Meccans' reaction. The Qurʾān records this from the mouths of its addressees: *"The deniers said: this is an astonishing thing (shayʾun **ʿajīb**)"* (Qāf 50/2), *"This is truly an astonishing thing (la-shayʾun **ʿujāb**)"* (Ṣād 38/5).

**Both parties use the same word, and then their roads part.** The jinn say "we listened to an astonishing recitation" and go on to say **"we believed in it"**. The Meccans say "an astonishing thing" and go on to reject it. Astonishment, then, produces no result of itself. The same reaction can lead onto two separate roads.

This is a measure set up in the first verse of the sūra and continuing to its end: **to hear, to understand and to accept are separate works.** The jinn did the three in succession; the addressees stopped at the first.

### What this says to the present

One detail is worth dwelling on: **the party that believed was not the text's target audience.**

The Qurʾān was coming down to a community that spoke Arabic, in their language, by way of their concerns. The jinn were not in that reckoning. Even so it was they who listened and responded; the actual addressees of the address largely rejected it.

This picture is part of a pattern the Qurʾān sets up in several places: a message finds its response not where it is most expected but where it was not reckoned with. Its present-day counterpart is plain enough: who a word will reach cannot be determined by the one who speaks it. A target audience is an instrument of estimation, not a boundary.

## 72/2 — يَهْدِي إِلَى الرُّشْدِ فَآمَنَّا بِهِ ۖ وَلَن نُّشْرِكَ بِرَبِّنَا أَحَدًا

*Yahdī ila'r-rushdi fa-āmannā bih, wa lan nushrika bi-Rabbinā aḥadā*

"It guides to right judgement; and we believed in it. We shall never associate anyone with our Lord."

### يَهْدِي — "it guides"

The verb is in the **imperfect**. In the preceding verse the listening was related in the *past* (*samiʿnā*); here the function of the text is given in the tense of *continuity*.

The difference is meaningful: the listening is a finished event, whereas guiding to right judgement is the text's **permanent attribute.** The jinn do not say "it guided us", they say "it guides". The finding they make about the text is not limited to their own experience.

### الرُّشْد — *rushd*

One of the key words of the sūra, and it occurs **four times**: *al-rushd* (2), *rashadā* (10), *rashadā* (14), *rashadā* (21). All four from the same root, three in the same pattern. The word is like a wire strung along the spine of the sūra.

**Root ر-ش-د.** Its concrete sense: to find what is right in a matter, to hit the mark, to come to maturity. The most tangible use of the root is legal: **a child's reaching the maturity to manage his own property.** The Qurʾān says this plainly: *"Test the orphans until they reach the age of marriage. If you perceive in them right judgement, hand over their property to them"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/6).

From here the core of the word becomes visible: **rushd is a person's capacity to conduct his own affairs rightly.**

### The difference between رشد and هدى

This distinction is necessary for understanding the sūra as a whole.

| | **هدى (hudā)** | **رشد (rushd)** |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete sense of the root | To show the way, to go ahead, to guide | To hit the mark, to mature, to conduct one's affairs rightly |
| Who does it | The one who shows the way — from outside | The one who finds the way — from inside |
| What it looks to | **The road** | **The one walking** |
| English rendering | Guidance, showing the right road | Sound sense, maturity, hitting the mark |

*Guidance* is something **given**; *rushd* is a **state.** The one is the guide's work, the other the attribute the traveller acquires.

The word order of the verse sets up exactly this relation: *yahdī ila'r-rushd* — **"it guides to rushd".** That is, guidance is the instrument of *rushd*; *rushd* is the destination of guidance. The text shows the way; where one goes is a person's becoming able to conduct his own affairs rightly.

This is a measured definition of the function of a religious text: the text does not decide in a person's stead, it makes him able to decide. The Qurʾān's saying *"right judgement has now become distinct from error"* (al-Baqara 2/256 — the word *rushd* occurs there too, set against *ghayy*) belongs in this frame.

The other occurrences of the root in the Qurʾān come out at the same place: *"Prepare for us right judgement in our affair"* (al-Kahf 18/10); *"Is there not among you a man of sound sense?"* (Hūd 11/78 — *rajulun rashīd*); *"You are indeed forbearing, of sound judgement"* (Hūd 11/87 — the mocking address of his people to Shuʿayb). And for the way of God: *"sabīla'r-rashād"* (Ghāfir 40/38).

### فَآمَنَّا بِهِ — "and we believed in it"

The **fāʾ** at the head again conveys "immediately after", and here it sets up a chain of reasoning: *it guides to rushd → therefore we believed.*

That is, the faith comes as the **result** of the finding. The jinn first say what the text does, then their own decision. Had the order been reversed — "we believed, because it guides to rushd" — the sense would have been the same but the rhythm different. As it stands, the sentence proceeds in the form of a **reasoning.**

And there is this: to what does the pronoun *bihī* refer? The nearest referent is *the Qurʾān*. What is believed in, then, is directly **the text.** The jinn say they believed not in a person but in a recitation — they had not seen the Prophet, did not know him, do not mention his name.

### وَلَن نُّشْرِكَ بِرَبِّنَا أَحَدًا — "we shall never associate anyone with our Lord"

**لَن (lan)** is in Arabic the strongest instrument for negating the future: *never*. Not simple negation (*lā*) but a definitive and comprehensive refusal.

This is the first undertaking that comes immediately after the faith. And note: **it is a negative sentence.** The jinn do not say "we shall serve", they say "we shall not associate". The first declaration is a declaration of **clearing away** — the same method as Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ.

**أَحَدًا — "anyone".** In the section on al-Ikhlāṣ the word's particular behaviour in Arabic was taken up: *aḥad* is overwhelmingly used in negative contexts, and there it means "no one, anyone at all".

This sūra displays exactly that **original** use of the word. *Aḥad* occurs **six times** in the sūra (2, 7, 18, 20, 22, 26) and **in all six in a negative sentence**, in the sense "no one". Sūrat al-Jinn, then, is a text showing the ordinary state of the word that is used exceptionally in the first verse of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. Read side by side the difference is clear: in al-Ikhlāṣ *aḥad* is **His attribute**; here *aḥad* is **what cannot be set against Him.**

Al-Ikhlāṣ 112/4 already brought the two uses together (*"lam yakun lahu kufuwan **aḥad**"*). Sūrat al-Jinn repeats that second use five times.

### Context

The first two verses together set up a testimony, and its order is this: **we heard → we were astonished → we saw what it does → we believed → we shall not associate.**

Five steps. None is skipped, none is abridged. A process the Qurʾān argues at length elsewhere is here fitted into two verses.

## 72/3 — وَأَنَّهُ تَعَالَىٰ جَدُّ رَبِّنَا مَا اتَّخَذَ صَاحِبَةً وَلَا وَلَدًا

*Wa annahu taʿālā jaddu Rabbinā ma'ttakhadha ṣāḥibatan wa lā waladā*

"And that: exalted is the majesty of our Lord; He has taken neither a consort nor a child."

### تَعَالَىٰ — "exalted is"

The root **ع-ل-و**: height. *Taʿālā* is in the **tafāʿul** form. This form normally conveys reciprocity (*taqātala* — they killed one another), but here it serves the function of **intensification and continuity**: He whose exaltedness is in Himself and is always.

The word's typical use in the Qurʾān is in places where some deficiency is ascribed to God: the pattern *"exalted is He; far above what they associate"* occurs many times. *Taʿālā*, then, is an elevation placed in front of a denial: **first He is raised up, then what belongs below is denied.**

### جَدُّ — "majesty"

The most disputed word of the verse.

**Root ج-د-د.** Its concrete sense is **to cut, to sever** — to separate a thing sharply. The derivatives of the root branch from this single image, and at first sight they look unrelated to one another:

- **jadīd** — new. (Newly cut, newly separated; the state of cloth just cut from the loom.)
- **jidd** — seriousness. (To cut off and discard a decision; to cut off hesitation. Its opposite is *hazl*, jest.)
- **jadd** — grandfather. (The point at which one stops at a given place in the chain of descent.)
- **judda** — a road, a line, a streak. It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"and in the mountains are streaks (*judadun*) white and red, of varying colours"* (Fāṭir 35/27).
- **jadd** — fortune, luck; and **greatness, majesty, loftiness.**

The word here is the sense in the last item. In the classical commentaries the explanations transmitted for *jadd* in this verse may be gathered thus:

| Explanation | Its emphasis |
|---|---|
| **Greatness, loftiness, majesty** | The most widespread explanation. "Exalted is the greatness of our Lord." |
| **Wealth, freedom from need (*ghinā*)** | From the word's sense of "fortune, wealth". It prepares the denial that follows: one who is not in need has no need of a child. |
| **Dominion, sovereignty** | The dimension of rule. |
| **Bounty, favour** | A less defended explanation. |

These explanations are less alternatives to one another than different faces of the same word; all of them turn about "greatness". I take the explanation of **greatness** as the basis, but state that this is not binding.

**A note is needed:** that the word *jadd* carries the sense "grandfather" is striking precisely because it stands in a verse where descent is denied. But to draw a sense from this — as though "descent is denied with the word for descent" — would be to force the root's settled use in Arabic. In Arabic the construct *jaddu fulān* is settled in the sense "so-and-so's majesty, his good fortune" and has nothing to do with kinship. I record this coincidence as an observation, not as an exegesis.

### مَا اتَّخَذَ — "He has not taken"

The choice of verb is very important and becomes visible when compared with Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ.

**Al-Ikhlāṣ 112/3:** *"lam yalid wa lam yūlad"* — **He has not begotten, and He has not been begotten.** The verb: **و-ل-د**, begetting. What is denied is a **biological / existential process.**

**Al-Jinn 72/3:** *"ma'ttakhadha ṣāḥibatan wa lā waladā"* — **He has taken neither a consort nor a child.** The verb: the iftiʿāl form of **أ-خ-ذ** (*ittikhādh*) — to take, to appropriate, to take a thing and join it to oneself.

The difference is this, and it is fine: **to beget is an act of nature; to take is an act of will.** Al-Ikhlāṣ denies the idea of reproduction. Sūrat al-Jinn denies the idea of **appropriation.**

These two are not the same thing. A being can "take a child" without begetting — adoption, declaring someone a son, counting a being of one's own line. The Qurʾān denies most of the claims of a child ascribed to God with exactly this verb: *"They said God has **taken** a child"* (*ittakhadha waladan* — al-Baqara 2/116, Yūnus 10/68, Maryam 19/88 and onward).

So **the two sūras close two separate doors:**

| | **al-Ikhlāṣ 112/3** | **al-Jinn 72/3** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *walada* — to beget | *ittakhadha* — to take |
| What is denied | Reproduction, the chain of descent | Appropriation, ascription |
| Direction | Existential | Volitional |
| The escape route closed | "He did not beget, but…" | "…He took" |

In the section on al-Ikhlāṣ it was said that the expression *"He has not been begotten"* "closes every road by which the defenders of the claim might escape". Sūrat al-Jinn closes one more road: a line of descent that could be set up without begetting.

**And one point more: the section on al-Ikhlāṣ already referred to this verse.** There the verse *"How should He have a child when He has no consort?"* (al-Anʿām 6/101) was set beside this one and it was said, *"And in the words of the jinn: exalted is the majesty of our Lord; He has taken neither a consort nor a child."* This is the counterpart of that reference.

### صَاحِبَة — "consort"

The root **ص-ح-ب**: to keep company, to be together, not to part. This root was taken up in the discussion of Sūrat al-Fīl by way of the word *aṣḥāb*.

**Ṣāḥiba** is the feminine form of the word and is used in Arabic in the sense "wife". The Qurʾān uses this word in a few places, and once counts it among those a person flees from at the gathering (ʿAbasa 80/36 — treated in that section).

**Why *ṣāḥiba* and not *zawja*?** In Arabic *zawja* is a more legal word: a wife by the bond of marriage. *Ṣāḥiba*, however, conveys **companionship** — one who is beside, who accompanies. The choice of the word widens the denial: what is denied is not merely a wedded wife but the idea of **any being accompanying God.**

### The order: first the consort, then the child

In Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ the order was polemical: first "He did not beget" (the addressee's claim), then "He was not begotten" (the logical extension of the claim).

Here the order is **logical**: first the consort, then the child. Because the idea of a child requires the idea of a consort. The sentence removes first the **condition** of the claim, then its **consequence**.

That the two sūras use two different orders shows that the orders are not arbitrary: al-Ikhlāṣ speaks inside a dispute, Sūrat al-Jinn frames a formula of profession.

### Who says this sentence

One detail must not be missed: **those who say this profession of tawḥīd are the jinn.**

And the Qurʾān records precisely that the jinn were made the **object** of this claim: *"And they set up a lineage (*nasab*) between Him and the jinn"* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/158). *"And they made the jinn partners with God"* (al-Anʿām 6/100).

That is, beings to whom a divine descent was ascribed deny that descent as their first act in the sūra. Those who are the subject of the claim refute the claim.

This is one of the sūra's most effective rhetorical moves, and it is set up entirely by structure; nowhere is it said, "look, even they say this." The sentence works only by whose mouth it comes out of.

## 72/4 — وَأَنَّهُ كَانَ يَقُولُ سَفِيهُنَا عَلَى اللَّهِ شَطَطًا

*Wa annahu kāna yaqūlu safīhunā ʿala'llāhi shaṭaṭā*

"And that: our fool used to say outrageous things about God."

### كَانَ يَقُولُ — "used to say"

The structure *kāna* + imperfect conveys in Arabic **a habit continuing in the past**. Not a single event; a repeated, settled behaviour. The function of the English "used to".

That is, what is related is not an accident but an **arrangement**: this was being said, it was being repeated, it was in circulation.

### سَفِيهُنَا — "our fool"

**The root س-ف-ه** was taken up in `002-bakara.md`, in the discussion of 2/13. The finding there was this: the concrete sense of the root is **lightness** — being light on the scale, having no weight; a *safīh* is one whose mind is light, who cannot govern himself. It was also recorded that the Qurʾān uses the word as a legal term for a person unable to manage his property (al-Nisāʾ 4/5). I do not repeat that analysis.

What is to be added here is the word's **place in this sūra.**

**The fool is the exact opposite of rushd.** In the preceding verse it was said that the Qurʾān guides to *rushd*. *Rushd* was a person's capacity to conduct his own affairs rightly — and in law a *safīh* is precisely the person who lacks that capacity. Al-Nisāʾ 4/5-6 already uses the two words side by side: do not hand over property to the foolish, hand it over if you perceive *rushd*.

So in two verses the jinn set up a contrast: **the text they listened to takes them to rushd; what they had listened to before was a fool.** Two sources of knowledge, two results.

**Its being singular.** Not "our fools" but "our fool". There are two readings:

- A particular person is meant.
- *Safīh* is used here as a generic noun: "the fools among us."

**The matter of who it is.** In the classical commentaries it is widely said that this word means Iblīs. There is a ground for this: the Qurʾān says plainly that Iblīs was of the jinn (*"Iblīs was of the jinn, and he departed from his Lord's command"* — al-Kahf 18/50). Another view is that there was among the jinn a party, or a leader, who ascribed descent to God.

**The Qurʾān does not answer this question** and neither do I. The verse names nobody; it leaves the word definite and the identity indeterminate. This is a choice often seen in the Qurʾān: not the person but the **position** is named.

### "Our" as a form of confession

What is really striking is the construct: *safīhu-**nā*** — **our** fool.

The sentence does not say "there was a fool"; it says "one of us". That is, the jinn locate the source of the false word **inside their own community.** They do not cast it outside, do not lay it on an enemy, do not attach it to an unknown cause.

This is the hardest finding a community can make about itself: **the corrupt idea did not come to us from outside, it came out from among us.** And this is exactly what the jinn do throughout this first half of the sūra — in verse 5 they will enumerate their own suppositions, in verse 11 their own divisions, in 14-15 those among themselves who went astray.

### شَطَطًا — "an outrageous word"

**Root ش-ط-ط.** Its concrete sense: **to grow distant, to go to excess, to overflow the bound.**

The most concrete residue of the root is geographical: **شَطّ (shaṭṭ)** — the bank, the edge of a river; the outermost line where the water meets the land. From the same root *shāṭṭ* — a distant place. The image of the root, then, is an **edge**: the place where a thing ends.

*Shaṭaṭ* is to pass beyond that edge. The same picture as the English "to overstep the bound" — a bound too is a limit.

The other occurrence of the root in the Qurʾān is in Sūrat Ṣād: the litigants who come to David say, *"judge between us with truth and **do not act unjustly** (lā tushṭiṭ)"* (Ṣād 38/22). There too the word relates a going outside the measure.

**The pattern of the word is meaningful too.** *Shaṭaṭan* stands in the sentence as the object of the verb *yaqūlu* and is a verbal noun. So it is not "he used to say an outrageous word" but, in exact translation: **"he used to say outrage about God."** The utterance and its content have been identified; what is said is named directly "excess itself".

And note: the sentence **does not say "he used to lie."** The next verse will use the word *kadhib* (lie) — but the word used in this verse is *shaṭaṭ*. The difference between the two is this:

- **A lie** is to say the opposite of the truth knowingly; it is a charge about intent.
- **Shaṭaṭ** is to go outside the measure; it is a charge about **measure.**

That the error made in speaking about God should be named "overstepping the bound" fits the nature of the subject. For here the matter is most often not deliberate lying but **losing the measure of the thing spoken about.** A person who ascribes a consort and a child to God may not, in his own view, be inventing a lie; he is applying his own measures to something that has no bound.

The finding set up in the "what this says to the present" part of the section on al-Ikhlāṣ attaches exactly here: *"to absolutise our own measures of justice, mercy and reasonableness and dress God in them… is to carve an idol whose material is the mind."* The word of this verse is the name of that operation.

## 72/5 — وَأَنَّا ظَنَنَّا أَن لَّن تَقُولَ الْإِنسُ وَالْجِنُّ عَلَى اللَّهِ كَذِبًا

*Wa annā ẓanannā an lan taqūla'l-insu wa'l-jinnu ʿala'llāhi kadhibā*

"And that: we had supposed that human beings and jinn would never speak a lie about God."

### The work the verse does

This is one of the most unexpected sentences of the sūra, and it is generally passed over quickly. Having said in the preceding verse "our fool used to say outrageous things", the jinn explain **why they believed him**:

*Because we had not thought that anyone could lie about God.*

That is, the error arose not from ill intent but from **an assumption.** The assumption was: on this subject nobody invents.

### ظَنَنَّا — "we had supposed"

**Root ظ-ن.** In Arabic this verb is used both in the sense "to suppose, to hold possible" and in the sense "to arrive at a firm conviction" — both uses are found in the Qurʾān (in the section on al-Inshiqāq the two ends of the root were taken up: those who **know for certain** that they will meet Him, and those who **suppose** that they will not return).

The sense here is closer to the "certain supposition" end of the word: the jinn speak not of a possibility but of **a settled acceptance.** This increases the weight of the sentence — it was an unquestioned acceptance.

The verb occurs **three times** in the sūra: 5, 7, 12. And the subject of the three differs:

| Verse | Subject of the supposition | Its state |
|---|---|---|
| **5** | "Nobody lies about God" | **Turned out false** |
| **7** | "God will not raise anyone" (the supposition of human beings, shared by the jinn) | **False** |
| **12** | "We cannot frustrate God" | **True** |

That is, the sūra uses the same verb three times and yields three separate results. **A supposition is of itself neither true nor false; it depends on what it is directed at.** The only supposition of the jinn that turned out true is the one about their own powerlessness.

### الْإِنسُ وَالْجِنُّ — "human beings and jinn"

The two kinds are named side by side, and **the human being is put first**.

This pair occurs often in the Qurʾān and the order is generally *jinn* first (*"O company of jinn and human beings"* — al-Anʿām 6/130, and many times in Sūrat al-Raḥmān). In the last verse of Sūrat al-Nās too the order is *al-jinnati wa'n-nās*, and in that section the point of that order was taken up.

Here the order is reversed: **the human being first.** Since the speakers are the jinn this is natural — they put themselves last. But the result is this: **they include themselves too in the list under charge.** They do not say "we supposed human beings do not lie"; they say "human beings and jinn".

The sentence, then, is not an accusation but a **diagnosis.** What was wrong was not "trusting human beings"; it was **assuming that nobody at all would invent on this subject.**

### كَذِبًا — "a lie"

**Root ك-ذ-ب**: to give a report that does not correspond to reality. In the Qurʾān it occurs most often in the *takdhīb* (denial) pattern.

The word's position here becomes clear when compared with the preceding one: in verse 4 there was **shaṭaṭ** (overstepping the bound), here **kadhib** (a lie). In the first sentence the jinn say what the act was, in the second what that act **actually was** — as they came to understand it afterwards.

The order, then, is: *our fool used to say outrageous things* → *and we supposed nobody would lie on this subject* → **so what was said must have been a lie.** The concluding sentence is not written; the reader is expected to frame it.

### The exploiting of good faith

This is the central matter of the verse and it needs to be expressed with care.

The error the jinn relate **is not ill intent.** Had it been ill intent the problem would be simple. What they relate is this: on a certain subject, because of the seriousness of that subject, **they assumed nobody would invent.**

This assumption is reasonable in itself and works most of the time in daily life. People speak more carefully on serious subjects; seriousness forms a kind of natural filter. What the jinn did was to trust that filter.

And the verse says the filter did not work. **The greatness of a subject does not guarantee the truth of what is said about it.** On the contrary: the greater the subject, the harder it is to verify; and in a field that is hard to verify the cost of inventing goes down.

This bears directly on the sūra's concern with knowledge. Here the jinn are telling the story of **unchecked trust in a source of knowledge.** And the sūra offers an alternative: in verses 1-2 the jinn **listened to the text they heard, saw what it does, and then accepted.** In the old error they relate here there is only acceptance — the stages of listening and assessing are absent.

### What this says to the present

**First: the sentence "nobody would invent on this subject" is still the most productive opening.** The more serious a field, the fewer questions are asked of what is said there. Health, law, religion, security, science — what these fields have in common is that they are both vital and not directly checkable. And precisely this combination leads to what is said in those fields receiving a privileged trust. This is the error the verse records.

**Second: the name of the error is not "believing a liar" but "not reckoning with the possibility of a lie".** These are not the same thing. The first is a deception, the second a lack of method. The jinn describe their own error in the second way — a harder and more honest description.

**Third: the position of those who say this.** Those who make this confession are a community that has believed in something new. So the place the sentence comes from is this: *we believed in something, and at the same moment we understood why we had believed the wrong thing before.* Faith has become a lens explaining a past acceptance. This is the common ground of all the confessions in the first half of the sūra: accepting and giving an account happen at the same moment.

## 72/6 — وَأَنَّهُ كَانَ رِجَالٌ مِّنَ الْإِنسِ يَعُوذُونَ بِرِجَالٍ مِّنَ الْجِنِّ فَزَادُوهُمْ رَهَقًا

*Wa annahu kāna rijālun mina'l-insi yaʿūdhūna bi-rijālin mina'l-jinni fa-zādūhum rahaqā*

"And that: some men among human beings used to seek refuge with some men among the jinn; and this only increased them in oppression."

### The position of the verse

This is the most concrete verse in the first half of the sūra. What is related is not a belief but a **practice.** And the party relating it is **the other side** of that practice — those sought refuge with are relating the seeking of refuge.

### يَعُوذُونَ — "used to seek refuge"

**The root ع-و-ذ** was analysed in full in `113-felak.md`. The finding there: the original sense of the root is **to take hold of something, to cling to it, to get behind it for protection**; in seeking refuge there are two movements at once — moving away from a danger and moving towards something. The derivatives (*istiʿādha*, *maʿādh*, *ʿiyādh*, *taʿawwudh*, *muʿawwidha*) were listed there. I do not repeat the analysis.

**And that section already referred to this verse.** In the introduction to Sūrat al-Falaq, in relating why the word of refuge is taught, this note was entered: *"The Qurʾān records a practice in which people sought refuge with the jinn, and states its outcome… That is, the act of 'seeking refuge' is not a neutral act; directed to the wrong address, it produces harm."* This verse is the source of that note.

What is to be added here is the **form** of the verb. In the verse *yaʿūdhūna* occurs — the simple triliteral form, not *istifʿāl*. So it is not "they were asking for refuge" but directly **"they were taking refuge".** In Sūrat al-Falaq and Sūrat al-Nās the command is in the form *aʿūdhu* (first person singular, simple form). The same verb, the same form — **the only thing that changes is the address.**

This shows where the whole weight of the verse lies: what is criticised is not the seeking of refuge but **with whom refuge is sought.** The act is right, the direction wrong.

### رِجَالٌ مِّنَ الْإِنسِ … رِجَالٍ مِّنَ الْجِنِّ

The same word is used of both parties: **rijāl** — "men, persons".

This choice must be dwelt on. The verse does not say "human beings used to seek refuge with jinn"; it says "**rijāl** among human beings used to seek refuge with **rijāl** among the jinn". The word is repeated on both sides.

There are two consequences:

**First: the relation is two-sided and reciprocal.** One kind is not seeking refuge with another kind. Particular persons are seeking refuge with particular persons. What is related, that is, is not a relation between kinds but established **individual connections**.

**Second: a human word is used of the jinn.** *Rijāl* is a word used in Arabic of human beings. Its use of the jinn drew the attention of the classical lexicographers and has generally been explained as "they too have their leading men, their strong ones, among themselves". This use of the word accords with verse 11 of the sūra: among the jinn too there are distinctions, degrees, groups.

**Its coming indefinite.** Both parties are indefinite: "some men". Not all human beings, not all jinn. The verse makes no generalisation; it speaks of a portion.

### فَزَادُوهُمْ رَهَقًا — "they increased them in oppression"

The verdict of the verse is in this clause, and it holds two grammatical questions.

**Who increased what in whom?** There are two readings:

| Reading | The agent of *zādūhum* | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | The jinn | The jinn increased the *rahaq* of the human beings who sought refuge. The party sought refuge with oppressed the seeker further. |
| **2** | The human beings | The human beings increased the *rahaq* of the jinn they sought refuge with — the demand shown them made the jinn insolent. |

Both readings have been defended in the classical commentaries. **The first reading is the more widespread** and sits better with the context: the jinn are telling their own story and what they relate is an error made by human beings; the consequence of an error is expected to fall on the one who made it. I record this as a preference; it is not binding. And the second reading says something meaningful too: **a being sought refuge with is harmed by being sought refuge with** — because it is settled into a position it does not deserve.

**The verb زَادَ**: to increase. And note: **it does not say "they did not protect", it says "they increased".** The verb is not negative but positive; but what it increases is bad.

This is a pattern of irony the Qurʾān uses often: in place of the expected outcome the exact opposite of the expected is put, and it is expressed as **an increase in the same direction.** The purpose of seeking refuge is to lighten a burden; the result has been the increase of the burden.

### رَهَقًا — *rahaq*

**Root ر-ه-ق.** Its concrete sense: **to overtake someone and cover him, to bear down, to overlay.** For a thing to follow after something, catch it up and ride upon it.

Its derivatives show the load of the root:

- **murhaq** — one on whom a load has been laid, one hard pressed, crushed under debt.
- **irhāq** — to lay on someone a burden he cannot carry.
- **rahiqa** — it caught up and covered.

Its occurrences in the Qurʾān make the image of the root clear:

- *"On that day there are faces **covered over** with dust"* (ʿAbasa 80/41 — *tarhaquhā qatara*). Dust settling over the face.
- *"Neither a darkening nor a humiliation **covers** their faces"* (Yūnus 10/26).
- *"Do not **lay** on me a hardship in my affair"* (al-Kahf 18/73 — Moses' words).
- *"We feared he would **impose** on them insolence and unbelief"* (al-Kahf 18/80 — *yurhiqahumā*).

The common image is this: **rahaq is something that comes from outside, settles over one, and cannot be got out from under.** The English "oppression" is an approximation; "to bear down on", "to smother", "to be weighed under" point to the same field.

The word occurs **twice** in the sūra: here (6) and in verse 13. The relation between the two occurrences is one of the tightest internal links of the sūra, and I shall take it up at verse 13.

### Historical background

In the classical commentaries this verse is connected with a custom known in pre-Islamic Arabia: **travellers, in a valley where they halted, would speak a word of refuge to the jinn counted the "master" of that valley, to be protected from him.** The formula transmitted is in the form of asking protection from the lord of that place.

I transmit this **as a report**. The existence of the practice is widely mentioned in the classical sources, and the verse's past tense (*kāna… yaʿūdhūna*) shows that a known custom is being pointed to. But I am not in a position to say anything certain about the wording of the formula, its currency or its regional distribution.

The verse itself is not concerned with the details of the practice in any case. The one thing it is concerned with is **the outcome**: this business did not lighten the burden, it increased it.

### The principle the verse sets up

In this text I do not enter, by way of this verse, a field of practices — amulets, sorcery, methods of protection, and the descriptions and classifications belonging to them. In this verse the Qurʾān describes no method, forbids no method, gives no alternative procedure. The only thing it says is the **outcome** of a turning.

The principle that follows from the verse is simple and can be stated in a single sentence:

> **Whether seeking refuge produces protection depends not on the intent of the one seeking but on the capacity of the one sought.**

And behind this principle stands the analysis of *al-Ṣamad* in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. This finding was made there: *al-Ṣamad* is the being in whom there is no hollow, and towards whom therefore all hollows turn; and "to treat as *ṣamad* what is not *ṣamad*" collapses, because it exceeds the capacity. Al-Jinn 72/6 is a concrete instance of that collapse. The party sought refuge with is a being in need of protection — indeed in verse 12 of the same sūra the jinn will say of themselves, *"we cannot escape."*

**If the being sought refuge with is itself in need of refuge, it will be no refuge.** The sūra will say this once more in verse 22, from the mouth of the Prophet, by way of the highest example: *"No one can save me from God."*

### What this says to the present

The structure the verse describes is a structure recognisable independently of the jinn: **a search for protection producing a dependence heavier than the thing protected against.**

The mechanism is this: a person attaches himself to a power in order to be protected from a danger. The power he attaches himself to does not remove the danger; but he now needs it, and that need becomes itself a burden. The original danger stands where it was, and a dependence has been added on top of it. This is exactly the image of the word *rahaq*: a load riding on top.

The measure of the verse can be used to test this structure: **if a way of protection has begun to take up more room than the thing you wanted protection from, it is producing not protection but rahaq.**

## 72/7 — وَأَنَّهُمْ ظَنُّوا كَمَا ظَنَنتُمْ أَن لَّن يَبْعَثَ اللَّهُ أَحَدًا

*Wa annahum ẓannū kamā ẓanantum an lan yabʿatha'llāhu aḥadā*

"And that: they too supposed, just as you supposed, that God would never raise anyone."

### The strangest place in the sentence: "just as you supposed"

In this verse an **addressee** suddenly appears: *kamā ẓanantum* — "just as you supposed". The second person plural.

In the flow of the sūra this is a break. Until that moment the jinn were speaking either of themselves ("we") or of third persons ("our fool", "some men among human beings"). Now they address someone directly.

**Whom?** Three main possibilities are treated in the classical commentaries:

| View | Who speaks, who is addressed |
|---|---|
| **1** | The jinn are relating this **to their own people**; "you" = the people of the jinn. That is: "they (human beings) too supposed as you supposed." |
| **2** | The jinn are speaking **of human beings** and the addressees are human beings; "they" = a party among the jinn, "you" = human beings. |
| **3** | The sentence has passed out of the jinn's speech into **the address of the revelation**; "you" = the addressees of the Qurʾān. |

The first view accords with the note in al-Aḥqāf 46/29 that *"they went back to their people"*, and is the reading that interferes least with the flow of the sentence. I find it reasonable but do not offer it as a preference; the pronouns of the verse do not permit a definite assignment.

**The indeterminacy may itself be serving a function.** Note what exactly the sentence says: *they too supposed as you did.* That is, what the verse does is to show **the meeting of two parties in the same error.** Once who is addressed is settled, this commonality narrows; while it remains indeterminate, it widens.

This is an instance of the technique known in Arabic rhetoric as **iltifāt** (a shift in the direction of address): the person changes in the middle of the sentence and the reader is suddenly drawn into the position of addressee. The Qurʾān uses this technique a great deal.

### أَن لَّن يَبْعَثَ اللَّهُ أَحَدًا — "God will not raise anyone"

**Root ب-ع-ث**: to lift a thing from its place and set it in motion, to send. The Qurʾān uses this root for two separate works, and both are readable here:

- **To raise the dead** — to lift the dead from the grave. *Baʿth* is the name of the hereafter.
- **To send** — to dispatch a messenger. The pattern *"He sent among them a messenger"* (*baʿatha fīhim rasūlan*) is widespread in the Qurʾān.

Both readings have been defended in the classical commentaries for this verse:

| Reading | Sense of the sentence |
|---|---|
| **Raising** | "God will not raise anyone after death" — a denial of the hereafter. |
| **Sending a messenger** | "God will not send anyone as a messenger" — a denial of prophethood. |

**The context supports both.** On the one hand the sūra is concerned with revelation (with messengership) — what the jinn heard is a revelation. On the other, verses 24-25 speak plainly of a promised day.

The word's densest use in the Qurʾān is in the direction of raising, and so are the like sentences transmitted from the mouths of deniers: *"Shall we be raised after death?"* I find this reading somewhat the stronger, but I make no definite preference between the two — and this must be added: **the two denials hang together.** If there is no day of reckoning there is no need of a messenger; if there is no messenger the report of the reckoning does not come.

**أَحَدًا** again in a negative sentence, in the sense "no one". Its second occurrence in the sūra.

### The function of the verse: a shared supposition

This verse is the continuation of verse 5 and forms a pair with it:

- **Verse 5:** "We supposed nobody lies about God." — Excessive trust in a source of knowledge.
- **Verse 7:** "They had supposed God would raise / send no one." — A deficient conception of God's act.

Both are framed with the verb *ẓann*. And what the two have in common is this: **assuming that something will not happen.** The jinn describe their errors not as "we believed a wrong thing" but as **"we did not reckon with a possibility".**

This is consistent with the sūra's understanding of knowledge. In verse 10 they will say *"we do not know"*; in verse 12 they will say *"we understood that we cannot escape."* The three together set up an attitude: **to accept the limit of one's own knowledge.**

## 72/8 — وَأَنَّا لَمَسْنَا السَّمَاءَ فَوَجَدْنَاهَا مُلِئَتْ حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا وَشُهُبًا

*Wa annā lamasna's-samāʾa fa-wajadnāhā muliʾat ḥarasan shadīdan wa shuhubā*

"And that: we felt out the heaven, and found it filled with strong guards and with flames."

### First a boundary: what this verse is not about

These two verses are among the passages of the Qurʾān most strained in the modern period. Attempts have been made to match them with notions such as meteorites, meteors, cosmic radiation.

**In this text I do not take that road, and I write the ground for it plainly:**

The verse does not describe a heavenly body. It does not relate a physical event. It gives no astronomical information. The verse's sentence is **a statement of experience** and its subject is **access to knowledge**: "we tried to go, we found it closed."

To say that the Qurʾān is not giving astronomical information here does not lower the verse's value; it sets in place what the verse says. To look for a scientific counterpart is to lose the **subject** of the sentence — the speaking party is the jinn, and what they relate is what befell them.

### لَمَسْنَا — "we felt out"

**Root ل-م-س**: **to touch with the hand, to search by groping.** What distinguishes it from seeing is that it requires contact. In the Qurʾān: *"Even if We had sent down to you a book written on parchment and they had **touched** it with their hands…"* (al-Anʿām 6/7).

The second direction of the root is **searching**: *iltimās*, to try to find something by groping for it. The word *iltimas* in Turkish — looking into someone's business and favouring it — comes from here too.

**The choice of the verb is very meaningful here.** The jinn do not say "we ascended to the heaven", do not say "we observed the heaven" — they say **"we felt out the heaven".** The word conveys a movement of **groping**: the movement of someone sweeping a surface with his hand in the dark.

This choice gives the sentence from the start a tone of **uncertainty and search**. One who gropes does not know what he will find. And what they found is not what they were looking for.

### فَوَجَدْنَاهَا — "and we found it"

**Root و-ج-د**: to find. The verb states the result of the search.

*Lamasnā* → *wajadnā*: we felt out → we found. There is no interval of time between the two verbs; the result emerged in the contact itself.

### مُلِئَتْ — "filled"

**Root م-ل-أ**: to fill. And the verb is **passive**: *muliʾat* — it was filled.

The passive is doing work here: the heaven did not fill of itself, it **was filled.** The agent is not stated but is known. What is related, that is, is not a state of nature but a **measure taken**.

And the tense is **past**. So what the jinn relate is a new situation they met with at that moment — and therefore presupposes an earlier situation as well. This will be said plainly in verse 9: *we used to sit.*

### حَرَسًا شَدِيدًا — "strong guards"

**Root ح-ر-س**: to protect, to keep watch, to guard. *Ḥāris* — a guard. *Ḥarasa* — he protected.

**Ḥaras** is not a singular word; in Arabic it is a **collective noun** — "guards, a body of watchmen". This is why its adjective (*shadīdan*) comes in the singular; collective nouns can take a singular adjective.

The word occurs in the Qurʾān only here.

**شَدِيدًا** — root ش-د-د: to bind, to tighten, to make firm. *Shadd* is to tie a knot tight. So what is meant by "strong" is not brute force but **tightness**: an order that leaves no gap.

### وَشُهُبًا — "and flames"

**Root ش-ه-ب**: the brightness of fire; the grizzled colour born of the mixing of white and black (*ashhab* — a grizzled horse). *Shihāb* is **a piece of kindled fire, an ember.**

The places where the word occurs in the Qurʾān fall into two groups:

- **In the sense of fire:** to Moses, *"that I may bring you **a burning brand**"* (al-Naml 27/7 — *bi-shihābin qabas*).
- **In accounts concerning the heaven:** *"If any steals a hearing, a clear **flame** pursues him"* (al-Ḥijr 15/18); *"a piercing **flame**"* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/10).

That is, the word itself is not the name of a heavenly body; it means **flame**. It can be rendered "a ball of flame", "an ember".

### Parallel passages within the Qurʾān

This subject occurs in several other places in the Qurʾān, and in all of them the same frame is set up:

- *"We have set constellations in the heaven and made it beautiful for beholders. And We have **guarded** it from every accursed satan. But if any steals a hearing, a clear flame pursues him."* (al-Ḥijr 15/16-18)
- *"We adorned the nearest heaven with an adornment, the stars; and **guarded it from every rebellious satan.** They cannot give ear to **the highest assembly**; they are pelted from every side… But if any snatches a word, a piercing flame pursues him."* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/6-10)
- *"We adorned the nearest heaven with lamps and made them **missiles** for the satans."* (al-Mulk 67/5)
- *"The satans did not bring it down… **They are kept far from hearing.**"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/210-212)

The last passage shows plainly why the matter is mentioned in the Qurʾān at all: **an answer to the claim that the source of the Qurʾān is the jinn or the satans.** One of the charges levelled at the Prophet in Mecca was that what he said came from a jinni. Sūrat al-Takwīr denies this directly: *"It is not the word of an accursed satan"* (al-Takwīr 81/25).

That is, this group of verses is part not of a lesson in cosmology but of **a dispute over sources.** The question is: is what Muḥammad says knowledge of the kind the soothsayers speak? The answer: no — because that channel is closed, and it is the channel itself that says it is closed.

### The classical exegetes' reading

In the classical commentaries these passages have widely been read as **the cutting off of divination**: with the beginning of the revelation, the road by which the soothsayers gathered knowledge was closed.

This reading accords with the text: verse 9 plainly sets up a **before and after** (*"we used to sit… **now** whoever gives ear"*). What is related, that is, is the change of a situation, and the time of the change is near the moment when the jinn heard the Qurʾān.

I transmit this as the general tendency of the classical exegetes. On when exactly the change began, whether it never happened before at all, and whether it was partial or complete, there are differing statements in the sources, and I am not in a position to classify them.

## 72/9 — وَأَنَّا كُنَّا نَقْعُدُ مِنْهَا مَقَاعِدَ لِلسَّمْعِ ۖ فَمَن يَسْتَمِعِ الْآنَ يَجِدْ لَهُ شِهَابًا رَّصَدًا

*Wa annā kunnā naqʿudu minhā maqāʿida li's-samʿ, fa-man yastamiʿi'l-āna yajid lahu shihāban raṣadā*

"And that: we used to sit there in seats for listening. But now whoever gives ear finds a flame lying in wait for him."

### كُنَّا نَقْعُدُ — "we used to sit"

Again *kāna* + imperfect: a habit continuing in the past. Not something that happened once, but **a settled arrangement.**

**Root ق-ع-د**: to sit. And here the word carries an idea of **place**: the place where one sits.

### مَقَاعِدَ لِلسَّمْعِ — "seats for listening"

**مَقَاعِد** is the plural of *maqʿad*: a place to sit, a station, a position.

There is a structure in the sentence resembling an **absolute object**: *naqʿudu … maqāʿida* — "we used to sit sittings". In Arabic the bringing of a noun from the same root as the verb either reinforces the verb or specifies its kind. Here the second function is to the fore: **particular seats, appointed points.**

That is, what is related is not a haphazard roaming; it is **a regular, settled listening activity that had taken up its stations.** Like watch-posts.

**لِلسَّمْعِ** — "for listening". The *lām* at the head conveys purpose. The aim of the sitting is stated plainly: **to obtain knowledge.**

The verbal noun here is *samʿ* — simple hearing. Compare with *istamaʿa* (to give ear) in verse 1: there was an attentive listening there, and its outcome was faith. Here too there is listening, but its aim is different.

### فَمَن يَسْتَمِعِ الْآنَ — "now whoever gives ear"

**الْآنَ — "now".** This may be the sharpest word of the sūra. A single adverb seats the whole passage on a **line of time**: there is a before, there is an after, and we are now in the after.

And the verb has returned: **يَسْتَمِعِ** — the same as *istamaʿa* in verse 1. The sūra uses the same verb twice, for two separate works:

| | **72/1** | **72/9** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | A company of the jinn | "Whoever" (indeterminate) |
| To what | To the Qurʾān | To the heaven |
| How | Uninvited but in the open | Secretly, taking up a station |
| Result | Faith, *rushd* | A flame |

**Two listenings, two results.** The same verb. The difference lies in what is listened to and in the manner of the listening.

This contrast carries the sūra's main argument: **there are two roads to knowledge — what is given and what is stolen.** The road of what is given is open and leads to *rushd*; the road of what is stolen has been closed. The jinn have tried both and report the outcome of both.

### شِهَابًا رَّصَدًا — "a flame lying in wait"

**رَصَد**, root **ر-ص-د**: **to watch, to wait in a place, to lie in ambush.**

The derivatives:

- **mirṣād** — a place of watching, a place of ambush. In the Qurʾān: *"Your Lord is indeed in the place of watching"* (al-Fajr 89/14). This was taken up in the sections on al-Fajr and al-Ghāshiya.
- **marṣad** — the point where an ambush is set. *"Sit for them at every place of watching"* (al-Tawba 9/5).
- **raṣad** — one who watches, or those who watch. It is used as a collective noun too.

*Raṣad* here stands as the adjective of *shihāb*, or in apposition to it. The sense: a flame that is not haphazard but **waiting, watching, standing ready.**

That is, the picture is this: there used to be seats — now others are sitting there. The place has not changed; **who sits there has changed.** The *raṣad* has taken the place of the jinn's *maqāʿid*.

**And this word will return in verse 27.** There it will be said that a *raṣad* is sent before and behind the revelation given to a messenger. The sūra, that is, uses the same word for two separate works:

| | **72/9** | **72/27** |
|---|---|---|
| Whom the *raṣad* watches | The one trying to steal knowledge | The revelation itself |
| Its function | To block | To protect |
| Its direction | Keeping the outside away | Bringing the inside through unspoilt |

The same watching, at two ends: **the channel is closed on the one side and guarded on the other.** The sūra secures the revelation from both sides at once — illicit entry is blocked, legitimate carriage is protected. This is one of the most elegant internal links of the sūra.

### What this says to the present

What can be drawn from these two verses is not a cosmology but a **measure for knowledge**, and it can be gathered under two heads.

**First: the matter of the source of a claim to hidden knowledge.** In the world the sūra addressed there was a trade selling knowledge unreachable by ordinary means. The ground of that trade was the claim that the knowledge came **through an unseen channel.** The verse declares that channel closed — and it is those at the other end of the channel who say it is closed.

This form of argument holds today as well: **the weakest place in a claim to hidden knowledge is the claimed channel itself.** Before the content of the knowledge is discussed, one may ask by what road that knowledge came; and that road most often cannot be described.

**Second: two manners of listening.** By using the same verb twice the sūra separates two attitudes: **listening to an open word** and **snatching something from a closed place.** The first is learning; the second is harvesting. Even their aims differ: the one listens in order to understand, the other in order to use.

## 72/10 — وَأَنَّا لَا نَدْرِي أَشَرٌّ أُرِيدَ بِمَن فِي الْأَرْضِ أَمْ أَرَادَ بِهِمْ رَبُّهُمْ رَشَدًا

*Wa annā lā nadrī a-sharrun urīda bi-man fi'l-arḍi am arāda bihim Rabbuhum rashadā*

"And that: we do not know whether ill is intended for those on earth, or whether their Lord intends right judgement for them."

### The key verse of the sūra

This verse is the most decisive sentence of the sūra on the matter of knowledge. And it is decisive because of who says it.

Against an order of divination built on the assumption that the jinn know the unseen, the sūra runs no polemic, writes no refutation, lines up no proof. It simply **makes the jinn speak**, and they say this:

> **لَا نَدْرِي — we do not know.**

Beings supposed to be a source of knowledge declare their own ignorance. And this declaration stands in the very middle of the sūra, with no mark of emphasis, in the same sequence as the other items.

### لَا نَدْرِي — "we do not know"

**Root د-ر-ي**: to know — but a particular kind of knowing. By the classical lexicographers' distinction, *dirāya* is **knowledge reached as the result of an effort and a search**; it is not absolute like *ʿilm*. From the same root *idrāʾ*, to make something known to someone.

The Qurʾān uses this root precisely to express **unknowability**: the pattern *"Wa mā **adrāka** mā…"* ("what will make you know what that is?") — this pattern was treated in the sections on al-Qadr, al-Humaza and al-Qāriʿa; the distinction established there was this: when *mā adrāka* (perfect) comes, an explanation follows; when *mā yudrīka* (imperfect) comes, it does not.

And in this sūra the root occurs **twice**: here (*lā nadrī* — from the mouth of the jinn) and in verse 25 (*in adrī* — from the mouth of the Prophet). Both are negative. Both say "I do not know".

This is one of the most striking parallels of the sūra:

| | **72/10** | **72/25** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | The jinn | The Prophet |
| What is not known | What is intended for those on earth | When what is promised will come |
| The expression | *lā nadrī* | *in adrī* |

That is, the sūra has it said in two separate places, from two separate mouths, that the unseen is not known — the one from beings supposed to be a source of divination, the other from God's messenger. **If the messenger does not know, then nobody knows.**

### أَشَرٌّ أُرِيدَ … أَمْ أَرَادَ بِهِمْ رَبُّهُمْ — the real point in the word order

The two halves of the sentence **are not mirrors of one another**, and this is the finest place in the verse.

**First half:** *a-sharrun **urīda** bi-man fi'l-arḍ* — "is ill **intended** for those on earth?" The verb is **passive**. Its agent is not stated.

**Second half:** *am **arāda** bihim **Rabbuhum** rashadā* — "or does **their Lord** **intend** right judgement for them?" The verb is **active**. And its agent is named plainly: *their Lord.*

The symmetry has been deliberately broken. The expected sentence would have been: "does their Lord intend ill for them, or right judgement?" The verse does not say this.

**What does this mean?**

In the classical commentaries this is explained as **decorum**: ill is not ascribed to God directly. There are other instances of this attitude in the Qurʾān — indeed within the content of this same sūra a similar nicety is seen in another sentence framed in the mouths of the jinn.

And beyond mere decorum, a difference of **sense** arises too:

- Ill is named as an **outcome**: something was intended, and this will be ill for those on earth. That the agent is not named suggests that the ill is not "a thing wanted for its own sake".
- Right judgement is named **directly** and together with its agent: their Lord intended it for them. And moreover, by saying *Rabbuhum* — "**their** Lord" — the relation of care between the one who intends and the ones intended for is stressed.

And one further observation: **in the second half the name "Lord" is used.** In the section on al-Fātiḥa it was shown that the word *Rabb* carries ownership together with **raising up**. So the sentence, in naming the possibility of right judgement, chooses the name that nurtures. *Rushd* — coming to maturity — is in any case the outcome of nurture. The name and the result answer to one another.

### Who are "those on earth"?

*bi-man fi'l-arḍ* — "those who are on the earth". The choice of word is wide: it does not say "human beings". The jinn may be counting themselves inside this expression too.

And there is this: the jinn frame this sentence **immediately after hearing something new.** The situation they relate, that is, is this: something is happening on the earth — a book is coming down, the channels of the heaven are being closed, an arrangement is changing. They do not know where this change will arrive.

**This is an extremely honest position.** They have believed (verse 2), but they do not claim to have grasped the whole of the event. They keep the difference between faith and knowledge: they know what they have believed in, they do not know what will happen.

### رَشَدًا — *rushd*, the second occurrence

The word occurred in verse 2; here it returns. Between the two occurrences there is an advance:

- **In verse 2** *rushd* was **where the text takes one**: "it guides to *rushd*."
- **In verse 10** *rushd* is **what God may intend**: "did their Lord intend *rushd* for them?"

The word, that is, passes from being the function of a text to being an **object of the divine will.** The jinn are asking whether what they saw in their own experience (the text takes one to *rushd*) may hold on the scale of the earth too.

### The matter of divination and knowledge of the unseen

This is where this verse has most been used throughout history, and it must be given its due.

The Qurʾān says in many places that the unseen belongs to God alone: *"Say: no one in the heavens or on the earth knows the unseen except God"* (al-Naml 27/65). Verse 26 of this sūra will give the same ruling.

But what this verse does is different and more effective: **it does not lay down the ruling, it transmits a confession.** Not a rule but a testimony. And the testimony comes from the party counted as the source of the claim.

Rhetorically this is the most economical way of refuting a claim. Had the Qurʾān said "the jinn do not know the unseen", an addressee could have said "how do you know?" When the jinn themselves say "we do not know", there is no place left to object.

The Qurʾān uses the same method once more: in the scene of Solomon's death. Solomon had died leaning on his staff, and the jinn went on working without noticing; his death was understood only when worms ate the staff and it fell. And the verse ends thus: *"The jinn realised that had they known the unseen they would not have remained in that humiliating torment"* (Sabaʾ 34/14). Here too the ruling is not given directly; it is shown by setting up a **scene**.

**The measure that follows:** to assess a claim to hidden knowledge there is no need to discuss the content of that knowledge. One asks after the capacity of the source. And the answer the Qurʾān gives comes from the mouth of the source itself.

## 72/11 — وَأَنَّا مِنَّا الصَّالِحُونَ وَمِنَّا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ ۖ كُنَّا طَرَائِقَ قِدَدًا

*Wa annā minna'ṣ-ṣāliḥūna wa minnā dūna dhālik, kunnā ṭarāʾiqa qidadā*

"And that: among us there are the righteous, and among us those below that. We were on separate roads."

### الصَّالِحُونَ — "the righteous"

**The root ص-ل-ح** was taken up in `002-bakara.md`, in the discussion of 2/11: to be fitting, to be in its right place, to be repaired; its opposite is *fasād* — to be spoilt, to depart from one's own order. From the same root comes *ṣulḥ* (peace).

The core of the word is **serviceability**: a thing's being in a condition to do the work expected of it. A *ṣāliḥ* instrument is a working instrument. A *ṣāliḥ* human being is one who fulfils what being human requires.

Its use here is striking: the jinn name the good portion among themselves **with a word used of human beings.** This is part of what is being done throughout the sūra: the jinn are described within the same moral categories as the human being. The same verbs, the same attributes, the same responsibility.

### وَمِنَّا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ — "and among us those below that"

The **restraint** of this expression must be dwelt on.

The verse does not say "among us are the righteous, among us are the **transgressors / the deniers**". It says **"دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ"** — "those below that", "those falling short of it".

**دُون** is an adverb in Arabic conveying "below, short of, on the other side of". The opposite is not named; only its **position** is stated: below the first group.

This does two things:

**First: it sets up a scale of degrees.** Not two sharp categories (good/bad) but an upper level and a wide field remaining below it. The expression "below that" does not say there is a single level below it either — there may be many.

**Second: it makes plain the position of the speaker.** The jinn are speaking of their own community and avoid naming the bad among them. This is not a covering over — the distinction is made plainly. But there is a measure in the choice of word.

And note this: **in verse 14 the same distinction will be made again, this time with names**: *"among us are those who submit, among us are the deviators."* The sūra, that is, uses a measured expression first, and the sharp one afterwards. What happens in the three verses between? The jinn relate their own experience (12-13): that they cannot escape, that they heard the guidance and believed. **The distinction sharpens only after the experience has been related.**

### كُنَّا طَرَائِقَ قِدَدًا — "we were separate roads"

The framing of the sentence is striking in Arabic: *kunnā ṭarāʾiqa qidadā* — translated exactly, **"we were separate roads."**

That is, not "we were on separate roads" — "**we were roads.**" Not what is on the road but the road itself. This is a structure used in Arabic for intensification: to declare a community to be the very **state** it is in. What we do in English when we say "that man is a disaster."

The effect is this: the dividedness is not an incidental condition but has become the community's **definition**.

### طَرَائِق — roads

The plural of **طريقة**. The root **ط-ر-ق**: to strike, to beat — and from this "road" (a track opened by being beaten with the feet).

In **`001-fatiha.md`** the Qurʾān's words for road were distinguished, and that distinction is of use here:

| Word | Its sense |
|---|---|
| **ṣirāṭ** | A wide, open, main road; a thoroughfare anyone can pass along |
| **sabīl** | A particular way of going, a route |
| **ṭarīq** | A path, a track followed |

In the section on al-Fātiḥa this was also recorded: the Qurʾān uses *ṣirāṭ* **always in the singular**; *subul* (roads) is plural. *"This is My straight road, so follow it. Do not follow the other roads; they will separate you from His road"* (al-Anʿām 6/153) — **one road, many turnings.**

Al-Jinn 72/11 stands exactly within this pattern: the jinn describe themselves with a **plural** word for road. *Ṭarāʾiq* — plural, and by its root a "path" (narrow, personal, a track each opens for himself).

**The choice of word is not chance.** For the main road there is *ṣirāṭ*, and it is singular. To relate dividedness, the plural of *ṭarīq* has been chosen. And the distinction recorded in the section on al-Ṭāriq is added here too: a *ṣirāṭ* is a road that has been laid, a *ṭarīq* is opened by being beaten with the feet. So each group has opened its own road by its own walking.

The word's other uses in the Qurʾān: *"And We created above you seven roads"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/17 — of the layers of the heavens), *"and do away with your exemplary way"* (Ṭāhā 20/63), *"the most judicious of them in the road he takes"* (Ṭāhā 20/104).

**And in verse 16 the word will return in the singular:** *"if they had gone straight upon that **road** (al-ṭarīqa)…"* That is, five verses after relating a plural dividedness the sūra will name a single, definite road. **Ṭarāʾiqa qidadā → al-ṭarīqa.** Scattered paths → that road.

### قِدَدًا — "in strips"

**Root ق-د-د.** Its concrete sense: **to split lengthwise, along the length; to cut into strips.**

In Arabic this root holds a distinct place among the verbs of cutting, and the lexicographers separate it from its sibling:

- **قدّ (qadd)** — to split lengthwise, along the length.
- **قطّ (qaṭṭ)** — to cut crosswise.

The difference looks small but it unties the knot of a story in the Qurʾān. In the story of Joseph, in the account of the tearing of the shirt: *"If his shirt is **torn** from the front (qudda min qubulin) she speaks the truth… if his shirt is **torn** from behind (qudda min duburin) she lies"* (Yūsuf 12/26-27). The same root. The lengthwise tearing of the shirt became the evidence showing who was pulling whom.

The root's other derivatives:

- **qadd** — stature, figure. (The line running along a person's length.)
- **qadīd** — meat separated into thin strips for drying. The most widespread way of storing meat in the desert: to slice it lengthwise, salt it and dry it in the sun.

**قِدَد** is the plural of *qidda*: pieces, strips, sections separated from one another.

Now the picture is complete: **the jinn were in separate roads like strips split lengthwise.** The word gives the form of the division too — not crosswise but **lengthwise**. That is, the break is not momentary and horizontal; each group has walked its own line separately from beginning to end.

This corresponds exactly to the expression "separate roads". The lengthwise splitting of a road means two parallel roads. Lines running side by side but never crossing.

### The ruling the verse carries

This verse makes the sūra's most important correction on the subject of "jinn": **jinn is not a moral category.**

Everyday usage in English (and in many languages) has confined the word almost wholly to a negative field. The verse rejects this directly: a word that is the name of a kind cannot carry a single moral verdict. Among them there are the righteous too.

And this has a consequence, bearing on the whole sūra: **the criticism in verse 6 does not rest on the jinn's being evil.** It was wrong for human beings to seek refuge with the jinn — but the reason for the wrongness is not the evil of the jinn but that **no created thing can be a refuge.** The sūra will say this of the Prophet too in verse 22.

This distinction matters because it sets the measure in place: the matter is not "keeping away from evil beings" but **turning to the right address.**

### What this says to the present

The expression *ṭarāʾiqa qidadā* is one of the rare findings a community can make about itself: **to acknowledge that it is divided.**

The jinn say this neither as a complaint nor as a defence. They say it as a **statement of a situation** — and immediately after (12-14) they make their own position clear.

The order is worth noting: first the dividedness is acknowledged, then the side is stated. The reverse was possible too — "we are on the right side" could have been said first and the others related afterwards. The text does not do this. First the state of the community, then the place of the person.

## 72/12 — وَأَنَّا ظَنَنَّا أَن لَّن نُّعْجِزَ اللَّهَ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَن نُّعْجِزَهُ هَرَبًا

*Wa annā ẓanannā an lan nuʿjiza'llāha fi'l-arḍi wa lan nuʿjizahu harabā*

"And that: we came to understand that we can never frustrate God on the earth, nor frustrate Him by fleeing."

### ظَنَنَّا — the third supposition

The verb occurs for the third and last time in the sūra. In verses 5 and 7 the suppositions turned out **false**; here the supposition is **true**.

And note its subject: the one supposition that turned out true is the one about **their own powerlessness.**

This is no accidental arrangement. The sūra lines up three suppositions, two of them about God (nobody lies about Him / He will raise no one), one about themselves (we cannot escape Him). The false ones are those that **assume too much about God**; the true one is the one that **assumes little about oneself**.

Rendering it "we came to understand" fits here; for in this context the verb conveys arriving at a firm conviction.

### نُّعْجِزَ — "to frustrate"

**Root ع-ج-ز.** And the concrete sense of the root lies in an unexpected place: **عَجُز (ʿajuz)** is the **hind part, the rear** of a thing. The rear section of a camel, the end of something.

From here the notion of *ʿajz* derives, and the logic of the derivation is this: **to remain at the back / at the end of a matter** — that is, not to catch up, to be left behind, to be unable. The English "to fall behind" carries a similar logic.

The pattern matters: *nuʿjiza* — the **ifʿāl** form (*aʿjaza*), causative. Not "to be unable" but **"to render unable"**. That is, to pass into a position someone's power cannot reach.

The Qurʾān uses this pattern regularly in the context of **the impossibility of fleeing from God**: *"You will not frustrate on the earth"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/22, and like expressions occur in several places). From the same root comes the word **muʿjiza**: what renders the one facing it unable.

### فِي الْأَرْضِ … هَرَبًا — two doors, both closed

The verse repeats the same verb **twice**, each time with a different complement:

- *lan nuʿjiza'llāha **fi'l-arḍ*** — "we cannot frustrate Him on the earth"
- *wa lan nuʿjizahu **harabā*** — "nor can we frustrate Him by fleeing"

**The first door: resistance.** To stand where you are and oppose. The expression "on the earth" states place: within the field we live in, where we are.

**The second door: flight.** To leave the field, to go outside it.

Two options, which cover everything a human being (and here the jinn) can do in the face of a power: **stay and resist**, or **go.** The verse closes both.

The grammatical position of the word **هَرَبًا** has been dwelt on. It is a verbal noun (*harab* — to flee) and can be analysed in the sentence in several ways: as a **circumstantial** ("fleeing"), or with a function resembling an object of purpose ("by fleeing"). It makes no serious difference to the sense; in either case fleeing stands as the **method** of the attempt to frustrate.

Rendering it "by fleeing" is enough.

### A parallel within the Qurʾān

This idea is expressed in the Qurʾān with a plain sentence of challenge as well, and its addressees are again jinn and human beings:

> *"O company of jinn and human beings! If you can pass beyond the bounds of the heavens and the earth, then pass. You will not pass save by an authority."* (al-Raḥmān 55/33)

The same two kinds, the same matter: the attempt to go outside the field. Al-Jinn 72/12 is like the answer to that challenge — the jinn have given the answer on their own behalf: we cannot pass.

### The verse's place in the sūra

Read together with verse 6, this verse yields a ruling.

In verse 6 human beings were seeking refuge with the jinn. Here the jinn say of themselves *"we cannot escape."*

**The party sought refuge with confesses that it has nowhere to flee.** The meaning of seeking refuge is to pass into a place where what is feared cannot reach. The jinn say they have no such place. Whoever seeks refuge with them, therefore, has hidden behind someone who has nowhere to flee.

Nowhere does the sūra make this finding plainly. It places the two verses in the same list and expects the reader to make the connection.

And the same structure will be repeated in verse 22 **with the highest example**: *"Say: no one can save me from God, and I shall find no refuge apart from Him."* The Prophet, that is, frames the same sentence about himself. The sūra completes from the bottom upward the list of beings that cannot be a refuge: the jinn cannot escape — nor can the messenger be saved.

## 72/13 — وَأَنَّا لَمَّا سَمِعْنَا الْهُدَىٰ آمَنَّا بِهِ ۖ فَمَن يُؤْمِن بِرَبِّهِ فَلَا يَخَافُ بَخْسًا وَلَا رَهَقًا

*Wa annā lammā samiʿna'l-hudā āmannā bih, fa-man yuʾmin bi-Rabbihī fa-lā yakhāfu bakhsan wa lā rahaqā*

"And that: when we heard the guidance we believed in it. Whoever believes in his Lord fears neither being given short nor being oppressed."

### The two halves of the sentence

The verse is one of the most structured sentences in the first half of the sūra. It consists of two parts:

- **First half: a testimony.** "We heard, we believed." Past tense, first person plural, a single event.
- **Second half: a general rule.** "Whoever believes does not fear." A conditional clause, the general present, everyone.

That is, having related their own experience, the jinn draw a **principle** from that experience. In the first half of the sūra this is done only here.

### سَمِعْنَا الْهُدَىٰ — "we heard the guidance"

**The object has changed.** In verse 1 what they listened to was *"an astonishing recitation"* — that is, a **text**. Here what they heard is *al-hudā* — **the guidance itself.**

This shows the distance the jinn have covered from the beginning of the sūra to here. First they heard a recitation and were astonished. Then they saw that this recitation *guides to rushd* (2). Now, looking back and naming the same thing, they name it by its fruit: what we heard was guidance.

In Arabic this may be called a form of naming known as *majāz mursal*: to name a thing by its result. But the real matter here is not a term of rhetoric but a **process of knowledge**: the same thing is named differently as it is understood.

**And the verb has changed again:** in verse 1 there was *istamaʿa* (to give ear), here *samiʿnā* (we heard). The move is from the verb of intent to the verb of result.

### فَمَن يُؤْمِن بِرَبِّهِ — "whoever believes in his Lord"

A conditional clause. And note: **the one to be believed in is named not "God" but "his Lord"** — and moreover not "our Lord" but **"his Lord"**: *bi-Rabbihī*.

The pronoun refers back to the subject of the condition (*man* — whoever). That is: *whoever believes in **his own** Lord.*

This recalls the finding set up in the section on al-Falaq. There it was taken up why the seeking of refuge is framed not with the name "God" but with the name "Lord", and this was said: a *Rabb* is the one who disposes; the one sought refuge with is also the owner of the field that is feared.

Here the same logic works to produce not fear but **assurance**: the reason for the person's not fearing is the bond between him and the one who nurtures him and holds his affairs in hand.

### فَلَا يَخَافُ — "does not fear"

A small but important grammatical note: the mood expected as the answer to the condition is the **jussive** (*fa-lā yakhaf*). In the verse the verb comes in the **indicative** (*yakhāfu*) — because the answer counts as a nominal sentence beginning with *fāʾ*.

The effect of this on the sense is: **not fearing is not an event that will occur as the result of the condition, but a state that emerges.** Not "if he believes he will not fear", but "the one who believes — he does not fear in any case."

A note on the readings: differing recitations of this verb are transmitted, and they yield a close result in point of sense. Since I am not sure of the details, I give no name of an imam and no classification.

### بَخْسًا — being given short

**The root ب-خ-س** was taken up in `083-mutaffifin.md`. The finding there: the sense of the root is **to take a thing by lowering its value, to give someone less than his due**; and the difference between *bakhs* and *taṭfīf* was set up thus — ***bakhs* is a diminishing made in the price, *taṭfīf* one made in the amount.** Its best-known use in the Qurʾān is in the story of Joseph: *"And they sold him for a paltry price, a few counted dirhams"* (Yūsuf 12/20 — *thamanin bakhsin*).

Here the word is in the sense of **the fear of being given the return short**: not receiving in full the return for what one has done.

And what is striking is this: **the word is a commercial word.** This was stressed in the section on al-Muṭaffifīn — that sūra is a sūra of trade: the measure, the balance, taking, giving, diminishing. Sūrat al-Jinn uses the same word for the requital of the hereafter.

That is, the verse says: **the one who believes does not fear that a diminishing will be made in his account.** This is something the Qurʾān says plainly elsewhere: *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it"* (al-Zalzala 99/7). The completeness of the measure is the reason the fear is lifted.

### وَلَا رَهَقًا — "nor being oppressed"

**The word returns from verse 6.** And this return is one of the tightest internal links of the sūra.

| | **72/6** | **72/13** |
|---|---|---|
| What was done | Human beings sought refuge with the jinn | A person believed in his Lord |
| *Rahaq* | **Increased** (*fa-zādūhum rahaqā*) | **Is no longer feared** (*wa lā rahaqā*) |
| Direction | Seeking refuge at the wrong address | Faith at the right address |

The same word, twice, seven verses apart. In the first the **product** of a wrong turning, in the second what a right turning **removes**.

The sūra thus sets up an equation:

> **Wrong refuge produces rahaq; right faith removes the fear of rahaq.**

And this equation is nowhere formulated plainly. The same word is simply used in two places and the reader is expected to make the link. This is a method the Qurʾān resorts to very often in the short sūras; like instances were recorded in the sections on al-Balad, al-Burūj and al-Ghāshiya.

### The complementarity of the two fears

*Bakhs* and *rahaq* are not two words chosen at random; together they cover **the two directions of fear**:

- **Bakhs:** not receiving what you deserve. The fear of **being given short**.
- **Rahaq:** a burden you do not deserve riding on top of you. The fear of **being overloaded**.

The one, that is, is **the lessening of the gain**, the other **the increase of the burden.** The two pans of the balance.

And these are the two wings of the Qurʾān's conception of justice: no one is given less than he deserves, no one is loaded with more than he can carry. The second principle is stated plainly in the Qurʾān: *"God does not burden any soul beyond its capacity"* (al-Baqara 2/286).

What the believer does not fear is precisely injustice in these two directions. **What he does not fear is not the punishment — it is injustice.**

This has to be stated separately, because the verse is very easily misread. The sentence does not say "the believer fears nothing". It enumerates two definite things, and both concern **treatment**: being measured short and being overloaded. The fear that faith removes is the fear that **the requital will be unjust.**

## 72/14 — وَأَنَّا مِنَّا الْمُسْلِمُونَ وَمِنَّا الْقَاسِطُونَ ۖ فَمَنْ أَسْلَمَ فَأُولَـٰئِكَ تَحَرَّوْا رَشَدًا

*Wa annā minna'l-muslimūna wa minna'l-qāsiṭūn, fa-man aslama fa-ulāʾika taḥarraw rashadā*

"And that: among us are those who have submitted, and among us are those who deviate from the truth. Those who have submitted are the ones who have sought out and found what is right."

### A repetition of verse 11 — but sharpened

This verse is the second and definitive form of verse 11:

| | **72/11** | **72/14** |
|---|---|---|
| First group | *al-ṣāliḥūn* — the righteous | *al-muslimūn* — those who submit |
| Second group | *dūna dhālik* — those below that | *al-qāsiṭūn* — those who deviate from the truth |
| Tone | Measured, not naming | Naming, sharp |
| Time | *kunnā* — past ("we were") | *minnā* — the present state ("among us") |

Three verses apart the same distinction is made twice. **What has changed?** In the three intervening verses the jinn related their own experience: they came to understand that they cannot escape (12), they heard the guidance and believed (13). The distinction sharpens only after that relating.

And there is a further grammatical difference: in verse 11, *kunnā* (we were) — **the past.** Here, *minnā* (among us) — **the present.** That is, the dividedness existed in the past and exists now too; but now the sides have been named.

### الْمُسْلِمُونَ — those who submit

**Root س-ل-م**: to be sound, to be free of defect, to be delivered. From the same root *salām* (peace), *silm* (peace), *salīm* (sound, without defect), *taslīm*.

*Muslim* is the active participle of the ifʿāl form (*aslama*): **one who hands himself over.** In the core of the word two ideas join — **soundness** and **submission**. To give a thing over to the other party complete, without defect, sound.

The word's use here must be dwelt on. The jinn name themselves *muslimūn*. This accords with the word's wide use in the Qurʾān: the Qurʾān uses the words *islām* and *muslim* of the prophets before it and their communities too (of Abraham: *"He was a ḥanīf, a muslim"* — Āl ʿImrān 3/67; Joseph's prayer: *"take me as a muslim"* — Yūsuf 12/101).

### الْقَاسِطُونَ — the deviators

The most interesting word of the sūra, and one of the cleanest instances of the Arabic matter of the **aḍdād**.

**Root ق-س-ط.** And from this root two words derive whose senses are **exact opposites** of one another:

- **قاسط (qāsiṭ)** — **one who deviates from the truth, who wrongs, who corrupts a share.**
- **مقسط (muqsiṭ)** — **one who acts justly, who renders what is due.**

The same root, opposite senses. How can this be?

### The matter of the aḍdād and its solution here

A root's carrying two opposite senses is called in Arabic **aḍdād**, and it is a subject the lexicographers have discussed at length. In some instances the explanation is hard; but **in this instance the explanation is clear and rests on the pattern.**

The difference is in the verb's **form**:

| Verb | Form | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **قَسَطَ (qasaṭa)** | Triliteral (simple) | He deviated from the truth, he wronged |
| **أَقْسَطَ (aqsaṭa)** | Ifʿāl (with hamza) | He acted justly |

And the function of the ifʿāl form here is called in grammar the **hamza of privation**: **to take away, to remove the sense of the root.**

That is:

> *qasaṭa* = he stinted, he deviated → *aqsaṭa* = **he removed the stinting**, he did away with the deviation → he acted justly.

This is a known function of the ifʿāl form, and there are other instances: *shakā* (he complained) → *ashkā* (he removed his complaint); *ʿajama* (he found a thing's speech unintelligible) → *aʿjama* (he took away its unintelligibility by pointing it).

**The opposition, then, is not random but regular.** The root carries a single sense — *deviation, the corrupting of a share* — and the ifʿāl form serves to **do away with** that sense. The two words say the same thing: the one the act, the other the removal of the act.

And there is a third word: **قِسْط (qisṭ)** — a share, a portion; and from there **justice.** It is widespread in the Qurʾān: *"Establish the weighing with justice (bi'l-qisṭ)"* (al-Raḥmān 55/9); *"Give full measure and weight with justice (bi'l-qisṭ)"* (al-Anʿām 6/152).

And of those God loves: *"God loves those who act justly (al-muqsiṭīn)"* (al-Māʾida 5/42; also the same expression at al-Ḥujurāt 49/9 and al-Mumtaḥina 60/8).

Now the picture is complete:

| Word | Sense | In the Qurʾān |
|---|---|---|
| *qisṭ* | Share, due, justice | al-Raḥmān 55/9, al-Anʿām 6/152 |
| *muqsiṭ* | One who acts justly | al-Māʾida 5/42 |
| **qāsiṭ** | **One who corrupts a share, who deviates from the truth** | **al-Jinn 72/14, 15** |

**The word occurs in the Qurʾān only in this sūra** — twice, in two consecutive verses.

### The sense the choice of word carries

The choice of the word *qāsiṭ* describes deviation in the language of **justice**. It does not say "unbeliever", does not say "transgressor", does not say "wrongdoer". It says **one who corrupts a share.**

The meaning of this is: not giving God His due is named as a matter of **measure**. Faith is spoken of in the language of the balance.

And this joins with the preceding verse. In verse 13 it was said that the believer will not fear *bakhs* (being given short) — again the language of the balance. Now in verse 14 the other party is named **those who corrupt a share.**

The two verses together, that is, set up this: **the reckoning has two sides. The believer does not fear being given short; the *qāsiṭ* is the one who gives short what should be given.**

### فَمَنْ أَسْلَمَ فَأُولَـٰئِكَ تَحَرَّوْا رَشَدًا

**Word order.** The condition begins with *man* (singular), the answer comes with *ulāʾika* (plural). In Arabic this transition is ordinary: *man* is singular in wording and plural in sense.

Its effect is this: the ruling is given to individual persons (whoever submits), but the result is named as a **community** (those are the ones). Submission is an individual decision; its result is joining a group.

### تَحَرَّوْا — "they sought out and found"

**Root ح-ر-ي.** And the word occurs in the Qurʾān only here.

The concrete sense of the root: to seek out **the most fitting, the most correct, the most apt** of a thing. *Ḥarā* — to be worthy, to be fitting. *Ḥariyyun bi-kadhā* — a settled expression in the sense "he is worthy of such".

**تحرّى (taḥarrā)** is in the tafaʿʿul form, and this form conveys in Arabic **effort and gradualness**: to obtain a thing step by step, with labour.

The explanation in the classical lexicons joins two elements:

1. **To seek** — deliberately, attentively, to try to find the most fitting.
2. **To hit the mark** — to find the thing sought.

*Taḥarrī*, then, is not "he sought" but **"he sought out and hit the mark"**. A verb that contains its result.

There is a residue of the word in Turkish: in legal language **"tahkikat ve taharri"** — investigation and inquiry. The word passed there from this root.

**The choice of this verb in this verse bears directly on the sūra's concern with knowledge.** The verse does not say "those who submitted **found** rushd"; it says "**they sought it out and found it**". Submission, that is, is named not as a passive acceptance but as **the outcome of a search.**

And this fits exactly the process the jinn related at the head of the sūra: they listened (1), they assessed what it does (2), they believed (2). Search, assessment, decision — three stages.

### رَشَدًا — *rushd*, the third occurrence

The word has returned again. Its third occurrence in the sūra:

- **In verse 2:** where the text takes one — *"it guides to rushd"*.
- **In verse 10:** what God may intend — *"did their Lord intend rushd for them?"*
- **In verse 14:** what the one who submits reaches — *"they sought out and found rushd"*.

Three verses, three stages: **what is shown → what is intended → what is reached.** The word traces an orbit through the sūra, and its last occurrence (21) will be in the mouth of the Prophet.

## 72/15 — وَأَمَّا الْقَاسِطُونَ فَكَانُوا لِجَهَنَّمَ حَطَبًا

*Wa amma'l-qāsiṭūna fa-kānū li-jahannama ḥaṭabā*

"As for those who deviate from the truth, they have become firewood for Hell."

### وَأَمَّا — "as for…"

The particle **ammā** is used in Arabic in passing from one subject to another, and sets up **a definite division** between two groups. The English "as for…" answers it well.

And note: in verse 14 two groups were named, but **only the outcome of the first** was given (those who submitted found *rushd*). Verse 15 gives the outcome of the second group. The two verses together set up a completed twofold structure.

A grammatical note: normally *ammā* is used twice (*ammā… wa ammā…*). Here only the second is present. This is because the ruling on the first group was given inside verse 14; the structure is not deficient but displaced.

### فَكَانُوا — "they have become"

The verb is in the **past tense**. The expected mood would be the future: "they will become." The verse uses the past.

This is a technique often used in the Qurʾān for the rulings of the hereafter: **to relate what is to come as though it had happened, in order to convey certainty.** The density of this mood in the Qurʾān's depictions of the resurrection was seen in the sections on al-Infiṭār, al-Takwīr and al-Qāriʿa too.

### حَطَبًا — "firewood"

**Root ح-ط-ب**: to gather wood, to collect fuel. *Ḥaṭab* — firewood; *ḥaṭṭāb* — a woodcutter.

And the word's other occurrence in the Qurʾān is in Sūrat al-Masad: *"ḥammālata'l-ḥaṭab"* — **the carrier of firewood** (al-Masad 111/4). It was taken up in that section.

**The image the word carries is hard and deliberate.** The human being (and the jinn) is named not as a being inside the fire but as the **fuel** of the fire. The difference matters:

- A being inside the fire is the fire's **object** — something is done to it.
- The fire's fuel is the fire's **substance** — the fire is made of it.

That is, the punishment is described not as something coming from outside but as something issuing from the person himself.

**This is a consistent pattern in the Qurʾān:**

- *"Guard yourselves against a fire whose fuel is people and stones"* (al-Baqara 2/24). This verse occurred in `002-bakara.md`, in the discussion of the challenge.
- *"O you who believe! Guard yourselves and your families against a fire whose fuel is people and stones"* (al-Taḥrīm 66/6).
- *"You and what you serve apart from God are the **firewood** of Hell"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/98 — there the word *ḥaṣab* is used, in the sense of fuel).

There is a difference between the *waqūd* (fuel) of al-Baqara 2/24 and the *ḥaṭab* (firewood) here, and *ḥaṭab* is the more concrete: *waqūd* is burning matter in general, while *ḥaṭab* is wood **gathered, carried, stacked.** The word contains a process of labour and preparation — the woodcutter gathers the wood, carries it, stacks it.

This concreteness accords with a method recorded in the section on al-Burūj: **the naming of the punishment in the language of the crime.** Those who deviate — those who corrupt a share — become fuel; the requital of the share they kept for themselves is their own being spent.

### لِجَهَنَّمَ — the fronting in the word order

The normal order of the sentence would have been *"kānū ḥaṭaban li-jahannama"*. In the verse *li-jahannama* has been brought forward.

In Arabic fronting conveys **emphasis and restriction**. That is: they became firewood **for Hell** — not for anything else. The address of the firewood is stated.

This fronting also sets up the **verse-ending**: by moving to the end, *ḥaṭabā* joins the sūra's weave of rhyme. That choices of word order in the Qurʾān often do the work of sense and sound at once has been recorded many times in earlier sections.

### What this says to the present (11-15)

Read together, these five verses yield one of the sūra's most practical measures.

**The jinn divide their own community in two — and they do it while speaking about themselves.** The division, that is, is not a judgement from outside but a finding from within.

This attitude has two sides:

**First: belonging to a community is not sharing that community's verdict.** The jinn do not say "we are jinn, and jinn are thus." They say "among us there are these, among us there are those." A collective verdict is rejected.

This comes out at the same place as the measure laid down from the start in `USLUP.md`: **the verdict is given to an attribute, not to a group.** This is exactly what the verse does — and it does it by having a group do it about itself.

**Second: being able to name the corruption within one's own community.** The list of what the jinn do in the first half of the sūra is this: naming their own fool (4), stating their own false suppositions (5, 7), acknowledging their own dividedness (11), naming the deviators among themselves (14-15).

This is a manner of speech that is not defensive, and it is rare. A community speaking about itself generally either defends wholesale or rejects wholesale. The third road here — **to distinguish** — is harder than both, because it requires a measure.

---

# The second layer: the voice of the revelation (16-28)

From here on the speaker changes. The jinn's report is over; the sūra begins to give its own rulings.

Exactly where the transition falls depends on the grammatical question taken up at the head of the sūra, and no definite boundary can be drawn. But in point of content the change is plain: from verse 16 on the sentences no longer say "we jinn"; general rules, rulings and commands come. The address of verse 18, *"so do not call upon anyone along with God"*, settles it that the speaker is no longer the jinn.

## 72/16 — وَأَن لَّوِ اسْتَقَامُوا عَلَى الطَّرِيقَةِ لَأَسْقَيْنَاهُم مَّاءً غَدَقًا

*Wa an lawi'staqāmū ʿala'ṭ-ṭarīqati la-asqaynāhum māʾan ghadaqā*

"And that: if they had gone straight upon that road, We would have given them abundant water to drink."

### The two verses have to be read together

Verses 16 and 17 are a single sentence divided in two. The first gives the promise, the second gives the **reason** for the promise. Because the reason is unexpected, the division of the verses is meaningful too: the reader hears good news in verse 16 and learns in verse 17 what the good news is.

### اسْتَقَامُوا — "if they had gone straight"

**Root ق-و-م**: to stand up, to rise, to be upright. The distribution of this root in the Qurʾān was taken up in the sections on al-Fātiḥa, al-Bayyina and al-Takwīr; the chain set up there was this: *mustaqīm* (the attribute of the road) → *iqāma* (the act of prayer) → *qayyima* (the character of the religion) → *yastaqīm* (the act of the person).

**اسْتَقَامَ** is in the istifʿāl form and here conveys **steadiness, continuity and remaining on the line.** The Turkish *istikamet* comes from here and carries both senses at once: **direction** and **uprightness.** That reflects the word's state in Arabic well.

**The conjugation of the verb is past tense + the conditional particle *law*.** *Law* conveys in Arabic **an unrealised condition**: "if it had been so — but it was not." So the sentence relates not an offer but a **counterfact**: this road was not taken; had it been taken, this would have followed.

There are parallels of this pattern in the Qurʾān, and all of them set up the same structure:

- *"Had the people of those towns believed and been mindful, We would have opened upon them blessings from the heaven and the earth"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/96).
- *"Had they upheld the Torah and the Gospel and what was sent down to them from their Lord, they would have eaten from above them and from beneath their feet"* (al-Māʾida 5/66).

All three verses set up the same equation: **uprightness → material abundance.**

**And a fourth is in the preceding sūra.** Noah offers his people exactly this equation: *"Ask forgiveness of your Lord… He will send the heaven upon you in abundant rain, and support you with wealth and sons, and make for you gardens and rivers"* (Nūḥ 71/10-12). In the discussion of Noah it was treated that these verses set up a social, conditional and unguaranteed link, and are not a formula for wealth.

Set side by side, the two sūras make the second half of the equation visible. **Sūrat Nūḥ gives the promise; Sūrat al-Jinn names the promise.** In Nūḥ it is not said what the water is — it is presented as a blessing. Here, one verse later, that same water will be called a *fitna*. Unless the two sūras are read together, the one looks too optimistic and the other too dark.

### عَلَى الطَّرِيقَةِ — "upon that road"

**The word returns from verse 11** — but changed:

| | **72/11** | **72/16** |
|---|---|---|
| Form | *ṭarāʾiq* — **plural** | *al-ṭarīqa* — **singular** |
| Definiteness | Indefinite | **Definite** (with *al-*) |
| Its adjective | *qidadā* — in strips, broken | None — it needs no adjective |

The same word five verses apart, in exactly the opposite form. The jinn had described themselves as "separate roads"; now **"that road"** is named.

This is another confirmation of the finding in the section on al-Fātiḥa: **one road, many turnings.** The Qurʾān names deviation with the plural, the right way with the singular.

**The definite article (al-) also sets up a restriction.** The word does not say "a road", it says "**that** road" — the road the addressee knows, that needs no describing. Which road it is is not said, because there is no need to say: it is what the sūra has been relating from the start.

**The particle "upon"** (*ʿalā*) is meaningful too. In the sections on al-Fātiḥa and al-Baqara the function of this particle was taken up: *ʿalā* conveys being on top, standing firmly — *"ʿalā hudan min Rabbihim"* (al-Baqara 2/5). For going astray, *fī* (inside) is used. Here too rightness comes with *ʿalā*: to stand upon the road.

### لَأَسْقَيْنَاهُم مَّاءً غَدَقًا — "We would have given them abundant water to drink"

**أَسْقَىٰ**, in the ifʿāl form: **to give to drink, to water** — not to give water but **to satisfy with water.** The lexicographers record a distinction between *saqā* and *asqā* in Arabic: *asqā* is closer to providing continuous and abundant water (irrigating a piece of land, giving access to a spring).

**غَدَقًا** — root **غ-د-ق**: water's being abundant, copious and uninterrupted. *Ghadiqa* — the water became abundant. *Ghaydāq* — very abundant.

**The word occurs in the Qurʾān only here.**

### Why "water"?

That what is promised should be **water** is no accident and has to do with the addressee's world.

In Arabia water is not the **measure** of prosperity but prosperity **itself**. The existence of a settlement depends on water; caravan roads are drawn by water points; the strength of a tribe is measured by the wells and springs it holds. In the geography where the Qurʾān came down, the expression "abundant water" means, in today's language, "plenty, prosperity, economic expansion".

The Qurʾān sets up this link elsewhere too: the cutting off of rain is the sign of straitness, the abundance of rain the sign of blessing.

That is, the verse does not promise an abstract spiritual reward. It speaks of **a worldly, measurable, tangible abundance.** And this prepares the surprise of the next verse.

## 72/17 — لِّنَفْتِنَهُمْ فِيهِ ۚ وَمَن يُعْرِضْ عَن ذِكْرِ رَبِّهِ يَسْلُكْهُ عَذَابًا صَعَدًا

*Li-naftinahum fīh, wa man yuʿriḍ ʿan dhikri Rabbihī yaslukhu ʿadhāban ṣaʿadā*

"So that We might test them by it. And whoever turns away from the remembrance of his Lord, He will drive him into a mounting punishment."

### لِّنَفْتِنَهُمْ — "so that We might test them"

The **lām** at the head conveys purpose: the **aim** of the abundance is stated.

And the aim is this: **a testing.**

This is where the whole weight of the verse gathers. In the preceding verse a promise was given — abundant water, blessing, expansion. And immediately after, what that promise is is stated: **an instrument of testing.**

### فَتَنَ — the root of *fitna*

**The root ف-ت-ن** was analysed in full in `085-buruc.md`. The finding there was this: the original sense of the root is **to put gold into the fire and melt it, thereby separating out the alloy within and bringing its purity to light**; the one who does this is called a *fattān*. And it was recorded that the usual English renderings of *fitna* hold only one end of this range ("sedition", "stirring up trouble"), while the centre of the field the Qurʾān uses is **testing**. I do not repeat that analysis.

Among the fields of the root's use in the table in the section on al-Burūj there was this too: **being tested by favour** — *"Your wealth and your children are only a fitna"* (al-Anfāl 8/28).

Al-Jinn 72/17 is the plainest expression of that item. For here the favour is given **with its being a fitna stated plainly.**

### The link with `089-fecr.md`: taking abundance for honouring

The most important link of this verse is with Sūrat al-Fajr, and the two texts together complete a single finding.

**Al-Fajr 89/15-16** said this: when a human being is given abundance he says *"my Lord has honoured me"*; when he is given straitness he says *"my Lord has humiliated me"*. And the Qurʾān rejected both: *kallā.*

The finding made in that section was this — and it is worth repeating, because it attaches here: *"The text is most often read as though only the second were being rejected; as though the first sentence were a proper expression of thanks. It is not. The structure of the sūra gives the two within the same grammatical pattern, attached to the same verb, and cuts both off with a single kallā."*

And this was added there too: *"Whoever counts abundance an honouring must count straitness a humiliation… a person who takes his own abundance for divine approval is bound to collapse when he falls into straitness."*

**Al-Jinn 72/17 is the other face of the same finding.**

| | **al-Fajr 89/15-16** | **al-Jinn 72/17** |
|---|---|---|
| Who speaks | The human being | The revelation |
| What is said | "Abundance is an honouring" (and this is rejected) | "Abundance is a testing" (and this is established) |
| Method | Recording the human being's inference and rejecting it | Saying the right thing directly |

Al-Fajr shows the wrong inference and cuts it off; al-Jinn gives the right name. **Abundance is neither an honouring nor a humiliation — it is a testing.**

And the necessary note entered in the section on al-Fajr holds here too: *"it is not being said that abundance is not a gift."* A favour is a favour; the verse does not reject the favour. What it says is **what the favour is for.**

### فِيهِ — "by it / within it"

The pronoun refers back to *māʾan ghadaqā* (the abundant water). And the particle is **fī** — "within".

This is a structure seen in the analysis of *fī taḍlīl* in the section on al-Fīl as well: *fī* conveys containment, and carries the idea of a vessel and an enclosing. That is, the testing is not something **added** to the abundance; it is something **inside** the abundance. Favour and testing are not two separate operations but two names of a single one.

### وَمَن يُعْرِضْ عَن ذِكْرِ رَبِّهِ — "whoever turns away from the remembrance of his Lord"

**أَعْرَضَ** — root **ع-ر-ض**: the breadth, the width of a thing; its side. *Aʿraḍa ʿan* — **to turn one's side** to someone, to turn one's face away and show one's flank.

This is the concrete image of the word, and it has the same logic as the English "to turn one's back on": a refusal expressed by the posture of the body. *Iʿrāḍ* is not an argument; it is not turning towards the one addressing you. No answer is given, no interest is taken, the speaker is not treated as an interlocutor.

**ذِكْرِ رَبِّهِ — "the remembrance of his Lord".** Root **ذ-ك-ر**: to remember, to call to mind, to utter. In the sections on al-Aʿlā and al-Ghāshiya the differences of pattern in this root (Form II *dhakkir* — reminding from outside; Form V *yadhdhakkaru* — remembering from within) were treated.

The construct can be read in two ways, and both are meaningful:

- **"The remembrance [sent down] by his Lord"** — that is, the revelation, the Qurʾān. The Qurʾān names itself *dhikr* in many places.
- **"Remembering his Lord"** — that is, the act of remembrance itself.

The two readings come out at the same place in practice: whoever turns away from the text has turned away from the remembering too.

### The link the context sets up

The relation between the two halves of the verse has been set up with care.

First half: **abundance is a testing.**
Second half: **whoever turns away from the remembrance is driven into a punishment.**

The link between them is not stated but is plain: **the way abundance is a testing is the possibility of its taking a person away from remembrance.** Expansion lessens the sense of need; when the sense of need lessens, the turning lessens too.

This is a link the Qurʾān sets up plainly elsewhere and it was taken up in the sections on al-ʿAlaq and al-Layl: **istighnāʾ** — seeing oneself as free of need. *"No! The human being does indeed transgress, in that he sees himself as free of need"* (al-ʿAlaq 96/6-7). Al-Jinn 72/16-17 is the expression of the same mechanism on the side of the promise: abundance will be given, and precisely for that reason that abundance will be a testing.

### عَذَابًا صَعَدًا — "a mounting punishment"

**Root ص-ع-د**: **to go up, to climb.** *Ṣuʿūd* — ascent. *Ṣaʿīd* — the surface of the earth, the ground (what one climbs upon). *Taṣaʿʿada* — he climbed with difficulty.

**صَعَدًا** is explained in the classical lexicons as **a slope hard to climb, exhausting to ascend.** That is, the punishment is described as a **climb**: a load a person cannot get out from under, forced constantly upward.

**The parallel within the Qurʾān is very close:** *"I shall drive him up a steep ascent (ṣaʿūdā)"* (al-Muddaththir 74/17). And the verb used in that verse is **أُرْهِقُهُ** — that is, the root **ر-ه-ق**, the word in verses 6 and 13 of this sūra. In al-Muddaththir the two notions (*rahaq* and *ṣaʿad*) join in a single sentence: **the load that rides on top** and **the slope that cannot be climbed.**

### A comparison with `090-beled.md`: two slopes

In Sūrat al-Balad too there was a slope: **العقبة (al-ʿaqaba)** — the steep ascent (al-Balad 90/11-12). And in that sūra the slope was what one is **asked to climb**: freeing a slave, feeding the hungry, seeing to the orphan.

Setting the two slopes side by side shows how the Qurʾān uses the same image for two separate works:

| | **al-Balad 90/11-12** (*ʿaqaba*) | **al-Jinn 72/17** (*ṣaʿad*) |
|---|---|---|
| The word | *al-ʿaqaba* — a steep pass | *ṣaʿad* — a slope hard to climb |
| Who climbs | The human being, by his own will | The person, being driven |
| The verb | *iqtaḥama* — to plunge in and dare it | *yasluk* — to drive in (in the passive position) |
| The content of the slope | Sacrifice: freeing, feeding, mercy | The punishment itself |
| Direction | Climbed | Made to climb |

**The same difficulty, in two separate places.** In al-Balad the difficulty is **chosen**; in al-Jinn the difficulty is **imposed.** And a relation can be set up between the two — I record this as my own reading, not as a transmitted report: **whoever does not climb the first slope is driven up the second.** The difficulty is present in either case; what changes is who chooses it.

### يَسْلُكْهُ — "He will drive him in"

**Root س-ل-ك**: to pass a thing into a place, to insert, to bring inside as one passes a thread. *Silk* — thread, a string. From the same root *sulūk* — to walk on a road.

The concrete image of the verb is threading a needle: to insert through a narrow place. In the Qurʾān this verb is used in the context of punishment and carries the idea of being passed through a narrow passage.

**The verb will return in verse 27** — there it will be used of God's **inserting / stationing** a *raṣad* before and behind the messenger. The sūra uses the same verb for two separate works: in one, driving into punishment; in the other, stationing a protection. This is another instance of the sūra's economy of vocabulary — the same happened with the word *raṣad* (9 and 27).

**Who is the agent?** The subject of the verb is not stated plainly. From the context it is understood to be God. It is transmitted that in some readings the verb is read in the first person plural (*naslukhu* — "We drive in"); this does not change the sense, only moves it into the plural of majesty.

## 72/18 — وَأَنَّ الْمَسَاجِدَ لِلَّهِ فَلَا تَدْعُوا مَعَ اللَّهِ أَحَدًا

*Wa anna'l-masājida li'llāhi fa-lā tadʿū maʿa'llāhi aḥadā*

"And that: the places of prostration belong to God; so do not call upon anyone along with God."

### The speaker becomes settled

This is the key verse of the grammatical question taken up at the head of the sūra. The command *"do not call upon"* could be read as a word the jinn said to their own people; but the flow and the cast of addressees show the sentence to be **the revelation's own address.** Indeed no divergence is transmitted over the fatḥa reading of the hamza in this verse.

### الْمَسَاجِد — places of prostration

**Root س-ج-د**: to bend, to fall to the ground, to bow the neck. *Masjid* is on the pattern of a noun of place: **the place where prostration is made.**

On the sense of the word in this verse there is more than one view in the classical commentaries:

| View | Ground | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Mosques — buildings of worship** | The settled sense of the word. The Qurʾān has the uses *"al-Masjid al-Ḥarām"*, *"masājida'llāh"* (al-Baqara 2/114, al-Tawba 9/18). | Places of worship are assigned to God; in them no one else is turned to. |
| **2. Places where prostration is made — everywhere** | The lexical sense of the word: the place of prostration. That prostration is not tied to a particular building. | Wherever prostration is made, that place is God's; there is no distinction of place. |
| **3. The limbs of prostration** | An explanation transmitted in the classical sources: the organs that touch the ground in prostration (forehead, hands, knees, feet) are called *masājid*. | Even a person's body does not belong to himself; the limbs that prostrate belong to God. |

All three explanations are found in the classical commentaries. **The first and second readings do not exclude one another** and are the ones that sit best with the context: the sentence gives a ruling about a place, and then says what is not to be done in that place.

The third explanation is possible in point of language but makes a weaker link with what follows (*"do not call upon"*). I transmit it; I do not prefer it.

**I make no definite preference between the first and second readings, but this has to be stated:** the second reading is more consonant with the sūra as a whole. For the sūra is not conducting a dispute about temples; it is speaking about **the address of a turning.** This preference is not binding.

### لِلَّهِ — "belong to God"

The **lām** here conveys **ownership and assignment**: belonging and appropriation. The sentence is a nominal sentence, without a verb — as was recorded in the sections on al-Fātiḥa and al-Ikhlāṣ, a nominal sentence conveys **fixity and continuity**. Not "they became God's" but "they are God's" — a state, not an event.

### فَلَا تَدْعُوا مَعَ اللَّهِ أَحَدًا

**فَ** — the particle of consequence. That the place belongs to God is the ground of the ruling: *since these places are His, therefore…*

**دَعَا** — root **د-ع-و**: to call, to summon, to ask for help. *Duʿāʾ* is to call to someone and ask something. The word's use in the Qurʾān is wide, and there are places where it is identified with **worship itself**.

**مَعَ اللَّهِ — "along with God".** The choice of this particle is very important and is generally passed over.

The verse does not say *"do not call upon any other than God"*. It says **"do not call upon anyone along with God"**.

The difference is this:

- *"other than God"* — forbids putting another **in the place** of the turning.
- *"along with God"* — forbids **adding another** to the turning.

The second is more comprehensive. For the Meccan mushrikūn were not rejecting God in any case; they accepted God and put others **beside** Him. The Qurʾān records this in several places: *"We serve them only that they may bring us near to God"* (al-Zumar 39/3 — this verse was cited in the section on al-Ikhlāṣ too).

That is, the prohibition is aimed at **association**, not at substitution. And this comes out at the same place as the finding set up in `002-bakara.md` in the discussion of *"andād"* (equals): *"One does not have to put a thing in God's place; it is enough to put it on the same line with Him."*

**أَحَدًا** — its third occurrence in the sūra, again in a negative context, again "anyone at all".

### The verse's place in the sūra

This verse is the **common solution** to two separate problems in the first half of the sūra:

- **In verse 6** human beings were seeking refuge with the jinn — a turning to the wrong address.
- **In verse 3** a consort and a child were being ascribed to God — a wrong conception.

Verse 18 cuts both off at once: the turning is single, and nothing is added to that turning.

And in verse 20 the Prophet will repeat the same sentence **from his own mouth**: *"I call only upon my Lord and I associate no one with Him."* That is, two verses after the command is given, the messenger of the one who gave it declares that he too obeys that command.

## 72/19 — وَأَنَّهُ لَمَّا قَامَ عَبْدُ اللَّهِ يَدْعُوهُ كَادُوا يَكُونُونَ عَلَيْهِ لِبَدًا

*Wa annahu lammā qāma ʿabdu'llāhi yadʿūhu kādū yakūnūna ʿalayhi libadā*

"And that: when the servant of God stood up to call upon Him, they were all but piled upon him like felt."

### قَامَ … يَدْعُوهُ — "stood up… calling upon Him"

The verb is from the root **ق-و-م** — the same root as *istaqāmū* three verses earlier. In a short span the sūra uses the same root for two separate works: **uprightness** (remaining on the line) and **standing** (rising for prayer).

*Qāma yadʿūhu* — "he stood up calling upon Him". A circumstantial clause. What is related is generally explained in the classical commentaries as **the night prayer**, and this is reasonable: the word *qiyām* has a settled link with prayer in the Qurʾān.

### عَبْدُ اللَّهِ — "the servant of God"

**Who?** The majority in the classical commentaries say this is **the Prophet**, and the context supports it: the scene the verse relates — someone standing up to pray and a crowd gathering around him — accords with the sūra as a whole, and that immediately after, in verse 20, the Prophet is told *"say: I call only upon my Lord"* reinforces this reading.

It is said that other possibilities are transmitted in a few sources. On these I cannot give a classification I am sure of; I transmit the view of the majority and find it reasonable.

**The point of the naming is the real matter.**

The verse does not say "Muḥammad". It does not say "Our messenger". It does not say "the Prophet". It says **"the servant of God"**.

This is the highest description the Qurʾān uses of the Prophet, and it is a deliberate choice. When the Qurʾān names the Prophet in the loftiest positions it regularly chooses the word **servant**:

- In relating the sending down of the revelation: *"what He sent down upon His servant"* (a like expression at al-Kahf 18/1; al-Furqān 25/1: *"who sent down the Furqān upon His servant"*).
- In making the challenge: *"if you are in doubt about what We have sent down upon Our servant"* (al-Baqara 2/23 — this verse was treated in `002-bakara.md`).
- In relating the night journey: *"who carried His servant by night"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/1).

**The pattern is this: the higher the station, the more the description moves towards "servant".**

And its function in this verse is very particular. The scene is this: a person is praying, a crowd is thronging around him. In such a scene the risk of the person's being **exalted** is at its highest — what draws those gathering around him is uncertain.

At precisely that moment the verse names the person **servant.** The man at the centre of the crowd is "the servant of God". And what is he doing? **He is calling upon Him** — that is, he too is asking for something, he too is in need.

This is the silent preparation for what the sūra will say plainly in verses 20-22: the person at the centre of the crowd has nothing in his hand.

### كَادُوا يَكُونُونَ — "they were all but"

**كَادَ** belongs in Arabic to the group called the **verbs of approximation**. Its function: to state that an event **came very close to happening but did not happen**. The English "almost" answers it well.

The predicate of the verb comes in the imperfect (*yakūnūna*), and this structure relates the event's remaining on the threshold.

**Why not "they became" but "they were all but"?** Because what is described is not a piling that took place but **a pressing that comes close to piling.** The scene is given frozen: the crowd is about to close over him, but has not quite done so.

This is a technique the Qurʾān often uses in relating a scene — giving the event in its uncompleted state and thereby holding the tension.

### عَلَيْهِ — "upon him"

The particle *ʿalā* conveys coming from above and pressing down. In the section on al-Fīl the function of this particle was taken up: punishment is sent "upon them"; a small particle draws the direction of the event.

Here too the direction is from above downward: the crowd rides **on top of** the one praying.

### لِبَدًا — like felt

**The root ل-ب-د** was analysed in full in `090-beled.md`, in the discussion of 90/6. The finding there: the concrete sense of the root is **to pile one on another, to stick together, to mat**; *libd/libda* is felt — the cloth obtained by wetting wool fibres and beating them so that they interlock, not woven but pressed; *lubda* is the matted mane on a lion's neck. And *lubad* does not mean simply "much" but **piled one on another, stuck together, become a single mass.**

**And that section already referred to this verse:** in relating the "felt-like" quality of the wealth at al-Balad 90/6, this verse was shown as the root's other occurrence in the Qurʾān and it was said: *"Not coins that can be counted one by one; a mass interlocked, indivisible."* This is the counterpart of that reference.

The word's function here is that analysis applied to human beings: **the crowd ceases to be separate persons and becomes a single mass.**

This image is extremely concrete. The property of felt is that the fibres can no longer be separated — in a woven cloth the threads can be pulled apart, in felt they cannot. By describing the crowd with this material, the verse pictures it as **a mass in which individuals cannot be picked out.**

### Who is the crowd, and what is its intent?

The verse does not say, and it has been read in two directions in the classical commentaries:

| Reading | Who the crowd is | Intent |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | The jinn | They throng to listen to the Qurʾān; wonder and curiosity. |
| **2** | The Meccans / the opponents | To hinder the Prophet, to silence him, to bear down on him. |

The verse is open to both readings and the choice of word supports this: *libad* is neither a positive nor a negative word; it conveys **density**, not intent.

**I make no definite preference.** But this can be recorded: on either reading the picture the verse gives is the same — **a person praying and a mass piling on top of him.** And the continuation of the sūra (20-22) gives what the Prophet is made to say against that mass: *I call only upon my Lord… I have nothing in my hand.*

That is, whatever the crowd's intent, the answer is the same: **the person at the centre is not in a position to carry what is loaded onto him.**

### What this says to the present (18-19)

The two verses side by side say something.

**Verse 18:** places of worship belong to God, no other addressee is joined to them.
**Verse 19:** while one person is praying, a crowd thrones about him as a mass.

The sequence does not look accidental. Immediately after the ruling about the address of a turning comes a scene in which **a human being might be put at the centre of the turning.** And that human being is named "the servant of God".

The side of this that looks on the present is one of the oldest risks of religious life: **the intermediary passing to the centre.** Gathering around the one who prays blurs the difference between him and the one prayed to. As the crowd grows, the person at the centre begins to resemble a source.

The sūra's solution is interesting: it does not disperse the crowd, does not censure the crowd. **It makes the person at the centre speak.** Across the next four verses the Prophet will enumerate, one by one, what he does not have in his hand.

---

# Four "quls": the messenger declaring his own position (20-25)

The last section of the sūra is built with four commands, and all four do the same work: **to make the Prophet say what he does not have in his hand.**

This is one of the most striking attitudes of the Qurʾān. The bearer of a message stands in the middle of the message and enumerates his own limits. And he does not say it of himself — he is **commanded** to say it.

The order of the four verses draws a descending line:

| Verse | What is declared |
|---|---|
| **20** | I too pray as you do; I do not associate |
| **21** | I can give you neither harm nor right judgement |
| **22** | No one can save me either; I too have no refuge |
| **25** | I do not know when it will be |

That is, in order: **my position, my power, my safety, my knowledge** — all four are limited.

## 72/20 — قُلْ إِنَّمَا أَدْعُو رَبِّي وَلَا أُشْرِكُ بِهِ أَحَدًا

*Qul innamā adʿū Rabbī wa lā ushriku bihī aḥadā*

"Say: I call only upon my Lord, and I associate no one with Him."

### إِنَّمَا — the particle of restriction

**Innamā** conveys in Arabic **restriction**: "only, solely". It assigns a ruling to one thing and cuts off what is outside it.

The sentence could have been framed without this particle: *"I call upon my Lord."* With the particle the sense becomes: **"What I do is only this."**

This is the direct answer to the scene in verse 19. A crowd has thronged, at the centre is a man. What is the man doing? *He is only praying.* Nothing else.

### أَدْعُو — "I call"

The verb returns from verse 18. There it was a command: *fa-lā tadʿū* — **do not call** (upon another along with God). Here it is a statement: *adʿū Rabbī* — **I call upon my Lord.**

The same verb, two verses apart: the one a prohibition, the other its application. The authority that gave the command records immediately after that the command is applied by the messenger.

**And the verb is imperfect:** a continuing, repeated state. Not "I called" but "I call, I am calling".

### رَبِّي — "my Lord"

Again the name *Rabb*, and again with **the first person singular possessive**: "**my** Lord". This construct will occur three times in the sūra: 20, 22 (*min dūnihī*), 25 (*Rabbī*).

The function of the possessive is this: the Prophet defines the relation between himself and God as a **relation of servanthood** — the same kind of relation as his addressees'. The person named "the servant of God" in verse 19 says the same thing here from his own mouth.

### وَلَا أُشْرِكُ بِهِ أَحَدًا

**أَشْرَكَ** — root **ش-ر-ك**: partnership. *Sharika*, *sharīk*, *mushtarak* are from the same root.

The sentence is very nearly an exact repetition of verse 2. The jinn had said: *"wa lan nushrika bi-Rabbinā aḥadā"* — "we shall never associate anyone with our Lord."

The Prophet says: *"wa lā ushriku bihī aḥadā"* — "and I associate no one with Him."

**The only meaningful difference between the two sentences is in the mood:**

| | **72/2 (the jinn)** | **72/20 (the Prophet)** |
|---|---|---|
| Mood | *lan nushrika* — **future**, definitively negative | *lā ushriku* — **general present**, continuing |
| Sense | "We shall never associate" | "I do not associate" |
| Position | Newly believing; giving an undertaking | Already so; stating a state |

The language of one newly believing is the future — he is giving a promise. The language of the messenger is the general present — he is stating a condition. The difference is small but separates the two positions exactly.

**And *aḥadā* is here again** — its fourth occurrence in the sūra, again in a negative sentence.

## 72/21 — قُلْ إِنِّي لَا أَمْلِكُ لَكُمْ ضَرًّا وَلَا رَشَدًا

*Qul innī lā amliku lakum ḍarran wa lā rashadā*

"Say: I have no power over any harm to you, nor over any right judgement."

### لَا أَمْلِكُ — "I do not possess / it is not in my power"

**Root م-ل-ك**: to own, to hold in hand, to be able to dispose. *Mulk*, *malik*, *mālik* are from the same root. In the section on al-Fātiḥa the distinction of *mālik / malik* was taken up.

The verb's sense here is wider than "to own": **to be in a position to dispose over something.** What we mean in English when we say "it is not in my hands."

That is, the sentence does not say "I do not do it"; it says **"I am not in a position to do it."** Not a choice but a statement of capacity.

### لَكُمْ — "for you"

Brought forward. In Arabic an element pulled forward from its normal place gains emphasis — this was taken up in the discussion of the fronting of *lahu* at al-Ikhlāṣ 112/4.

Here the fronting does this: **the addressee is directly targeted.** Not "I have no power over anything" but "I have no power over anything **for you**". It is said to the crowd, to those thronging, to those waiting.

### ضَرًّا وَلَا رَشَدًا — an unexpected pair

This is the most striking place in the sentence.

The **expected** form of this pattern in Arabic is: *"neither harm nor benefit"* — *ḍarran wa lā nafʿan.* The Qurʾān uses this pair elsewhere:

- *"Say: I have no power to bring benefit or harm even to myself, except as God wills"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/188).
- *"Say: I have no power to bring harm or benefit to myself"* (Yūnus 10/49).

In this verse the second term has **changed**: in place of *nafʿ* (benefit) has come **رَشَدًا (rushd)**.

**Why?**

Because the subject of the sūra is not benefit and harm but **finding the right way.** The word *rushd* has been at work throughout the sūra:

- **2:** the Qurʾān guides to *rushd*.
- **10:** did their Lord intend *rushd* for them?
- **14:** those who submitted sought out and found *rushd*.
- **21:** I cannot give you *rushd*.

**In the fourth and last occurrence the word is attached to a negation.** Throughout the sūra it was related where *rushd* comes from; now it is said where it does **not** come from: not from the messenger.

This is one of the sharpest rulings of the sūra. For the messenger is the one who **brings** *rushd*. But bringing and giving are not the same thing. The messenger carries the text; what the text will do in a person is not in his hands.

This distinction is set up many times in the Qurʾān and was treated in the sections on al-Aʿlā and al-Ghāshiya: the difference of form between the command *dhakkir* (remind) and the verb *yadhdhakkaru* (he remembers) drew the limit of responsibility in the grammar. Al-Ghāshiya 88/22: *"You are not a tyrant over them."* The same limit is drawn here with a different word.

**And note the order: first harm, then rushd.** That is, not in the order "I can do you neither evil nor good"; but in the order **"I can give neither punishment nor guidance."** These are the two things an addressee might expect of a messenger: a frightening power and a saving capacity. Both are refused.

## 72/22 — قُلْ إِنِّي لَن يُجِيرَنِي مِنَ اللَّهِ أَحَدٌ وَلَنْ أَجِدَ مِن دُونِهِ مُلْتَحَدًا

*Qul innī lan yujīranī mina'llāhi aḥadun wa lan ajida min dūnihī multaḥadā*

"Say: No one can protect me from God, and I shall find no refuge apart from Him."

### يُجِيرَنِي — "protects me / takes me under his protection"

**Root ج-و-ر.** And this root carries **one of the most concrete institutions** of the society in which the Qurʾān came down.

The original sense of the root is **neighbourhood**: *jār* — a neighbour. From here **جِوار (jiwār)** — neighbourhood, proximity, taking under protection. And **إجارة (ijāra)** or **أجار (ajāra)** — to take someone under one's protection.

**In pre-Islamic Arabia *jiwār* was a legal institution.** In the tribal order the individual's safety rested on the tribe he belonged to. Someone cut off from his tribe, come from a distant land, or fallen out with his own tribe could ask **jiwār** of a tribal chief or of a man of influence. If it was accepted, that person was thereafter under the protector's protection; to touch him counted as touching the protector.

Traces of this institution are plainly visible in the Qurʾān:

- *"If one of the mushrikūn **asks you for protection** (istajāraka), **grant him protection** (fa-ajirhu) until he hears the word of God"* (al-Tawba 9/6). A direct use of the institution.
- The word transmitted as Satan's at Badr: *"I am **your protector** (innī **jār**un lakum)"* — and then his withdrawal (al-Anfāl 8/48).
- And the most critical: *"He **protects**, and against Him no one is protected"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/88 — *wa huwa yujīru wa lā yujāru ʿalayh*). The same root, the same pattern.

That is, the verse speaks by using **a legal institution the addressee knows.** And what it says is: **this institution does not work before God.**

### إِنِّي لَن يُجِيرَنِي — the shock of the sentence

The addressee of the sentence has to be dwelt on.

This is not a warning. It does not say "no one can save you". It says **"no one can save me"**.

That is, the messenger gives himself as the example. And the example he gives is unprotectedness.

Its place in the sūra is very marked: in verse 6 human beings were seeking refuge with the jinn; in verse 12 the jinn were saying that they themselves cannot escape; now the Prophet says that he too cannot be protected. **The sūra completes the list of beings that cannot be a refuge** and adds its own messenger to the list last.

**لَن** again a definitive negation: never.

**أَحَدٌ** — its fifth occurrence in the sūra. And note: this is the word's only occurrence in the sūra in the **nominative** (in the position of subject); the other five are in the accusative (as object or complement). Here, that is, *aḥad* is in the position of the one acting, and again it is absent: *no one can save.*

### مُلْتَحَدًا — a place of refuge

**Root ل-ح-د.** Its concrete sense: **to lean to one side, to swerve from the line, to incline sideways.**

Its derivatives branch from this single image of the root:

- **لحد (laḥd)** — the hollow cut into the **side wall** of a grave. In Arabic two kinds of grave are distinguished: *ḍarīḥ* (a pit dug straight down in the middle) and *laḥd* (a niche opened in the side wall). *Laḥd* means exactly "swerving to the side".
- **إلحاد (ilḥād)** — to swerve from the line, to depart from the truth. The words *ilhad* and *mülhid* in Turkish come from here.

The uses of *ilḥād* in the Qurʾān:

- *"Leave those who **twist** His names"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/180).
- *"Those who **twist** Our signs are not hidden from Us"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/40).
- *"Whoever inclines there to **wrongdoing** by injustice…"* (al-Ḥajj 22/25).
- And in a linguistic context: *"…the tongue of the one they refer it to is foreign"* (al-Naḥl 16/103 — *yulḥidūna ilayh*, "to whom they **incline**").

**مُلْتَحَد** is a noun of place from the iftiʿāl form: **a place one swerves aside to and takes refuge in.**

The image of the word is very concrete: a hollow one dodges into **at the side** of a road, a recess, a cover. The place where one fleeing swerves out of his line of travel and hides.

**The word occurs in two places in the Qurʾān** and in both with the same wording: here and *"you will find no refuge apart from Him"* (al-Kahf 18/27). In both there is the construct *min dūnihī multaḥadā*.

### مِن دُونِهِ — "apart from Him"

*Dūn* conveys "below, on this side of, outside" — it occurred in *dūna dhālik* in verse 11 too.

The structure of the sentence is one that closes a door: *"I shall find no refuge apart from Him."* That is, seeking refuge is not forbidden; the **one address** where refuge is to be sought is stated.

And this sets up an exact contrast with verse 6:

| | **72/6** | **72/22** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Some among human beings | The Prophet |
| With whom refuge is sought | With some among the jinn | With no one — because there is no other address |
| Result | *rahaq* increased | The one refuge is settled |

The finding in the section on al-Falaq is completed here: the act of seeking refuge is not neutral; **the address is decisive.** Verse 6 showed the wrong address, verse 22 declares the singleness of the right one.

## 72/23 — إِلَّا بَلَاغًا مِّنَ اللَّهِ وَرِسَالَاتِهِ ۚ وَمَن يَعْصِ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ فَإِنَّ لَهُ نَارَ جَهَنَّمَ خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا أَبَدًا

*Illā balāghan mina'llāhi wa risālātih, wa man yaʿṣi'llāha wa rasūlahu fa-inna lahu nāra jahannama khālidīna fīhā abadā*

"Only a conveying from God and His messages. And whoever disobeys God and His messenger — for him is the fire of Hell, wherein they abide for ever."

### إِلَّا بَلَاغًا — what does the exception attach to?

This is the most disputed grammatical question of the verse. There are three main views:

| View | What the exception attaches to | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | *multaḥadā* in verse 22 | "I shall find no refuge apart from Him — **except a conveying.**" That is, the messenger's deliverance lies in doing his task. |
| **2** | *lā amliku* in verse 21 | "I can give you neither harm nor right judgement — **only a conveying**." This is the one thing I have. |
| **3** | A disconnected exception | "But there is this: a conveying from God…" — the particle of exception here functions as "however". |

**The second view is the most fluent in point of sense** and is widely defended in the classical commentaries: having enumerated what he does not have, the Prophet says the one thing he does have. **I find this reasonable; I do not offer it as a binding preference.**

And on this reading the verse becomes one of the most balanced sentences of the sūra: after the **absences** enumerated across four verses, a single **presence**. The one thing the messenger has in his hand: **to carry.**

### بَلَاغ — conveying

**Root ب-ل-غ**: to arrive at a place, to reach, to attain. *Balagha* — it reached. *Bāligh* — one who has **reached** the age of maturity. *Balāgha* — the art of bringing a word's sense to its target.

**بَلَاغ**, as a verbal noun, means "conveying"; as a noun, "what is conveyed, the message".

The idea at the core of the word is this: **the carrier is not the message itself.** The messenger's work is to bring something from one place to another. This is the positive expression of what is said in verse 21 of the sūra: I cannot give *rushd* — but I can bring what guides to *rushd*.

The Qurʾān draws this limit plainly in many places: the pattern *"Yours is only the conveying"* occurs in more than one place.

### وَرِسَالَاتِهِ — "and His messages"

**Root ر-س-ل**: to let loose, to send. This root was taken up in the section on al-Fīl: *rasūl* (a messenger sent), *risāla* (messengership), *mursal* (one sent).

**The word is plural:** *risālāt* — messages, things sent. Not the singular (*risāla*).

The plural has two readings, and both are found in the classical sources:

- **The multiplicity of the content:** not a single message but many rulings, reports and commands.
- **The continuity of the revelation:** the whole chain of the prophets. The Qurʾān's positioning of itself as the continuation of the earlier revelations.

**And the word will return in verse 28:** *"so that He may know that they have conveyed the messages of their Lord (risālāti Rabbihim)."* The same word, the same plural form. In its last two verses the sūra will repeat both key words of verse 23: *balāgh* → *ablaghū*, *risālāt* → *risālāt*.

### وَمَن يَعْصِ اللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُ — the connection

The second half of the sentence gives a ruling, and its link with the first half is this: **if the conveying has been done, the responsibility of responding has passed to the addressee.**

So the order is: the messenger has nothing in his hand but the conveying → the conveying has arrived → what comes after is the hearer's.

**عَصَىٰ** — root **ع-ص-ي**: to oppose, to rebel. Concerning the concrete sense of the root the lexicographers set up a link with the word *ʿaṣā* (a staff): to grasp a staff, to break away from the group.

**"God and His messenger"** — the two are named together. This is the natural consequence of the notion of conveying: whoever rejects the message has rejected both the sender of the message and its carrier.

### خَالِدِينَ فِيهَا أَبَدًا

**خُلْد** — root **خ-ل-د**: to remain, to be lasting. **أَبَد** — time without end.

A note on word order: the sentence begins with *"fa-inna **lahu**"* (singular) and continues with *"**khālidīna** fīhā"* (plural). This is because the particle *man* (whoever) is singular in wording and plural in sense — the same transition was present in verse 14 (*man aslama* → *fa-ulāʾika*).

## 72/24 — حَتَّىٰ إِذَا رَأَوْا مَا يُوعَدُونَ فَسَيَعْلَمُونَ مَنْ أَضْعَفُ نَاصِرًا وَأَقَلُّ عَدَدًا

*Ḥattā idhā raʾaw mā yūʿadūna fa-sa-yaʿlamūna man aḍʿafu nāṣiran wa aqallu ʿadadā*

"Until, when they see what they are promised, they will know whose helper is weaker and whose number fewer."

### حَتَّىٰ إِذَا — "until, when…"

*Ḥattā* states the **end point** of a process: "until, at last". The structure relates that the earlier situation continued up to a point and changed there.

That is: the rejection goes on and on — until at last that day comes.

### رَأَوْا — "when they see"

The verb is in the **past tense**, but because it comes after the conditional particle *idhā* it has a **future** sense. This structure is widespread in Arabic and conveys **certainty**: what is certain to happen is related as though it had happened. The *kānū* in verse 15 served the same function.

And that the verb should be a verb of **seeing** is meaningful. Throughout the sūra the matter of knowledge was argued: who knows what, and from where. Here the final form of knowledge comes: **seeing.** Where the argument ends is where there is not proof but direct witnessing.

### فَسَيَعْلَمُونَ — "they will know"

The particle **سَ (sīn)** conveys the future, and in Arabic this pattern (*sa-yaʿlamūna*) generally carries a **tone of threat**: "they will see", "they will find out". The tone of the English "they will find out."

And the verb is again a verb of knowing: *aʿlamū* — they will know. The sūra had recorded two confessions of not knowing (10 and 25); here a knowing is promised. But the knowledge promised is knowledge that **will no longer be of use** — this pattern was recorded in the sections on al-Ghāshiya and al-Fajr: *"a remembering that is no longer of any use."*

### مَنْ أَضْعَفُ نَاصِرًا وَأَقَلُّ عَدَدًا — an unanswered question

The sentence is in the form of a question but its answer is not given: **"whose helper is weaker, whose number fewer?"**

This is an effective technique in Arabic rhetoric. The answer is not said because there is no need to say it — and because it is not said it stands the more sharply in the reader's mind.

**And the question turns the addressee's own argument back on him.**

One of the objections levelled at the Prophet and the first Muslims in Mecca was about **numbers**: you are few, you are weak, there is no one behind you. The Qurʾān records this argument in several places — the word of the owner of the garden is its plainest instance: *"I am richer than you in wealth and mightier **in company**"* (al-Kahf 18/34).

The verse does not refute that argument; it **changes the measure.** It does not say "we are numerous too". It says: when that day comes, it will be understood who is really few.

### نَاصِرًا وَعَدَدًا — two specifications

Grammatically both words are **specifications**: the element that comes after a comparative and removes its indeterminacy. "Weaker" — in what? *In respect of a helper.* "Fewer" — in what? *In respect of number.*

**نَاصِر** — root **ن-ص-ر**: to help; especially to give support at a moment of difficulty. *Nuṣra*, *anṣār*, *naṣīr* are from the same root. This root was taken up in the section on Sūrat al-Naṣr.

**عَدَد** — root **ع-د-د**: to count. In the section on al-Humaza the verb *ʿaddada* (to count over and over) was treated.

**Two measures:** **quality** (the strength of the helper) and **quantity** (number). In the society of the Jāhiliyya what determined a person's weight was exactly these two things: the men of influence behind him and the number of men behind him. The verse names both and puts both on the scale.

### عَدَدًا — preparing the sūra's last word

The word occurs here and will return **in verse 28, as the last word of the sūra**: *"wa aḥṣā kulla shayʾin **ʿadadā**"* — "and He has counted all things by number."

The relation between the two occurrences sets up one of the sūra's most elegant closes:

| | **72/24** | **72/28** |
|---|---|---|
| Who counts | Human beings (their own crowds) | God |
| What is counted | Partisans, men | **All things** |
| Result | Who is few will be understood on that day | It has already been counted |

That is, to a society boasting of numbers the sūra first says "you will see who is few", and then shows the real owner of the work of counting. **A claim of superiority in numbers closes before the one who counts all things.**

---

## 72/25 — قُلْ إِنْ أَدْرِي أَقَرِيبٌ مَّا تُوعَدُونَ أَمْ يَجْعَلُ لَهُ رَبِّي أَمَدًا

*Qul in adrī a-qarībun mā tūʿadūna am yajʿalu lahu Rabbī amadā*

"Say: I do not know whether what you are promised is near, or whether my Lord will set for it a term."

### إِنْ أَدْرِي — "I do not know"

**إِنْ** is used here not as a conditional particle but as a **negative**: *in adrī* = *mā adrī* = "I do not know". This use of *in* is settled in Arabic and occurs in several places in the Qurʾān.

**And the verb is the verb of verse 10:** the root *د-ر-ي*. The jinn said *lā nadrī*; the Prophet says *in adrī*.

This parallel was taken up at verse 10; here it is completed. The sūra has it said from two separate mouths that the unseen is not known:

- **The jinn (10):** we do not know what is intended for those on earth.
- **The Prophet (25):** I do not know when what is promised will come.

**And the next verse lays down the ruling:** only God knows the unseen.

That is, the sūra sets up a sequence: **two instances, then the rule.** Beings supposed to be a source of divination do not know; God's messenger does not know; because the unseen is His alone.

### أَقَرِيبٌ … أَمْ … أَمَدًا — the structure of verse 10

The sentence uses **the same grammatical pattern** as verse 10: *a- … am …* — "…or…?"

| | **72/10 (the jinn)** | **72/25 (the Prophet)** |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | *a-sharrun … am arāda* | *a-qarībun … am yajʿalu* |
| What is unknown | What will happen | When it will happen |
| Named in the second half | *Rabbuhum* — "their Lord" | *Rabbī* — "my Lord" |

And one further point in common: **in both verses the name "Lord" is named in the second half.** The owner of what is unknown is named with the name that nurtures.

### أَمَدًا — "a term"

**Root أ-م-د**: an interval of time — and especially an interval that **has an end.** *Amad* is a period whose beginning and end are settled.

This is what distinguishes it from *zamān*: *zamān* conveys duration in general, *amad* a period that is **limited and whose ends are known.** The classical lexicographers also explain *amad* as "the terminus of a thing".

Its occurrences in the Qurʾān:

- *"A long term (al-amad) passed over them and their hearts hardened"* (al-Ḥadīd 57/16).
- *"That We might know which of the two parties better reckoned the term (amadan) of their stay"* (al-Kahf 18/12).
- *"He wishes there were a long distance (amadan baʿīdā) between himself and it"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/30).

**The choice of the word says something:** even in saying "or will it be long", the verse speaks of a period that **has an end.** That is, what is uncertain is **when** it will be, not **whether** it will be. Both halves of the sentence assume the event will take place: near, or far?

This draws exactly the scope of the declaration of ignorance. **The Prophet does not know the date, not the event.**

### What this says to the present (20-25)

**First: being able to say "I do not know" strengthens authority rather than weakening it.**

The accustomed expectation is this: someone bearing a teaching should be able to answer every question of the teaching he bears; to say that he does not know weakens what he bears.

The sūra does the opposite. And its logic is plain: **whoever draws the limit of his knowledge makes the knowledge within the limit trustworthy.** A source that answers every question has no answer that can be checked; a source that stops somewhere and says "this is not my field" makes the word within its field weighable.

This is the practical consequence of the matter of knowledge the sūra has worked on from the start.

**Second: separating what is in one's hand from what is not.** What is enumerated across the four verses is not a list of absences but a **description of a task**. The one thing the messenger has in his hand has been stated: *balāgh* — to carry. Everything outside it — the outcome, the timing, the addressee's response — is someone else's business.

To know the limits of a work makes that work doable. Whoever does not know the limit either goes under a load he cannot carry or drops what he could have carried.

**Third: the person at the centre of the crowd making himself small.** In verse 19 there was a scene: a man praying and a mass piling upon him. These four verses are the answer to that scene. And the form of the answer is striking — the crowd is not dispersed, not warned, not censured. The person at the centre speaks and corrects his own position.

This is an answer given to the oldest problem of religious structures: **the intermediary passing to the centre** is most often prevented, or not prevented, by the intermediary's own attitude.

# The close of the sūra: the limit of the unseen (26-28)

The last three verses are a single sentence and settle the whole matter of the sūra in a ruling. In verse 25 the Prophet said "I do not know"; verse 26 says **why** this is so.

---

## 72/26 — عَالِمُ الْغَيْبِ فَلَا يُظْهِرُ عَلَىٰ غَيْبِهِ أَحَدًا

*ʿĀlimu'l-ghaybi fa-lā yuẓhiru ʿalā ghaybihī aḥadā*

"The Knower of the unseen; and He gives no one access to His unseen."

### عَالِمُ الْغَيْبِ — "the Knower of the unseen"

**Word order.** The sentence begins with a construct and attaches to the word *Rabbī* in the preceding verse: "my Lord… is the Knower of the unseen." The grammarians count this either an adjective of *Rabbī* or the predicate of an implied subject; the sense does not change.

What matters is that **it has been brought forward.** The sentence does not say "He knows the unseen"; it is framed as **"the Knower of the unseen is He"**. In Arabic this structure conveys **restriction** — this was taken up in the section on al-Ikhlāṣ in the discussion of the definite article of *al-Ṣamad*.

### الْغَيْب — the unseen

**The root غ-ي-ب** was taken up in `002-bakara.md`, in the discussion of 2/3. The finding there: the sense of the root is **to disappear from sight, to be covered** — the setting of the sun is called *ghaybūba*; and the critical distinction is this: **the unseen is not what does not exist but what is not seen.**

This distinction works exactly here. The sentence does not say "God knows the things that are not known" — such a sentence would be empty. It says **"the unseen field is His."** That is, the unseen is not a lack of knowledge; it is a **field**, and the owner of that field is known.

And note: in its second occurrence the word comes **with a possessive**: *غَيْبِهِ* — **"His unseen".** The unseen, that is, is not an ownerless unknowability; it is a field belonging to someone.

**This is the sentence that sets in place the whole of the sūra's argument about knowledge.** The jinn had felt out the heaven and found it closed (8-9). The jinn had said they do not know (10). The Prophet had said he does not know (25). Now the reason is given: **that field belongs to another.**

**The nearness between ج-ن-ن and غ-ي-ب** is worth noticing here too. The root of the word that gives the sūra its name is "to be covered"; the root of the word at the sūra's close is "to disappear from sight". That is, the sūra opens with **covered beings** and closes with **covered knowledge.** I record this as an observation; the two roots are distinct, but their shared field of meaning is plain.

### فَلَا يُظْهِرُ — "He gives no access"

**Root ظ-ه-ر**: the outer face of a thing, its back, its visible side. *Ẓāhir* — what is visible (its opposite *bāṭin*). *Ẓahr* — the back.

The pattern **أَظْهَرَ عَلَىٰ** carries a particular sense in Arabic: **to raise someone above a thing, to give him mastery over it, to give him access to it.**

The image is this: you cannot see behind a wall; **if you are lifted onto it** you see. *Iẓhār ʿalā* is exactly this: to be raised to a point of view.

That is, the verse describes knowledge of the unseen as a matter of **position.** Knowledge is not given — **a point of view** is given. And no one can climb to that point by himself; he must be lifted.

**The verb is imperfect:** continuity. Not "He did not give access" but "He does not give" — a general and continuing ruling.

**أَحَدًا** — the word's **sixth and last** occurrence in the sūra (2, 7, 18, 20, 22, 26). Again in a negative context, again "anyone at all". The word is one of the most repeated meaningful words of the sūra and each time states an absence; a table of the six occurrences is given in the section on sound at the end of the sūra.

### The Qurʾān's other rulings

This principle is laid down in the Qurʾān plainly and in more than one place:

- *"Say: no one in the heavens or on the earth knows the unseen except God"* (al-Naml 27/65).
- *"God will not give you access to the unseen; but God chooses of His messengers whom He wills"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/179). **This verse sets up almost exactly the same structure as al-Jinn 72/26-27:** a general prohibition + the exception of the messenger.

---

## 72/27 — إِلَّا مَنِ ارْتَضَىٰ مِن رَّسُولٍ فَإِنَّهُ يَسْلُكُ مِن بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِ رَصَدًا

*Illā mani'rtaḍā min rasūlin fa-innahu yasluku min bayni yadayhi wa min khalfihī raṣadā*

"Except a messenger with whom He is well pleased; and then He stations watchers before him and behind him."

### إِلَّا مَنِ ارْتَضَىٰ — "except one with whom He is well pleased"

**Root ر-ض-و**: to be pleased, to be content, to approve and accept. This root was taken up in the sections on al-Bayyina and al-Ghāshiya; the reciprocity of *riḍā* was dwelt on there.

**اِرْتَضَىٰ** is in the iftiʿāl form and conveys here **choosing and approving**: not at random but by preference.

The exception, then, is bound by two conditions:

1. **Being a messenger** (*min rasūlin*).
2. **Being chosen** (*irtaḍā*).

The second condition limits the first as well: not every messenger, but the messenger **with whom He is pleased.** And the subject of the verb is God — the one who chooses is not the messenger himself.

**This opens the door of knowledge of the unseen and narrows it at the same moment.** The door exists but its lock is on the outside: no one can enter by his own decision.

### The link with the head of the sūra

This verse is the **rule** of verse 1.

In verse 1 the Prophet said *"it was revealed to me"* — he had received a report of an event he could not see. In that section this was recorded: "The Prophet does not know the unseen; he knows what is notified to him of the unseen."

Verse 27 is the general expression of that sentence. The sūra opens with an instance and closes with the rule:

| | **72/1** | **72/26-27** |
|---|---|---|
| What | An event: the jinn's listening was notified | A rule: the unseen is opened only to a chosen messenger |
| Mood | Passive: *ūḥiya* — "it was revealed" | Active: *lā yuẓhiru* — "He does not give access" |
| Emphasis | The **manner of the knowledge's coming** | The **owner of the knowledge** |

### فَإِنَّهُ يَسْلُكُ — "He stations"

**The verb returns from verse 17.** There it was *yaslukhu ʿadhāban ṣaʿadā* — "He drives him into a mounting punishment". Here it is *yasluku … raṣadā* — "He stations watchers".

The same verb, in two separate works. The root's sense (to pass a thing into a place, to insert) is the same in both; what changes is what is inserted where: in the one a person into punishment, in the other a guard onto a road.

**Who is the agent?** The pronoun refers to God (it is transmitted that in some commentaries it is referred to the messenger, but the context indicates God: the subject of the verb *irtaḍā* is He too).

### مِن بَيْنِ يَدَيْهِ وَمِنْ خَلْفِهِ — "before him and behind him"

*Bayna yadayhi* — literally "between his two hands", that is, **in front of him**. *Khalfihī* — **behind him**.

This pair is a widespread pattern in the Qurʾān and conveys **encompassment**: all the directions of a thing. The wholeness we mean in English by "front and back".

To whom does the pronoun refer? There are two possibilities:

| View | The pronoun | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | To the messenger | Watchers are set before and behind the messenger; the messenger is protected. |
| **2** | To the revelation / to the unseen notified | Watchers are set before and behind what is notified; the message is protected. |

The two readings do not exclude one another; to protect the messenger is to protect the message.

### رَصَدًا — the word of verse 9 has returned

**This is the most elegant internal link of the sūra** and was pointed to at verse 9; here it is completed.

The word is from the root **ر-ص-د** and was taken up at verse 9: to watch, to wait in ambush; *mirṣād* (a place of watching, al-Fajr 89/14), *marṣad* (al-Tawba 9/5).

Now let us set the two occurrences side by side:

| | **72/9** | **72/27** |
|---|---|---|
| The sentence | *yajid lahu shihāban **raṣadā*** | *yasluku … **raṣadā*** |
| Against whom | The one trying to steal knowledge | The one trying to spoil the revelation |
| Its function | **To block** — to close the entrance | **To protect** — to secure the carriage |
| Its position | At the gate of the heaven | Before and behind the messenger |

**The same watching, at the two ends of the channel.**

This completes the sūra's argument on the matter of knowledge. The question asked throughout the sūra was: *where does this word come from?* And the addressee's supposition was: *from the jinn, from where the soothsayers get theirs.*

The answer is given in two steps:

1. **That channel is closed** (8-9). And it is those who used the channel who say so.
2. **This channel, however, is guarded** (27). With watchers before it and behind it.

That is, the revelation comes by a road that is both **closed to illicit entry** and **guarded while it is carried.** The Qurʾān sets up this claim plainly elsewhere too: *"The satans did not bring it down… They are kept far from hearing"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/210-212); *"It is not the word of an accursed satan"* (al-Takwīr 81/25).

---

## 72/28 — لِّيَعْلَمَ أَن قَدْ أَبْلَغُوا رِسَالَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ وَأَحَاطَ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ وَأَحْصَىٰ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ عَدَدًا

*Li-yaʿlama an qad ablaghū risālāti Rabbihim wa aḥāṭa bi-mā ladayhim wa aḥṣā kulla shayʾin ʿadadā*

"So that He may know that they have conveyed the messages of their Lord. He has encompassed what is with them, and has counted all things by number."

### لِّيَعْلَمَ — "so that He may know": to whom does the pronoun refer?

This is the last grammatical dispute of the sūra, and there are several views in the classical commentaries:

| View | The subject of the verb | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **God** | "So that He may **know** that the messengers have conveyed" — that is, the actual realisation of the knowledge, the event's being known as having taken place. |
| **2** | **The messenger** | "So that the messenger may know that those before him conveyed" — or: that he may know that he too is watched over and that his conveying is protected. |
| **3** | The watchers, or a general subject | "So that it may be known." |

**The first view is the most widespread** but carries a theological difficulty: God's learning afterwards something He did not know beforehand cannot be conceived. The classical theologians resolve this thus: the *ʿilm* here is not **the coming to be of knowledge** but **the coming to pass of what is known** — that is, "so that what He knew would happen should actually happen and be known as such". Like patterns occur elsewhere in the Qurʾān (expressions of the type *"so that We may know / try which of you is best in deed"*).

**The second view sits more easily with the context:** the preceding verse spoke of the protection of the messenger; this verse gives the **aim** of that protection — so that the messenger may be sure the conveying has arrived without loss.

**I make no definite preference.** Both readings have been defended in the classical commentaries and neither changes the sūra's conclusion: **the conveying has been completed and put on record.**

### أَبْلَغُوا رِسَالَاتِ رَبِّهِمْ — the two words of verse 23

**Both come from verse 23:**

- *balāghan* (23) → *ablaghū* (28). The same root **ب-ل-غ**.
- *risālātih* (23) → *risālāti Rabbihim* (28). The same word, the same plural form.

In verse 23 the one thing the messenger has in his hand was stated: **the conveying.** Verse 28 records that this work **has been done.**

That is, the sūra defines the task of messengership in one sentence and five verses later registers that the task has been completed. **The description of the task and the record of its completion are within the same sūra.**

**The verb is plural:** *ablaghū* — "they conveyed". Not one messenger but messengers. The ruling, that is, concerns the whole chain of the prophets.

And the Qurʾān describes this task elsewhere thus: *"Those who convey the messages of God, and fear Him, and fear no one but God"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/39). The same word (*risālāt*), the same verb (*yuballighūna*).

### وَأَحَاطَ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ — "He has encompassed what is with them"

**The root ح-و-ط** was taken up in `002-bakara.md`, in the discussion of 2/81. The finding there: the root means **to surround, to enclose, to shut in**, and from the same root comes **ḥāʾiṭ** (a wall). There the word was used of sin's encompassing a person and this was recorded: *"in the first case the person still has some point from which he can look at his own error… in the second, the outside has become invisible."*

Here the same root is used of **knowledge**, and the image is reversed:

| | **al-Baqara 2/81** | **al-Jinn 72/28** |
|---|---|---|
| What encompasses | Sin | God's knowledge |
| What is encompassed | The person | Everything with the messengers |
| Result | No way out — being closed in | Nothing left outside — completeness |

The same verb, in two separate places: in one, to be encompassed is a condemnation; in the other, a completeness. The image of the wall is in both; what changes is whom the wall encloses.

**بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ** — "what is with them". *Ladā* is an adverb conveying **beside, in the presence of**, and it is said to express a closer adjacency than *ʿinda*.

That is, what is encompassed is what the messengers **carry, hold in hand, have beside them.** The message itself and everything belonging to it.

### وَأَحْصَىٰ كُلَّ شَيْءٍ عَدَدًا — "and has counted all things by number"

**Root ح-ص-ي.** And the concrete sense of the root is astonishingly tangible: **حَصَاة (ḥaṣāt)** — **a pebble.**

*Iḥṣāʾ* is to count with pebbles.

This is the oldest technique of counting: one stone for each object; when the stones are done, the objects are done too. This is how a shepherd counted his flock, how a merchant counted his goods — a handful of pebbles, a pouch, a notch. The word carries the notion of number in its state of being **tied to body and object.**

From here *iḥṣāʾ* comes to mean "to count completely and one by one". *Iḥsâiyyât*, the Turkish word for **"statistics"**, comes from this root too.

Its occurrences in the Qurʾān show the load of the word:

- *"What a book this is! It leaves out nothing small or great but has **counted** it"* (al-Kahf 18/49).
- *"He has indeed encompassed them and **counted** them one by one"* (Maryam 19/94 — *wa ʿaddahum ʿaddā*).
- *"And everything We have **counted** in a clear register"* (Yā Sīn 36/12).
- *"And everything We have **counted** in writing"* (al-Nabaʾ 78/29).

**عَدَدًا** — grammatically a specification, or a verbal noun standing in place of an absolute object. Its function is to reinforce the verb *iḥṣāʾ*: not merely "He has counted" but **"by number, in counting"** — rounding nothing off, passing over nothing in the lump.

This reinforcement is not idle. *Iḥṣāʾ* already means to count; the adding of *ʿadadā* stresses that the counting is **unit by unit.**

### The last word of the sūra

And the sūra ends here: **عَدَدًا.**

In verse 24 the same word occurred in the context of human beings counting their own crowds: *"whose number is fewer"*. Now it returns as the last word — this time the one counting is God and what is counted is **all things.**

The close of the sūra thus makes a reversal:

> A society boasting of numbers is reminded **to whom number belongs.**

And this ties back to the first half of the sūra as well. The jinn's report was given item by item, counted off as *wa anna… wa anna…* — like a deposition. The sūra begins with a counting and ends with a counting.

## The sound texture of the sūra

The verse-ending of this sūra is one of the most uniform instances in the Qurʾān and deserves recording separately.

**All twenty-eight verses end with the same pattern of sound:** an open syllable + the lengthening *-ā*, and nearly all with **د (dāl)** or sounds close to it:

*ʿajabā, aḥadā, waladā, shaṭaṭā, kadhibā, rahaqā, aḥadā, shuhubā, raṣadā, rashadā, qidadā, harabā, rahaqā, rashadā, ḥaṭabā, ghadaqā, ṣaʿadā, aḥadā, libadā, aḥadā, rashadā, multaḥadā, abadā, ʿadadā, amadā, aḥadā, raṣadā, ʿadadā.*

**Twenty-eight verses, a single verse-ending.** In the Qurʾān the verse-ending of the long sūras generally changes with the sections; in the short sūras a single ending is widespread, but in a sūra of this length such tight unity of sound is striking.

**The count:** **nineteen** of the twenty-eight verses end directly with the *-dā* sound (*aḥadā, waladā, raṣadā, rashadā, qidadā, ṣaʿadā, libadā, multaḥadā, abadā, ʿadadā, amadā*…). The rest close on the same measure and with the same lengthening.

**What is the effect of this?**

First, **the effect of listing.** Sentences ending in the same sound, coming one after another, set up a rhythm of enumeration. And the first half of the sūra already consisted of items strung out as *wa anna… wa anna…*. The sound reinforces what the structure is doing: **this is a deposition.**

Second, **a sense of closure.** Each verse is sealed with the same sound. This is the extended form of the finding recorded in the section on al-Ikhlāṣ: *"four different rulings, sealed with the same sound."* Here twenty-eight rulings, with the same sound.

Third — and this is particular to this sūra — **the last word's coinciding with the sound.** The sūra ends with *ʿadadā* (number); and the verse-ending itself has set up a rhythm of counting. Sound and sense close at the same place.

### The six occurrences of the word أَحَدًا

The most repeated meaningful word in the sūra is **أَحَدًا**: 2, 7, 18, 20, 22, 26.

And **in all six it is inside a negative sentence**:

| Verse | Sentence | What is refused |
|---|---|---|
| **2** | "We shall associate **no one** with our Lord" | Association |
| **7** | "God will raise **no one** [they supposed]" | (The content of a false supposition) |
| **18** | "Do not call upon **anyone** along with God" | Adding to the turning |
| **20** | "I associate **no one** with Him" | Association (from the messenger's mouth) |
| **22** | "**No one** can save me" | Protection |
| **26** | "He gives **no one** access to His unseen" | A sharing of knowledge |

**The word serves as a sweep across the sūra.** At each occurrence it says "there is no one" in a different field: in association, in prayer, in protection, in knowledge.

And this forms a pair with Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. In the section on al-Ikhlāṣ this was recorded: *"the sūra closes by using the same word once for Him and once for anything that might be set against Him."*

Sūrat al-Jinn is the **extended form** of that second use: the *"lam yakun lahu kufuwan aḥad"* said once in al-Ikhlāṣ is repeated here across six separate fields.

| | **al-Ikhlāṣ 112** | **al-Jinn 72** |
|---|---|---|
| Occurrences of *aḥad* | 2 (one an attribute, one "no one") | 6 (all "no one") |
| Method | To close by describing | To sweep field by field |
| Result | He has no equal | There is no position that can take His place |

## The sūra as a whole — the single argument of two layers

The twenty-eight verses of the sūra are a long answer given to a single question: **where does this word come from?**

In Mecca the alternative answer to this question was known: like the word of the soothsayers, from unseen beings. The sūra makes three separate moves to reject this possibility, and the three complete one another:

**First move — closing the channel (8-9).** The road by which knowledge was stolen is now closed. And it is those who used that road who say so.

**Second move — questioning the capacity of the source (10).** The unseen beings do not know in any case. *Lā nadrī.* The party supposed to be the source declares its own ignorance.

**Third move — describing the real channel (26-27).** The unseen belongs to God alone and is given only to a messenger He has chosen, with his front and back guarded.

And in between, **the carrier himself enumerates his limits (20-25):** I have no power, no safety, my knowledge is limited. The one thing in my hand: to carry.

That is, the sūra sets up this: **the word is neither stolen knowledge nor the product of its carrier.**

The second layer of this argument is that the first half of the sūra comes from the mouths of the jinn. The jinn are speaking — and every sentence they speak refutes the claims made about them:

| The claim (in Mecca) | The jinn's word |
|---|---|
| The jinn are of God's lineage | "Our Lord has taken neither a consort nor a child" (3) |
| The jinn are protectors, refuge is sought with them | "Nor can we frustrate Him by fleeing" (12) |
| The jinn know the unseen | "We do not know" (10) |
| "Jinn" means an evil being | "Among us there are the righteous too" (11, 14) |

**Four claims cut off by four sentences** — and in none of them is it said "no, it is not so". The party is simply made to speak.

This is the sūra's most striking choice of method and it explains the sūra's structure: **that the first layer is given from the mouths of the jinn is not a rhetorical ornament but the argument itself.**

## What this says to the present

**First: asking after the source of knowledge comes before discussing its content.**

This is the whole method of the sūra. Before deciding whether a claim is true, it is asked **by what road that claim came.** And if that road cannot be described, there is no need to enter into a discussion of the content.

This measure works independently of the order of divination the sūra addressed. Knowledge whose source cannot be shown is, however reasonable its content may look, **uncheckable** knowledge. The sūra's making the jinn speak is precisely one way of making that check: the party supposed to be the source was asked, and an answer was received.

**Second: an intermediary declaring his own limit.**

The four "quls" in the last section of the sūra describe an attitude that holds today too: **not confusing oneself with what one carries.**

The messenger says that the text he brings guides to *rushd* (verse 2, from the mouths of the jinn). But he also says that he himself cannot give *rushd* (21). The two do not conflict: the value of what he carries does not turn into a power of the carrier.

Wherever this distinction is lost the same thing happens: a mass forms around the intermediary (the *libad* of verse 19), and that mass begins to take the intermediary for the source. The sūra's solution is not to scold the crowd; it is to make the one at the centre state his own position.

**Third: "I do not know" said twice.**

The confession of ignorance in the sūra comes from two separate mouths: from the jinn (10) and from the Prophet (25). Both are parties in high positions — the one was counted a source of hidden knowledge, the other carries revelation.

The side of this that looks on the present is the matter of the authority of knowledge. The trustworthiness of a source is measured not by **its answering every question** but by **its being able to show where it cannot answer.** A source that does not draw its limit makes the word within its limit unverifiable too.

**Fourth: what abundance is.**

Verses 16-17 give the ruling that is least dwelt on but most practical: **the return for uprightness is abundance, and abundance is a testing.**

The two sentences do not cancel one another. Uprightness does genuinely produce a return — the verse says so plainly. But the return produced is not a certificate of reward; it is a new examination paper.

The finding set up in the section on al-Fajr is completed here: whoever counts abundance divine approval is bound to count straitness a divine refusal, and is condemned by his own measure. Sūrat al-Jinn gives a third option: **abundance is neither approval nor refusal; it is the examination itself.**

**Fifth: a community's being able to make the distinction within itself.**

The hardest sentences in the jinn's report are the ones about themselves: our fool (4), we had supposed (5), we were separate roads (11), among us are deviators (14).

This is a work no community does easily. Generally one of two extremes is chosen: wholesale defence or wholesale rejection. The third road the sūra shows — **to distinguish** — is harder, because it requires a measure, and using a measure requires weighing oneself too.

And this comes out at the same place as the principle laid down from the start in `USLUP.md`: **the verdict is given not to a group but to an attribute.** Sūrat al-Jinn shows that principle applied by a community to itself.

## Where this section does not speak with certainty

At several points across the text certain language was deliberately not used; the reader should know these:

- **The place and time of the event.** The accounts concerning the return from Ṭāʾif and Nakhla were transmitted as reports; it was not said "the sūra came down upon this". No number or name was given about the number of the jinn, where they came from, or their identities — because the Qurʾān does not give them and the reports do not agree.

- **Whether al-Jinn 72/1-15 and al-Aḥqāf 46/29-32 relate the same event.** The two passages were compared; no claim of identity was set up.

- **Whether the Prophet met with the jinn separately.** The material of reports on this matter was not classified; it was only recorded that verse 1 speaks not of a meeting but of a notification.

- **What the "wa anna" chain attaches to, and the variant reading.** Two views were given in a table; no name of an imam of the readings was given, because I am not sure which imam took which road. That the transition falls somewhere between 15 and 16 was said as a preference, with the note that it is not binding.

- **Who "our fool" in verse 4 is.** The view that it is Iblīs was transmitted; no ruling was issued.

- **The agent of *fa-zādūhum* in verse 6.** Two readings were given in a table; the first was preferred and it was written that this is not binding.

- **The addressee of "just as you supposed" in verse 7.** Three possibilities were given; no definite assignment was made.

- **Whether the verb *yabʿath* in verse 7 is "to raise" or "to send a messenger".** Two readings were given; one was found somewhat stronger but the preference was not settled.

- **The physical counterpart of verses 8-9.** No scientific matching was made and the ground for not making one was written out plainly. The classical exegetes' reading of "the cutting off of divination" was transmitted as a general tendency; no ruling was issued on the exact time and extent of the change.

- **The sense of "masājid" in verse 18.** Three views were given in a table; it was said that the second reading is more consonant with the sūra and stated that this is not binding.

- **Who "the servant of God" in verse 19 is, and the crowd's intent.** The view of the majority (the Prophet) was transmitted and found reasonable; on who the crowd is and what its intent was, two readings were given and no preference made.

- **What the exception in verse 23 attaches to.** Three views were given; the second was found reasonable and was not offered as a binding preference.

- **The referent of the pronoun in "before him and behind him" in verse 27.** Two readings were given; it was stated that they do not exclude one another.

- **The subject of the verb *li-yaʿlama* in verse 28.** Three views were given in a table; no preference was made, and the theological difficulty and its classical resolution were recorded.

- **The nature of the being of the jinn.** What the Qurʾān gives and what it does not give were listed separately. The material of accounts, descriptions, numbers and practices from outside the Qurʾān was deliberately not entered into; the ground was written out.

- **The variant readings of the verb *yakhāfu* in verse 13** were stated to be transmitted but were not classified.

- **That the word جد bears the sense "grandfather" and stands in a verse where descent is denied** was recorded as an observation, not as an exegesis.

- **The relation set up between the *ʿaqaba* of al-Balad 90/11-12 and the *ṣaʿad* of al-Jinn 72/17** was marked as my own reading, not as a transmitted report.
