# 57. Sūrat al-Ḥadīd

Twenty-nine verses. Medinan. It takes its name from the word **الحديد** in verse 25 — the only sūra in the Qurʾān that bears the name of a metal.

That name is remarkable in itself. Sūra names in the Qurʾān usually come either from a kind of creature (al-Baqara, al-Naḥl, al-Naml, al-ʿAnkabūt), or from a natural event (al-Raʿd, al-Zilzāl, al-Qāriʿa), or from a concept (al-Ikhlāṣ, al-Qadr, al-Takāthur), or from a person or a people (Yūsuf, Nūḥ, Quraysh). The name of a **metal** — and a metal that human beings work into tools and weapons at that — looks at first glance as though it does not belong on that list.

But once the sūra is read the name settles into place. For this sūra is built from beginning to end on **the things one holds in one's hand**: property, possession, inheritance, debt, measure, the balance, weapons. And behind all of them lies a single question: *is what is in your hand yours?*

## What does the sūra do?

The skeleton of the sūra can be set out thus:

1. **The introduction** (1-6): who is speaking. For six verses nothing but the description of God — dominion, life and death, four names, six days, His being with them, night and day. Not a single command.
2. **The first command** (7): *believe and spend.* And the ground of the spending is loaded onto a single word: **mustakhlafīn**.
3. **Two questions** (8, 10): "What is the matter with you that you do not believe?" and "What is the matter with you that you do not spend?" The same pattern, two different subjects.
4. **A measure of timing** (10): the same act, at a different moment, of different weight.
5. **The offer of a loan** (11): *who will lend God a goodly loan?*
6. **The scene of separation** (12-15): light, light asked for as a loan, and the wall raised between them.
7. **The breaking point** (16): *has the time not come?* — and the address is not to those outside but to those within.
8. **Dead ground** (17): the earth coming alive, immediately after the hardened heart.
9. **Alms and witness** (18-19).
10. **A description of this world** (20): five words, followed by the likeness of a farmer.
11. **The command to race** (21).
12. **Decree and the balance of feeling** (22-24).
13. **The centre of the sūra** (25): the Book, the balance and iron — all three together.
14. **The prophets and monasticism** (26-27).
15. **The close** (28-29): a double share, a light, and in whose hand the bounty lies.

It will be noticed that twice the sūra builds a sentence opening with "**اعلموا**" (know that): in verses 17 and 20. Both do the same work — they set a piece of knowledge directly before the reader and ask him to draw the conclusion himself. And there are only two verses between them.

One more weave: the sūra uses the word **light** five times (9, 12, 13 twice, 19, 28). Light is the most repeated concrete image of the sūra; and in one of the last verses (28) what is promised to the believers is again a light — *"a light by which you will walk."* The image the sūra opens in verse 9 with a bringing out of darkness into light it closes in verse 28 with a light that is walked in.

## Its being Medinan, and its historical place

There is a broad agreement among the sources that the sūra is Medinan. The content supports this as well:

- **The hypocrites** are named plainly and within a scene (13-15). The use of the concept of the hypocrite in so settled a form belongs to the Medinan period.
- **Fighting** (*qitāl*) and **spending** stand side by side (10). Permission to fight was given at Medina.
- Those who spent **before "the conquest"** are distinguished from those who spent after (10) — a reference to a particular historical moment.
- **The People of the Book** are named as an addressee and as a term of comparison (16, 27, 29).
- The verses are long and the sentences compound; the rhyme-weave is loose. This is the Medinan manner.

One note must be entered: some reports say that a part of the sūra came down in another period. Since these carry no certainty, I impose no preference here. The sūra as a whole has Medinan content, and that is enough for understanding the text.

**The historical ground** is this, and it matters for understanding the sūra: the community at Medina is no longer a persecuted minority. It has an order, a treasury, an army, and a steadily growing wealth. At the same time it is **a mixed community** — it contains those who came first and those who joined later; those who are sincere and those who are calculating.

The whole tension of the sūra arises from this. The one addressed is not outside but inside. And a community can rot from within in two ways: **miserliness** and **cooling.** The sūra takes up each separately — the first in verses 7-11 and 24, the second in verse 16.

## The *musabbiḥāt*

Seven sūras in the Qurʾān open with an expression of glorification, and together they are called **al-musabbiḥāt**. In the order of the codex:

| Sūra | Opening | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **17. al-Isrāʾ** | *subḥāna'lladhī* | verbal noun (a noun) |
| **57. al-Ḥadīd** | *sabbaḥa li'llāh* | **perfect** (past-tense verb) |
| **59. al-Ḥashr** | *sabbaḥa li'llāh* | **perfect** |
| **61. al-Ṣaff** | *sabbaḥa li'llāh* | **perfect** |
| **62. al-Jumuʿa** | *yusabbiḥu li'llāh* | **imperfect** (present/general tense) |
| **64. al-Taghābun** | *yusabbiḥu li'llāh* | **imperfect** |
| **87. al-Aʿlā** | *sabbiḥi'sma rabbika* | **imperative** |

This table makes visible the difference of verb form we shall take up in the first verse of the sūra: the same root in four different patterns. Al-Ḥadīd is the first of those that open with the perfect.

Since the basmala was treated in earlier sections it is not taken up again here; the verse numbers are counted without the basmala.

## 57/1 — سَبَّحَ لِلَّهِ مَا فِي ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۖ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

*Sabbaḥa li'llāhi mā fi's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ, wa huwa'l-ʿAzīzu'l-Ḥakīm*
"Whatever is in the heavens and the earth has declared God free of all imperfection. He is al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm."

### سَبَّحَ — *tasbīḥ*

The analysis of the root was made in the section on al-Naṣr: **س-ب-ح**, whose concrete sense is **to swim in water or in air, to move swiftly through a medium**; in Form II (*tafʿīl*) it takes on the sense of **tanzīh** — to hold God far from everything that does not befit Him. It was also noted there that the function of *tasbīḥ* is **negative**: it does not say "He is such", it says "He is far from such".

I do not repeat it here. What is to be taken up in this section is not the meaning of the root but **the tense of the pattern.**

### Opening with a perfect verb

The sūra opens with **سَبَّحَ** — the past tense. Yet the imperfect of the same expression (*yusabbiḥu* — glorifies / is glorifying) exists in the Qurʾān, and two sūras (al-Jumuʿa and al-Taghābun) open with exactly that.

This difference is not accidental, and the classical commentaries have dwelt on it. The views may be gathered thus:

| Reading | What the perfect conveys | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Completedness** | The glorification has already happened, is happening and will happen; the matter is closed | In Arabic the perfect conveys what has come to pass and is no longer in dispute |
| **Priority** | The glorification began before the existence of the one addressed | The subject is "whatever is in the heavens and the earth"; their existence begins together with the glorification |
| **Emphasis of certainty** | Telling of something that will happen in the future with a perfect conveys the certainty of its happening | A widespread usage in the Qurʾān (*"The trumpet was blown"*, *"The Garden was brought near"*) |
| **No difference** | No difference of meaning was intended between the two patterns | The same expression occurs in both patterns |

In this commentary I find the last line weak, and I set out my ground — this is my own assessment and is not binding:

**If the Qurʾān builds the same sentence in two patterns, it has built both deliberately.** A language does not change a pattern for nothing in a structure that has patterns. And the function of the two patterns is well established in Arabic: **the perfect is a record, the imperfect a depiction.** The perfect says "it happened" and closes the matter; the imperfect says "it is happening" and shows the flow.

We saw this in the section on al-Takāthur as well: *alhākumu't-takāthur* is a perfect, and there too its function was the same — not a warning but a record. The same logic works here, but its direction is reversed. There the perfect was the record of an **offence**; here it is the record of a **state**.

**The practical consequence of the difference is this:** a sūra that opens with *yusabbiḥu* says to the reader "the universe is glorifying at this moment", and calls him to join that flow. A sūra that opens with *sabbaḥa* does something else: **the matter is settled already, whether you join in or not.** The decision of the one addressed adds nothing to what has taken place.

And this accords with the sūra as a whole. Al-Ḥadīd is a sūra of spending; it asks the one addressed to give of his property. That such a sūra should open by saying "the universe is already His and has already glorified Him" sets the frame for what will be asked from the outset: **what is to be given is not the giver's contribution.** This idea will be repeated in verse 7 with the word *mustakhlafīn*, and in verse 10 with the sentence *"to God belongs the inheritance of the heavens and the earth"*.

One more note: all three of the sūras that open with the perfect (al-Ḥadīd, al-Ḥashr, al-Ṣaff) are Medinan, and all three contain **a heavy demand made upon a community** — al-Ḥadīd spending, al-Ḥashr the order of exile and spoils, al-Ṣaff standing in rank and fighting. The two that open with the imperfect (al-Jumuʿa, al-Taghābun) are weighted more towards invitation and description. This may be a pattern; but the sample is small and I do not present it as a fixed rule.

### مَا فِي ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ — "whatever there is"

As the subject **مَا** has been used, not **مَنْ**.

The difference between these two relative pronouns in Arabic is well known: *man* is used for rational beings (human, angel, jinn), *mā* for non-rational beings and for "thing" in general. In the verse *mā* has been chosen.

The consequence of this is a **widening** of scope. For *mā* does not exclude rational beings; in Arabic *mā* can be used so as to include rational beings too, in the sense of "whatever there is" — especially when a mixed group is meant and the dominant element is non-rational.

That is, the verse does not confine the one who glorifies to rational beings. Stone, tree, star, animal, human — all are the subject of the same verb.

From this arises a familiar question: **how does a non-rational being glorify?** In the classical commentaries there are two main answers:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **By the language of its state (indication)** | Every being, by its own constitution, indicates the perfection of its maker. Glorification here is figurative: the being says something to whoever looks at it. |
| **In the literal sense** | Beings have a glorification we do not know of; the utterance lies outside our apprehension. |

The ground of the second view is another verse where the subject occurs: *"The seven heavens and the earth and all that is in them glorify Him. There is nothing that does not glorify Him with praise; but you do not understand their glorification."* (al-Isrāʾ 17/44). The last clause of this verse — *"you do not understand"* — is the strongest evidence supporting the second view; for a glorification in the sense of "indication" is not something that cannot be understood, but on the contrary exists in order to be understood.

In this commentary I impose no preference. Both views are found in the classical sources and neither runs against the text. But this must be recorded: **the function of the verse is the same on either reading.** The sūra builds this sentence in order to say to the one addressed, "you are an exception." Everything that exists is inside an order; what is peculiar to the human being is the possibility of stepping outside that order.

### لِلَّهِ — "for God"

The verb has not been construed as directly transitive. Not "glorified God" but, literally, **"glorified for God."** The **ل** at the head here conveys either emphasis (*taʾkīd*) or particularisation (*ikhtiṣāṣ*).

As we shall see in verse 24 of this same sūra, this particle is frequently used in the Qurʾān to convey ownership and direction. The tone it adds here is this: glorification is an act **directed towards** God — not a praise left hanging in the air but an act with a definite address.

### ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ — *ʿazīz* and *ḥakīm*

**عزيز** — root **ع-ز-ز**. Its concrete sense: **to be hard, to be difficult to come by, to be unconquerable.** From the same root come *ʿizza* (honour, strength) and *aʿazz* (stronger). In Arabic something rarely found is also called *ʿazīz* — this is the fineness in the Turkish expression *aziz dostum*, "my dear friend": the one who is hard to come by.

The three senses of the word are recorded together in the classical lexicons, and all three complete one another:
1. **Prevailing, unconquered** — the one who cannot be withstood
2. **Strong** — possessed of power
3. **Rare, unattainable** — the one who has no like

**حكيم** — root **ح-ك-م**. Its concrete sense is interesting: *ḥakama* is the **bit** put in a horse's mouth — the piece that guides and reins in the animal. From this derive *ḥukm* (a decision), *ḥikma* (putting a thing in its proper place), *iḥkām* (making firm).

That is, *ḥikma* carries in its root **restraint and direction**: to hold a thing in the place where it belongs, not to let it scatter.

**The coming together of the two names** is a pair met with very often in the Qurʾān, and it is not idle. *ʿAzīz* is power; *ḥakīm* is the right use of that power. When power is named alone the possibility of arbitrariness arises; when wisdom is named alone the possibility of ineffectiveness arises. The two together close off both possibilities.

And that this pair should stand at the head of the sūra is meaningful: in verse 25 al-Ḥadīd will tell of the sending down of **iron** — that is, of power — and will bind it to the condition that **justice should stand.** The *ʿazīz-ḥakīm* pair in the first verse of the sūra stands like a summary of that verse: power, and its right use.

## 57/2 — لَهُۥ مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۖ يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ ۖ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

*Lahu mulku's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ, yuḥyī wa yumīt, wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr*
"To Him belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth. He gives life and He gives death. He is powerful over everything."

### مُلْك — *mulk*

Root **م-ل-ك**: to have the power of disposal over a thing. From the same root come *malik* (a king), *mālik* (an owner), *mamlūk* (a thing owned), *malakūt*.

The analysis of the word was made in the section on al-Mulk. Here I record only its function in this context.

**The word order of the sentence is critical.** The Arabic order is: *lahu* (to Him belongs) + *mulku's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* (the dominion of the heavens and the earth). That is, the predicate has been brought forward and the subject placed last.

In Arabic this is the operation called **taqdīm** (fronting), and it produces a definite sense: **ḥaṣr** — particularisation, restriction. Not "the dominion of the heavens and the earth is His", but in a more decisive tone: **"the dominion of the heavens and the earth is His alone."**

This emphasis is the ground of the whole demand for spending in the sūra. To whom property belongs is settled in the second verse, before the seventh is even reached.

### يُحْىِۦ وَيُمِيتُ — He gives life and He gives death

Both verbs are **imperfect** — that is, they convey continuity and repetition. The *sabbaḥa* of the previous verse was a perfect; here the passage is to the imperfect. The difference is functional: glorification is a completed state, whereas life and death are a working that is going on at this moment.

**The order is striking: first giving life, then giving death.** This is not the natural order — one might think that a living thing first dies and then is raised, or that it is first created and then dies. The Qurʾān uses this pair in other places in different orders too.

The ground of the order here may be the following, and I write this as my own reading: the sūra is one built upon **giving.** And life is the first of the things given to a human being. The verse begins the list with what is given; what is taken comes afterwards.

Moreover these two verbs will be repeated in verse 17 of the sūra — there the subject will again be God and the object **the earth**: *"God gives life to the earth after its death."* The principle set down abstractly in the second verse comes back fifteen verses later with a concrete example.

### قَدِير — *qadīr*

Root **ق-د-ر**: **to measure, to apportion, to assign a limit and a quantity to a thing.** From the same root come *qadar* (a measure decreed), *miqdār*, *taqdīr*, *qudra*.

This analysis of the root matters for the question of decree that we shall take up in verse 22 of the sūra, because at the centre of the word stands not **compulsion** but **measure.** The one who is *qadīr* is, before being possessed of limitless power, **the one who sets a measure to everything.**

Indeed the Qurʾān says so plainly: *"We created everything according to a measure"* (al-Qamar 54/49). And: *"There is nothing whose stores are not with Us; and We send it down only in a known measure"* (al-Ḥijr 15/21).

I note this second verse; I shall return to it when we come to the question of **the sending down of iron** in verse 25.

## 57/3 — هُوَ ٱلْأَوَّلُ وَٱلْـَٔاخِرُ وَٱلظَّٰهِرُ وَٱلْبَاطِنُ ۖ وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

*Huwa'l-Awwalu wa'l-Ākhiru wa'ẓ-Ẓāhiru wa'l-Bāṭin, wa huwa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm*
"He is the First, the Last, the Outermost, the Innermost. He is the Knower of everything."

One of the densest sentences in the Qurʾān. Four names, two groups joined by conjunctions, a single pronoun. And none of the four names stands alone — each is paired with its opposite.

This verse must not be passed over without pausing, because what is done here is a different method from that of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. It was seen in the section on al-Ikhlāṣ: there the description was made by **negation** — *lam yalid wa lam yūlad, wa lam yakun lahu kufuwan aḥad*. By drawing a boundary, by leaving things outside. Here the method is the reverse: **four positive names** are given. But the result comes out at the same place, and how it comes out is the matter itself.

### The structure of the four names: two axes

The names are not ranged at random. They come in two pairs, and each pair sets up an **axis**:

| Axis | Names | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | *al-Awwal* ↔ *al-Ākhir* | Before and after; beginning and end |
| **Visibility / distance** | *aẓ-Ẓāhir* ↔ *al-Bāṭin* | Outer and inner; the seen and the hidden |

And what emerges when the two axes are taken together is this: **no direction is left in which to escape.**

There are two ways to encompass a thing. Either it is encompassed in time — its front and its back are held; or it is encompassed in place, in depth — its outside and its inside are held. The verse does both. The two ends of time, the two ends of visibility.

And **the second axis is especially important**, because the first axis by itself leaves a gap: when it is said "He was there before and He will be there after", a distance may be imagined in between — a beginning far off, an end far off, and in the middle a field in which a human being is on his own. The second axis closes this gap: **both at the very outside and at the very inside.**

### ٱلْأَوَّل — al-Awwal

The question of the root is disputed. The word is said to come either from the root **و-ء-ل** (to take refuge, to return — from the same root *maʾāl*: the place to which one returns) or from the root **أ-و-ل** (to return, to go back to one's origin — from the same root *taʾwīl*: to bring a word back to its origin). There is no firm agreement among the lexicographers.

The meaning is plain: **the one who is before, before whom there is nothing.**

The fineness here is this: *awwal* is a word of **sequence.** It reports the first member of a series. But when used of God it cannot mean being part of the series — because what is part of a series is subject to the rule of the series.

In classical theology this is expressed as: "*awwaliyya* is not priority in time but priority in **being**." That is, not "there was a time before Him and He existed in that time"; rather, **time itself stays inside His being before.**

This distinction is the direct continuation of the measure of *tanzīh* set down in the sections on al-Fātiḥa and al-Ikhlāṣ, and there is no need to repeat it here. I record only this: **all four of the names carry the same risk.** All four are words taken from the human being's own world — before, after, outside, inside. These are words that report a position, and position is a property of limited beings.

How the verse meets this risk lies in the names being given **in pairs**. I shall return to this below.

### ٱلْـَٔاخِر — al-Ākhir

Root **أ-خ-ر**: **what comes after, what stays behind, what is late.**

The analysis of this root was made at al-Baqara 2/4. What was recorded there was this: the pair *al-ākhira* (the hereafter) and *ad-dunyā* reports not a **value** but a **position** — one "what comes after", the other (from the root *d-n-w*) "what is near". The world is not called "the world" because it is bad; it is so called because it is near.

That finding is directly useful here, and it raises a question: if *al-ākhira* is "the life that comes after", does the name **al-Ākhir** place God at the end of that life too?

The answer is no, and its ground is in the word itself. *Al-ākhira* is a **vessel** — a life lived in, a period. *Al-Ākhir*, by contrast, is an **attribute**, and it reports not a period but a position: **the one beyond every end.**

The Qurʾān sets this out plainly elsewhere: *"Everyone upon it passes away; and there remains the face of your Lord, possessor of majesty and honour"* (al-Raḥmān 55/26-27). Here two verbs are set against one another: *fanāʾ* (to come to an end) and *baqāʾ* (to remain). The name *al-Ākhir* is the noun form of this second verb.

**What the presence of al-Awwal together with al-Ākhir does is this:** when either is named alone the human mind builds a story — "in the beginning He was, then the universe came to be, and at the end He alone will remain." That story places God at **the two ends of time** and leaves the middle empty.

The verse closes this gap **with the second pair that follows immediately.** *Ẓāhir* and *bāṭin* have to do with "now" and "here".

### ٱلظَّاهِر — aẓ-Ẓāhir

Root **ظ-ه-ر**. Its concrete sense is surprising: ***ẓahr* means the back.** The back of a human being or of an animal.

Look at what derives from it:
- **ظهر (ẓuhr)** — noon; the clearest, shadowless moment of the day
- **ظاهر (ẓāhir)** — what is seen, what is open
- **ظهير (ẓahīr)** — a backer, a supporter (one who lends his back)
- **مظاهرة** — support, mutual backing
- **إظهار** — bringing into the open
- **ظهور** — coming out into the open

The logic of the root is this: **the back is the outermost, the most visible, the most touched surface of a thing.** This is why both "visibility" and "support" come out of the same root — the one who lends his back is the one who offers his outermost surface.

That is, *aẓ-Ẓāhir* means, before "the one who is seen", **"the one who is outermost."** There is nothing beyond a thing — because it is the outermost surface.

### ٱلْبَاطِن — al-Bāṭin

Root **ب-ط-ن**. Its concrete sense is again from the body: ***baṭn* means the belly.** What is inside, what is covered.

Derivatives:
- **باطن (bāṭin)** — the inner face, what is hidden
- **بطانة (biṭāna)** — the lining of a garment; and, figuratively, a confidant, an inner circle
- **تبطّن** — to go inside a thing

That is, *al-Bāṭin* means, before "hidden", **"the one who is innermost."** There is nothing further inside a thing — because it is the innermost layer.

### What the two names build together

Now what the pair does becomes visible. *Ẓāhir* and *bāṭin* are opposites in the dictionary, and of any being the two cannot be said at once. A thing is either outside or inside.

The verse says both at once. And this is not a contradiction but **the declaration of a limit:** a being of which two opposite attributes can be predicated at the same time and in full is outside the order from which the attributes are drawn.

The method is this, and it is the sibling of the method in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ. There the description was made by negation — *He did not beget, He was not begotten.* Here the description is made **by affirmations that cancel one another.** The result is the same: in either case the reader can draw no picture with the concepts he has.

We may also put it thus: the four names do not give the reader a piece of **knowledge**, they show him an **impossibility.** You cannot say "there", because He is both here and there; you cannot say "at that time", because He is both before and after.

And the close of the verse confirms this: **وَهُوَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ** — "He is the Knower of everything." After the four names have said what the human being cannot know, the sentence closes by saying **who does know.** The direction is reversed: you cannot encompass Him; He has encompassed everything.

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

The two axes are also set up separately in other places in the Qurʾān:

**For the axis of time:** *"Everything will perish except His face. His is the judgement, and to Him you will be returned"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/88).

**For the inner axis:** *"We created the human being, and We know what his soul whispers to him. We are nearer to him than his jugular vein"* (Qāf 50/16).

This second is the plainest counterpart of the name *al-Bāṭin* and must be read with care. The expression **"nearer than the jugular vein"** tells of distance becoming unmeasurable — for the jugular vein is already inside the human being; something nearer than that is no longer in a place the word "nearness" can measure.

And precisely here a note is needed, because these are the verses most open to being misunderstood.

### A limit: *bāṭin* does not mean "a being inside"

The name *al-Bāṭin* and the expression "nearer than the jugular vein" have been strained in two directions throughout history:

**The first strain — corporealisation.** To think that God is somewhere inside the human being, physically. This is the direct application of the concrete sense of the word (belly, inside) and violates the measure of *tanzīh*.

**The second strain — identification.** To draw from the sentence "He is the inner reality of everything" the conclusion that being and God are the same thing. This is not what the verse says; for the verse says *aẓ-Ẓāhir* as well, and a thing cannot be its own outer face and its own inner face at the same time.

This commentary takes no side in these disputes. The measure is the one the Qurʾān itself sets: **لَيْسَ كَمِثْلِهِۦ شَىْءٌ** — *"There is nothing like Him"* (al-Shūrā 42/11). This sentence determines how all four names are to be read: the names are real, but their counterparts in human language do not work as they do in human language when they are applied to Him.

The measure set down in the section on al-Ikhlāṣ holds exactly and is not repeated here.

### Word order: all four names carry the article *al-*

All four come **definite**: *al-Awwal, al-Ākhir, aẓ-Ẓāhir, al-Bāṭin.* Had they been indefinite — *awwalun, ākhirun* — the sense would have been "He is a first, He is a last", implying that other firsts and other lasts might be found.

The article of definiteness closes this off: **the one who is first is unique, the one who is last is unique.** In Arabic this is called *lām al-istighrāq* — the definiteness that takes in the whole of a genus.

And between the four names there is the conjunction **وَ**, not asyndeton. This shows that the names are not explanations of one another but **separate predications added to one another.** All four are true separately, and all four together build a single picture.

### Its place within the sūra

That this verse should stand in this sūra is no accident. In verse 13 of the sūra, when the wall drawn between the hypocrites and the believers is described, **the same two words** will be used:

> **بَاطِنُهُۥ فِيهِ ٱلرَّحْمَةُ وَظَٰهِرُهُۥ مِن قِبَلِهِ ٱلْعَذَابُ**
> *"On its inner face is mercy, and on its outer face punishment."*

That is, the *ẓāhir/bāṭin* pair the sūra uses of God in the third verse it uses ten verses later of a **wall**. This is a link built within the sūra, and it is too marked to be counted a coincidence.

What might it mean? I write this as my own reading: in the third verse *ẓāhir* and *bāṭin* were united **in the same being** — outer and inner, in the One. In the wall of the thirteenth verse, by contrast, the two are **separated**: one face mercy, the other face punishment. The same object, two different things according to the side from which it is looked at.

The sūra shows the picture of *tawḥīd* once as a joining and once as a parting. In the one, two opposites are gathered in a single being; in the other, a single object is divided into two opposite faces. And the difference is where the one who looks is standing.

## 57/4 — هُوَ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ فِى سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ ثُمَّ ٱسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى ٱلْعَرْشِ ۚ يَعْلَمُ مَا يَلِجُ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَمَا يَخْرُجُ مِنْهَا وَمَا يَنزِلُ مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَمَا يَعْرُجُ فِيهَا ۖ وَهُوَ مَعَكُمْ أَيْنَ مَا كُنتُمْ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

*Huwa'lladhī khalaqa's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍa fī sittati ayyāmin thumma'stawā ʿala'l-ʿarsh; yaʿlamu mā yaliju fi'l-arḍi wa mā yakhruju minhā wa mā yanzilu mina's-samāʾi wa mā yaʿruju fīhā; wa huwa maʿakum ayna mā kuntum; wa'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna baṣīr*
"It is He who created the heavens and the earth in six days, then took His place upon the Throne. He knows what enters the earth and what comes out of it, what comes down from the sky and what ascends into it. Wherever you may be, He is with you. God sees what you do."

One of the longest verses in the sūra, made up of four separate sentences. The third verse made its description on an abstract plane; this verse builds the same description through **movement.**

### فِى سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ — six days

This expression recurs in several places in the Qurʾān and needs attention, because it is very open ground for forced interpretation.

The word **يَوْم** in Arabic does not mean only "a day of twenty-four hours". The original sense of the root is **a stretch of time, a period.** The Qurʾān shows this itself: *"A day with your Lord is like a thousand years of what you count"* (al-Ḥajj 22/47); and in a later verse in the same sūra a day of fifty thousand years is named for the ascent of the angels and the Spirit (al-Maʿārij 70/4).

That is, *yawm* is a unit whose measure changes with the context. It is also plain that "day" cannot be used in its present-day sense when the sun has not yet been created.

The position taken in this commentary is this: **how long the six "days" lasted has not been reported and is not known.** To close this indefiniteness and try to match it one-to-one with the periods of modern cosmology or geology is the forced scientific reading that `STYLE.md` forbids. The only thing that must be recorded is this: the verse tells of the creation as a **process** — not instantaneous but in stages.

And this does not conflict with the expression *kun fa-yakūn* at al-Baqara 2/117. It was recorded there: *kun fa-yakūn* says whose the command is, while "six days" says how the process works. Two separate answers to two separate questions.

### ٱسْتَوَىٰ عَلَى ٱلْعَرْشِ — *istiwāʾ*

One of the most debated expressions in the Qurʾān. It occurs in seven places.

**استوى** — root **س-و-ي**: to be equal, to be level, to become straight, to be completed. From the same root come *sawāʾ* (equal), *taswiya* (levelling), *musāwāt* (equality), *istiwāʾ*.

**عرش** — root **ع-ر-ش**: to raise, to build a bower. In Arabic *ʿarsh* answers to a throne, a bower, a high roofed structure. The word is also used as the symbol of sovereignty — indeed the Qurʾān uses the same word for the throne of the queen of Sabaʾ (al-Naml 27/23).

**The disagreement is over how the expression is to be understood.** The main positions are these:

| Position | Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Tafwīḍ** (referring the meaning to God) | "The *istiwāʾ* is known, its modality is unknown, and to ask about it is an innovation." The wording is accepted as it stands and its how is not asked. | The position ascribed to the majority of the early generations; to stop at the limit of the revealed text |
| **Taʾwīl** | *Istiwāʾ* means "to take sovereignty in hand, to begin to govern the affair" | Al-Shūrā 42/11 (*"there is nothing like Him"*); the use of *istawā ʿalā* in Arabic in the sense of "he gained mastery" |
| **Acceptance of the literal sense** | The wording is taken according to its outward sense, but without falling into likening | Adherence to the revealed text |

I take no side in this disagreement. Per the method (`STYLE.md`) no partisanship is exercised in theological disputes, and this is one of the oldest theological divisions in exegesis.

What must be recorded is this:

**One.** All parties agree on the limit set by al-Shūrā 42/11: however it is understood, no likening to created things may be made.

**Two.** The context of the verse narrows what the matter is. The sentence comes with *thumma* (then), and immediately after it the verbs of **knowing** and **seeing** are ranged. That is, in the place where the expression stands the subject is not a location but a picture of **governance and oversight.** This is the strength the *taʾwīl* view draws from the context; but I do not impose it as a preference.

**Three.** That this expression should stand in the fourth verse of the sūra, and that **"wherever you may be, He is with you" should come immediately after**, is meaningful. The verse sets images of distance and nearness side by side: height (the Throne) and being-with (*maʿiyya*). Like the continuation of the *ẓāhir/bāṭin* pair in the third verse.

### Four verbs: entering, coming out, descending, ascending

In the middle of the verse there is a list of four verbs, and its symmetry is faultless:

| Verb | Direction | To where / from where |
|---|---|---|
| **يَلِجُ** (*yaliju*) | inward | enters the earth |
| **يَخْرُجُ** (*yakhruju*) | outward | comes out of the earth |
| **يَنزِلُ** (*yanzilu*) | downward | comes down from the sky |
| **يَعْرُجُ** (*yaʿruju*) | upward | ascends to the sky |

Two pairs, two axes: on **the axis of the earth**, going in and coming out; on **the axis of the sky**, coming down and going up. Note this — it is the same two-axis structure as in the third verse, but built this time **with verbs.** There a state was reported; here, movement.

**ولج** — root **و-ل-ج**: to go into a narrow place, to slip in. This root will recur in the verses that follow (57/6) and I shall take it up in detail there.

**عرج** — root **ع-ر-ج**: to go up a sloping road, to climb. From the same root come *miʿrāj* (a means of ascent, a ladder) and *maʿārij* (ways of ascent); this is the name of Sūrat al-Maʿārij. The concrete sense of the word also carries **limping, going lame** (*aʿraj*: lame) — because walking on a sloping road is not level.

The function of the list is this: the four verbs count **every direction of movement** possible on the earth and in the sky. The seed going into the soil, the shoot coming out, the rain coming down, the vapour going up — all are within these four verbs. And likewise: something hidden being kept hidden, and coming to light; a revelation coming down, a deed going up.

And all four of these verbs are **imperfect**: happening now, continuously, again and again.

### وَهُوَ مَعَكُمْ أَيْنَ مَا كُنتُمْ — "wherever you may be, He is with you"

One of the most quoted sentences of the sūra, and one of those that must be read most carefully.

**مَعَ** — an adverb conveying accompaniment. The English "with", "together with", "beside".

At al-Baqara 2/115 — *"wherever you turn, there is the face of God"* — a note was entered, and that note holds here exactly, and is indeed more necessary:

**What the verse says:** there is no place that can be kept outside God's knowledge, oversight and disposal. Wherever you may be.

**What the verse does not say:** that God is beside you in place, corporeally. *Maʿiyya* (being with) is used in Arabic outside physical juxtaposition as well — and in the Qurʾān it is used so for the most part.

The text itself shows this. At the **beginning** of the same verse God's *istiwāʾ* upon the Throne is spoken of; at the **end** it says "He sees what you do". That is, the sentence places the being-with within a frame of **knowledge and oversight**, not within a frame of place.

Indeed this becomes clear when the Qurʾān's uses of *maʿiyya* are examined:

- *"Do not fear; I am with you both, hearing and seeing"* (Ṭāhā 20/46) — to Moses (Mūsā) and Aaron (Hārūn) as they go to Pharaoh. The content of the being-with is explained immediately afterwards: **hearing and seeing.**
- *"Do not grieve; God is with us"* (al-Tawba 9/40) — in the cave. Here the being-with is a promise of **protection.**
- *"Wherever they may be, He is with them"* (al-Mujādala 58/7) — and that verse comes in a context that speaks of secret conversations. The being-with is again in the sense of **knowledge.**

In the classical commentaries this is called *maʿiyya ʿilmiyya* (being-with in knowledge) and is widely adopted for the general verses of being-with. A further distinction is made, of *maʿiyya khāṣṣa* (a particular being-with) — in verses such as "God is with the patient", the being-with is in the sense of help and support.

This distinction also accounts for two readings of the verse:

| Reading | Its sense | Its tone |
|---|---|---|
| **General (of knowledge)** | Nowhere are you outside the oversight | Warning |
| **Particular (of help)** | Wherever you are, you are not alone | Consolation |

**The context brings the first reading forward**, because the sentence closes with "God sees what you do" and this part of the sūra is preparing a demand for spending. But the second reading does not run against the text either, and it is plainly present elsewhere in the Qurʾān. The two do not cancel one another.

And this must be said: **the strength of this sentence lies in its carrying both readings at once.** "You are seen" and "you are not alone" are two faces of the same sentence. Which of them weighs more is determined by the state of the one who reads.

### وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ بَصِيرٌ

**بصير** — root **ب-ص-ر**: to see, but especially **to see discerningly.** From the same root comes *baṣīra* (inner sight, discernment). The Qurʾān uses this root for blindness (rather than *aʿmā*) and says *"the eyes do not go blind; it is the hearts within the breasts that go blind"* (al-Ḥajj 22/46).

In the sentence there is again a **fronting**: *bimā taʿmalūn* (what you do) has come before the verb. This puts the emphasis on the object: it is **what you do in particular** that He sees.

And the verse closes from a striking place. From the beginning up to here the subject has been cosmic — the heavens, the earth, six days, the Throne, the things that come down and go up. In the last sentence the scale suddenly narrows and the subject becomes **what you did today.**

This narrowing is an instance of the sūra's method: the large picture is built, then the one addressed is placed inside the picture. The command in the seventh verse is the consequence of this narrowing.

## 57/5 — لَّهُۥ مُلْكُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ وَإِلَى ٱللَّهِ تُرْجَعُ ٱلْأُمُورُ

*Lahu mulku's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ, wa ila'llāhi turjaʿu'l-umūr*
"To Him belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth. And to God all affairs are returned."

### The repetition itself

The first sentence of this verse is **a word-for-word repetition of the first sentence of the second verse.** The same sentence three verses apart.

Repetitions of this kind in the Qurʾān are not random, and here the function is visible: **in the second verse dominion was named together with life and death** (*yuḥyī wa yumīt*); **in the fifth verse dominion is named together with the returning of affairs.**

That is, the same ruling is tied to two different consequences:

| | To whom dominion belongs | Its consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **57/2** | To God | He gives life and death — that is, what is within the dominion is in His hand |
| **57/5** | To God | Affairs return to Him — that is, what happens within the dominion arrives at Him |

The first covers **being**, the second **process.** And the two verses in between (3 and 4) fill the space between them: time, visibility, movement, being-with.

The picture that emerges at the end of five verses is this: **the dominion is His, the movement is within His knowledge, and the outcome returns to Him.** The command that will come in the seventh verse — *"spend of that in which He has made you successors"* — can only be understood once these five verses have been set up.

### تُرْجَعُ ٱلْأُمُورُ — "affairs are returned"

**رجع** — root **ر-ج-ع**: to return, to come back. From the same root come *rujūʿ*, *marjiʿ* (the place to which one returns), *irtijāʿ*. The *innā ilayhi rājiʿūn* of al-Baqara 2/156 — "to Him we are returning" — is from the same root.

**The verb has come in the passive:** *turjaʿu* — "are returned", not "return". This is a fine but important difference. Had it been active, it would have been understood that affairs return of themselves; the passive pattern implies that there is an agent who returns them.

**أمور** — the plural of *amr*. The word has two senses and both hold here: **matter, affair** and **command, order.** So it may be read "all affairs" or "all commands / decrees".

And there is again a **fronting**: *ila'llāhi* (to God) has come before the verb. The sense: **not somewhere else, but to God.**

## 57/6 — يُولِجُ ٱلَّيْلَ فِى ٱلنَّهَارِ وَيُولِجُ ٱلنَّهَارَ فِى ٱلَّيْلِ ۚ وَهُوَ عَلِيمٌۢ بِذَاتِ ٱلصُّدُورِ

*Yūliju'l-layla fi'n-nahāri wa yūliju'n-nahāra fi'l-layl, wa huwa ʿalīmun bi-dhāti'ṣ-ṣudūr*
"He makes the night enter into the day and makes the day enter into the night. He is the Knower of what is in the breasts."

### إيلاج — *īlāj*

**Root: و-ل-ج.** Concrete sense: **to go into a narrow place, to slip in, to seep inside.**

Its derivatives show the colour of the root:
- **ولج (walaja)** — he went in
- **وليجة (walīja)** — a person who has slipped into a group from outside, who has got in among them; a confidant. It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"…those who have taken no *walīja* apart from God, His Messenger and the believers"* (al-Tawba 9/16). There the word means "an outsider slipped into the inner circle".
- **ولج (walaj)** — a cleft in a road, a hollow in a rock

The picture the root carries is this: **not passing through a wide opening but seeping through a narrow gap.** A thing entering inside another thing, into a place that does not belong to it.

The verb in the verse is in the **إفعال (*ifʿāl*)** form: *walaja* (he entered) → *awlaja* (he made enter). That is, it has been made transitive. The night does not enter of itself; it **is made to enter.**

The best-known use of this root in the Qurʾān is the expression *"He knows what enters the earth"* in the previous verse of this same sūra (57/4). The same root, two verses apart, once in the context of knowledge and once in the context of an act.

### What is being described?

The sentence is two-directional and symmetrical: night into day, day into night. The same verb, twice, with its objects exchanged.

As to what is being described there are several readings in the classical commentaries, and all of them point to concrete events:

| Reading | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The changing length of the seasons** | In one half of the year the day lengthens and the night shortens; in the other half the reverse. What is taken from one is added to the other. |
| **Dawn and twilight** | The edge of the night mingles with the day and the edge of the day with the night; there is no sharp boundary between the two. |
| **Both** | The word answers to both phenomena |

The word itself suits the third reading better. For *īlāj* describes not a sharp exchange of places but **a seeping.** The night does not take the day's place; it enters into it.

**And this genuinely fits the phenomenon itself.** The turning of day into night is not an instantaneous event; dawn and dusk are transitional zones in which the two states mingle. In the same way the change in the length of the seasons is not by leaps but by a shift of a few minutes from day to day.

A note is needed here: this is an **agreement with observation**, not a claim of scientific miracle. That the change from night to day is gradual is something that can be seen with the naked eye, and those addressed by the verse saw it too. What the verse does is to name a phenomenon everyone sees with exactly the right word.

### Why is this verse here?

Look at the sequence:

- **57/2**: He gives life and gives death
- **57/4**: entering, coming out, descending, ascending
- **57/6**: He makes the night enter into the day and the day into the night

All three describe **the passing of opposites into one another.** Life and death, descent and ascent, night and day. And the four names in the third verse were opposite pairs too: first-last, outer-inner.

This is the structure repeated across the first six verses of the sūra: **the gathering of opposites into a single hand.**

What this is a preparation for will be seen in the seventh verse. The sūra will ask the one addressed to give what is in his hand. And the greatest obstacle to the act of giving is the human assumption that "what is in my hand is mine". The first six verses dismantle this assumption systematically, without issuing a single command.

### وَهُوَ عَلِيمٌۢ بِذَاتِ ٱلصُّدُورِ — "the Knower of what is in the breasts"

**ذات الصدور** — literally "that which the breasts possess", that is, what is in the breasts, the contents of the breast.

**صدر** — root **ص-د-ر**: the front or upper part of a thing, the place from which it issues. From the same root come *ṣudūr* (to issue forth), *maṣdar* (a source, a place of issuing), *ṣadāra*. *Ṣadr* is the front part of the body — the breast.

In Arabic *ṣadr* is mostly named as the seat of the feelings, of intention and of hidden thoughts; *qalb*, rather, as the seat of apprehension and decision. The distinction is not absolute and there is no unified description in the sources.

**That this sentence should be placed here is striking.** The verse had been speaking of night and day — the largest-scale and most visible rhythm of the universe. And the sentence closes with the most hidden thing in a human being's breast.

This is the same movement as the close of the fourth verse: a descent from the cosmic scale to the personal. But here a further step is taken — there it said "what you do" (visible acts), here "what is in the breasts" (unseen intentions).

And considering what follows in the sūra this is entirely apt. The sūra will ask for spending; spending is a visible act. But the sūra will also speak of **the hypocrites** — that is, of those who perform the same act outwardly while being otherwise within. The scene in the thirteenth verse is built on exactly this.

The close of the sixth verse prepares that scene six verses in advance: **what cannot be told apart when looked at from outside can be told apart when looked at from here.**

A further link is also made with the third verse. There God was named *al-Bāṭin* — the one who is innermost. Here what He knows is **the inside of the breasts.** Name and act are matched three verses apart.

## 57/7 — ءَامِنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ وَأَنفِقُوا۟ مِمَّا جَعَلَكُم مُّسْتَخْلَفِينَ فِيهِ ۖ فَٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ مِنكُمْ وَأَنفَقُوا۟ لَهُمْ أَجْرٌ كَبِيرٌ

*Āminū bi'llāhi wa rasūlih, wa anfiqū mimmā jaʿalakum mustakhlafīna fīh; fa'lladhīna āmanū minkum wa anfaqū lahum ajrun kabīr*
"Believe in God and His Messenger, and spend of that in which He has made you successors. For those of you who believe and spend there is a great reward."

The first command of the sūra. And the place to which the six verses of preparation are tied.

This verse is one of the most important in the sūra — perhaps, together with verse 25, one of the two most important. For here **the ground of spending** is loaded onto a single word, and that word is the word that expresses the Qurʾān's understanding of ownership most plainly.

### Two commands, one sentence

There are two commands in the sentence: **believe** and **spend.** Side by side, with the same address, joined by the same conjunction.

This joining will recur throughout the sūra. In verse 8 a question will be asked about faith; in verse 10 a question in the same pattern will be asked about spending. In verse 19 those who believe will be named, in verse 18 those who give alms. The sūra keeps building a link between faith and giving.

And this link is present across the Qurʾān generally. In the third verse of Sūrat al-Baqara, when those who guard themselves are described, three things were counted: faith in the unseen, prayer, and **"spending of what We have provided them with."** The same three.

### أَنفِقُوا۟ — *infāq*

**Root: ن-ف-ق.** The concrete sense of this root is unexpected and instructive.

Let us look at the derivatives:
- **نفق (*nafiqa*)** — for property to be used up, to run out; and for a thing to find currency, to pass as valid coin
- **نفق (*nafaq*)** — an underground tunnel, a passage. From the same root **نافقاء (*nāfiqāʾ*)**: the hidden exit hole in a field-mouse's burrow
- **منافق (*munāfiq*)** — like one who comes out of a burrow with two doors; one who goes out by a door other than the one he came in by
- **نفقة (*nafaqa*)** — what is spent for maintenance
- **إنفاق (*infāq*)** — to spend, to put out

**There are two groups of senses, and their relation has been debated by the lexicographers:** one is "to be used up / to spend", the other "tunnel / hidden exit". The common ground is probably the idea of **passage**: a thing going out from one place. Property leaves the hand; the mouse leaves the burrow. This connection is reasonable but not certain; I record it as the lexicographers' interpretation.

What is striking is this: **infāq** and **munāfiq** are from the same root. And this sūra is the sūra that uses both — *anfiqū* in verse 7, *al-munāfiqūn* in verse 13. In the verbal texture of the sūra this does not look like a coincidence, but I draw no semantic conclusion from it; it is ordinary in Arabic for words from the same root to move apart in meaning.

**The difference between *infāq* and *zakāt*** must be recorded. *Zakāt* is an obligation tied to a fixed proportion, to particular kinds of property and to particular channels of disbursement. *Infāq* is a **general** word: it covers every kind of spending. In this verse it is not *zakāt* but *infāq* that is said; that is, the demand is not reduced to the obligatory proportion.

### مِمَّا — "of some of it"

The **مِن** at the head conveys the partitive (*tabʿīḍ*): **"of some of it."**

This is a detail that must not be missed. The verse does not say "spend what is in your hand"; it says "of some of what is in your hand". The Qurʾān bounds the demand for spending with this particle, and it is a pattern repeated in many places: *mimmā razaqnāhum* (al-Baqara 2/3), *mimmā razaqnākum* (al-Baqara 2/254), *mimmā tuḥibbūn* (Āl ʿImrān 3/92).

That is, the sūra is not making a call to dispossession. The demand is bounded, and the bound is set by the language itself.

This is also consistent with the question of **monasticism** to be taken up in verse 27 of the sūra: the Qurʾān asks not for a complete withdrawal from the world but for giving out of what is in one's hand.

### مُّسْتَخْلَفِينَ — *mustakhlafīn*

One of the key words of the sūra, and the whole weight of the verse rests here.

**Root: خ-ل-ف.** The full analysis of this root was made at al-Baqara 2/30: **to come after, to take someone's place following him.** From the same root *khalaf*, *khilāfa*, *ikhtilāf*. And the two readings of the word — **deputy** and **successor** — were given there in a table. I do not repeat them here.

What must be taken up here is not the meaning of the root but **the pattern.**

**The pattern: the passive participle of the استفعال (*istifʿāl*) form.** Let us set it out step by step:

1. **خلف (*khalafa*)** — he came after
2. **استخلف (*istakhlafa*)** — he made someone his successor, left him in his place. The *istifʿāl* form here has the sense "to bring someone into that state".
3. **مستخلَف (*mustakhlaf*)** — the **passive participle**: one who *has been made* a successor, one who has been put in another's place. Passive.

**That the word is passive is the heart of the matter.**

The verse does not say "of that in which He made you successors" — that too is a correct rendering, but it misses the emphasis — it says, more exactly, **"of that in which you have been made successors."** You are not the subject of the verb. You were **brought** into that position.

So there are three elements in the sentence:
- **The real owner** — not named, but He is the subject of the sentence (*jaʿalakum* — He made you)
- **The deputy / successor** — you
- **The trust** — the property

And the particle **فيه** (in it, within it) is meaningful too. Not "you were made successors of it" but **"within it"** you were made successors. That is, the property is not the object of the succession but its **field.** The domain within which you exercise authority.

### Two readings

The word has two readings, and both are present in the classical sources. Both come out at the same place, but by different routes:

| Reading | In whose place the successor stands | What the property becomes |
|---|---|---|
| **In God's place (deputyship)** | The human being disposes in God's dominion on His behalf | A trust: the owner is another, the user is you |
| **In the place of those before (succession)** | The human being is the successor of those who held the same property before him | Something handed on: it came to you, and it will pass from you |

At al-Baqara 2/30 it was noted that some scholars have entered a reservation about the first reading, and that their ground is sound: a deputy stands where the principal is absent.

**But in this verse the second reading is also strong, and this needs emphasising.** For the second reading agrees with something said plainly only three verses later (57/10):

> **وَلِلَّهِ مِيرَٰثُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ**
> *"To God belongs the inheritance of the heavens and the earth."*

The word **inheritance** is the direct counterpart of the "successor" reading. Inheritance is what passes from one to another. And the verse says where all inheritance finally returns.

That is, the concept the sūra sets up in verse 7 it repeats with another word in verse 10. Read together, the two yield this: **the property in your hand belonged to someone else before you; it will belong to someone else after you; and the whole chain belongs to God.** What falls to your share is to hold it in your hand for a while, at one link of the chain.

### The plainest expression of the measure of al-Baqara 2/29-30

At al-Baqara 2/29 this measure was set down, and it attaches exactly here:

> The earth has been given to the human being for **use**, not for **ownership**.

There this measure was drawn from two verses standing side by side: 2/29 says "whatever is on the earth is for you", 2/30 calls the human being a "successor". A deputy has no property of his own; he has an account to render.

**Al-Ḥadīd 57/7 is the plainest single-sentence expression of that measure in the Qurʾān.** For two things come together here:

**One.** In al-Baqara the measure was drawn from two separate verses. Here it is gathered into a single verse, into a single word: *mustakhlafīn*.

**Two.** In al-Baqara the practical consequence of the measure was not stated. Here it is stated: **spending.** That is, the finding "it is not yours" is not left as a philosophical proposition hanging in the air; it is tied directly to a behaviour.

### The link between property as a trust and the ground of spending

This is the most important idea of the sūra and needs attention.

**A demand for spending can be built on two separate grounds.** Both are found in religious literature and in general moral thought, but they are different things:

**The first ground: generosity.** "It is your property, but give. Giving is good, miserliness is bad." This accepts the ownership of the property and demands giving as a **virtue.**

**The second ground: trust.** "The property is not yours to begin with. In giving you are not giving anything of your own; you are delivering to its place something you were holding." This opens the ownership of the property to question from the outset.

**The verse builds the second ground.** And the difference shows in the consequences:

| | The ground of generosity | The ground of trust |
|---|---|---|
| The position of the giver | A donor, one who bestows | One doing his duty |
| The state of the one who does not give | Lacking in virtue | Having exceeded his authority |
| The position of the receiver | Expected to be grateful | Receiving what is his by right |
| The limit of what is given | Depends on the giver's heart | Depends on the terms of the trust |
| The feeling it produces | The risk of superiority | Responsibility |

**The most critical line is the third.** On the ground of generosity the receiver stands in the position of a debtor; on the ground of trust he does not. And the Qurʾān says this plainly elsewhere:

*"In their properties there is a known right for the one who asks and the one who is deprived."* (al-Maʿārij 70/24-25). The word is **right** — not gift.

And the warning about conduct after giving is a continuation of the same logic: *"Do not void your alms by reproach and by injury"* (al-Baqara 2/264). Reproach is the natural consequence of the first ground — a person who thinks he has given of his own property expects a return for it.

**The fourth line is important too.** On the ground of generosity the answer to the question "how much shall I give?" is at the giver's discretion. On the ground of trust the question changes: **"what were the terms of what was left with me?"** This is an entirely different question, and it reduces arbitrariness.

It is possible to make this concrete with an example: a person has been handed the cash box of an institution and told to make the payments. When this person makes a payment he does not say "I have been generous"; he has done his duty. And when he does not, he does not say "I have been miserly"; **he has acted against a trust.** This is the difference between the two descriptions.

The Qurʾān's counting property as a trust does not abolish ownership — indeed the same Qurʾān regulates the law of inheritance, treats trade as lawful and lays down a penalty for theft. That is, legal ownership exists. What is abolished is **absolute** ownership: an unlimited and unaccountable right of disposal over what is in one's hand.

### What this says to the present

The present-day counterpart of this distinction becomes concrete at several points.

**One.** The sentence "I can do what I like with my own money" is the purest expression of the first ground and is the default assumption of modern economic thought. The verse opens the first word of that sentence to question: *my own.*

**Two.** The language of donation and philanthropy is today built largely on the first ground — the giver's name is announced, the giver is photographed, the giver is thanked. Within the frame the verse sets up this becomes odd: no ceremony is held for the person who delivers a trust to its place.

**Three.** That the word inheritance occurs in verse 10 carries the matter onto an intergenerational plane. The expression "that in which you have been made successors" can be read directly of natural resources as well — for they too pass from one generation to another, and no generation produced them. The link made at al-Baqara 2/29 with al-Rūm 30/41 (*"corruption has appeared on land and sea because of what people's hands have earned"*) holds here too.

I record this not as a ruling but as a reading the wording of the verse allows.

### أَجْرٌ كَبِيرٌ — a great reward

**أجر** — root **أ-ج-ر**: the return for a piece of work, a wage. The word comes from commercial and employment relations; *ijāra* (hiring out) and *ajīr* (a hired worker) are from the same root.

That is, the verse names the return with the word **wage** — not with the word gift or favour. This is consistent with the economic language of the sūra generally: the sūra will use the words loan (*qarḍ*), inheritance (*mīrāth*), double share (*kiflayn*), ransom, balance.

The adjective **كبير** states no measure, only greatness. In verses 11 and 18 of this same sūra the adjective **كريم** (*karīm*) will be used of the reward — that conveys not so much greatness as **superiority of kind.** Two different adjectives, two different emphases.

## 57/8 — وَمَا لَكُمْ لَا تُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ ۙ وَٱلرَّسُولُ يَدْعُوكُمْ لِتُؤْمِنُوا۟ بِرَبِّكُمْ وَقَدْ أَخَذَ مِيثَٰقَكُمْ إِن كُنتُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ

*Wa mā lakum lā tuʾminūna bi'llāh, wa'r-rasūlu yadʿūkum li-tuʾminū bi-rabbikum wa qad akhadha mīthāqakum in kuntum muʾminīn*
"What is the matter with you that you do not believe in God, when the Messenger calls you to believe in your Lord and He has taken your pledge — if you are going to believe?"

### وَمَا لَكُمْ — "what is the matter with you?"

This pattern in Arabic is not a question but an expression of **reproach and astonishment**: "what is going on, that you do this?" In English it answers to "what is the matter with you?" or "what has come over you?"

The function of the pattern is not to obtain information from the one addressed but to show that the behaviour is **without ground.** It is a question for which no answer is expected, because it is assumed that no reasonable answer can be given.

This pattern is used **twice** in the sūra:
- **57/8**: *"What is the matter with you that you do not believe in God?"*
- **57/10**: *"What is the matter with you that you do not spend in the way of God?"*

Two verses apart, in the same pattern, on two different subjects. And this is a repetition, in order, of the two commands of verse 7 (believe, spend).

**The structure itself builds an argument:** faith and spending are taken up on the same level. The second does not stand as an optional addition to the first; both are the subject of the same question.

### وَقَدْ أَخَذَ مِيثَٰقَكُمْ — "He has taken your pledge"

**ميثاق** — root **و-ث-ق**: to be firm, to be trustworthy, to bind. From the same root come *wathīqa* (a document), *thiqa* (a trustworthy person), *withāq* (a bond), *īthāq*. In its concrete sense **wathāq** is the rope used to tie a thing.

That is, a *mīthāq* is not a loose word; it is a **knotted** undertaking.

**Which pledge is meant?** In the classical commentaries there are three main views:

| View | Which pledge | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The pledge of primordial nature** | The pledge given at the human being's creation | Al-Aʿrāf 7/172: *"Your Lord took from the loins of the children of Adam their offspring and made them bear witness against themselves: Am I not your Lord?"* |
| **The oath of allegiance** | The pledge of loyalty given to the Prophet | The Medinan context; al-Fatḥ 48/10, 18 |
| **Reason and evidence** | The necessity established by the proofs | *Mīthāq* figuratively, "an unavoidable conclusion" |

In this commentary I impose no preference. All three have been defended in the sources, and the wording of the verse does not settle which is meant. Moreover the three do not exclude one another.

### إِن كُنتُم مُّؤْمِنِينَ — "if you are going to believe"

This condition at the end of the sentence looks strange at first glance. The verse is addressing those who do not believe; to say "if you believe" at the end seems contradictory.

Several explanations are given for this in the classical commentaries:

| Explanation | Sense |
|---|---|
| **"If you are people who will believe"** | The condition looks to the future: if you have the capacity to believe |
| **"If you believe in the binding force of a pledge"** | The condition attaches to the *mīthāq* in the middle of the verse |
| **"If you lay claim to faith"** | The one addressed is a person who counts himself a believer but whose conduct does not bear it out |

**The third explanation is the one most consistent with the sūra as a whole**, and I state this not as a preference but as a reading that the context points to. For in verse 16 the sūra will direct its address straight at *alladhīna āmanū* (those who believe) and will report a deficiency in them. The one the sūra addresses is not outside but inside.

On this reading the tone of the sentence is: *"if you count yourselves believers, do what the thing you say you believe in requires."*

## 57/9 — هُوَ ٱلَّذِى يُنَزِّلُ عَلَىٰ عَبْدِهِۦٓ ءَايَٰتٍۭ بَيِّنَٰتٍ لِّيُخْرِجَكُم مِّنَ ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ إِلَى ٱلنُّورِ ۚ وَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بِكُمْ لَرَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Huwa'lladhī yunazzilu ʿalā ʿabdihi āyātin bayyinātin li-yukhrijakum mina'ẓ-ẓulumāti ila'n-nūr; wa inna'llāha bikum la-raʾūfun raḥīm*
"It is He who sends down clear signs upon His servant, that He may bring you out of the darknesses into the light. God is indeed most kind to you, most merciful."

### يُنَزِّلُ — the Form II pattern

The verb is in the **تنزيل (*tanzīl*)** form, not *inzāl*. The difference between the two forms is recorded by the classical lexicographers thus: *inzāl* conveys sending down all at once, *tanzīl* sending down **piece by piece, at intervals.**

This difference fits the context here: the verses were sent down not at one time but spread over years.

In verse 25 of this same sūra **أَنزَلْنَا** (*anzalnā* — the *inzāl* form) will be used, and there the things sent down will be the Book, the balance and iron. The difference of form will come up again when we take up that verse.

### ءَايَٰتٍۭ بَيِّنَٰتٍ — clear signs

**آية** — root **أ-ي-ي** (or, according to some lexicographers, **أ-و-ي**): a sign, a mark, a proof. An *āya* is what points to something. The Qurʾān uses this word for the sentences of the text, for the signs in nature, and for miracles alike.

**بينة** — root **ب-ي-ن**: to separate two things. The full analysis of the word was made in the section on al-Bayyina, and what was recorded there is this: a *bayyina* means, before "clear", **what distinguishes.** What comes between two things and separates them from one another.

That is, the expression "clear signs" is nearer to meaning **distinguishing marks** than easily understood verses. And this is consistent with what follows in the sūra: in verse 25 it will be said that the prophets were sent *bi'l-bayyināt*, and in verses 12-15 a scene of **separation** will be set up.

### مِّنَ ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ إِلَى ٱلنُّورِ — from the darknesses into the light

It was recorded at al-Baqara 2/17-20: **in the Qurʾān *nūr* is always singular and *ẓulumāt* always plural.** The right is one, the wrong innumerable.

This pattern recurs many times in the Qurʾān, and always in the same direction: from the darknesses **into the light.** For the reverse direction the pattern changes: *"…they bring them out of the light into the darknesses"* (al-Baqara 2/257) — there the subject is *ṭāghūt*.

**What is striking here is that the verb is transitive:** *li-yukhrijakum* — "that He may bring you out." It is not you who come out; you are brought out.

At al-Baqara 2/17-20 the common conclusion of the two similitudes was recorded thus: **the light comes from outside.** Neither could the man who kindled a fire hold the light in his hand, nor could the man in the storm summon the lightning. This verse says the same thing from the positive side: coming out of the darkness is not something that happens of itself.

And this finding is a preparation for the scene that will be set up three verses later. In verse 13 the hypocrites will **ask for light** and will not be given it. Where the light comes from has been said here, in verse 9.

### عَبْدِهِۦ — "His servant"

This expression, used of the Prophet, occurs in many places in the Qurʾān and always does the same work: to keep the messenger in the position of a **servant.**

In a sūra that will take up **monasticism** in verse 27 and **the sending of the prophets** in verse 25, this naming is meaningful. The messenger is not exalted; he is named as a servant given a charge.

### رَءُوفٌ رَّحِيمٌ — *raʾūf* and *raḥīm*

**رؤوف** — root **ر-أ-ف**: tenderness, delicacy, pity. Most lexicographers count *raʾfa* finer and more intense than *raḥma*: *raḥma* is general, while *raʾfa* is in particular the feeling of **not being able to bear that someone should be harmed.**

**رحيم** — root **ر-ح-م**: mercy. The detailed analysis of the word was made in the section on al-Fātiḥa; it is from the same root as *raḥim* (the womb).

The coming together of the two names occurs in several places in the Qurʾān. And its place here is meaningful: **the verse closes with mercy in the middle of a demand (faith and spending).** The demand is not hardened; the intention behind the demand is stated.

## 57/10 — وَمَا لَكُمْ أَلَّا تُنفِقُوا۟ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ وَلِلَّهِ مِيرَٰثُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ ۚ لَا يَسْتَوِى مِنكُم مَّنْ أَنفَقَ مِن قَبْلِ ٱلْفَتْحِ وَقَٰتَلَ ۚ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ أَعْظَمُ دَرَجَةً مِّنَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَنفَقُوا۟ مِنۢ بَعْدُ وَقَٰتَلُوا۟ ۚ وَكُلًّا وَعَدَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْحُسْنَىٰ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ خَبِيرٌ

*Wa mā lakum allā tunfiqū fī sabīli'llāhi wa lillāhi mīrāthu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ; lā yastawī minkum man anfaqa min qabli'l-fatḥi wa qātal; ulāʾika aʿẓamu darajatan mina'lladhīna anfaqū min baʿdu wa qātalū; wa kullan waʿada'llāhu'l-ḥusnā; wa'llāhu bimā taʿmalūna khabīr*
"What is the matter with you that you do not spend in the way of God, when to God belongs the inheritance of the heavens and the earth? Those of you who spent and fought before the conquest are not equal to those who spent and fought afterwards; their degree is greater. Yet to all God has promised the best. God is aware of what you do."

One of the longest verses in the sūra, and it does three separate things within itself: it asks a question, gives a ground, and sets up a **grading.** The third is especially important.

### وَلِلَّهِ مِيرَٰثُ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ

**ميراث** — root **و-ر-ث**: to receive what is left by someone. From the same root come *wārith*, *waratha*, *īrāth*, *turāth* (inheritance, cultural heritage).

The sentence is the unfolding of the word *mustakhlafīn* in verse 7. There it was said: that in which you have been made successors. Here it is said: **the final heir of that thing is God.**

And the word order is again fronted: *lillāhi* (to God belongs) has been brought forward, *mīrāthu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* (the inheritance of the heavens and the earth) comes after. The emphasis: **not to anyone else.**

**The argumentative value of the sentence is this:** the economic logic of miserliness is "if I do not give it, it stays with me". The verse demolishes the premise of that logic — it will not stay with you. What withholding secures is not keeping but only a change in **the manner of its leaving.**

This argument is built elsewhere in the Qurʾān as well: *"In the end We shall inherit the earth and all who are upon it"* (Maryam 19/40).

There is a simple observation that ties this to the present, and it is not forced: **no fortune goes with its owner.** This is not a religious claim but an observable fact. What the verse does is to make this fact a ground for giving.

### لَا يَسْتَوِى — "they are not equal"

Root **س-و-ي**: to be equal. The same root as *istawā* in the fourth verse.

The verb **يستوي** is typically used in the Qurʾān where comparisons are made: *"The blind and the seeing are not equal"* (Fāṭir 35/19), *"Are those who know equal to those who do not know?"* (al-Zumar 39/9).

The difference in the use here is this: the two groups compared are people who have **done the same act.** In the comparison of the blind and the seeing there are two different conditions; here what both sides have done is the same — spending and fighting.

The difference lies only in **when** it was done.

### ٱلْفَتْح — which conquest?

**فتح** — root **ف-ت-ح**: to open. From the same root come *miftāḥ* (a key), *fattāḥ*, *istiftāḥ*. The word's sense of "victory" comes from the metaphor of "opening the door": a place that was closed has been opened.

Which conquest is meant is disputed:

| View | Which event | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Al-Ḥudaybiya** | The treaty of al-Ḥudaybiya | The Qurʾān uses the word *fatḥ* of al-Ḥudaybiya at the head of Sūrat al-Fatḥ: *"We have granted you a manifest conquest"* (al-Fatḥ 48/1). Moreover entry into Islam accelerated after the treaty. |
| **The conquest of Mecca** | The taking of Mecca | The unqualified use of the word mostly calls this to mind; and after the conquest of Mecca the risk was greatly reduced |

Both views have been defended in the classical commentaries. The sources state that the first view is the more widespread, but this is not an *ijmāʿ*.

**In this commentary I impose no preference**, because the measure the verse sets up is the same whichever event is meant: there is a **threshold**, and what was done before that threshold does not have the same weight as what was done after.

### The value of timing

This is the real matter of the verse and needs attention.

The verse says this: **the same act has a different weight at a different moment.**

This is an unfamiliar measure in moral thought. The value of an act is usually measured by two things: its **intention** and its **outcome.** The verse adds a third measure: its **moment.**

And the logic of the measure is plain: **the value of an act is proportionate to the risk it carries at that moment.**

Let us make this concrete. The person who spends before the conquest:
- does not know what the outcome will be
- has no sign that the property he gives will come back
- incurs a cost by taking sides
- is one of few; his contribution is relatively large

The person who spends after the conquest:
- has seen the outcome settled
- finds the cost of taking sides lowered, and it may even bring a return
- is one of many; his contribution is relatively small

**From outside the two acts look the same.** The same amount, the same place, the same form. But one was done in uncertainty and the other in certainty.

**The lessening of risk lowers the value of the act.** The verse says so plainly.

One consequence of this is: **to join something after its rightness has become clear is not the same as joining it while its rightness is not yet clear.** Both are good; but in the language of the verse there is a difference of "degree".

The Qurʾān sets up this measure elsewhere as well:

*"The first forerunners — of the Muhājirūn and the Anṣār — and those who followed them in doing good: God is well pleased with them, and they with Him."* (al-Tawba 9/100). Here too there are two groups and the order is kept: those who came first, and those who then followed in doing good. The second are praised too, but the ordering is fixed.

*"And the forerunners, the forerunners."* (al-Wāqiʿa 56/10). The word is *as-sābiqūn*, its root **س-ب-ق** — to get ahead in a race. The same root will come in verse 21 as a command: *sābiqū*.

### And the note that comes immediately after

Having made the grading, the verse does not stop there:

> **وَكُلًّا وَعَدَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْحُسْنَىٰ**
> *"Yet to all God has promised the best."*

Without this sentence the verse would have established a **hierarchy** and those who came later would have been left outside. The sentence prevents this: there is a difference of degree, but **the promise is the same for both.**

The word **كلاًّ** has been brought to the head of the sentence (a fronting of the object) and comes with *tanwīn* — here the *tanwīn* is counted as *ʿiwaḍ* (standing in place of an omitted annexed term): "to every one of them". Its being brought forward is for emphasis: **to all of them without exception.**

This is the sentence that establishes the balance of the verse and must be read with care. For a grading can easily turn into a **claim of superiority**: "we came first, you came later." The verse closes that door.

And the manner of the closing is striking too: not by denying the difference of degree, but **by equalising the promise.** The difference is real but not exclusionary.

### What this says to the present

The present-day counterpart of this measure is wide and not confined to a religious context.

**One.** To join a view, a cause or a person after their rightness has become plain is common and easy. The measure the verse sets is this: such joining is not without value, but **it cannot be counted equal to joining at the moment of uncertainty.**

**Two.** The most demanding side of the measure is applying it to one's own past. When did the sentence "I supported it too" begin to be said? While there was risk, or after it had passed?

**Three.** The measure can also be read in reverse, and this too matters: **what is risky today?** The logic of the verse is a compass showing where value lies — and the compass points not to where the crowd is but to where the crowd is not yet.

**Four.** And the note in the last sentence must be remembered: *"to all He has promised the best."* This measure cannot be used to belittle those who came later. When it is so used, one is acting against the verse's own note.

### خَبِير — *khabīr*

The verse ends with a sentence resembling the close of the fourth verse, but the attribute has changed: there *baṣīr* (seeing), here **khabīr.**

**Root: خ-ب-ر.** An interesting root. A *khabīr* is one who knows the **inner face** of a thing — not its outside. From the same root come *khabar* (news), *ikhbār*, *mukhbir*, and **خبرة (*khibra*)**: deep knowledge acquired by experience.

There is another concrete sense recorded by the lexicographers: *khabara'l-arḍa* — to plough the ground and turn its underside up. That is, at the centre of the root stands **bringing the inside to light.**

That *khabīr* and not *baṣīr* should be said at the end of this verse is exactly right. For the subject of the verse is **two acts that look the same from outside.** Seeing (*baṣar*) is not enough here; what distinguishes them is the inner face of the act — under what conditions, at what risk, at what moment it was done.

---

## 57/11 — مَّن ذَا ٱلَّذِى يُقْرِضُ ٱللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا فَيُضَٰعِفَهُۥ لَهُۥ وَلَهُۥٓ أَجْرٌ كَرِيمٌ

*Man dha'lladhī yuqriḍu'llāha qarḍan ḥasanan fa-yuḍāʿifahu lahu wa lahu ajrun karīm*
"Who is it that will lend God a goodly loan, so that He may multiply it for him many times over? And for him there is a noble reward."

One of the boldest expressions in the Qurʾān. One must not pass on without noticing what the sentence says: **God is asking His servants for a loan.**

### قرض — the concrete sense of the root

**Root: ق-ر-ض.** And the original sense of the root gives the whole colour of the word: **to cut, to break off, to separate a piece with the teeth or with shears.**

Let us look at the derivatives:
- **قرض (*qaraḍa*)** — he cut, he gnawed off. This verb is used when a mouse gnaws at something
- **مقراض (*miqrāḍ*)** — shears
- **قراضة (*qurāḍa*)** — a piece cut off and fallen, a clipping (especially clippings of gold and silver)
- **انقرض (*inqaraḍa*)** — it was cut off, it ran out, its line died out. The word used today in Turkish, *inkıraz* (extinction), and the Arabic term for an extinct species come from this same root
- **قرض (*qarḍ*)** — a loan, a debt

**That the word for a loan should come from this root is striking.** For the word pictures lending thus: **to cut a piece off the whole in one's hand and give it.**

This is a different image from the neutral tone of the English word "loan". In English, to lend is for part of one's property to change hands for a time; the Arabic root rests on the verb **to cut.** What is given is a piece subtracted from the giver's whole.

**And the sense of "comparison" comes out of the same root.** The lexicographers also record for *qarḍ* the sense of "mutual cutting / matching": to set two things side by side and measure them, to cut one to match the other. This suits the nature of a loan — what is given and what is taken back are matched.

This second sense is consistent with the general texture of the sūra too: al-Ḥadīd is a sūra of **measure**; in verse 25 the *mīzān* (the balance) will come.

### قَرْضًا حَسَنًا — "a goodly loan"

**حسن** — root **ح-س-ن**: beauty, goodness. It was recorded at al-Baqara 2/112: *iḥsān* is both "to do good" and "to do a thing well".

What, then, makes a loan "goodly"? The conditions listed in the classical commentaries are these, and all of them are drawn not from the wording of the verse but from the Qurʾān's other verses on spending:

- **That it be from lawful earnings** — *"Spend of the good things you have earned"* (al-Baqara 2/267)
- **That it not be followed by reproach** — *"Do not void them by reproach and by injury"* (al-Baqara 2/264)
- **That it be given with a willing heart** — not under compulsion
- **That no return be expected** — from human beings; it is expected from God
- **That it be given from the good of what one has** — *"You will not attain to goodness until you spend of what you love"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/92)

That these are listed as conditions is an inference, and I do not present it as an *ijmāʿ*. But because it rests on the Qurʾān's own verses it is a sound inference.

**That the word comes indefinite** is meaningful as well: *qarḍan ḥasanan* — "a goodly loan." No amount is stated. The verse sets no threshold.

### The boldness of the expression

Now let us look at what the sentence does.

The verse places a being who is **absolutely rich** in the position of one **asking for a loan.** And it does this directly, without noting that it is a figure of speech.

This was startling for the first hearers as well. It is reported that when the verse came down some people said, "Is God poor, that He asks a loan of us?" I do not speak with certainty about the soundness of these reports and I ascribe them to no source; but the reaction is meaningful for showing the tension the expression carries. The Qurʾān itself records a similar objection: *"God has certainly heard the word of those who said: God is poor and we are rich"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/181).

What, then, does the expression do? **It carries over into spending the three elements a loan relation holds:**

**One. The certainty of return.** A loan differs from a gift: its coming back is expected. By naming spending a loan, the verse lodges the certainty of its return inside the language. A gift is one-directional; a loan is two-directional.

**Two. The obligation of the receiver.** The lender has a right over the borrower. The expression makes the return not a favour but an **undertaking.**

**Three. The ownership of what is given.** To be able to lend, the thing given must belong to the giver. And here an interesting tension arises: verse 7 said that the property does not belong to the human being (*mustakhlafīn*), while verse 11 puts him in a position to lend it.

This is not a contradiction but an **act of honour.** To entrust what is one's own and then, when asking for a part of it back, to say "lend me this" — is to raise the giver's standing. In the classical commentaries this point is generally put thus: the expression shows not God's need but **the servant's encouragement.**

We may also put it thus: to say "loan" when asking back something that was a trust is the greatest courtesy language can offer.

### فَيُضَٰعِفَهُۥ — multiplying many times over

**ضعف** — root **ض-ع-ف**. The root has two groups of senses, and they are interestingly opposite to one another: **weakness** (*ḍaʿf*) and **doubling** (*ḍiʿf*).

The lexicographers connect the two thus: a thing's being folded over on itself both multiplies it and carries a sense of bending or slackening. This connection is the lexicographers' interpretation; it is not certain.

**ضِعْف (*ḍiʿf*)** in Arabic is "the like of a thing" — that is, its being doubled. **مضاعفة (*muḍāʿafa*)** is a folding again and again.

The verb here is not the verbal noun of the *mufāʿala* form but stands in a transitive pattern derived from *mufāʿala*, and **the amount of the increase is not stated.** The same sentence occurs at al-Baqara 2/245, and there is an addition there: *"…that He may multiply it for him, **many times over**"* (*aḍʿāfan kathīra*). Here that addition is absent; the amount is left indefinite.

### *Qarḍ ḥasan* in the Qurʾān

The expression occurs in several places in the Qurʾān and has the same structure in all of them:

- **Al-Baqara 2/245** — *"Who is it that will lend God a goodly loan, that He may multiply it for him many times over?"* This verse is almost word for word the same as al-Ḥadīd 57/11.
- **Al-Muzzammil 73/20** — *"…establish the prayer, give the alms, and lend God a goodly loan."* Here in the imperative.
- **Al-Ḥadīd 57/18** — it will be repeated in this same sūra, this time together with those who give alms.

**That al-Baqara 2/245 should be so close to this verse** is striking, and the context of the two verses is similar as well: both come in the context of fighting and spending.

### Word order: the question pattern itself

The sentence opens with a question: **مَّن ذَا ٱلَّذِى** — "who is it that…"

This is a strong pattern of emphasis in Arabic. Instead of a simple "who?" (*man*), the threefold *man* + *dhā* (demonstrative) + *alladhī* (relative pronoun) has been used. Its approximate sense: "come now, who is the one who…"

The function of the pattern is this: **the question is asked not to receive an answer but to call for candidates.** Like the announcement of an offer. The tone of the English "who is there?" or "is there such a one?"

And this tone separates the verse from a command. In verse 7 the command *anfiqū* (spend) was given. Here there is no command; there is an **invitation.** The same act is asked for once with a command and once with a call.

### أَجْرٌ كَرِيمٌ — a *karīm* reward

**كريم** — root **ك-ر-م**: to be precious, to be superior, to be generous. One of the concrete uses of the word is for soil and plants: *arḍ karīma* — fertile ground; *karm* — the vine.

*Karīm* is different from "great". **Greatness reports quantity, *karam* reports quality.** That is, what is promised is not much but **of a superior kind.**

This looks like a deliberate difference from *ajrun kabīr* (a great reward) in verse 7. In the seventh verse faith and spending were named together and the return was called **great**; here a loan is named and the return is called **precious.** A move from quantity to kind.

I do not present this as a firm distinction; whether the Qurʾān separates adjectives this finely is open to debate. But that the words differ must be recorded as an observation.

# 57/12-15 — The scene of separation

A single scene is built across four verses, and this is the most visual part of the sūra. The elements of the scene are: **a running light**, **a light asked for as a loan**, **a command to turn back**, and **a wall drawn between them.**

The structure of the section is symmetrical:

| Verse | Who | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **12** | The believers | Their light runs before them; they receive good news |
| **13** | The hypocrites | They ask for light; they are sent back; a wall is raised |
| **14** | An exchange | "Were we not with you?" — and an answer of five verbs |
| **15** | The verdict | No ransom is accepted |

And the nearest relative of this scene in the Qurʾān is verse 17 of Sūrat al-Baqara. This link is central and will be taken up in detail below.

---

## 57/12 — يَوْمَ تَرَى ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ يَسْعَىٰ نُورُهُم بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَبِأَيْمَٰنِهِم بُشْرَىٰكُمُ ٱلْيَوْمَ جَنَّٰتٌ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَا ۚ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ

*Yawma tara'l-muʾminīna wa'l-muʾmināti yasʿā nūruhum bayna aydīhim wa bi-aymānihim: bushrākumu'l-yawma jannātun tajrī min taḥtiha'l-anhāru khālidīna fīhā; dhālika huwa'l-fawzu'l-ʿaẓīm*
"On the day you will see the believing men and the believing women, their light running before them and on their right: 'Your good news today is gardens beneath which rivers flow, in which you will abide for ever. That is the great triumph.'"

### ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَٱلْمُؤْمِنَٰتِ — men and women

The Qurʾān uses this paired pattern in particular places. The verse's symmetry with the next is preserved here as well: in verse 13 it will say *al-munāfiqūna wa'l-munāfiqāt* — again a pair.

And in verse 18 *al-muṣṣaddiqīna wa'l-muṣṣaddiqāt* (the men and women who give alms) will come. The sūra uses this pattern three times.

This is plainly a choice that makes the scope visible. In Arabic the masculine plural can cover both sexes; counting them separately is an emphasis.

### يَسْعَىٰ نُورُهُم — "their light runs"

One of the most striking images of the sūra.

**سعى** — root **س-ع-ي**: to walk fast, to run, to strive. The word carries both physical speed and **effort.** From the same root come *saʿy* (the walking between al-Ṣafā and al-Marwa) and *masʿā*. The Qurʾān uses this root when it says *"the human being has only what he strives for"* (al-Najm 53/39).

**The subject of the verb is the light.** The light is not carried, not held in the hand; it **runs.** It is described like something that moves of itself.

This gives the light a kind of autonomy. And this autonomy explains why in verse 13 the hypocrites **cannot be given** light: light is not an object that can be handed over or borrowed. It is something that moves along with the person.

**بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ** — literally "between their hands", idiomatically **"before them."** That is, the light goes not behind but in front — it shows the way.

**وَبِأَيْمَٰنِهِم** — "and on their right." Here there is a disagreement:

| Reading | Sense | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The right side** | The light is before them and at their right side | *Aymān* is the plural of *yamīn* (right); in the Qurʾān the right side is the side of good |
| **The records of deeds** | The light comes from the book given into their right hands | *Yamīn* occurs often in the Qurʾān in the context of the record of deeds (al-Ḥāqqa 69/19, al-Inshiqāq 84/7) |

The two readings do not exclude one another. The second accords with verse 19 of the sūra — there it will be said *"theirs is their reward and their light."*

**A question:** why not only "before them" but also "on their right"? The explanation given for this in the classical commentaries is the **encompassing** character of the light — it does not merely show the way, it surrounds the person. I record this as an inference.

### The visual logic of the scene

The narrative technique of this verse is worth noting.

The verse begins with **"you will see"** (*tarā*) — that is, the reader is placed in the position of a spectator. And what he sees is light. No face is described, no dress, no number. The only thing visible is light.

This has a consequence: **what distinguishes the believers in the scene is light.** In the next verse the hypocrites will ask for light — because light is the distinguishing mark itself. To be without light is not to be invisible; it is **not to be recognised.**

### بُشْرَىٰكُمُ ٱلْيَوْمَ — "your good news today"

**بشرى** — root **ب-ش-ر**: skin, the outer surface of the body. The analysis of the word was made at al-Baqara 2/25: good news is the **appearing of good tidings on the face** — when the news is received the colour of the skin changes.

**The speaker of the sentence is not named.** Who is speaking: the angels, or a direct address? The text does not say, and in such places the Qurʾān frequently conceals the agent.

The bringing forward of the word **ٱلْيَوْمَ** (today) is for emphasis: the waiting is over.

---

## 57/13 — يَوْمَ يَقُولُ ٱلْمُنَٰفِقُونَ وَٱلْمُنَٰفِقَٰتُ لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ٱنظُرُونَا نَقْتَبِسْ مِن نُّورِكُمْ قِيلَ ٱرْجِعُوا۟ وَرَآءَكُمْ فَٱلْتَمِسُوا۟ نُورًا فَضُرِبَ بَيْنَهُم بِسُورٍ لَّهُۥ بَابٌۢ بَاطِنُهُۥ فِيهِ ٱلرَّحْمَةُ وَظَٰهِرُهُۥ مِن قِبَلِهِ ٱلْعَذَابُ

*Yawma yaqūlu'l-munāfiqūna wa'l-munāfiqātu lilladhīna āmanū: unẓurūnā naqtabis min nūrikum. Qīla: irjiʿū warāʾakum fa'ltamisū nūrā. Fa-ḍuriba baynahum bi-sūrin lahu bāb: bāṭinuhu fīhi'r-raḥmatu wa ẓāhiruhu min qibalihi'l-ʿadhāb*
"On the day when the hypocrite men and the hypocrite women will say to those who believed: 'Look at us, that we may take a portion of your light.' It will be said: 'Turn back behind you and seek a light there.' Then a wall with a gate will be raised between them. Within it there is mercy; on its outer side, before it, punishment."

One of the densest verses of the sūra. It contains three separate movements: a request, a refusal, and a separation.

### ٱنظُرُونَا — "look at us"

The word has two readings, and both are found in the classical sources:

| Reading | Analysis of the root | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **"Look at us"** | *naẓara* (he looked) | Turn to us, turn your faces towards us — so that your light may fall on us too |
| **"Wait for us"** | the sense of *naẓara* as "to wait" | Slow down, wait for us — so that we may catch up |

The second sense is well established in Arabic: *intiẓār* (waiting) is from the same root. Moreover a variant reading *anẓirūnā* (grant us a respite, wait for us) is also transmitted. Since I cannot state with certainty which reciter this reading belongs to, I write no name.

**Both senses fit the same scene** and complete one another: wait, so that we may catch up; look, so that your light may fall on us too.

### نَقْتَبِسْ — "let us take a portion"

The finest word of the verse.

**Root: ق-ب-س.** Its concrete sense: **to take a live coal from a fire.** A *qabas* is a small piece of fire taken from a large one — an ember carried on the end of a branch, a torch.

The practice of life behind the word is plain: lighting a fire was laborious. If there was fire in one house, a neighbour would come and take a coal from it and kindle his own hearth. **Fire was borrowed.**

The Qurʾān uses this concrete sense in another place:

*"When he saw a fire and said to his family: 'Stay here; I have seen a fire. Perhaps I shall bring you a brand from it, or find guidance at the fire.'"* (Ṭāhā 20/10)

It is Moses' scene in the valley. The same root, the same verb: *ātīkum minhā bi-qabas.* And there too what is taken from the fire is for finding the way.

**اقتباس (*iqtibās*)** — the word used today in Turkish and in Arabic for a "quotation" is from this same root. To take a piece of someone's words has been thought of like taking a coal from a fire. This shows how living a metaphor the word carries.

**The verb in the verse is in the افتعال (*iftiʿāl*) form:** *naqtabis*. This form mostly adds the sense that **the act is done by the person for himself.** That is, "let us take a portion of your light for ourselves."

### The link with al-Baqara 2/17

This is one of the most important links in the commentary and deserves a heading of its own.

At al-Baqara 2/17 there was this similitude:

> *"Their likeness is that of one who kindles a fire: when it lights up what is around him, God takes away their **light** and leaves them in darknesses, unable to see."*

The finest detail recorded there was this: the man kindled a **fire**, but God took away their **light**. The text does not say *nārihim* (their fire), it says **nūrihim** (their light). The illuminating side of the fire was taken, the burning side left.

**Now look at al-Ḥadīd 57/13.** The same elements are there, but the scene has moved on:

| | **Al-Baqara 2/17** | **Al-Ḥadīd 57/13** |
|---|---|---|
| The source of the light | A fire the person kindled himself | The light of others |
| What happens | The light **is taken away** | The light **is asked for as a loan and not given** |
| The outcome | Being left in darkness | Being left on the other side of the wall |
| The time | This world | The hereafter |
| The word | *nūrihim* (their own light) | *nūrikum* (your light) |

**The two verses are two stages of the same story.** In al-Baqara the light goes in this world; in al-Ḥadīd it is sought in the hereafter and not found.

And the difference between them shows the heart of the matter:

**At al-Baqara 2/17 the person had a light, and it was taken.** That is, for a time it was bright — the expression "when it lights up what is around him" said as much. The belonging was real, then it was emptied out.

**At al-Ḥadīd 57/13 the person has no light at all and asks for someone else's.** And this request is the diagnosis of the matter: **the hypocrite's relation is always built on borrowing.** In this world he stood inside the community, benefited from its returns, lived in its safety — but did not kindle his own light. In the hereafter he tries the same thing.

And the answer is one of the harshest sentences in the sūra.

### ٱرْجِعُوا۟ وَرَآءَكُمْ فَٱلْتَمِسُوا۟ نُورًا — "turn back behind you and seek a light"

**By whom the answer is given is not said.** The verb is passive: *qīla* — "it was said." Is it the believers who speak, the angels, someone else? The text is silent.

This silence is meaningful. The answer ceases to be a personal refusal and becomes a **verdict.**

**ورائكم** — "behind you." That is, where you came from: this world. And the command is plain: *that is where light was kindled.*

**التمسوا** — root **ل-م-س**: **to touch with the hand, to search by groping.** *Lams* is to touch; *iltimās* is to search by groping — the Turkish word *iltimas* in use today comes from this root as well, though its sense has drifted.

The choice of the word is merciless. The verb used for someone seeking light is **to search by touch** — that is, it says in advance that he is in darkness. One who can see searches by seeing; one who is blind searches by groping.

**Carrying out the command is impossible.** There is no going back. So this is not a proposed solution; it is **the declaration of an impossibility.** In Arabic such commands are called commands of *taʿjīz* (of reducing to incapacity): what cannot be done is commanded, and by this the finality of the situation is shown.

### The thing that cannot be borrowed

This is the central idea of the scene and deserves to be said plainly.

Light is treated here like an **object** — something that can be asked for as a loan, of which a portion can be taken. And it is refused.

Why is it refused? The verse gives no ground. But the elements within the sūra itself give an answer:

**In verse 12 the light "ran"** — that is, it was something that moved along with the person, not a lantern held in the hand.
**In verse 9 the light had to do with being brought out of darkness** — a state, a position.
**In verse 28 the light will be something that is "walked in"** — *nūran tamshūna bih*.

That is, in the sūra's understanding of light, light is not an object that is carried but **the person's condition itself.** And a person's condition cannot be transferred to another.

The general counterpart of this is: **some things cannot be transferred.** A person's knowledge, his habits, his character, the bond he has built — none of these can be handed over to another. What can be borrowed are the things outside these: property, status, standing, belonging.

And this part of the sūra is built precisely on that distinction. The hypocrite had taken everything that can be borrowed — membership of the community, safety, standing. What could not be borrowed he had not taken.

### فَضُرِبَ بَيْنَهُم بِسُورٍ — "a wall is raised between them"

**ضرب** — root **ض-ر-ب**: to strike. But in Arabic this verb has an extremely wide range of uses: *ḍaraba mathalan* (he gave a parable), *ḍaraba fi'l-arḍ* (he travelled in the earth), *ḍaraba khayma* (he pitched a tent).

Here it means **to raise a wall**, and the concrete tone of the verb is preserved: the wall is not gently set in place, it is **struck up.** A sudden and decisive movement.

The verb is again **passive**: who raises it is not said.

**سُور (*sūr*)** — root **س-و-ر**: a wall that surrounds a thing; a city wall. The word has passed into Turkish as *sur*. From the same root comes *siwār* (a bracelet — what encircles the arm); and some lexicographers connect the word **sūra** with this root too (a piece of text with a boundary drawn round it, with definite limits) — but this connection is disputed and not certain.

The choice of the word is meaningful: not *jidār* (wall) or *ḥāʾiṭ* (wall) but **sūr**. A *sūr* is the wall that surrounds a city — that is, the structure that **defines** inside and outside. Whoever is inside it belongs to the city; whoever is outside does not.

### لَّهُۥ بَابٌ — "with a gate"

**The wall has a gate.** This detail may look superfluous, but it is not.

A wall with no gate is an absolute division. A wall with a gate implies that **at one time it could be passed through.** The gate is there; but it is no longer passed.

In the classical commentaries there are different explanations of this gate's function: the place where mercy enters, the place where the believers pass, or the place the hypocrites could once have passed but did not. The text does not say, and I impose no preference.

But there is something to record: **the gate shows that the separation came later.** The two sides were once on the same side — in verse 14 the hypocrites will say so themselves: *"Were we not with you?"*

### بَاطِنُهُۥ فِيهِ ٱلرَّحْمَةُ وَظَٰهِرُهُۥ مِن قِبَلِهِ ٱلْعَذَابُ

"Within it there is mercy; on its outer side, before it, punishment."

**Here the link within the sūra closes.** The two names used of God in the third verse — **aẓ-Ẓāhir** and **al-Bāṭin** — are used here of the two faces of a wall.

At the level of verbal repetition this is too marked to be a coincidence. In the same sūra, the same pair of words, ten verses apart, in two entirely different contexts.

What I recorded above (57/3) as my own reading I complete here:

**In the third verse two opposites were united in a single being:** *huwa'ẓ-Ẓāhiru wa'l-Bāṭin.* There the uniting of opposites was the expression of *tawḥīd*.

**In the thirteenth verse a single object is divided into two opposite faces:** the same wall, on one side mercy and on the other punishment. And the difference is **where the one who looks is standing.**

This gives a description of what separation is: the two sides are not looking at different things. **They are looking at the same thing, from different places.** Mercy and punishment are not two separate objects; they are two faces of a single reality.

I offer this not as a ruling but as a reading to which the text's choice of words points.

**مِن قِبَلِهِ** — "from its side, from that quarter." *Qibal* conveys direction and side; from the same root comes *qibla*. That is, the punishment meets those on the outer side of the wall from that quarter.

### The link with al-Aʿrāf 7/46

There is a similar screen elsewhere in the Qurʾān:

*"Between the two there is a screen. And upon the heights (*al-aʿrāf*) are men who know both sides by their marks."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/46)

There the word is *ḥijāb* (screen), here *sūr* (wall). And there is a group standing upon the screen; here there is none. The two scenes are not the same and must not be reduced to one another; but it is a common method of the Qurʾān to tell of the separation of two sides through an architectural image.

## 57/14 — يُنَادُونَهُمْ أَلَمْ نَكُن مَّعَكُمْ ۖ قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ وَلَٰكِنَّكُمْ فَتَنتُمْ أَنفُسَكُمْ وَتَرَبَّصْتُمْ وَٱرْتَبْتُمْ وَغَرَّتْكُمُ ٱلْأَمَانِىُّ حَتَّىٰ جَآءَ أَمْرُ ٱللَّهِ وَغَرَّكُم بِٱللَّهِ ٱلْغَرُورُ

*Yunādūnahum: a-lam nakun maʿakum? Qālū: balā, wa lākinnakum fatantum anfusakum wa tarabbaṣtum wa'rtabtum wa gharratkumu'l-amāniyyu ḥattā jāʾa amru'llāh, wa gharrakum bi'llāhi'l-gharūr*
"They will call out to them: 'Were we not with you?' They will say: 'Yes, but you led yourselves into temptation, and you waited, and you doubted, and false hopes deceived you until God's command came; and the great deceiver deceived you about God.'"

### يُنَادُونَهُمْ — "they will call out"

**نداء** — root **ن-د-و/ن-د-ي**: to call from a distance, to shout. It is not used for speaking to someone nearby; there is **distance.**

The choice of the word completes the scene: the wall has been raised, and now there is no longer conversation but **calling out.** In the previous verse the hypocrites were *saying* to the believers directly (*yaqūlu*); now they are *calling out* (*yunādūna*).

### أَلَمْ نَكُن مَّعَكُمْ — "were we not with you?"

One of the most familiar sentences of the sūra, and the whole defence lies in this one sentence.

**The claim of the sentence is true.** And in the answer this is granted: **بَلَىٰ** — "yes."

*Balā* in Arabic is a particular particle of answer that affirms a negative question. Had *naʿam* (yes) been said to the question "were we not?", it would have meant "yes, you were not"; *balā* breaks the negation: "no, that is not so — you were."

That is, the answer does not **refute** the claim. The being-together really was there.

**This is the crux of the matter.** Hypocrisy is not standing outside. It is standing inside. In the same rank, in the same mosque, in the same community. And the verse does not deny it.

What is rejected is **the assumption that being-together was enough.**

This is the same measure as the one set up at al-Baqara 2/62 and 2/111-112: **the label does not save.** There the matter was membership of a group; here it is physical being-together. Both come out at the same place: belonging is not the criterion.

### Five verbs

The body of the answer consists of five verbs, and these five verbs describe a **process.** In order:

| | Verb | Root | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **فَتَنتُمْ أَنفُسَكُمْ** | ف-ت-ن | You led yourselves into temptation |
| 2 | **تَرَبَّصْتُمْ** | ر-ب-ص | You waited, you watched |
| 3 | **ٱرْتَبْتُمْ** | ر-ي-ب | You doubted |
| 4 | **غَرَّتْكُمُ ٱلْأَمَانِىُّ** | غ-ر-ر / م-ن-ي | False hopes deceived you |
| 5 | **غَرَّكُم بِٱللَّهِ ٱلْغَرُورُ** | غ-ر-ر | The deceiver deceived you about God |

**The sequence itself is a diagnosis.** The subject of the first three verbs is **you**; the subject of the last two is **someone else.** That is, the process advances by agency slipping out of one's hands: first the person does something to himself, then something is done to him.

Now one by one.

### فَتَنتُمْ أَنفُسَكُمْ — "you led yourselves into temptation"

**فتنة** — root **ف-ت-ن**. Its concrete sense is perfectly clear: **to put gold into the fire and separate the pure from the dross.** To melt a metal and test it.

Two directions come out of this:
- **Testing** — *"We test you with evil and with good as a trial"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/35)
- **Corruption, confusion, leading astray** — the consequence of failing the test

In the verse the object of the verb is **yourselves**: *fatantum anfusakum.* That is, the tester and the tested are the same person.

**What this expression describes needs attention.** Two readings are possible and both are found in the sources:

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| **You brought yourselves to ruin** | You destroyed yourselves by hypocrisy |
| **You tested yourselves with your own hand and lost** | You produced the risk yourselves |

The concrete sense of the word leaves room for the second as well, and that reading is the more striking: **the gold that goes into the fire chose the fire itself.** The person put himself into a trial — appearing to stand on one side while remaining open to the other — and in that trial he was left behind.

### تَرَبَّصْتُمْ — "you waited"

**Root: ر-ب-ص.** Its meaning: to wait, to watch, to await the outcome of a thing and act accordingly.

In the root of the word there is **watching for an opportunity.** *Tarabbuṣ* is not an idle waiting; it is **waiting in order to see the outcome.**

The Qurʾān uses this word several times in the context of the hypocrites:

*"…they watch for the disasters that will befall you."* (al-Tawba 9/98). The word is *yatarabbaṣu*, the same form.

*"They keep watching you. If a conquest comes to you from God they say, 'were we not with you?'; and if the deniers have a share they say, 'did we not gain the upper hand over you and protect you from the believers?'"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/141)

**This last verse coincides word for word with al-Ḥadīd 57/14** and is striking: the same interrogative sentence — *"were we not with you?"* — occurs there too. In al-Nisāʾ this sentence is spoken in this world; in al-Ḥadīd in the hereafter. The same defence, twice.

**The diagnostic value of *tarabbuṣ* is this:** the one who waits has not decided. And not deciding is not neutrality — it is **planning to take the side the outcome indicates.** This is the exact reverse of the measure in verse 10: there value was given to what was done before the outcome was clear.

That such an opposition should be set up between the two verses shows the texture of the sūra. **Verse 10 exalts the one who acted early; verse 14 indicts the one who waited.** The two are the two ends of the same measure.

### ٱرْتَبْتُمْ — "you doubted"

**Root: ر-ي-ب.** *Rayb* in Arabic is a particular kind of doubt: **the doubt that unsettles, that gives no peace within.** Unlike ordinary ignorance (*shakk*), in *rayb* there is a disquiet.

This word occurred in the second verse of Sūrat al-Baqara: **لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ** — "there is no doubt in it." What was set down at the very beginning of that sūra as an attribute of the Book comes here as the state of the hypocrite.

**The order is striking: doubt comes third.** That is, doubt is not the beginning of the process but its middle. First comes waiting, then doubt.

This is the reverse of the usual order. One usually says "I doubted, and therefore I waited." The verse says the opposite: **you waited, then you doubted.**

This ordering says something, and I record it as my own reading: **not deciding gives birth to doubt.** The longer a matter is held in suspense, the more the suspension itself turns into a conviction. A person begins in time to doubt what he has not decided about — because not deciding itself looks for a ground, and doubt is the readiest ground.

### وَغَرَّتْكُمُ ٱلْأَمَانِىُّ — "false hopes deceived you"

The fourth verb, and here the subject changes: no longer you, but **false hopes.**

**أَمَانِىّ** — root **م-ن-ي**. It was fully analysed at al-Baqara 2/78 and 2/111. What was recorded there was this: the word has two senses — **fancy / wishful hope** (a conviction resting not on reality but on desire) and **to recite through** (to voice a text without understanding it). And at 2/111 the word was used of the claim that reserves salvation to one's own group: *tilka amāniyyuhum.*

I do not repeat it here. What must be taken up is the function of the word in this context.

**At al-Baqara 2/111 *amāniyy* was a group's claim to salvation.** Here it is the same: the hypocrite's fancy is the conviction that "being together will suffice". And that conviction was voiced in the question at the head of the verse: *"Were we not with you?"*

That is, **the fourth of the five verbs explains the first sentence of the verse.** The hypocrite's defence is in fact the content of his self-deception.

The link recorded in al-Baqara holds here too: **a religion that does not rest on knowledge rests on wishing.** And the most widespread form of wishing is to make salvation depend on a membership.

**That the word comes in the plural** is meaningful as well: *al-amāniyy* — fancies. Not a single fancy; many fancies feeding one another.

### حَتَّىٰ جَآءَ أَمْرُ ٱللَّهِ — "until God's command came"

**حتى** — a particle that draws a limit. It was taken up in the section on al-Takāthur: *ḥattā* says where a process stops.

And here the place where the process stops is **death** — or the resurrection; both can answer to the expression "God's command". Both readings are found in the classical commentaries.

The structure is the same as in the first two verses of Sūrat al-Takāthur: a distraction is described, then *ḥattā* comes and a limit is set. There the limit was *al-maqābir* (the graves); here it is *amru'llāh.*

**And this fixes the duration of the fourth verb:** the fancy lasted until the command came. That is, a fancy has a lifetime, and that lifetime is the same as the person's.

### وَغَرَّكُم بِٱللَّهِ ٱلْغَرُورُ — "the deceiver deceived you about God"

The fifth and last verb. And it is almost a word-for-word repetition of the expression treated in the section on al-Infiṭār.

At al-Infiṭār 82/6 the question was: **مَا غَرَّكَ بِرَبِّكَ ٱلْكَرِيمِ** — *"What deceived you about your generous Lord?"* What was recorded there:

- **غرّ (*gharra*)** — he deceived, he made a thing appear other than it is
- **غرور (*ghurūr*)** — being deceived; and at the same time the thing that deceives
- **الغرور (*al-gharūr*)** — an intensive form: the great deceiver; used in the Qurʾān as a name of Satan
- **the particle بِ** — "by God", that is, an attribute of God is made the material of the deception

And the link there with Luqmān 31/33 was recorded: *"Let not the life of this world deceive you, and let not the great deceiver deceive you about God."*

I do not repeat it here. The **difference** that must be taken up is this:

**In al-Infiṭār a question was being asked; here an answer is given.** There, *mā gharraka* — "what deceived you?" Here, *gharrakum… al-gharūr* — "the deceiver deceived you."

And it was recorded in the section on al-Infiṭār: that question was asked not because it has no answer but **because its answer cannot be given** — every answer would be a confession of having been deceived.

In al-Ḥadīd the answer is given not by the one deceived but by the other side. That is, the answer comes from outside, and the one deceived is obliged to accept it.

**That the last two of the five verbs are from the same root** (*gharratkum* and *gharrakum*) is no coincidence. The same verb is repeated with two different subjects: first the fancies, then *al-gharūr*. That is, there are two things that deceive, and the second uses the first.

At Luqmān 31/33 exactly these two deceivers were counted: **the life of this world** and **al-gharūr.**

### The five verbs as a whole: a description of decay

Now let us look at the list as a whole. What emerges is not an event but a **process**:

1. **To put oneself into a trial** — to keep both sides open
2. **To wait** — not to decide before seeing the outcome
3. **To doubt** — indecision turning into conviction
4. **To take refuge in a fancy** — to say "I am with them anyway"
5. **To be deceived** — the collapse of the refuge

**And there is no "great sin" in the list.** No denial, no betrayal, no open crime. What is described is a series of attitudes taking place entirely **within.** Someone looking from outside could see none of it.

This recalls the close of the sixth verse of the sūra: **وَهُوَ عَلِيمٌۢ بِذَاتِ ٱلصُّدُورِ** — "the Knower of what is in the breasts." Eight verses later, what is in the breasts is counted out one by one.

## 57/15 — فَٱلْيَوْمَ لَا يُؤْخَذُ مِنكُمْ فِدْيَةٌ وَلَا مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ ۚ مَأْوَىٰكُمُ ٱلنَّارُ ۖ هِىَ مَوْلَىٰكُمْ ۖ وَبِئْسَ ٱلْمَصِيرُ

*Fa'l-yawma lā yuʾkhadhu minkum fidyatun wa lā mina'lladhīna kafarū; maʾwākumu'n-nār, hiya mawlākum, wa biʾsa'l-maṣīr*
"Today no ransom will be taken from you, nor from those who denied. Your refuge is the Fire; it is your *mawlā*. And an evil destination it is!"

### فِدْيَة — *fidya*

**Root: ف-د-ي**: to rescue by paying a price. From the same root come *fidāʾ* (a ransom price), *fidya*, *fadāʾ*. The price paid for the release of a prisoner of war was named with this word.

**That the word should occur in this sūra is meaningful**, because from the beginning the sūra has been using an **economic language**: spending, loan, inheritance, wage, multiplying many times over. And here comes the last term of that same language: ransom.

The sūra says this: **there was a time when this language was current, and that time has passed.** In this world property could be given and its return multiplied many times over (57/11). Now, even if property is offered, it is not taken.

And note: no ransom is taken not because there is no property. There may be property; but **it is no longer of any use.** The Qurʾān says this plainly elsewhere: *"Even if they had the earth full of gold and offered it as ransom…"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/91).

### مَوْلَىٰكُمْ — "your *mawlā*"

The harshest word of the verse.

**Root: و-ل-ي**: to be near, to be adjacent. From the same root come *walī* (friend, protector), *walāya* (the bond of mutual protection), *mawlā*, *awlā*. The concrete sense of the root is **nearness** — adjacency in place.

**مولى (*mawlā*)** is a wide word in Arabic: master, one who frees a slave, one who has been freed, helper, friend, protector, near kinsman. The sense changes with the context. What is common to them is **the presence of a bond.**

And in the Qurʾān the word is used overwhelmingly in a **favourable** context: *"God is the *mawlā* of the believers"*, *"You are our *mawlā*."*

**Here, however, it is used of the Fire.**

This is an instance of **tahakkum** (ironic inversion) and is a recognised device in classical rhetoric: a favourable word is used in an unfavourable place and a bitter contrast is produced.

But there is a finer sense as well. Read with the lexical meaning preserved, what emerges is this: **a *mawlā* is the one nearest to a person.** And the hypocrite had always sought a nearness in this world — he had stood near the community, had asked to borrow light, had said "were we not with you?"

The verse gives him a nearness. But not the nearness he wanted.

I record this as my own reading; I do not ascribe it to any classical exegete.

### مَأْوَىٰكُمْ and ٱلْمَصِير

**مأوى** — root **أ-و-ي**: to take refuge, to shelter. A *maʾwā* is a place of refuge, a shelter. The word carries associations of **protection and rest.**

**مصير** — root **ص-ي-ر**: to turn from one state into another, to arrive. A *maṣīr* is the place one arrives at or the state one turns into.

Both words carry the same inversion: **shelter** and **destination.** Both are normally words that convey peace.

The three words of the verse (*maʾwā*, *mawlā*, *maṣīr*) thus do the same work: refuge, nearness and arrival — the three things a human being looks for — are used of the Fire.

## 57/16 — أَلَمْ يَأْنِ لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ أَن تَخْشَعَ قُلُوبُهُمْ لِذِكْرِ ٱللَّهِ وَمَا نَزَلَ مِنَ ٱلْحَقِّ وَلَا يَكُونُوا۟ كَٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ مِن قَبْلُ فَطَالَ عَلَيْهِمُ ٱلْأَمَدُ فَقَسَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ ۖ وَكَثِيرٌ مِّنْهُمْ فَٰسِقُونَ

*A-lam yaʾni lilladhīna āmanū an takhshaʿa qulūbuhum li-dhikri'llāhi wa mā nazala mina'l-ḥaqq, wa lā yakūnū ka'lladhīna ūtu'l-kitāba min qablu fa-ṭāla ʿalayhimu'l-amadu fa-qasat qulūbuhum; wa kathīrun minhum fāsiqūn*
"Has the time not come for those who believe that their hearts should soften to the remembrance of God and to the truth that has come down? And let them not be like those who were given the Book before: a long time passed over them, and then their hearts hardened. Many of them are transgressors."

The verse of the sūra that looks most towards the present, and probably its hardest verse. Its difficulty comes from who is addressed.

### To whom is the address?

**لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟** — "to those who believe."

This is the single most important element of the verse and must not be missed.

Whom had the sūra addressed up to this point? Those who do not believe (8), those who do not spend (10), the hypocrites (13-15). Now the address changes and turns **explicitly to those who believe.**

And what is said is not good news but a **reproach.**

This is one of the most direct warnings the Qurʾān directs at its own community. And exactly here the measure `STYLE.md` sets down begins to operate: what the text describes is an **attribute**, and here that attribute is sought not in a group outside but in **those inside.**

It is reported that Ibn Masʿūd said that only a few years lay between their becoming Muslims and the coming down of this verse, and that he mentioned this as a reproach. I do not quote the wording of the report exactly, and since I cannot state with certainty in which hadith collection it stands I ascribe it to no source; but the idea the report carries is important for understanding the verse: **however short the period, the cooling can begin.**

### أَلَمْ يَأْنِ — "has the time not come?"

**Root: أ-ن-ي.** Its meaning: **for the time of a thing to come, for it to ripen, to reach its due state.**

The derivatives show the colour of the root:
- **أنى (*anā*)** — its time has come
- **إنى / أناة (*ināʾ / anāt*)** — a time, a moment; and gravity, not being hasty
- **آنية (*āniya*)** — a vessel. The relation of the word to this sense is disputed among the lexicographers
- **آن (*ān*)** — boiling, brought to a scalding heat. It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"they will be given to drink from a boiling spring"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/5) — *ʿaynin āniya*

**This last derivative opens up the logic of the root.** For a thing to be *ānī* is for it to heat up and come to its proper state. That is, in the root there is **a process reaching its end**: the food is cooked, the water has boiled, the fruit has ripened.

The Qurʾān uses this root in one more place in exactly this sense: *"…without waiting for the food to be ready"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/53) — *ghayra nāẓirīna ināh*, "without watching for its time."

**So the question asked is: "has it not come to its proper state?"**

This is different from saying "you ought to". It is not a command; it is a **question about timing.** And the question itself implies something: this thing has a time, and that time is passing.

**The function of the question pattern:** a negative question opening with *a-lam* conveys in Arabic *taqrīr* (extracting an admission) — it is asked in order to make the one addressed grant something. The only reasonable answer to "has the time not come?" is "it has." And the one addressed gives that answer himself.

This is more effective than a direct reproach. For the one addressed is not accused; **he passes the verdict on himself.**

### أَن تَخْشَعَ قُلُوبُهُمْ — the softening of hearts

**خشوع** — root **خ-ش-ع**. Its full analysis was made at al-Baqara 2/45: **to soften, to be lowered, to bend.** And it was recorded there that the Qurʾān uses this root of dry ground as well: *"You see the earth barren (*khāshiʿa*); when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/39).

What was recorded there was this: *khushūʿ* is the breaking of hardness, becoming able to take in what comes. Dry and hard ground does not absorb water; it lets it run off.

**قسوة** — root **ق-س-و**. Its full analysis was made at al-Baqara 2/74: **to harden, to become rigid, to lose elasticity.** And there the opposition of the two words was established plainly: *qaswa* is the exact opposite of *khushūʿ*.

At al-Baqara 2/74 it was also recorded: the matter is not whether the knowledge arrives but **whether the place it arrives at takes it in.** The same water does two different things in two soils.

**I do not repeat these here.** What must be recorded is that the two words stand **in the same verse.**

In al-Baqara *khushūʿ* was in verse 45 and *qaswa* in verse 74 — twenty-nine verses apart. Al-Ḥadīd 57/16 sets the two face to face in a single sentence:

> *"…has the time not come for their hearts to **soften**? … and then their hearts **hardened.**"*

Two states, in one sentence, as alternatives to one another. And between them there is something — the truly new element of the verse.

### فَطَالَ عَلَيْهِمُ ٱلْأَمَدُ — "a long time passed over them"

**This is the centre of the verse**, and here the Qurʾān gives a **cause** for hardness of heart. It is a striking cause.

**أمد** — root **أ-م-د**. Its meaning: **the end of a term, a duration, a term, a distance.** An *amad* is the interval of time between two points.

The lexicographers record a distinction between *amad* and *zamān*: *zamān* is duration in general; *amad* is duration **that has an end, that runs towards a finish.** Like a term.

Another place where it occurs in the Qurʾān: *"…he will wish that between him and that evil there were a long distance (*amad*)"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/30).

**طال** — root **ط-و-ل**: to grow long, to be long. From the same root come *ṭūl* (length), *ṭawīl* (long), *ṭāʾila* (power, means).

**The structure of the sentence is important.** Three verbs, ranged with two *fāʾ* conjunctions:

> **أُوتُوا۟ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ** ← **فَطَالَ عَلَيْهِمُ ٱلْأَمَدُ** ← **فَقَسَتْ قُلُوبُهُمْ**
> the Book was given → the term grew long → the hearts hardened

The conjunction **فَ (*fāʾ*)** conveys in Arabic **sequence and causation**: "then, whereupon." Unlike *wāw* it does not merely add; it builds a chain.

That is, the verse gives **duration** as the cause of the hardening.

### "Duration" as a cause

This is something that needs attention.

**Other causes for hardness of heart are given in the Qurʾān as well:** breaking a covenant (al-Māʾida 5/13), committing sins, rejecting a warning. These are **causes of act** — something was done and a consequence followed.

**The cause given here, however, is not one of act.** No one has done anything. Only **time has passed.**

This changes the nature of the diagnosis. The verse says: **cooling need not be the result of an event. It can happen of itself.**

And this is a much harder warning. For if there is a cause of act, the way to put it right is plain: leave off the act. If the cause is duration, the passing of duration cannot be prevented.

**The practical consequence of this — and it is the most demanding side of the verse — is:** doing nothing also produces a result. A bond, left untended, does not stay where it was; it weakens. Stillness is not a neutral state.

### Why does duration cool things?

The verse does not explain this; it only records it. What follows is not an explanation of what the verse says but my own assessment.

The **repetition** of a thing makes it familiar. And familiarity lowers attention — this is a known property of the human mind and is called "habituation". As the same stimulus is repeated, the response diminishes. This is one of the most basic and most widespread properties of the nervous system and is observed from single-celled organisms up to human beings.

And this property is functional: to respond at the same intensity continually would exhaust the organism.

But the same property produces a loss when the thing repeated is **meaningful.** A sentence that shakes a person the first time he hears it becomes a background noise the thousandth time. The content of the words has not changed; **the sensitivity of the receiver** has changed.

I do not present this finding as a "scientific miracle"; the verse contains no claim about neuroscience. I record only this: **the phenomenon the verse describes is an observable phenomenon** and everyone recognises it in daily life.

### To whom the warning is made: the shift of address

Now let us look again at the structure of the verse. The sentence has two parts:

**The first part:** "Has the time not come for the hearts of those who believe to soften?"
**The second part:** "And let them not be like those who were given the Book before."

The second part sets up a **comparison.** And the direction of the comparison is striking: those who believe are compared with those who were given the Book before them.

**The logic of the comparison is this:** they too had been given a Book. That is, the starting point was the same. The difference lies in what came afterwards — and the verse states that "afterwards" in a single word: **duration.**

This is the heaviest side of the verse. For it implies: **the same process can happen to you too.** The difference between "them" and "you" is not a difference of kind; it is a **difference of time.** They began earlier; your term is still short.

The measure `STYLE.md` sets down operates here exactly and must be stated plainly: **the verse passes no wholesale verdict on a community.** What it sets up is the description of a mechanism, and on whom that mechanism works is stated plainly — the one addressed is not the side being compared to, but the **persons** the comparison is made about.

Indeed the verse enters this note within itself: **وَكَثِيرٌ مِّنْهُمْ فَٰسِقُونَ** — "**many** of them are transgressors." It says *many*, not *all*. The same note will be repeated in verses 26 and 27 of the sūra; and in verse 27 it will say *"We gave those among them who believed their reward."*

These notes recur three times across the sūra, and that recurrence is the in-text evidence that the verdict is not wholesale.

### فَٰسِقُون — *fāsiq*

**Root: ف-س-ق.** Its concrete sense: **to come out of one's shell.** In Arabic the slipping of the ripening date out of its skin is called *fasaqati'r-ruṭabatu*; and the verb is used when a mouse comes out of its hole.

That is, *fisq* is **to go outside the boundary.** To leave the place where one ought to be.

Its difference from unbelief is important: **unbelief is a covering over** (analysed at al-Baqara 2/6), **while *fisq* is a going out.** Unbelief denies a thing; *fisq* goes outside the thing it accepts.

This distinction is exactly in place here. The verse speaks of those who were given the Book — that is, of those who have a Book, who know, who have accepted. The word used of them is not *kāfir* but **fāsiq**: they have gone outside a boundary they knew.

### What this says to the present

This verse is the place in the sūra that looks most directly to the present, and it becomes concrete at several points.

**One: the direction of the warning.**

Religious texts are mostly read outwards — to show who is in the wrong, who has gone astray. This verse makes that impossible, because it names its addressee plainly: *alladhīna āmanū.*

This is the same trap recorded in the section on al-Humaza, and here it is sharper still: **to read this verse about other people is itself the very state the verse describes.** For what the verse describes is a text ceasing to touch the person. The one who turns the text towards someone else is doing exactly that.

**Two: the scale of the duration today.**

For those the verse addressed, "a long time" was at most a few generations. The duration in question today is measured in centuries. The mechanism the verse describes works more strongly as time passes.

This has visible and observable consequences: a text memorised and its meaning never looked at; an act of worship kept up with its form complete and its effect nil; religious language becoming part of everyday speech and expressing nothing. None of this is denial. **All of it happens within continuity.**

**Three: the two faces of repetition.**

Religion is built on repetition — the daily prayer, the yearly fast, the text read continually. And repetition both fixes and wears away. The same act.

The question of the verse touches this tension: *"has the time not come?"* That is, repetition by itself is not enough; attention has to be placed inside the repetition once more.

For this there is a measure the Qurʾān itself gives, and it is in this sūra: **verse 17.** What comes immediately after the hardness of hearts is the coming alive of dead ground. I take it up below.

**Four: an early warning.**

The idea carried by the saying ascribed to Ibn Masʿūd — that the reproach came despite the shortness of the period — determines how the verse is read. The warning is made not at an advanced stage of the decay but at its **beginning.**

The reason for this is probably the following: cooling that comes with duration is not noticed. A person who cools because of an event knows that he has cooled; one who cools with time does not, because every day resembles the one before. The warning is made early because of this property of the phenomenon.

## 57/17 — ٱعْلَمُوٓا۟ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحْىِ ٱلْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا ۚ قَدْ بَيَّنَّا لَكُمُ ٱلْـَٔايَٰتِ لَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

*Iʿlamū anna'llāha yuḥyi'l-arḍa baʿda mawtihā; qad bayyannā lakumu'l-āyāti laʿallakum taʿqilūn*
"Know that God gives life to the earth after its death. We have made the signs clear to you, that you may reason."

### The juxtaposition itself

**That this verse should stand in this place is more important than the content of the verse.**

The preceding verse described hardened hearts. This verse describes the coming alive of dead ground. Between them there is no transitional sentence at all.

And the link is plain:

| **57/16** | **57/17** |
|---|---|
| The heart hardened | The ground is dead |
| *qasat qulūbuhum* | *baʿda mawtihā* |
| Hardening | Death |
| — | **Giving life** |

At al-Baqara 2/45 it was recorded that the root of *khushūʿ* is used of ground (Fuṣṣilat 41/39: *"You see the earth barren; when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells"*). And at al-Baqara 2/74 *qaswa* was set down as the opposite of that root.

**Al-Ḥadīd 57/16-17 completes this triad.** *Khushūʿ* was softening, *qaswa* hardening; now it is said that hardened ground too **can be brought to life.**

That is, the function of the verse's standing in this place is: **the diagnosis of verse 16 is not a sentence but a finding.** The heart can harden — but hardened ground also comes alive.

This is the most hopeful place in the sūra, and it gives the hope not with a promise but with an **observation**: every year, before everyone's eyes, dead ground comes alive.

### يُحْىِ ٱلْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ مَوْتِهَا

This expression recurs many times in the Qurʾān and is generally used as evidence for two things:

**One. Evidence that resurrection is possible.** *"As We give life to dead ground… such is the resurrection"* (Qāf 50/11). And: *"You see the earth barren. When We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and brings forth every lovely pair. That is because God is the truth itself, and He gives life to the dead"* (al-Ḥajj 22/5-6).

**Two. Evidence that the coming alive of the heart is possible.** This reading comes directly out of the context of al-Ḥadīd 57/17 — for the verse comes immediately after the hardness of hearts.

**The second reading is peculiar to this sūra** and is strongly supported by the context. The same expression is used elsewhere in the Qurʾān as evidence for the resurrection; here the sequence turns it into evidence for something else.

In Sūrat al-Rūm this link is made plainly: *"Look at the traces of God's mercy: how He gives life to the earth after its death. He will surely give life to the dead in the same way"* (al-Rūm 30/50).

### Where does the force of the likeness lie?

The function of the image of dead ground needs attention, because it is not an ordinary consolation.

**Dead ground looks dead from outside.** There is no sign at all. Its surface is dry, hard, cracked. And this state lasts for months.

**But the ground is not empty.** There is seed in it, there is root, there is the capacity to come alive. What is not seen is not what does not exist.

And the coming alive does not happen through the ground's own effort: **the water comes from outside.**

These three elements (invisibility, the persistence of capacity, water coming from outside) fit exactly the situation described in verse 16. A hardened heart is not a dead heart. And the softening is not something to be produced by oneself.

The measure recorded at al-Baqara 2/17-20 holds here again: **the light comes from outside.** And so does the water.

### قَدْ بَيَّنَّا لَكُمُ ٱلْـَٔايَٰتِ

**بيّنّا** — *bayyana*, Form II. It was recorded in the section on al-Bayyina: the root **ب-ي-ن**, to separate two things. *Tabyīn* is to make a thing distinguishable.

**آيات** — here it can be read in two senses: the verses of the text, or the signs in nature. The context supports both — for the first sentence of the verse speaks of a sign in nature.

**لَعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ** — "that you may reason."

**عقل** — root **ع-ق-ل**. Its concrete sense: **to bind.** An *ʿiqāl* is the cord tied to a camel's knee; it is used so that the camel does not run off. The ring that holds the Arab headdress is called an *ʿiqāl* today as well.

That is, *ʿaql* means, before "the faculty of thinking", **the faculty of binding**: to bind one thing to another, to relate, to rein in.

The use of the word in this verse is apt, because the verse asks for **a link to be made**: between the ground and the heart. Between a visible phenomenon and an invisible one.

And in the Qurʾān the root *ʿaql* always occurs in verbal form — *taʿqilūn*, *yaʿqilūn* — never as a noun. That is, "intellect" is named not as something possessed but as **a work that is done.**

## 57/18 — إِنَّ ٱلْمُصَّدِّقِينَ وَٱلْمُصَّدِّقَٰتِ وَأَقْرَضُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا يُضَٰعَفُ لَهُمْ وَلَهُمْ أَجْرٌ كَرِيمٌ

*Inna'l-muṣṣaddiqīna wa'l-muṣṣaddiqāti wa aqraḍu'llāha qarḍan ḥasanan yuḍāʿafu lahum wa lahum ajrun karīm*
"Truly, the men who give alms and the women who give alms, and those who lend God a goodly loan — it will be multiplied for them, and theirs is a noble reward."

### ٱلْمُصَّدِّقِين — two readings of the word

There is a variant reading of the word, and it changes the meaning:

| Reading | Analysis of the root | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **al-muṣṣaddiqīn** (with shadda) | From the *iftiʿāl* form of *taṣaddaqa* (to give alms), the *t* assimilated to the *ṣ* | Those who give alms |
| **al-muṣaddiqīn** (without shadda) | *ṣaddaqa* (he confirmed), Form II | Those who confirm, who affirm the truth |

Both readings are transmitted in the sources. Since I cannot state with certainty which reading belongs to which reciter, I write no name.

**The context supports the first reading**, because the verse goes on to speak of lending, and the sūra as a whole is about spending. But the second reading is meaningful too and fits the texture of the sūra: in verse 19 *aṣ-ṣiddīqūn* (those who greatly affirm the truth) will come — from the same root.

**The two readings do not exclude one another**; they even complete one another. And this comes from the root itself.

### The root صدق and the name of *ṣadaqa*

**Root: ص-د-ق.** Its original sense is **truthfulness, correspondence to reality.** The derivatives:

- **صدق (*ṣidq*)** — truthfulness, correspondence to what is real
- **صادق (*ṣādiq*)** — one who speaks the truth
- **تصديق (*taṣdīq*)** — confirming
- **صديق (*ṣiddīq*)** — one who greatly affirms the truth; an intensive form
- **صداق (*ṣadāq*)** — the dower, what is given to the woman at marriage
- **صدقة (*ṣadaqa*)** — the property given

**That *ṣadaqa* should come from this root is striking and is dwelt upon in the classical sources.**

Why has the act of "giving" been named from the root of "truthfulness"? The widespread explanation is this: **alms are the confirmation of the claim to faith.** A person says that he believes; the alms show whether that word is real.

This explanation suits the structure of the word and unites the two readings of the verse: *muṣaddiq* (one who confirms) and *muṣṣaddiq* (one who gives alms) are doing the same thing — one with words, the other with property.

And *ṣadāq* (the dower) follows the same logic: the material counterpart of the claim of marriage.

### وَأَقْرَضُوا۟ — a point of word order

There is a grammatical feature in the sentence, and it has been noted by the exegetes.

The sentence runs: *inna'l-muṣṣaddiqīna wa'l-muṣṣaddiqāti* **وَأَقْرَضُوا** *'llāha…*

After two **nouns** are counted, a **verb** is joined on: "the men who give alms and the women who give alms and they lent…" This structure, which looks odd in English, is noticeable in Arabic too, because normally the elements joined are expected to be of the same kind.

The explanation is this: *al-muṣṣaddiqīn* is an active participle, and an active participle carries verbal force. That is, the sentence is in sense like "they gave alms and they lent"; the coordination is made not with the wording but with the **sense.** In Arabic this is called *ʿaṭf ʿala'l-maʿnā*.

Another explanation is that *wa aqraḍū* is the predicate of *inna*; on this construal the sentence reads "the men who give alms and the women who give alms — these have lent God a goodly loan."

Both explanations are found in the sources, and the difference of sense is not great.

### يُضَٰعَفُ لَهُمْ — the passive pattern

In verse 11 the verb was active and its subject was named: *fa-yuḍāʿifahu lahu* — "that He may multiply it for him." Here the verb is **passive**: *yuḍāʿafu lahum* — "it will be multiplied for them."

The difference shows in the tone. Verse 11 was a sentence of invitation, and there it was emphasised who gives the return. Here there is a sentence of statement, and the emphasis has shifted to **those who receive.**

And the adjective of the return is the same: **أَجْرٌ كَرِيمٌ** — a word-for-word repetition of the expression in verse 11. Seven verses apart, the sūra repeats the same promise.

## 57/19 — وَٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرُسُلِهِۦٓ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلصِّدِّيقُونَ ۖ وَٱلشُّهَدَآءُ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ لَهُمْ أَجْرُهُمْ وَنُورُهُمْ ۖ وَٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ وَكَذَّبُوا۟ بِـَٔايَٰتِنَآ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ أَصْحَٰبُ ٱلْجَحِيمِ

*Wa'lladhīna āmanū bi'llāhi wa rusulih, ulāʾika humu'ṣ-ṣiddīqūn; wa'sh-shuhadāʾu ʿinda rabbihim lahum ajruhum wa nūruhum; wa'lladhīna kafarū wa kadhdhabū bi-āyātinā ulāʾika aṣḥābu'l-jaḥīm*
"And those who believe in God and His messengers — they are the *ṣiddīqūn*. And the witnesses are with their Lord; theirs is their reward and their light. And those who deny and reject Our signs — they are the companions of the blaze."

### ٱلصِّدِّيقُون — the *ṣiddīqūn*

**The pattern: فِعِّيل (*fiʿʿīl*).** In Arabic this is a strong intensive pattern: one who does the act much, who has made that quality a habit. Like *sikkīr* (a heavy drinker), *khimmīr*.

*Ṣiddīq* means **one who greatly affirms the truth.** And affirming can go in two directions: being truthful in one's own word, and confirming another's truth. The lexicographers record both.

The Qurʾān uses this word of several persons: Abraham (Maryam 19/41), Idrīs (Maryam 19/56), Joseph (Yūsuf 12/46 — there as a form of address), and Mary (al-Māʾida 5/75).

**And at al-Nisāʾ 4/69 an ordering of ranks is made:** the prophets, the *ṣiddīqūn*, the witnesses, the righteous. In al-Ḥadīd 57/19 the two middle steps of that ordering — the *ṣiddīqūn* and the witnesses — are named side by side.

**The striking side of the verse is this:** this high rank is given not in return for some very particular condition but in return for **faith.** "Those who believe in God and His messengers — they are the *ṣiddīqūn*."

This is a kind of raising. And in the context of the sūra it is meaningful: in verse 16 those who believe were reproached; three verses later the same group is given a higher rank.

### وَٱلشُّهَدَآءُ — the witnesses: a disagreement of syntax

In this part of the sentence there is a disagreement of *iʿrāb* (syntax), and it directly affects the meaning:

| Reading | How the sentence is divided | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Joined** | *"…they are the *ṣiddīqūn* and the witnesses; with their Lord they have their reward and their light"* | The believers are counted both *ṣiddīq* and witness |
| **Separate** | *"…they are the *ṣiddīqūn*. And the witnesses are with their Lord; theirs is their reward and their light"* | Two separate groups; the witnesses are named additionally |

Both readings have been defended in the classical sources. The difference is whether *wa'sh-shuhadāʾ* attaches to the preceding clause or begins a new one.

I impose no preference. But this must be said: **on the second reading an emphasis is lost.** The first reading raises ordinary believers to the rank of *ṣiddīq* and witness, which accords with the encouragement in the verse's context.

### شهيد — *shahīd*

**Root: ش-ه-د**: to be present, to be a witness. *Shāhid* is a witness; *shahāda* is witnessing; *mashhad* is the place where something is witnessed.

The word's sense of "one who gives his life" **is not its primary sense in the Qurʾān**; it is a usage that settled later. In the Qurʾān *shahīd* is overwhelmingly in the sense of **witness**: *"God is witness over everything"*, *"that you may be witnesses over mankind."*

This is a point that must be recorded, because the present-day rendering "martyr" overshadows the word's Qurʾānic sense. Which sense the verse means here is disputed, and there is room for both readings.

### وَنُورُهُمْ — "and their light"

The light-texture of the sūra continues here. In verse 12 the believers' light was running; in verse 13 the hypocrites asked for light and were not given it; here light is named together with the reward as a **return.**

And in verse 28 light will come again as something to be given: *"He will give you a light by which you will walk."*

The sequence of the four occurrences marks out a path:
- **9**: bringing out of darkness into light (the source)
- **12-13**: having light and not having it (the division)
- **19**: light as a return (the recompense)
- **28**: light being given and being walked in (request and promise)

## 57/20 — ٱعْلَمُوٓا۟ أَنَّمَا ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَا لَعِبٌ وَلَهْوٌ وَزِينَةٌ وَتَفَاخُرٌۢ بَيْنَكُمْ وَتَكَاثُرٌ فِى ٱلْأَمْوَٰلِ وَٱلْأَوْلَٰدِ ۖ كَمَثَلِ غَيْثٍ أَعْجَبَ ٱلْكُفَّارَ نَبَاتُهُۥ ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ فَتَرَىٰهُ مُصْفَرًّا ثُمَّ يَكُونُ حُطَٰمًا ۖ وَفِى ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَرِضْوَٰنٌ ۚ وَمَا ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ إِلَّا مَتَٰعُ ٱلْغُرُورِ

*Iʿlamū annama'l-ḥayātu'd-dunyā laʿibun wa lahwun wa zīnatun wa tafākhurun baynakum wa takāthurun fi'l-amwāli wa'l-awlād; ka-mathali ghaythin aʿjaba'l-kuffāra nabātuhu thumma yahīju fa-tarāhu muṣfarran thumma yakūnu ḥuṭāmā; wa fi'l-ākhirati ʿadhābun shadīdun wa maghfiratun mina'llāhi wa riḍwān; wa ma'l-ḥayātu'd-dunyā illā matāʿu'l-ghurūr*
"Know that the life of this world is only a play, an amusement, an adornment, a boasting among yourselves and a rivalry in the multiplying of wealth and children. Like a rain whose growth delights the tillers; then it withers, and you see it turn yellow; then it becomes chaff. And in the hereafter there is a severe punishment, and forgiveness from God, and good pleasure. The life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion."

The Qurʾān's most detailed description of the life of this world. Five words, then a similitude, then a verdict.

This verse was mentioned in the section on al-Takāthur as "the sūra's dictionary" — for what Sūrat al-Takāthur does not say (the multiplying of what) this verse says. Now we take up the verse itself.

### Five words, one order

The five words are not ranged at random. The order needs examining:

| | Word | Root | What it describes | Who is included |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **لَعِب** (*laʿib*) | ل-ع-ب | Play — an occupation with no purpose | The child |
| 2 | **لَهْو** (*lahw*) | ل-ه-و | Amusement — what keeps one from the real work | The young |
| 3 | **زِينَة** (*zīna*) | ز-ي-ن | Adornment — occupation with appearance | — |
| 4 | **تَفَاخُر** (*tafākhur*) | ف-خ-ر | Mutual boasting | — |
| 5 | **تَكَاثُر** (*takāthur*) | ك-ث-ر | Rivalry in multitude | — |

**In the classical commentaries it is said that this ordering corresponds to the stages of a human life:** the child plays, the young man amuses himself, then he adorns himself, then he boasts, then he accumulates. This reading is widespread and reasonable; but since it is not stated plainly in the text I record it as an interpretation.

**There is another logic to the ordering, visible from within the text**, and it is firmer: **the first three are for one person, the last two are reciprocal.**

Play, amusement and adornment are things a person can do on his own. But *tafākhur* and *takāthur* are in the **tafāʿul** form — analysed fully in the section on al-Takāthur: this form conveys **reciprocity** (*mushāraka*); it shows the act going back and forth between two parties.

That is, the list moves **from things done alone towards things done with another.** And this is where the harm increases.

### لَعِب and لَهْو

**لعب** — root **ل-ع-ب**: to play. At the centre of the word stands **purposelessness**: an occupation with no outcome, done for its own sake. The lexicographers describe *laʿib* as "an act with no outcome".

**لهو** — fully analysed in the section on al-Takāthur. What was recorded there: the root **ل-ه-و**, whose original sense is **to turn away from a thing, to be occupied with something else**; and the usual English rendering "amusement" is misleading, because at the centre of *lahw* stands not merriment but **a shifting of place** — attention is taken from one place and set upon another.

And the distinction the lexicographers make was recorded there as well: *laʿib* is purposeless play, while *lahw* is what draws a person away from what matters.

I do not repeat it here. What is to be recorded is that **the two stand side by side in this verse too.** In the section on al-Takāthur it was noted that this pair frequently occurs together in the Qurʾān, and al-Anʿām 6/32 was given as an example: *"The life of this world is only a play and an amusement."*

Al-Ḥadīd 57/20 **raises that short description to five.** Al-Anʿām is content with two words; al-Ḥadīd adds three more. And the three added words take the matter out of the individual and make it social.

### زِينَة — adornment

**Root: ز-ي-ن**: to beautify, to adorn. From the same root come *tazyīn* (adorning), *muzayyan*.

The Qurʾān uses this root in an interesting way: **mostly in the sense of a thing being *"made to seem fair"***. *"Their deeds were made to seem fair to them"* (al-Anʿām 6/43, al-Naml 27/4 and elsewhere) — *zuyyina lahum.*

This usage shows the risk the word carries: **an adornment is not the thing itself; it is what is put upon the thing.** And what is adorned looks as good as its adornment.

The word is directly related to *ghurūr* (deception) in the last sentence of the verse. It was recorded in the section on al-Infiṭār: *gharra* is "to make a thing appear other than it is". Adornment does exactly that.

**And *zīna* is a turning point in the verse's list.** Play and amusement concern the person himself. Adornment, however, requires **someone looking.** If no one is going to see, one does not adorn oneself.

That is, at the third word another person enters the scene. And the fourth and fifth words describe the relation formed with that other person.

### تَفَاخُر — mutual boasting

**Root: ف-خ-ر**: to boast. There is a note on the concrete sense of the word: some lexicographers connect the word **فخّار (*fakhkhār*)** — baked clay, earthenware — with the same root. It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"He created the human being from dry clay like baked pottery"* (al-Raḥmān 55/14).

This connection is the lexicographers' interpretation and is not certain. But if it is made, the picture that emerges is striking: **hollow within, ringing when struck, easily broken.** As a description of boasting.

The analysis of this root and of the word *fakhūr* was made in the section on al-Humaza, and the link between property and standing was taken up there. I do not repeat it here.

**The pattern: تفاعل (*tafāʿul*)** — reciprocity. *Fakhara* (he boasted) → *tafākhara* (they boasted against one another). This verb was counted among the examples when this form was illustrated in the section on al-Takāthur.

That is, *tafākhur* is not boasting on one's own. **It is boasting becoming reciprocal** — one side's boasting pushes the other into boasting.

### تَكَاثُر — competing in multiplication

Fully analysed in the section on al-Takāthur. What was recorded there:

- **Root ك-ث-ر**, pattern **تفاعل**: reciprocity
- *Takāthur* is not "to multiply" (that is *taktīr*); it is **to vie with one another by multitude**
- What is criticised is not accumulation itself but **accumulation becoming relational**
- And most importantly: **in a pattern of reciprocity there is no finish line.** An absolute threshold can be crossed; a relative measure has no threshold, because the measure keeps moving

I do not repeat it here. The only thing that must be recorded is this:

**Sūrat al-Takāthur left the word without an annexed term** — of what the multitude consisted was not said, and it was noted there that this was a deliberate *ibhām* (leaving indefinite). **Al-Ḥadīd 57/20 supplies the annexed term:** *fi'l-amwāli wa'l-awlād* — in wealth and in children.

That is, the two sūras complete one another: one gives the pattern, the other its commonest material.

**That "children" should be named here** is striking and must be read with care. The Qurʾān does not condemn having children — on the contrary it counts them a favour in many places. What is criticised is the child's becoming **material for a competition in multitude** as well: being seen as something to boast of by number, by success, by position.

The measure recorded in the section on al-Takāthur operates here exactly: **to compete in the multitude of a good thing is also *takāthur*.** The moral value of the material does not change the pattern.

### كَمَثَلِ غَيْثٍ — the similitude

Now the verse passes from a five-word description to an image.

**غيث** — root **غ-ي-ث**: to rush to help, to come to the rescue. From the same root come *ighātha* (help) and *mughīth* (one who helps). It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"You were seeking help of your Lord"* (al-Anfāl 8/9) — *tastaghīthūna.*

**That this word should be used of rain is meaningful:** a *ghayth* is not ordinary rain (*maṭar*) but **the awaited rain, the needed rain, the rain that comes to the rescue.** The rain that comes after drought.

This is the first element of the similitude and it sets the tone: what is described is not a bad thing. **It is a good thing.** A thing awaited.

### أَعْجَبَ ٱلْكُفَّارَ نَبَاتُهُۥ — "its growth delights the tillers"

**Here is the place where the Qurʾān uses its own word in its own sense.**

At al-Baqara 2/6 the root **ك-ف-ر** was fully analysed: its original sense is **to cover, to put something over a thing, to conceal.** And there, when the derivatives were counted, **the tiller** stood first — because he covers the seed with soil. And **this very verse** was cited as evidence:

> *"Like a rain whose growth delights the tillers (*kuffār*)"* (al-Ḥadīd 57/20). The same word, in the same sūra, in an agricultural sense.

Now we are at the verse itself and can use the link exactly where it belongs.

**كُفَّار** here means not "the deniers" but **"the tillers."** This is the reading widely adopted in the classical commentaries, and its ground is the context: the one who rejoices at the growth of a plant is the one who sowed it.

A minority view reads the word as "the deniers" and explains it thus: those who rejoice most in this world are those who cover over the hereafter. This reading too does not run against the text, but the context supports the first more strongly — for the sentence is an agricultural scene and the word *nabāt* (growth) is named directly.

**And here there is a fineness.** That the two senses of the word should stand in the same sūra makes the original sense of the root visible: **to cover.** The tiller covers the seed, the denier covers the truth. The same act, two objects.

The definition recorded at al-Baqara 2/6 may be recalled here again: **unbelief is not not-knowing; it is covering over what one knows.** And the example of the tiller is the plainest proof of that definition — the tiller does not deny the existence of the seed, he covers it over.

**أَعْجَبَ** — root **ع-ج-ب**: to be astonished, to admire. *ʿAjīb* (astonishing), *iʿjāb* (to fill with admiration), *ʿujb* (self-admiration) are from the same root.

The verb is in the **إفعال** form: "it pleased him, it filled him with admiration." That is, the plant impresses the tiller.

**And the choice of the word is striking here too.** *Aʿjaba* conveys not a neutral satisfaction but **admiration.** The tiller looks at his crop and is filled with wonder. This is the exact counterpart of the relation to the world the sūra describes: what he sees really is beautiful and really is the fruit of his own labour.

**The similitude does not make the world out to be worthless.** On the contrary: it grants its value and its attraction.

### ثُمَّ يَهِيجُ — "then it withers"

**هيج** — root **ه-ي-ج**: to boil, to seethe, to be set in motion; and, of a plant, **to dry up and wither.**

These two senses of the root look unrelated at first glance. The lexicographers make the link thus: a thing's reaching its final point, its overflowing. When a plant reaches the final point of its growth it does not stop; it begins to dry. This link is the lexicographers' interpretation.

**The choice of the word is extremely economical.** A single verb carries both "it reached ripeness" and "it began to dry". That is, **the peak and the decline are in the same word.**

It would have been possible to tell this with two separate verbs: "it grew, then it dried." The verse tells it with one verb, and thereby implies that there is no separate moment between the two.

The conjunction **ثُمَّ** conveys *tarākhī* (an interval): "then", but after some time has passed. It differs from *fāʾ*. There are two *thumma*s in the verse and both set time between the stages.

### فَتَرَىٰهُ مُصْفَرًّا — "you see it turn yellow"

**مصفرّ** — root **ص-ف-ر**: to be yellow. The pattern is **افعلال (*ifʿilāl*)** — a particular form used in Arabic for colours and defects. This form conveys that the colour is **thoroughly settled**: *iṣfarra* — it turned wholly yellow.

That is, not "it went yellowish" but **"it turned utterly yellow."**

**That the verb is turned towards the one addressed** is striking: *fa-tarāhu* — "you see it." The verse suddenly turns to the reader. This is the device called *iltifāt* in Arabic (a shift in the direction of address), and its function here is plain: to move from describing the scene to placing the reader inside it.

### ثُمَّ يَكُونُ حُطَٰمًا — "then it becomes chaff"

**حطام** — root **ح-ط-م**: to break, to crumble, to shatter. *Ḥuṭām* is what has been broken up and crumbled — the state of dry grass when it is rubbed in the hand and falls apart.

From the same root comes **حطمة (*ḥuṭama*)**, which occurs in verses 4-5 of Sūrat al-Humaza as a name of the Fire. That the root was treated there is useful here: *ḥuṭama* is the fire that breaks and crumbles.

### The link with al-Aʿlā 87/5

It was treated in the section on al-Aʿlā: *fa-jaʿalahu ghuthāʾan aḥwā* — "then He made it darkened chaff."

What was recorded there:
- **غثاء (*ghuthāʾ*)** — the debris a flood carries on its surface
- **أحوى (*aḥwā*)** — a darkened colour; not pure black but green or red with black mixed into it
- And it was noted that there are two readings as to which word *aḥwā* qualifies, and that the first requires fewer assumptions

**Al-Ḥadīd 57/20 and al-Aʿlā 87/4-5 draw the same picture** but with different words:

| | **Al-Aʿlā 87/4-5** | **Al-Ḥadīd 57/20** |
|---|---|---|
| The beginning | *al-marʿā* (pasture) was brought out | *ghayth* fell, the plant came up |
| The colour | *aḥwā* — darkened | *muṣfarr* — yellow |
| The final state | *ghuthāʾ* — flood debris | *ḥuṭām* — crumbled chaff |
| Number of stages | Two verbs | **Three stages** |

**The difference itself is meaningful.** Al-Aʿlā finishes in two steps: He brought it out, He made it chaff. Al-Ḥadīd sets down three steps: it filled with wonder, it yellowed, it crumbled.

And the difference suits the work of each sūra. Al-Aʿlā is a short and sharp sūra; it moves fast. Al-Ḥadīd, on the other hand, wants to tell **the process itself** — for verse 16 of the sūra also described a process (*fa-ṭāla ʿalayhimu'l-amad*).

**The difference of colour should also be noted:** in al-Aʿlā the colour is darkened, in al-Ḥadīd yellow. Yellow is the **first** sign of drying; darkening is a later stage. Al-Ḥadīd catches the process at an earlier point.

### The real point of the similitude: speed

What the similitude describes needs attention, because it is easily summed up as "the world is transient" and passed over. The verse says something finer than that.

**What is described is not the plant's dying but how quickly its death comes.**

The plant's life is a season. And the three stages within the season — sprouting, drying, crumbling — follow one another fast. Between the plant the tiller looks upon with wonder and its yellowed state there are weeks.

**The two *thumma* conjunctions supply this speed.** Each conveys an interval; but because the three stages are counted in a single sentence, the intervals are compressed in the reader's mind.

And the root sense of the word **دنيا** comes into play here. It was recorded at al-Baqara 2/4: *ad-dunyā* is from the root **د-ن-و** — **what is near.** And this finding was made there: "The human being's basic problem is in fact described here as well — it is easy to see what is near, and hard to take into account what comes after."

**The similitude is precisely about this problem of seeing.** When the tiller looks at the plant he sees its greenness; he does not see its yellowing, because it has not happened yet. And the verse, by setting the three stages side by side in a single sentence, **makes visible what is not seen.**

This is the technique of the similitude: **compressing time.** A process lasting months is lived through in a sentence.

### وَفِى ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ عَذَابٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَرِضْوَٰنٌ

The similitude ends and the verse turns suddenly to the hereafter.

**The word order is striking:** first the punishment, then forgiveness and good pleasure. In the Qurʾān the good news usually comes first; here it is the reverse.

In the classical commentaries it is said by way of explanation that the first ruling after a description of this world ought to be a warning. This is a reasonable explanation, but since it is not stated plainly in the text I record it as an interpretation.

**رِضْوَان** — root **ر-ض-و**: to be content, to be well pleased. *Riḍwān* is an intensive verbal noun: great good pleasure.

**That the word is placed last is meaningful.** It was recorded in the section on al-Bayyina: that sūra set something higher than the description of the Garden above it — **mutual good pleasure** (98/8). The same hierarchy is here as well: forgiveness is counted, then *riḍwān* comes.

And *riḍwān* will recur in verse 27 of the sūra — there as the intention of monasticism: *ibtighāʾa riḍwāni'llāh.*

### وَمَا ٱلْحَيَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنْيَآ إِلَّا مَتَٰعُ ٱلْغُرُورِ

The closing sentence of the verse and one of the most quoted expressions of the sūra.

**متاع** — root **م-ت-ع**: to derive benefit from a thing, to profit by it. A *matāʿ* is what is benefited from; goods, provisions, temporary property. From the same root come *mutʿa*, *tamattuʿ*, *istimtāʿ*.

**At the centre of the word stands transience.** A *matāʿ* is the provision a traveller carries with him — taken in order to be used up, enough to last to the end of the road.

**غرور** — fully analysed in the section on al-Infiṭār. What was recorded there: *ghurūr* is both being deceived and the thing that deceives; *al-gharūr* is an intensive form and is used in the Qurʾān as a name of Satan. And Āl ʿImrān 3/185 was given as an example: *"The life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion"* — *matāʿu'l-ghurūr.*

**That is, this sentence is word for word the same as Āl ʿImrān 3/185.** The same construction in two verses.

**There is a disagreement over how the construction is to be read:**

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| ***Ghurūr* = being deceived** | "The material of the deception" — the passing benefit that causes the deception |
| ***Ghurūr* = the thing that deceives** | "The goods of the deceiver" — the *matāʿ* that fools a person |

Both readings are possible and the difference of sense is not great.

### The balance of the verse: what it does not say

The commonest mistake in reading this verse is to take it as a **rejection of the world.** The text does not permit this and gives its grounds itself.

**One. The elements of the similitude are favourable.** *Ghayth* is the rain that comes to the rescue; the plant really is beautiful; the wonder is real. The verse does not present the world's attraction as an illusion — it presents it as **real but transient.**

**Two. The verse does not say "the world is bad"; it says "the life of this world is only these things".** The *innamā* pattern conveys restriction. That is, the matter is not the badness of the world but **the limitedness of its scope.** A statement of a boundary, not a value judgement.

**Three. The same sūra lists things to be done in the world:** spending (7), fighting (10), lending (11), the upholding of justice (25). All of these are things done in the world. A text that rejected the world would not command them.

**Four. And in verse 27 monasticism will be criticised** — that is, withdrawal from the world is criticised by the sūra itself.

If we look again at the five-word list this becomes clear: in the list there is no *property*, no *child*, no *food*, no *work*. What is in the list is **a particular relation** formed with these: turning them into a game, being distracted, reducing them to adornment, making them material for boasting, making them the subject of a rivalry.

**That is, what is criticised is not the objects but the relation formed with the objects.** This is the same measure recorded in the section on al-Humaza: there too it was not property but the relation formed with property that was criticised.

### What this says to the present

**One: the present-day counterparts of the five words.**

Because the list describes not a historical society but a structure, counterparts are easily established. But care is needed in establishing them, because the strength of the list lies in its **abstractness** — as was recorded in the section on al-Takāthur, the indefiniteness requires the one addressed to make his own list.

The only thing to record is this: the last two words are **reciprocal**, and reciprocity means that the measure lies outside oneself. The person loses his own measure; the measure becomes the position of the other party.

**Two: the measure being movable.**

This was recorded in the section on al-Takāthur and is worth repeating here, because the verse sets two reciprocal words side by side: **a relative measure has no threshold.** To be able to say "enough" a fixed measure is needed; in a comparative measure a point of "enough" cannot be defined.

**Three: the time-scale of the similitude.**

The similitude is built on a season. And there is a reason for this: a season is the longest cycle a human being can see in its entirety. A person sees the birth and the death of a plant within the same year.

He cannot do the same for his own life — because he is inside it. What the similitude does is **to make a cycle that can be seen from outside a model for a cycle that is lived from within.**

## 57/21 — سَابِقُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰ مَغْفِرَةٍ مِّن رَّبِّكُمْ وَجَنَّةٍ عَرْضُهَا كَعَرْضِ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ أُعِدَّتْ لِلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرُسُلِهِۦ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ فَضْلُ ٱللَّهِ يُؤْتِيهِ مَن يَشَآءُ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ ذُو ٱلْفَضْلِ ٱلْعَظِيمِ

*Sābiqū ilā maghfiratin min rabbikum wa jannatin ʿarḍuhā ka-ʿarḍi's-samāʾi wa'l-arḍ, uʿiddat lilladhīna āmanū bi'llāhi wa rusulih; dhālika faḍlu'llāhi yuʾtīhi man yashāʾ; wa'llāhu dhu'l-faḍli'l-ʿaẓīm*
"Race towards forgiveness from your Lord and a garden whose breadth is like the breadth of the sky and the earth, prepared for those who believe in God and His messengers. That is God's bounty; He gives it to whom He wills. God is the possessor of great bounty."

### سَابِقُوا۟ — "race"

**Root: س-ب-ق**: to get ahead in a race. It was fully analysed at al-Baqara 2/148: it is the word used of horse-racing; *sābiq* (the one in front) and *musābaqa* are from the same root.

And a table was set up there: at al-Takāthur 102/1 *takāthur* (prevailing by multitude) was blamed, at al-Muṭaffifīn 83/26 *tanāfus* and at al-Baqara 2/148 *istibāq* were commanded. The conclusion was recorded thus:

> **The Qurʾān does not forbid competing; it changes the subject of the competition.** The desire in a human being to get ahead is treated not as a defect to be torn out but as an energy whose direction is to be set.

And it was noted that the difference lies in **whether the subject is divisible**: property and rank are limited, and one person's winning is another's losing; good is not limited.

I do not repeat it here. What must be recorded is **the difference of pattern.**

**At al-Baqara 2/148 the verb is استبقوا (*istabiqū*) — the *istifʿāl* form.**
**At al-Ḥadīd 57/21 the verb is سابقوا (*sābiqū*) — the *mufāʿala* form.**

Both are from the same root but their patterns differ:

| Pattern | Its function | Difference of sense |
|---|---|---|
| **استبق (*istifʿāl*)** | Seeking the act, wanting it for oneself | "Strive to get ahead" — emphasis on effort |
| **سابق (*mufāʿala*)** | Reciprocity | "Race with one another" — emphasis on rivalry |

**That the *mufāʿala* form should be used here is striking**, because like *tafāʿul* it conveys reciprocity — and one verse earlier (57/20) *tafākhur* and *takāthur* were blamed; both are in a pattern of reciprocity.

That is, two verses apart, the sūra blames the same structure of reciprocity once and commands it once. **The only thing that changes is the subject of the competition.**

This is the plainest proof of the measure set up at al-Baqara 2/148, and the two verses standing side by side does not look like a coincidence.

### عَرْضُهَا كَعَرْضِ ٱلسَّمَآءِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ — "its breadth like the breadth of the sky and the earth"

**عرض** — root **ع-ر-ض**: breadth, width (the opposite of length). From the same root come *ʿarīḍ* (wide), *iʿrāḍ* (turning away — turning aside), *ʿāriḍ* (what comes from the side).

**The choice of "breadth" as the measure** has been dwelt upon. The explanation given in the classical commentaries is this: the breadth of a thing is less than its length; the length of something with such a breadth cannot be described. That is, the measure is given in order to show that it cannot be grasped.

This is a reasonable explanation. But a simpler reading is possible too: in Arabic *ʿarḍ* is also used generally in the sense of "breadth, extent", and what is meant here is not a precise geometrical measure but the greatness of the scope.

**At Āl ʿImrān 3/133 the same expression** comes slightly differently: *"…a garden whose breadth is as the heavens and the earth."* There "heavens" is plural, here singular. The difference does not change the meaning.

### أُعِدَّتْ — "it has been prepared"

**Root: ع-د-د**: to count, to prepare. This root was treated in the section on al-Humaza — there one of the two readings of the verb *ʿaddada* was "to make preparation" (*ʿudda*).

**That the verb should come in the perfect and in the passive** is meaningful: the preparation **is complete.** The race is not for a prize that does not yet exist; it is for something already waiting.

### ذَٰلِكَ فَضْلُ ٱللَّهِ — "that is God's bounty"

The verse opens with a command to race and closes with a note about bounty. And that note is necessary.

For the language of racing easily becomes a language of **deserving**: the one who races wins, the winner deserves. The last sentence of the verse cuts this off: **the prize is not the wage of the race; it is a bounty.**

This is the same balance as in verse 10. There too a grading was made, and immediately afterwards the balance was established by saying *"to all God has promised the best."*

The sūra makes this movement twice: it sets down a distinction, then prevents the distinction from becoming absolute.

**فضل** — root **ف-ض-ل**: to increase, to be in excess, to be superior. *Faḍl* is the surplus added on top of what is required. That is, what is given beyond what is deserved.

The word will occur three more times in the last verse of the sūra (29), and there it will be the centre of the matter.

## 57/22-23 — مَآ أَصَابَ مِن مُّصِيبَةٍ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَلَا فِىٓ أَنفُسِكُمْ إِلَّا فِى كِتَٰبٍ مِّن قَبْلِ أَن نَّبْرَأَهَآ ۚ إِنَّ ذَٰلِكَ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ يَسِيرٌ • لِّكَيْلَا تَأْسَوْا۟ عَلَىٰ مَا فَاتَكُمْ وَلَا تَفْرَحُوا۟ بِمَآ ءَاتَىٰكُمْ ۗ وَٱللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ

*Mā aṣāba min muṣībatin fi'l-arḍi wa lā fī anfusikum illā fī kitābin min qabli an nabraʾahā; inna dhālika ʿala'llāhi yasīr. Li-kaylā taʾsaw ʿalā mā fātakum wa lā tafraḥū bimā ātākum; wa'llāhu lā yuḥibbu kulla mukhtālin fakhūr*
"No affliction befalls the earth or your own selves but that it is in a book before We bring it into being. That is easy for God. So that you may not grieve over what has escaped you nor exult over what He has given you. God does not love any conceited boaster."

The two verses are a single sentence: verse 23 is the **ground** of verse 22. The conjunction *li-kaylā* (so that … not) binds the two together.

And this link is the most striking side of the verse: **emotional balance is given as the purpose of the knowledge of the decree.**

### مُّصِيبَة — *muṣība*

**Root: ص-و-ب.** Fully analysed at al-Baqara 2/155-157. What was recorded there:

- The sense of the root is **to hit, to find the mark**
- From the same root come *iṣāba* and *ṣawāb* (right)
- A *muṣība*, in the logic of the word itself, is **"what hits the mark"** — not what falls at random but what finds its target
- The word says in advance that there is a direction within the event
- And the sentence *innā lillāhi wa innā ilayhi rājiʿūn* contains two separate propositions: a declaration of **ownership** and a declaration of **direction**

I do not repeat it here. Al-Ḥadīd 57/22 adds something to the frame set up in al-Baqara.

**At al-Baqara 2/155 the afflictions were listed:** fear, hunger, loss of property, of lives and of fruits. And what was said was what should be uttered at the moment of loss.

**At al-Ḥadīd 57/22 the affliction is not listed; its scope is drawn:** *fi'l-arḍi wa lā fī anfusikum* — in the earth and in your own selves. That is, everything that happens both outside and within.

**مِن مُّصِيبَة** — the *min* at the head here has the function of *zāʾida* (emphatic), and in a negative sentence it makes the generality absolute: **"there is no affliction at all but that…"** Without exception.

### فِى كِتَٰبٍ مِّن قَبْلِ أَن نَّبْرَأَهَا

**كتاب** — here a writing, a record. That the word comes indefinite is striking: *fī kitābin* — "in a book." No particular book is named.

**نبرأها** — root **ب-ر-أ**: to create, but with a particular sense. *Baraʾa* is to bring a thing out of non-existence and make it **separate and distinct.** From the same root come *al-Bāriʾ* (the Maker — one of God's names), *barāʾa* (being clear of a thing), *barīʾ* (clean, free of).

The concrete sense recorded by the lexicographers: **to slip out of something, to separate from it.** *Bariʾa mina'l-maraḍ* — he recovered from the illness.

That is, *barʾ* emphasises the "separating" side of creation: to set a thing apart from the others and give it its own existence.

**The question of the الضمير (pronoun):** *nabraʾahā* — "before We bring it into being." To what does this *hā* refer? There are two possibilities:

| To what the pronoun refers | Sense |
|---|---|
| **To the affliction** | It was written before the affliction was brought into being |
| **To the self or to the earth** | It was written before the human being / the earth was brought into being |

Both readings are found in the sources. The first has the nearer antecedent (the affliction has just been mentioned); the second widens the scope.

The difference of sense is not great; on either the record precedes the event.

### Not entering the theological dispute

This verse is one of the central texts of one of the longest debates in Islamic thought — the question of decree and human will.

**This commentary takes no side in that debate.** The position adopted at al-Baqara 2/7 (the verse of sealing) holds here too, and its ground is this: the debate is theological, it exceeds the limit of exegesis, and none of the parties has been able to draw a decisive conclusion from the wording of the text alone.

What must be recorded is the following:

**One.** The verse speaks of **the priority of knowledge.** "Being written" and "being compelled" are not the same thing. This distinction is accepted by all parties to the debate; where they differ is in how knowledge determines the act.

**Two.** The verse is not discussing human responsibility. Its subject is **afflictions** — what befalls a person; not what a person does. This is an important detail that narrows the scope of the verse and must not be missed.

**Three.** And most importantly: **the verse does not give this knowledge as a theory.** Verse 23 says what the knowledge is for. The purpose the verse itself indicates is not metaphysical but **practical.**

### لِّكَيْلَا — "so that you may not…"

This conjunction puts the two verses into a relation of ground. And this is the most striking side of the verse.

What is given as the purpose of the knowledge of the decree is:
- Not an **article of belief**
- Not a **formula of consolation**
- And certainly not an **exemption from responsibility**

What is given is **the balancing of two extreme feelings.**

### Two extremes: أَسَى and فَرَح

**تَأْسَوْا۟** — root **أ-س-و/أ-س-ي**: to grieve, to sorrow. *Asā* is a deep and continuing grief.

The root has an interesting relative: **أسو (*asā*)** also means **the treating of a wound**; an *āsī* is a physician, *isāʾ* a medicine. The lexicographers connect the two senses thus: grief is like a wound that needs treating. This connection is the lexicographers' interpretation and is not certain.

**تَفْرَحُوا۟** — root **ف-ر-ح**: to rejoice. But in the Qurʾān this root is mostly used with an **unfavourable** tone: to rejoice and become insolent, to be carried away.

The Qurʾān itself shows this: *"Do not exult; God does not love those who exult"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/76) — the words said to Qārūn. And: *"They rejoiced in what they had"* (al-Raʿd 13/26 and elsewhere).

**That is, what is forbidden in the verse is not rejoicing itself.** Elsewhere the Qurʾān commands rejoicing: *"Say: in God's bounty and in His mercy — in that let them rejoice"* (Yūnus 10/58), and there the same root is used.

The difference lies in **what one rejoices at.** The subject of the prohibition of joy in the verse is *bimā ātākum* — "what He has given you"; that is, property, position, gain.

### Symmetry

The two verbs set up a faultless symmetry:

| | **Grief** | **Joy** |
|---|---|---|
| Its object | *mā fātakum* — what escapes you | *mā ātākum* — what comes to you |
| Its direction | What goes | What comes |
| The verb | *taʾsaw* | *tafraḥū* |

**فَاتَكُمْ** — root **ف-و-ت**: to let slip, to escape one's hand, for a gap to be left between. From the same root come *fawt* (missing) and *tafāwut* (a difference between things).

**ءَاتَىٰكُمْ** — root **أ-ت-ي**: to come; in Form IV *ātā* — he gave, he brought.

**The sound-resemblance of the two verbs is striking:** *fātakum* and *ātākum*. On the same measure, with similar sounds. This is a technique the Qurʾān often uses: to give two opposite concepts in similar sounds so that the symmetry between them becomes audible.

### The measure itself

Now let us look at what the verse says.

**The verse bounds two extreme feelings at once.** And this is striking, because usually only one is aimed at.

A consoling text tries to lighten the pain of loss — it bounds grief. A warning text unsettles the confidence gain gives — it bounds joy. **The verse does both at once.**

The reason is that the two arise from **the same assumption**:

**The assumption behind excessive grief:** what I lost was mine.
**The assumption behind excessive joy:** what I gained is my own work.

Both assumptions rest on **ownership.** And the sūra has been dismantling exactly this assumption since the seventh verse: *mustakhlafīn* — that in which you have been made successors.

That is, verses 22-23 are the counterpart of verse 7 in the sphere of feeling. There ownership was taken up economically; here psychologically.

**And the sentence at al-Baqara 2/156 confirms this link:** *innā lillāhi wa innā ilayhi rājiʿūn.* It was recorded there: the first half of this sentence is **a declaration of ownership** — "what was lost was never the person's own."

The same idea is applied in al-Ḥadīd in both directions: neither what was lost nor what was gained was yours.

### Does the verse deny pain?

At al-Baqara 2/155-157 a note was entered, and it needs repeating here:

> The two sentences together do not deny pain. The verse does not say "do not grieve"; grieving is indeed not a forbidden thing.

The same note holds for al-Ḥadīd 57/23, and the text supports it itself.

**The Qurʾān records the grief of the prophets** and does not blame it: Jacob's grief for Joseph (Yūsuf 12/84), and the words said to the Prophet, *"do not grieve over them"* (al-Naḥl 16/127) — which shows that the grief was there.

**What is forbidden is not the feeling itself but its measure.** Look at the words: *asā* is not ordinary grief but **deep and continuing** sorrow. *Faraḥ* is not ordinary joy but **insolent exultation.** Both words name extremes.

And the continuation of the verse confirms this: *"God does not love any conceited boaster."* Joy is criticised at the point where it **turns into pride.**

### وَٱللَّهُ لَا يُحِبُّ كُلَّ مُخْتَالٍ فَخُورٍ

**مختال** — root **خ-ي-ل.** And the relatives of this root explain the word just as it stands:

- **خيال (*khayāl*)** — imagination, what is pictured in the mind
- **تخيّل (*takhayyul*)** — to imagine
- **خيلاء (*khuyalāʾ*)** — pride, self-importance
- **مختال (*mukhtāl*)** — conceited, one who walks with a swagger
- **خيل (*khayl*)** — horse

**The most instructive side of the word is that "pride" and "imagination" come from the same root.**

A *mukhtāl*, in the logic of the word itself, is a person who **holds an imagining about himself.** Pride is named here not as a behaviour but as a **false conception**: the person sees himself as other than he is.

This fits the context of the verse exactly. For the first half of verse 23 was setting up a **true conception**: what you lost was not yours, and neither is what you gained. A *mukhtāl* is the reverse of that conception.

And the connection with the **horse** has also been noted: the lexicographers connect the word *khayl* (horse) with this root and give as their ground the swagger in a horse's gait. This connection is the lexicographers' interpretation and is not certain; but it is interesting, because the commonest Arabic image of pride is **the walk.**

The Qurʾān uses this plainly: *"Do not walk on the earth with insolence"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/37, Luqmān 31/18).

**فخور** — root **ف-خ-ر**. It was taken up above (57/20) in the context of *tafākhur*. *Fakhūr* is in an intensive pattern: one who boasts much.

**The difference between the two words** is recorded in the classical sources thus:

| | *Mukhtāl* | *Fakhūr* |
|---|---|---|
| Where it shows | In the walk, in the bearing | In speech |
| Its direction | Turned to oneself (seeing oneself as great) | Turned to another (a claim of superiority) |

That is, one is *ḥāl* (bearing) and the other *qāl* (speech). Silent pride and spoken pride.

This distinction is found in the sources but there is no firm agreement; I record it as a tendency.

The word **كل** here too conveys *istighrāq* (universality): **"whoever is like this."** The measure recorded in the section on al-Humaza holds exactly: the verse aims not at a person but at an attribute.

### What the two verses say to the present

**One: the function of knowledge.**

The structure of the verse shows a method: a piece of metaphysical knowledge is given (22), then **what that knowledge is for** is said (23). The knowledge is given not for its own sake but for its consequence.

This is the point most discussions of decree miss. The verse answers not the question "what is decree?" but the question **"what does this knowledge do to a person?"**

**Two: the present-day forms of the two extremes.**

Grieving excessively over loss and rejoicing excessively over gain look like two independent states. The verse ties the two together with a single ground, and this has an observable counterpart: **the same person lives both.** The one who rejoices most when he gains is the one most shattered when he loses. Because the source of both is the same — the assumption that the outcome belongs to oneself.

**Three: that balance is not insensibility.**

What the verse asks for is not a state of indifference. This is clear from the words of the text: what is forbidden is *asā* and *faraḥ*, and both are words of extremity. And the sūra itself gives a **glad tiding** in verse 12 and speaks of *riḍwān* in verse 20.

What is sought is not the absence of feelings but **a right knowledge of whose the feelings' object is.**

## 57/24 — ٱلَّذِينَ يَبْخَلُونَ وَيَأْمُرُونَ ٱلنَّاسَ بِٱلْبُخْلِ ۗ وَمَن يَتَوَلَّ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ هُوَ ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ

*Alladhīna yabkhalūna wa yaʾmurūna'n-nāsa bi'l-bukhl; wa man yatawalla fa-inna'llāha huwa'l-ghaniyyu'l-ḥamīd*
"Those who are miserly and enjoin miserliness upon people. And whoever turns away — God is the Rich, the Praiseworthy."

### بخل — miserliness

**Root: ب-خ-ل**: to hold back, not to give. *Bukhl* is not giving where giving is due — the lexicographers enter this note: not every withholding is miserliness; **withholding where there is a right** is miserliness.

### وَيَأْمُرُونَ ٱلنَّاسَ بِٱلْبُخْلِ — "and enjoin miserliness upon people"

This is the striking side of the verse, and it describes a step further.

**Miserliness is described in two stages:**
1. He himself does not give
2. He wants others not to give either

Why is the second necessary? Because a behaviour does not like to be left alone.

We may explain it thus, and this is my own assessment: **the existence of people who give unsettles the position of one who does not.** If no one gives, not giving is normal; if others give, not giving becomes a choice, and a choice has to be defended. The easiest defence is to make the choice general.

For this reason the verse adds the second stage. Miserliness does not stay by itself; **it tries to produce a norm.**

The Qurʾān uses this twofold structure elsewhere as well: *"They are those who are miserly and enjoin miserliness upon people and conceal what God has given them of His bounty"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/37). There a third stage is added: **concealing what one has.**

### وَمَن يَتَوَلَّ — "whoever turns away"

**تولّى** — root **و-ل-ي**. It was taken up in verse 15 in the word *mawlā*: the sense of the root is **nearness.**

*Tawallā*, however, interestingly carries the opposite sense: **to turn one's back, to turn away.** The lexicographers explain this thus: *walā* can also convey turning one's back on someone and going off — leaving a relation of nearness.

That is, the same root conveys both "to draw near" and "to draw away". This is not rare in Arabic.

### ٱلْغَنِىُّ ٱلْحَمِيدُ — *ghanī* and *ḥamīd*

**غني** — root **غ-ن-ي**: to be without need, to be sufficient. *Ghanī* means, before "rich", **the one who has no need.** From the same root come *ighnāʾ* (to enrich) and *istighnāʾ* (to have no need).

**حميد** — root **ح-م-د**: to praise. Fully treated in the section on al-Fātiḥa. *Ḥamīd* is an intensive pattern in the sense of "praised" (*maḥmūd*).

**That these two names should stand in this verse sets the sūra's whole demand for spending in its place.**

For in verse 11 God had **asked for a loan.** And it was recorded there: the boldness of the expression lies in placing the absolutely rich in the position of one asking for a loan.

Now, thirteen verses later, the note on that boldness is entered: **الْغَنِيُّ** — the one who has no need.

That is, the sūra qualifies its own expression itself. The one who asked for a loan did not ask because He was in need.

**And the practical consequence of this is:** spending is not a contribution to God. The one who does not give produces no deficiency; **he fails to take his own share.**

The Qurʾān says this plainly elsewhere: *"Here you are, called to spend in the way of God. And some of you are miserly. Whoever is miserly is miserly only against himself. God is the Rich, and you are the poor."* (Muḥammad 47/38)

The addition of the name **حميد** is meaningful as well. Had *ghanī* been said alone, it would have set up a distance: "He has no need, therefore no interest either." *Ḥamīd* balances this — the one without need is at the same time worthy of praise; that is, the relation continues.

## 57/25 — لَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا رُسُلَنَا بِٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ وَأَنزَلْنَا مَعَهُمُ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ وَٱلْمِيزَانَ لِيَقُومَ ٱلنَّاسُ بِٱلْقِسْطِ ۖ وَأَنزَلْنَا ٱلْحَدِيدَ فِيهِ بَأْسٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَنَٰفِعُ لِلنَّاسِ وَلِيَعْلَمَ ٱللَّهُ مَن يَنصُرُهُۥ وَرُسُلَهُۥ بِٱلْغَيْبِ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَوِىٌّ عَزِيزٌ

*La-qad arsalnā rusulanā bi'l-bayyināti wa anzalnā maʿahumu'l-kitāba wa'l-mīzāna li-yaqūma'n-nāsu bi'l-qisṭ; wa anzalna'l-ḥadīda fīhi baʾsun shadīdun wa manāfiʿu li'n-nāsi wa li-yaʿlama'llāhu man yanṣuruhu wa rusulahu bi'l-ghayb; inna'llāha qawiyyun ʿazīz*
"Assuredly We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and We sent down with them the Book and the balance, that people might uphold justice. And We sent down iron, in which there is great force and benefits for people — and so that God might mark out those who help Him and His messengers unseen. God is indeed strong, mighty."

The verse that gives the sūra its name and its densest place. In a single sentence the sending down of three things is told, a purpose is set, and a condition of testing is added.

### Three things: the Book, the *mīzān*, iron

The structure of the verse is this:

| What is sent down | What it serves | Its domain |
|---|---|---|
| **الكتاب** (the Book) | The **knowledge** of the measure | Knowing |
| **الميزان** (the *mīzān*) | The **instrument** of the measure | Measuring |
| **الحديد** (iron) | The **protection** of the measure | Enforcing |

And the purpose is one: **لِيَقُومَ ٱلنَّاسُ بِٱلْقِسْطِ** — "that people might uphold justice."

**This triad is the most original idea of the sūra** and needs attention. For the verse builds justice on three separate legs and ties all three to the same source.

Let us try leaving one out:

- **The Book without the *mīzān*:** the principle is there, the measure is not. Everyone's justice is in his own head.
- **The Book and the *mīzān* without iron:** the measure is there, the guardian is not. There is nothing to stop the one who does not keep to the measure.
- **Iron without the Book:** the force is there, the measure is not. What the strong says is right.

The verse closes all three gaps. And the order is meaningful: **iron comes last.** First knowledge, then measure, then force.

### أَرْسَلْنَا رُسُلَنَا بِٱلْبَيِّنَٰت

**بيّنة** — fully analysed in the section on al-Bayyina: the root **ب-ي-ن**, to separate two things; a *bayyina* means, before "clear", **what distinguishes.**

And it was recorded there: in Sūrat al-Bayyina the *bayyina* was not a book, not a text, not a miracle; **it was a human being reciting.**

Here *bayyināt* is plural and is named together with the messengers. Two separate things are counted: the messengers are sent *with clear proofs*, and *together with them* the Book and the balance are sent down.

### وَأَنزَلْنَا مَعَهُمُ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ وَٱلْمِيزَانَ

**مِيزَان** — root **و-ز-ن**: to weigh. *Mīzān* is a noun of instrument on the pattern *mifʿāl*: **an instrument for weighing, a balance.** From the same root come *wazn* (weight, a poetic metre), *mawzūn* (measured), *tawāzun* (equilibrium).

**Its being a noun of instrument is important.** In Arabic the *mifʿāl* pattern conveys an instrument: *miftāḥ* (a key), *miqrāḍ* (shears — met with above), *miṣbāḥ* (a lamp). *Mīzān* is like these: the **thing** by which weighing is done.

That is, the verse speaks not of an abstract "principle of justice" but of a concrete **instrument.**

**There is disagreement over what the word means:**

| View | What the *mīzān* is |
|---|---|
| **A balance (concrete)** | The weighing instrument used in trade; it was taught to human beings |
| **Justice (abstract)** | The measure that separates truth from falsehood; the measure of judgement |
| **Sharīʿa / law** | The whole body of measures by which people are to be judged |
| **Reason** | The faculty of distinguishing right from wrong |

All of these views are found in the classical sources. I impose no preference.

But this must be recorded: **the concrete sense of the word can carry all the abstract ones.** The balance is the oldest and most widespread symbol of justice — because what a balance does is exactly what justice does: to compare two things and show which weighs heavier, and to do this **independently of the parties' opinion.**

That is the property of a balance: **the arbiter is not one of the parties.** Both sides look at the same instrument and cannot dispute the result.

### The link with al-Muṭaffifīn

In the section on al-Muṭaffifīn the question of short measure was fully treated. What was recorded there is directly useful here:

- **Cheating with the balance is called *ṭughyān* (overstepping)** — the word the Qurʾān uses of Pharaoh, of insolence
- **Measure is set down as part of the order of the universe** — in the same sentence as the raising of the sky
- **In seventh-century Arabia there was no standard system of measures and no institution to oversee them.** Every trader had his own vessel, his own balance, his own weights
- **What is aimed at is not an error of measure but deliberate shortfall** — the note at the end of al-Anʿām 6/152, *"We charge no soul beyond its capacity"*, shows this

And this finding was made there: *"a rusted balance cannot show its own deviation."*

**Al-Ḥadīd 57/25 gives the solution to the problem al-Muṭaffifīn describes.** Al-Muṭaffifīn tells of short measure; al-Ḥadīd says where the measure comes from.

And the connection is that the measure **has been given.** If the balance were an instrument people agreed upon among themselves, the strong party who broke the agreement would always come out right. By counting the *mīzān* among the things sent down, the verse puts the measure outside the parties.

### لِيَقُومَ ٱلنَّاسُ بِٱلْقِسْطِ

**قسط** — root **ق-س-ط**. An interesting root: it carries two opposite senses.

- **قِسْط (*qisṭ*)** — justice, giving what is due, a share
- **قاسط (*qāsiṭ*)** — one who wrongs, who deviates
- **أقسط (*aqsaṭa*)** — he did justice (Form IV)
- **مقسط (*muqsiṭ*)** — just

That is, *qasaṭa* is to wrong and *aqsaṭa* is to do justice. The same root reverses when the form changes. This is a function called in Arabic the form of **salb** (removal): the *ifʿāl* form sometimes conveys the removal of the sense in the root — *aqsaṭa* means "he removed the deviation".

The Qurʾān uses both senses: *"As for us, some of us are those who submit and some are those who deviate (*qāsiṭūn*)"* (al-Jinn 72/14).

**The concrete sense of the word: a share.** A *qisṭ* is the portion falling to one from something divided. The Turkish word *taksit* (an instalment) in use today comes from this root — a divided payment.

That is, *qisṭ* describes justice by way of **apportionment**: the giving to each of what falls to him.

**يَقُومَ** — root **ق-و-م**: to rise to one's feet, to stand up, to stand. From the same root come *qiyām*, *qawm* (a people), *maqām*, *iqāma*, *mustaqīm* (straight), *qayyim*.

**The choice of verb is meaningful.** It does not say "that people might be just"; it says **"that they might uphold justice."** The construction *qiyām bi-* in Arabic means "to take a thing upon oneself, to carry it on, to hold it up."

That is, justice is described not as a **state** but as a **work.** Not a quality possessed but a structure that must be continually held up. Left alone, it falls.

**And this explains why iron is needed.** Something that must be held up must have a force to hold it up.

**That the subject is "people"** should also be noted: *li-yaqūma'n-nās.* The one who is to uphold justice is not God, nor the messengers — **people.** The Book and the balance are sent down; the work of holding up is left to the human being.

### وَأَنزَلْنَا ٱلْحَدِيدَ — "We sent down iron"

The most debated expression of the verse, and here care is needed.

**حديد** — root **ح-د-د**. And the sense of the root comes before the name of the metal:

- **حدّ (*ḥadd*)** — a boundary, an edge, a point; and the sharp edge of a blade
- **حديد (*ḥadīd*)** — sharp; and iron
- **حادّ (*ḥādd*)** — sharp, pointed
- **حدّاد (*ḥaddād*)** — a blacksmith; and a doorkeeper (one who holds a boundary)
- **حدود (*ḥudūd*)** — boundaries; and, in law, the fixed penalties
- **إحداد** — to sharpen

**That is, iron takes its name from its sharpness.** And the same root means **boundary.**

This is strikingly consistent with the context of the verse. The verse speaks of **measure** (*mīzān*) and of **justice** (*qisṭ*); and the name of the thing it sends down carries **boundary** in its root.

The Qurʾān uses the root *ḥadd* in the sense of boundary: *"These are God's boundaries, do not approach them"* (al-Baqara 2/187), *"…those who transgress God's boundaries"* (al-Baqara 2/229).

I record this connection not as a claim about meaning but as a resonance the word carries.

### The question of إنزال: did iron come from space?

**A very widespread claim has been built on this expression in the modern period, and it must be rejected with reasons.**

**The claim is this:** the expression "We sent down iron" reports that iron did not form on the earth but was produced in the stars and came to the earth with meteors; and this is a fact discovered by modern astrophysics.

**This reading is problematic in several respects.**

**The first problem: the verb *inzāl* is not a verb reserved in the Qurʾān for physical descent.**

The Qurʾān uses the same verb of things that do not physically fall from the sky. Examples:

- **Al-Zumar 39/6** — *"He **sent down** for you eight pairs of cattle."* Livestock did not come down from the sky. The verb is *anzala*.
- **Al-Aʿrāf 7/26** — *"Children of Adam! We have **sent down** upon you a garment to cover your nakedness, and a garment of adornment."* A garment does not come down from the sky; it is woven.
- **Al-Fatḥ 48/4** — *"It is He who **sent down** tranquillity (*sakīna*) into their hearts."* *Sakīna* is not a physical object.
- **Al-Baqara 2/57** — *"We **sent down** upon you manna and quails."* Here the same verb is used of quails as well.

What is common to these uses? **Being given, being bestowed, being placed at the human being's disposal.**

In Arabic *inzāl* conveys a transfer from a higher place to a lower; and this transfer may be physical, but it may also be **a matter of rank.** The passing of something from what is high to what is low.

**The second problem: the explanation the Qurʾān itself gives.**

The verse that settles this question is already inside the Qurʾān and was noted earlier in this commentary (57/2):

> **وَإِن مِّن شَىْءٍ إِلَّا عِندَنَا خَزَآئِنُهُۥ وَمَا نُنَزِّلُهُۥٓ إِلَّا بِقَدَرٍ مَّعْلُومٍ**
> *"There is nothing whose stores are not with Us; and We send it down only in a known measure."* (al-Ḥijr 15/21)

**This verse defines directly what "sending down" means in the Qurʾān:** the source of everything is with God, and everything is given from there **in a known measure.** That is, "to send down" is a general expression used to show the source of every existing thing.

And note: in this verse too the word **قدر** (measure) occurs — just as *mīzān* occurs in al-Ḥadīd 57/25.

**The third problem: a problem of method.**

The principle recorded in `STYLE.md` operates here exactly: **a verse is not to be forcibly dressed in a modern piece of knowledge.**

The methodological problem with this claim is: if the verb *anzalnā* shows that iron came from space, the same verb must show that the garment and the livestock came from space as well. The same verb, the same form, the same structure. For a word to be read physically in one place and figuratively in another there must be a ground from the context; here there is no such ground — on the contrary, the context of the verse does not support a physical reading.

**The fourth problem: the verse already says what it says.**

The verse does not tell where iron came from. It tells **what iron is:** *"in it there is great force and benefits for people."* The continuation of the sentence shows that the subject is not the origin of iron but its **function.**

**The fifth problem: the claim weakens itself.**

To try to prove the truth of a text by contemporary scientific findings binds the text to the lifetime of those findings. Scientific explanations change; and when they change, the meaning loaded onto the text collapses with them. This is a method that weakens the text while trying to strengthen it.

**In conclusion, the reading adopted in this commentary is:** *anzalna'l-ḥadīd* means "iron was placed at the disposal of the human being, it was bestowed." This accords with the settled use of the verb in the Qurʾān and is consistent with the context of the verse.

One further note: that iron is formed in the stars of the universe is a piece of information given by astrophysics and may well be true. **What is rejected is not that information but the claim that it is derived from the verse.**

### Why was the verb "We sent down" chosen?

Having rejected the claim, the real question remains: does the choice of verb say something?

It does, and it shows in the word order.

**The verb *anzalnā* occurs twice in the verse:**
1. *"We **sent down** with them the Book and the balance"*
2. *"And We **sent down** iron"*

**The repetition of the same verb puts the three things in the same category.** The Book is sent down, the balance is sent down, iron is sent down. All three have been given; none of the three is something the human being produced.

**And for iron this is a particularly important claim.** For iron is the thing a human being most counts as "his own work". That the Book comes from heaven, granted; that the balance is an instrument of measure, perhaps. But iron? Iron is dug from a mine, melted in fire, hammered, shaped. From beginning to end it looks like human labour.

The verse breaks this appearance: **iron too has been given.**

And this is the natural extension of the idea of *mustakhlafīn* in verse 7 of the sūra. What was said there of property is said here of force: **the force in your hand is not yours either.**

I offer this as my own reading; I do not ascribe it to any classical exegete. But it is consistent with the inner texture of the sūra: from the beginning the sūra asks "is what is in your hand yours?", and here it directs the question at the hardest object of all — force.

### فِيهِ بَأْسٌ شَدِيدٌ وَمَنَٰفِعُ لِلنَّاسِ — the two faces of iron

**بأس** — root **ب-أ-س**. Its meaning: **violence, hardness, martial force, harm.** From the same root come *baʾsāʾ* (hardship), *bāʾis* (brave), *biʾsa* (how evil!).

The word is used in the Qurʾān both for martial force and for punishment: *"God's force is more severe"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/84) — *ashaddu baʾsan.*

**منافع** — the plural of *manfaʿa*; root **ن-ف-ع**: to be of use. The Qurʾān uses this word of animals as well: *"In them there are benefits for you"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/21, al-Naḥl 16/5).

**The verse counts the two faces of iron in a single sentence:**

| | *Baʾs* | *Manāfiʿ* |
|---|---|---|
| What | Great force | Benefits |
| Its concrete counterpart | Sword, spear, armour | Plough, nail, axe, cauldron, knife |
| Its direction | Destructive | Constructive |

And **the order is striking: force first, then benefit.** The destructive face of iron is named first.

The reason for this may be the context of the verse: the sentence comes after the purpose *"that people might uphold justice."* That is, the real function of iron here is the protection of justice — and that work requires the *baʾs* side.

**Iron occurs elsewhere in the Qurʾān too, and always with these two faces:**

- *"We softened iron for him"* (Sabaʾ 34/10) — of David (Dāwūd). And what follows describes the making of armour: *"Make full coats of mail, and measure the links well"* (Sabaʾ 34/11). Note: there too there is a command of **measure.**
- *"We taught him the craft of making armour for you, to protect you from your violence"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/80) — again David.
- *"Bring me blocks of iron"* (al-Kahf 18/96) — the barrier of Dhū'l-Qarnayn. There iron is used in the making of a **barrier.**

**In all three examples iron is a means of defence:** armour, armour, a barrier. Not a weapon of attack.

This may be a pattern; but since the sample is small I do not present it as a fixed rule.

### The relation of justice to force

Now we come to the central idea of the verse.

**Having tied the Book and the balance to the purpose of justice, the verse adds iron.** And the structure of the sentence ties iron to the same purpose — for the clause *wa anzalna'l-ḥadīd* comes immediately after the purpose *li-yaqūma'n-nāsu bi'l-qisṭ*.

What is said is this: **justice does not stand on knowledge alone.**

This is a finding that needs attention and must be read in two directions.

**One direction: the insufficiency of knowledge.**

In a society everyone may know what is just and justice may still not come about. Because knowledge does not stop the one who does not comply. Knowing the measure does not prevent short measure — the traders al-Muṭaffifīn describes knew perfectly well what the measure was.

**For justice to be applied a sanction is needed.** And the last resort of sanction is force.

**The other direction: the insufficiency of force.**

But the verse does not send down iron on its own. It sends it down **after** the Book and the balance. That is, force is bound to the measure.

And this binding is necessary, because **force by itself produces no direction.** Iron protects justice and it protects oppression. The same metal is both armour and sword. What determines what it will do is the measure of the one who holds it.

That the Qurʾān gives these two directions in a single sentence establishes the balance. **Force without measure is oppression; measure without force is ineffective.**

### And what the word order says

Let us look at the sequence once more: **Book → balance → iron.**

This order could have been reversed. But it has not been, and the order itself sets up a hierarchy:

**Iron is last.** That is, force is at the bottom of the list — not the first resort but the last.

**And iron is not a purpose in itself.** A purpose clause was given for the Book and the balance (*li-yaqūma'n-nāsu bi'l-qisṭ*); iron has been added on to that purpose.

This recalls the pair of names in the first verse of the sūra: **al-ʿazīzu'l-ḥakīm** — power and wisdom. It was recorded there: when power is named alone the possibility of arbitrariness arises; when wisdom is named alone the possibility of ineffectiveness arises.

**Twenty-five verses later the same balance is set up again through a concrete substance.** And the verse will close with another form of the same pair of names: *qawiyyun ʿazīz.*

### وَلِيَعْلَمَ ٱللَّهُ مَن يَنصُرُهُۥ وَرُسُلَهُۥ بِٱلْغَيْبِ

"And so that God might mark out those who help Him and His messengers unseen."

**The *wāw* at the head of the clause** joins it to the preceding: a second purpose is added. That is, the sending down has two purposes — the upholding of justice and this marking out.

**لِيَعْلَمَ ٱللَّهُ** — "so that God may know." This expression occurs in several places in the Qurʾān and raises a question: does God not already know?

The answer given in the classical commentaries is this, and it is widely accepted: **the "knowing" here means the coming out of the knowledge, its taking place in fact.** That is, "the occurrence of what is known" — what is known potentially becoming actually visible.

Another explanation is that the expression means "so that God's messengers and the believers may know."

Both explanations are found in the sources. I impose no preference. But this must be recorded: **the function of the verse is not to set up a theological proposition but to report the existence of a test.**

### بِٱلْغَيْبِ — "unseen"

**غيب** — root **غ-ي-ب**: to disappear from sight, to become invisible. The *ghayb* is the domain that cannot be reached by the senses.

The particle **بِ** here conveys a circumstantial sense: "while in the state of the unseen", that is, **without seeing.**

**This note is directly connected with verse 10 of the sūra**, and the connection is striking.

It was recorded at verse 10: the degree of those who spent before the conquest is greater, because they did it **without knowing the outcome.** What is done at the moment when the risk has lessened falls in value.

Here the same measure comes with a different word: **bi'l-ghayb.** To help without seeing.

**That the two verses say the same thing is plain:** the value lies in the act performed at the moment of uncertainty. And the sūra says this twice, in two different contexts — once with a historical threshold (the conquest), once with an epistemic qualifier (the unseen).

**And this note takes on a further meaning when named together with iron.** Iron is a force, and force produces visible results. By setting the condition of "helping unseen" immediately after the sending down of force, the verse says that force is not by itself a measure: **to be on the winning side is not to be on the right side.**

In the section on al-Naṣr, the command to glorify at the moment of victory was explained for exactly this reason: *"victory is a moment at which it becomes easy to think wrongly about God. The winning side is inclined to turn its own winning into a certificate of divine approval."*

Al-Ḥadīd 57/25 enters the same risk right beside the sending down of iron.

### إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَوِىٌّ عَزِيزٌ

**قوي** — root **ق-و-ي**: to be strong. *Quwwa* is actual power — the capacity to do.

**عزيز** — taken up in the first verse of the sūra: root **ع-ز-ز**, to be hard, unconquerable, rare.

**The difference between the two names** is recorded thus: *qawī* is the power itself; *ʿazīz* is the **unconquerableness** of that power. One is capacity, the other position.

**That the verse should close with these two names is meaningful and enters a note.**

The verse said that iron was sent down. Iron is force, and the human being who takes force into his hand easily supposes he is the source of the force. The closing sentence cuts this off: **the real possessor of power is He.**

That is, iron is a force that has been given — a loan. And what is taken on loan is not used to stand against its true owner.

This forms a link with the first verse of the sūra: there it was *al-ʿazīzu'l-ḥakīm*, here *qawiyyun ʿazīz.* The name **ʿAzīz** is in both; the opening of the sūra and its central verse are joined by the same name.

### What this says to the present

**One: where the triad falls short.**

The triad the verse sets up — knowledge, measure, force — offers a frame, and the cases in which the frame falls short can be recognised:

- **Knowledge and measure without force:** there is law, and it is not applied. Judgements are given and not carried out.
- **Force without measure:** there is enforcement, and the measure is in dispute. The one who holds the force determines the measure as well.
- **The measure in the hands of one of the parties:** the arbiter is one of the parties. The balance is in the hand of the one weighing.

The third is the most insidious and is the situation al-Muṭaffifīn describes: there is a balance, but it is the trader's own.

**Two: the meaning of the measure coming from outside.**

That the verse counts the Book and the balance among "the things sent down" says that the measure is not produced by the parties. Its practical counterpart is: **a measure is a measure only to the extent that the parties cannot change it.**

This is a principle that holds even without a religious claim. For any system of measurement to work, the measure must be independent of what is measured. The man who makes his own ruler measures whatever length he likes.

**Three: the binding of force to measure.**

The word order of the verse — Book, balance, then iron — sets an order, and what happens when the order is reversed has been seen many times in history. If force comes first, it produces the measure itself.

This finding is not the taking of a political side; the word order of the verse says it itself, and examples of it are found on every side throughout history.

**Four: the present-day counterpart of iron.**

The verse speaks of a metal, but what it describes is a **capacity for force.** Iron's place has today been taken by far more varied instruments. The frame the verse draws — the double face (*baʾs* and *manāfiʿ*), the binding to measure, and the having-been-given — can be read for all of them.

I record this not as a ruling but as the generality of the frame the verse sets up.

## 57/26 — وَلَقَدْ أَرْسَلْنَا نُوحًا وَإِبْرَٰهِيمَ وَجَعَلْنَا فِى ذُرِّيَّتِهِمَا ٱلنُّبُوَّةَ وَٱلْكِتَٰبَ ۖ فَمِنْهُم مُّهْتَدٍ ۖ وَكَثِيرٌ مِّنْهُمْ فَٰسِقُونَ

*Wa la-qad arsalnā Nūḥan wa Ibrāhīma wa jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihima'n-nubuwwata wa'l-kitāb; fa-minhum muhtad, wa kathīrun minhum fāsiqūn*
"Assuredly We sent Noah (Nūḥ) and Abraham (Ibrāhīm), and We placed prophethood and the Book in the offspring of the two. Among them there is one rightly guided; but many of them are transgressors."

### Two names, two beginnings

The preceding verse named the messengers in general; this verse gives two names.

**That Noah and Abraham should be chosen is not random.** In the Qurʾān's narrative both are points of **beginning**:

- **Noah** — he is named as the forefather of humanity after the flood. The Qurʾān records his offspring separately: *"And We made his offspring the ones who remained"* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/77).
- **Abraham** — he is the second beginning of the chain of prophethood; two branches open out through Isaac (Isḥāq) and Ishmael (Ismāʿīl).

That is, the verse sums up the history of prophethood in two great points of juncture.

### فِى ذُرِّيَّتِهِمَا — "in the offspring of the two"

**ذرّيّة** — the lexicographers differ over the root: **ذ-ر-ر** (to scatter, to strew), **ذ-ر-و** (to winnow) or **ذ-ر-أ** (to create and multiply). All three carry the idea of "spreading, multiplying".

**The tying of prophethood to lineage** needs attention, because it is open to misunderstanding.

The verse does not say that prophethood **passes by inheritance.** What it says is that the institution of prophethood was continued within these two lines — a historical finding, not a declaration of entitlement.

And the second half of the verse already cuts this off: **"among them there is one rightly guided; many of them are transgressors."** That is, lineage is not a guarantee of salvation.

This is the same measure as the one set up at al-Baqara 2/124: *"My covenant does not reach the wrongdoers."* That is the answer given when the question was asked about Abraham's line.

### فَمِنْهُم مُّهْتَدٍ ۖ وَكَثِيرٌ مِّنْهُمْ فَٰسِقُونَ

**The word order itself sets up a balance** and must be read with care.

| | Word | Marking of number |
|---|---|---|
| The one rightly guided | *muhtad* | **Singular and indefinite** — "some of them" |
| The one who transgresses | *fāsiqūn* | **Plural** — and with the qualifier *kathīr* (many) |

**مُّهْتَدٍ** comes singular, **فَٰسِقُونَ** plural. This implies a numerical disproportion.

But **both sides are there.** And this is the most important note of the verse: the verdict is not wholesale.

The word **كثير** is critical here. It does not say "all", it says **"many".** The same note was there in verse 16 and will come once more in verse 27.

**The sūra repeats this note three times** (16, 26, 27), and this repetition is the in-text counterpart of the principle set down in `STYLE.md`: **no wholesale verdict is passed on a religious group; what the verse describes are attributes.**

The text does this itself — three times, with the same word.

## 57/27 — ثُمَّ قَفَّيْنَا عَلَىٰٓ ءَاثَٰرِهِم بِرُسُلِنَا وَقَفَّيْنَا بِعِيسَى ٱبْنِ مَرْيَمَ وَءَاتَيْنَٰهُ ٱلْإِنجِيلَ وَجَعَلْنَا فِى قُلُوبِ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱتَّبَعُوهُ رَأْفَةً وَرَحْمَةً وَرَهْبَانِيَّةً ٱبْتَدَعُوهَا مَا كَتَبْنَٰهَا عَلَيْهِمْ إِلَّا ٱبْتِغَآءَ رِضْوَٰنِ ٱللَّهِ فَمَا رَعَوْهَا حَقَّ رِعَايَتِهَا ۖ فَـَٔاتَيْنَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ مِنْهُمْ أَجْرَهُمْ ۖ وَكَثِيرٌ مِّنْهُمْ فَٰسِقُونَ

*Thumma qaffaynā ʿalā āthārihim bi-rusulinā wa qaffaynā bi-ʿĪsa'bni Maryama wa ātaynāhu'l-Injīla wa jaʿalnā fī qulūbi'lladhīna'ttabaʿūhu raʾfatan wa raḥmah; wa rahbāniyyatani'btadaʿūhā mā katabnāhā ʿalayhim illa'btighāʾa riḍwāni'llāhi fa-mā raʿawhā ḥaqqa riʿāyatihā; fa-ātayna'lladhīna āmanū minhum ajrahum; wa kathīrun minhum fāsiqūn*
"Then We sent Our messengers one after another upon their traces, and We sent Jesus (ʿĪsā) son of Mary after them and gave him the Injīl. And We placed tenderness and mercy in the hearts of those who followed him. As for monasticism: they invented it themselves; We did not prescribe it for them — only the seeking of God's good pleasure. Yet they did not observe it as it should have been observed. So We gave those among them who believed their reward; but many of them are transgressors."

The verse of the sūra that must be read with the greatest care. It is disputed grammatically, and its subject is delicate.

### قَفَّيْنَا — "We sent them one after another"

**Root: ق-ف-و**: to go behind someone, to follow his trace. Its concrete sense is related to the word **qafā** (the nape): the *qafā* is the back of the neck — that is, a person's back.

From the same root comes *qāfiya* (rhyme, the following of one line-end upon another). And *iqtifāʾ* is to follow someone's trace.

**The verb is in Form II:** *qaffā* — to send one after another, to range them in succession.

**عَلَىٰٓ ءَاثَٰرِهِم** — "upon their traces." An *athar* is a footprint. That is, the messengers go along the trace left by those before them — they do not open a new road.

This is another expression of the idea recorded at al-Baqara 2/4: the text presents itself not as a new religion but as the continuation of a chain that was interrupted.

### رَأْفَةً وَرَحْمَةً — tenderness and mercy

Both words occurred in verse 9 as *raʾūf* and *raḥīm*. It was recorded there: most lexicographers count *raʾfa* finer and more intense than *raḥma*.

**That these two qualities should be placed in the hearts of those who followed Jesus is the Qurʾān's own statement and is a favourable finding.** The verse names it not as a fault but as something given: *jaʿalnā* — "We placed."

The Qurʾān makes this finding elsewhere as well:

*"And you will find the nearest of them in affection to the believers those who say 'we are Christians'. That is because among them there are priests and monks, and because they are not arrogant."* (al-Māʾida 5/82)

**This verse is important for how al-Ḥadīd 57/27 is read** and needs mentioning here. For the same Qurʾān names the word *ruhbān* in a favourable context in one place.

That is, the matter is not a wholesale assessment of a religious group.

### رَهْبَانِيَّة — monasticism

**Root: ر-ه-ب**: to fear, to be in awe. From the same root come *rahba* (fear), *irhāb* (to terrify), *rāhib* (one who fears; and a monk).

The word **راهب (*rāhib*)** means "one who fears God, who holds back", and it was given as a name to the person living the monastic life.

**رهبانيّة (*rahbāniyya*)** is the name of that way of life: withdrawal from the world, not marrying, holding no property, retiring into solitude.

### ٱبْتَدَعُوهَا — "they invented it"

**Root: ب-د-ع.** The word *badīʿ* was treated at al-Baqara 2/117, and it was recorded there: **that which has no precedent, what is done for the first time.** And it was noted that from the same root come *bidʿa* (something brought in later) and *ibdāʿ* (original production).

I do not repeat it here. What must be recorded is that the root is **neutral**: *badʿ* is "to bring forth anew". The value judgement comes from the context. Used of God it is praise (*badīʿu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ*); used of a human being it conveys the bringing into religion of something with no source.

**The verb is in the *iftiʿāl* form:** *ibtadaʿa* — he brought it forth for himself.

### The grammatical knot of the verse

**This verse is one of the sentences of the Qurʾān whose *iʿrāb* is most disputed.** The knot is where the word *rahbāniyyatan* attaches and what the exception *illā* excepts.

There are two main readings:

| Reading | How the sentence is construed | Its sense |
|---|---|---|
| **The first** | *Rahbāniyya* does not attach to the two preceding words (*raʾfa*, *raḥma*); it is the object of a separate sentence. Supplied: "and they instituted a monasticism which they themselves invented." | God did not lay down monasticism; they brought it forth |
| **The second** | *Rahbāniyya* attaches to the objects of *jaʿalnā*; but *ibtadaʿūhā* comes in between as a qualifier | We laid down monasticism too, but they developed its form |

**The first reading is far more widespread** and its ground is strong: the clause that comes immediately after, **مَا كَتَبْنَٰهَا عَلَيْهِمْ** — "We did not prescribe it for them" — makes the second reading difficult.

In this commentary I take the first reading as the basis; this is a preference and it is not binding.

**The second knot: إِلَّا ٱبْتِغَآءَ رِضْوَٰنِ ٱللَّهِ**

To what does this exception attach? Two readings:

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| **A disjunctive exception (*istithnāʾ munqaṭiʿ*)** | "We did not prescribe it; they did it only to seek God's good pleasure." That is, the intention was good |
| **A connected exception (*istithnāʾ muttaṣil*)** | "We prescribed it for them only that they might seek God's good pleasure" — that is, what We prescribed was the seeking of good pleasure, not monasticism |

The first reading is the more widespread and accords better with what follows in the verse.

### A twofold criticism

**The structure of the verse sets two separate criticisms one after the other.** And this is its most striking side:

**The first criticism: We did not prescribe it.** — A problem of source. This is something added to religion later.

**The second criticism: they did not observe it as it should have been observed.** — A problem of practice. They were not faithful even to what they themselves had laid down.

**That the two criticisms should come together is logically interesting.** For if the first is granted, the second looks unnecessary: since it was not an obligation, what harm in not keeping to it?

The answer is in the note in the middle of the verse: **ٱبْتِغَآءَ رِضْوَٰنِ ٱللَّهِ** — "to seek God's good pleasure."

That is, the intention is legitimate and the verse records it. And once a thing has been taken on, what it requires follows.

**This is the same as the Qurʾān's position on the vow (*nadhr*):** a vow is not an obligation; but once vowed it must be fulfilled. *"And let them fulfil their vows"* (al-Ḥajj 22/29).

So the matter is this: **an obligation taken upon oneself does not cease to be an obligation.**

### فَمَا رَعَوْهَا حَقَّ رِعَايَتِهَا

**رعى** — root **ر-ع-ي**. Its concrete sense: **to herd, to pasture animals.** From the same root come *rāʿī* (a shepherd), *raʿiyya* (the flock that is herded; and a populace), *marʿā* (pasture — it occurs at al-Aʿlā 87/4), *riʿāya*.

**The choice of the word is instructive.** It does not say "they did not keep to it"; it says **"they did not shepherd it."**

Shepherding is not a one-off task. It is a continuous, daily, wearying care. The flock keeps scattering, keeps being gathered. Left for a day it is lost.

That is, *riʿāya* differs from keeping to a rule: **it is holding up something that needs continual tending.**

And this is directly connected with verse 16 of the sūra. What was described there was the cooling that comes with duration: *fa-ṭāla ʿalayhimu'l-amadu fa-qasat qulūbuhum.*

**Here the same phenomenon is told with a different word:** something was taken on, but its continual tending was not done. And the addressee of the verse is again "those who were given the Book before" — the same group as in verse 16.

That is, the sūra makes the same diagnosis twice: **the initial state is not maintained.**

**حَقَّ رِعَايَتِهَا** — "the due of its observance." In Arabic this construction means "as it ought to be, giving it its due". The same structure occurs elsewhere in the Qurʾān: *"They did not esteem God as He should be esteemed"* (al-Anʿām 6/91), *"Fear God as He should be feared"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/102).

### A balanced reading: what the verse says and what it does not

Care is needed in this matter, and the measure `STYLE.md` sets down is especially important here.

**What the verse says:**

1. Monasticism is not an obligation laid down by revelation.
2. The intention of those who instituted it was — in the verse's own words — to seek God's good pleasure.
3. What was instituted was not given its due.
4. Those among them who believed were given their reward.
5. Many of them are transgressors.

**What the verse does not say:**

1. A wholesale verdict on a religious community. The verse says "many" three times and once says "those among them who believed".
2. That asceticism and keeping away from the world are in themselves forbidden. What is criticised is their being set up as a **religious obligation.**
3. That tenderness and mercy are a fault. On the contrary, the verse counts them among the things God placed there.

**And one further note:** the verse does not define what monasticism is. The scope of the word — not marrying, solitude, holding no property — is not specified in the text. Different descriptions are given in the classical commentaries. I do not close this indefiniteness.

### Islam's position: the middle way

The place of this verse within the whole of the sūra is meaningful and establishes a balance.

**In verse 20 the sūra described the life of this world with five words** — play, amusement, adornment, boasting, rivalry in multitude. That is a strong criticism of the world.

**And in verse 27 it criticises withdrawal from the world.**

The distance between the two verses shows the place the sūra holds: **neither burial in the world nor withdrawal from it.**

And the sūra's commands stand at that middle point: spend (7), race (21), uphold justice (25). All of these are things done in the world, within the world.

The Qurʾān sets up this balance plainly elsewhere:

*"You who believe! Do not forbid the good things God has made lawful for you, and do not transgress."* (al-Māʾida 5/87)

And: *"Seek the abode of the hereafter in what God has given you; but do not forget your share of this world."* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/77)

**These two verses are the background of al-Ḥadīd 57/27.** The Qurʾān does not set up renunciation of the world as a virtue; it sets up the world as an **instrument.**

### Another link: 57/16 and 57/27

The two verses carry the same structure, and set side by side they bring one idea of the sūra into focus:

| | **57/16** | **57/27** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Those given the Book | Those who followed Jesus |
| The beginning | The Book was given | Tenderness and mercy were placed; monasticism was taken on |
| What happened | The term grew long, the hearts hardened | It was not observed as it should have been |
| The note | *"Many of them are transgressors"* | *"Many of them are transgressors"* |
| Addressee | **Those who believe** (by way of comparison) | — |

**In verse 16 this description was used as a warning to those who believe:** "do not be like them."

In verse 27 the same description is repeated, and this time the warning is not made explicitly — but once the link within the sūra is made, the warning is already there.

And the idea common to the two verses is: **beginning a thing and keeping it up are separate works.** To be given a Book is a beginning; to take on monasticism is a beginning. In both, the problem arises afterwards.

## 57/28 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَءَامِنُوا۟ بِرَسُولِهِۦ يُؤْتِكُمْ كِفْلَيْنِ مِن رَّحْمَتِهِۦ وَيَجْعَل لَّكُمْ نُورًا تَمْشُونَ بِهِۦ وَيَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanu'ttaqu'llāha wa āminū bi-rasūlih, yuʾtikum kiflayni min raḥmatihi wa yajʿal lakum nūran tamshūna bihi wa yaghfir lakum; wa'llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm*
"You who believe! Guard yourselves against God and believe in His messenger, and He will give you a double share of His mercy, and give you a light by which you will walk, and forgive you. God is forgiving, merciful."

### Who is addressed?

The address of the verse is **يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟** — "you who believe."

And immediately afterwards they are asked **to believe**: *wa āminū bi-rasūlih.* That those who believe should be told "believe" raises a question.

**There are two readings:**

| Reading | Who is addressed | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **The People of the Book** | Those who believed in the earlier prophets | "You believed in your own Book; now believe in this messenger too" — *kiflayn* (a double share) supports this reading |
| **The Muslims** | Those who already believe | "Keep up your faith, deepen it" — there are similar usages in the Qurʾān (al-Nisāʾ 4/136) |

**The first reading is strong in terms of context**, because the verse has spoken of the People of the Book in the preceding verse and will address them directly in the next (29). Moreover the expression "a double share" stands as a natural return for a person who believes in two prophets at once.

**The second reading has also been defended**, and there are other instances in the Qurʾān of believers being told "believe".

I impose no preference; both are found in the sources.

### كِفْلَيْنِ — "a double share"

**Root: ك-ف-ل**: to stand surety, to take on; and a portion, a share of a thing.

**كفل (*kifl*)** is used in Arabic in several senses: a share, a portion; and the folded cloth placed on a camel's back behind the rider so that he does not fall.

This second concrete sense is interesting: a *kifl* is something **folded.** From this comes the sense "fold, multiple".

The Qurʾān uses the word in one other place: *"Whoever intercedes in an evil cause has a share (*kifl*) of it"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/85). There the sense is "share".

**What the "double" is double of** is not said in the verse. There are two readings: the reward of the earlier faith plus the reward of the new; or, generally, an expression of multiplicity in the sense of "many times over".

On this subject a hadith is transmitted that speaks of people who will receive a reward twice over. It is stated that the report stands in the sound hadith collections; but since I cannot give its wording and its source with certainty I give no detail and do not make it a ground of the commentary.

### نُورًا تَمْشُونَ بِهِۦ — "a light by which you will walk"

**The light-texture of the sūra is completed here.**

Let us put the five occurrences in order:

| Verse | The state of the light |
|---|---|
| **9** | **Bringing out** of darkness into light — the source |
| **12** | The light **runs** before the believers — possession |
| **13** | The light **is asked for** as a loan and not given — absence |
| **19** | The light is given as a **return** |
| **28** | The light **will be given** and **walked in** |

**And in the last occurrence there is a new element: walking.**

In the first four occurrences light was a state or a recompense. Here it is an **instrument**: *tamshūna bihi* — "you will walk by it."

This states the function of the light. Light is given not because it is beautiful but **so that one may walk.**

**And this coincides exactly with the finding recorded at al-Baqara 2/17-20.** There the common conclusion of the two similitudes was set down thus:

> Both pictures are built on **movement**: in the first the movement has stopped entirely ("they do not return"), in the second it is intermittent. … The one thing in a human being's hand is to walk when the light comes.

Al-Ḥadīd 57/28 is the positive counterpart of that sentence: **the light is given and one walks.**

In al-Baqara the man whose light was taken stood in the darkness; the man in the storm walked when the lightning flashed and stood still in the dark. Here the light is continuous and the walking unbroken.

**The order of the فِعْل (verbs) is meaningful as well:** two things are asked for **before** the light is given — *taqwā* and faith. That is, the light is not the reward of the walking; it is its **condition.** First the turning, then the light, then the walking.

### ٱتَّقُوا۟ — *taqwā*

**Root: و-ق-ي**: to protect, to guard oneself, to be on one's guard. Its concrete sense is **to set a barrier before a thing**; a *wiqāya* is a protective covering.

The full analysis of the word was made in the sections on al-Fātiḥa and al-Baqara; I do not repeat it here.

The order in the verse is striking: **first *taqwā*, then faith.** One would usually expect the reverse order.

This may be seen as an element supporting the first reading (that the addressee is the People of the Book): people who already carry a sense of responsibility are being asked to keep up that sense and to take one further step.

## 57/29 — لِّئَلَّا يَعْلَمَ أَهْلُ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ أَلَّا يَقْدِرُونَ عَلَىٰ شَىْءٍ مِّن فَضْلِ ٱللَّهِ ۙ وَأَنَّ ٱلْفَضْلَ بِيَدِ ٱللَّهِ يُؤْتِيهِ مَن يَشَآءُ ۚ وَٱللَّهُ ذُو ٱلْفَضْلِ ٱلْعَظِيمِ

*Li-allā yaʿlama ahlu'l-kitābi allā yaqdirūna ʿalā shayʾin min faḍli'llāhi wa anna'l-faḍla bi-yadi'llāhi yuʾtīhi man yashāʾ; wa'llāhu dhu'l-faḍli'l-ʿaẓīm*
"So that the People of the Book may know that they have no power over anything of God's bounty; that the bounty is in God's hand, and He gives it to whom He wills. God is the possessor of great bounty."

The closing verse of the sūra and one of the most grammatically debated sentences in the Qurʾān.

### لِّئَلَّا — the knot

The sentence begins with **لِّئَلَّا يَعْلَمَ**. Literally: **"so that they may not know."**

But what follows in the verse makes this reading impossible: "so that they may not know… that the bounty is in God's hand." This is meaningless — the Qurʾān does not want a piece of knowledge to remain unknown.

**The widespread solution is this:** the **لا** here is *zāʾida* — that is, a particle inserted for emphasis that conveys no negation. On this construal the sense is: **"so that they may know."**

This usage is recognised in Arabic and other instances of it are pointed out in the Qurʾān:

- *"What kept you from prostrating?"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/12) — literally *"allā tasjuda"*, "from your not prostrating". The *lā* there too is counted redundant.
- *"…so that people may have no argument against you"* (al-Baqara 2/150) — here, by contrast, it is a real negation.

**A second solution** is also found in the sources: the *lā* is a real negation and the sentence attaches to the preceding verse. The sense would then be: "…(this bounty was given) so that the People of the Book should not remain unable to know that they can possess nothing of God's bounty" — that is, an indirect affirmation.

**A third reading:** the *lā* carries a negation in the sense of "let them not suppose", and the sentence is understood as "so that they may not suppose the bounty is their own monopoly."

**In this commentary I take the first solution as the basis** — that the *lā* is redundant and the sense is "so that they may know". This is the reading most widely accepted in the classical sources. The preference is not binding.

### أَلَّا يَقْدِرُونَ عَلَىٰ شَىْءٍ مِّن فَضْلِ ٱللَّهِ

**يقدرون** — root **ق-د-ر**, taken up in the second verse: to measure, to apportion, to have power over.

Here the sense is: **to have power over, to dispose of.** "They have no power over even a part of the bounty" — that is, they have no authority to distribute the bounty, to restrict it, to hold it as a monopoly.

**شَىْءٍ** indefinite and in a negative sentence: **"over anything of it."**

### The function of the verse

Why does the sūra close with this sentence?

In the preceding verse (28) a promise was given: a double mercy, a light, forgiveness. And the addressee of that promise — on the first reading — were those of the People of the Book who believe.

**Such a promise naturally raises an objection:** "prophethood and bounty were ours; why has it been given to another?"

The Qurʾān records this objection elsewhere: *"Or do they envy people for what God has given them of His bounty?"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/54). And at al-Baqara 2/109: in the face of envy, pardon was commanded.

**The last verse answers this objection directly:** the bounty is no one's monopoly.

And it does so in two steps:
1. **Negative:** they have no power over the bounty.
2. **Positive:** the bounty is in God's hand; He gives it to whom He wills.

### فَضْل — the word's place in the sūra

**Root: ف-ض-ل** — taken up in verse 21: to increase, to be in excess. *Faḍl* is the surplus added on top of what is required.

**The word occurs three times in this verse** and becomes one of the last words of the sūra: *faḍli'llāh*, *al-faḍl*, *dhu'l-faḍli'l-ʿaẓīm.*

And it had occurred three times in verse 21 as well: *dhālika faḍlu'llāhi yuʾtīhi man yashāʾ; wa'llāhu dhu'l-faḍli'l-ʿaẓīm.*

**The last sentence of the two verses is word for word the same:** *"God is the possessor of great bounty."* The sūra uses this sentence twice, in verses 21 and 29.

**And both occurrences do the same work:** after a language of deserving, they prevent the deserving from becoming absolute.

- **In verse 21** the command to race was given; then, by saying "that is God's bounty", the prize of the race was taken out of the category of desert.
- **In verse 29** a promise was given; then the same sentence comes.

**Across the sūra this movement recurs three times:**

| Verse | What is set up | How it is balanced |
|---|---|---|
| **10** | A difference of degree (the earlier are superior) | *"To all God has promised the best"* |
| **21** | The command to race | *"That is God's bounty"* |
| **29** | A promise | *"The bounty is in God's hand"* |

This is the most consistent weave of the sūra: **every distinction is bounded immediately afterwards by a note.**

### بِيَدِ ٱللَّهِ — "in God's hand"

**يد** — hand. This is one of the *mutashābih* expressions in the Qurʾān (those whose meaning cannot be determined with certainty), and the position recorded in the matter of *istiwāʾ* (57/4) holds here too.

In Arabic the idiom *bi-yadi fulān* is well established in the sense of **"in his disposal, in his hand, within his authority"**, and it is used in this sense in everyday speech. The English expression "in his hands" is the same.

In this commentary the expression is read within the limit set by al-Shūrā 42/11, and the theological dispute is not entered.

### The close of the sūra

The sūra had begun with the word **مُلْك** (dominion) — more exactly, twice, in the second and fifth verses: *"To Him belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth."*

And it ends with the word **فَضْل** (bounty): *"The bounty is in God's hand."*

**The two words hold the two ends of the sūra.** And both say the same thing: what is in your hands is not yours.

The sūra set up this finding first through **property** (7, 10, 11), then through **force** (25), and at the very end through **spiritual standing** (29). Three domains, one idea.

# The sūra as a whole: a few weaves

Reading the sūra from beginning to end, several weaves become visible. I record them separately.

## One: the question "is what is in your hand yours?"

The sūra asks a single question in three separate domains:

| Domain | Verse | Key word | Answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Property** | 7, 10 | *mustakhlafīn*, *mīrāth* | You have been made a successor within it; the inheritance is God's |
| **Force** | 25 | *anzalna'l-ḥadīd* | Iron was sent down; the real possessor of power is He |
| **Standing** | 29 | *faḍl* | The bounty is in God's hand |

And all three have the same conclusion: **what is to be done with what one holds has not been left to the holder's whim.**

## Two: the function of the first six verses

For six verses the sūra gives no command. It only describes. And the content of the description is what makes the coming command possible:

- **The dominion is His** (2, 5) → what is to be given is not yours
- **Opposites are united in Him** (3) → there is no direction in which to escape
- **Wherever you are He is with you** (4) → there is no place to hide
- **He knows what is in the breasts** (6) → giving outwardly and withholding inwardly is of no use

The command in the seventh verse is the natural consequence of these four findings.

## Three: three uses of time

In three places the sūra makes time a measure, and the three complete one another:

| Verse | What time determines |
|---|---|
| **10** | The **value** of the act — before the conquest or after |
| **16** | The **state** of the heart — the term grew long, it hardened |
| **25** | The **condition** of the act — *bi'l-ghayb*, without seeing |

10 and 25 are two expressions of the same measure: value lies in the act performed at the moment of uncertainty.
16 says the same thing from the reverse direction: as uncertainty disappears, attention diminishes too.

**Together the three set up this:** faith is a burden carried before the outcome is clear. When the outcome becomes clear the carrying becomes easier, but its value falls — and after a while it is no longer even noticed that anything is being carried.

## Four: the course of the light

Given above (57/28) in a table. In brief: the light comes from outside (9), moves together with the one who has it (12), cannot be transferred (13), is given as a return (19), and is for walking by (28).

## Five: the sūra of opposites

From beginning to end the sūra is built on opposite pairs:

- First / last, outer / inner (3)
- Giving life / giving death (2)
- Entering / coming out, descending / ascending (4)
- Night / day (6)
- Before the conquest / after (10)
- Those with light / those without (12-13)
- The inner face of the wall / its outer face (13)
- Softening / hardening (16)
- Dead ground / ground brought to life (17)
- Grieving / rejoicing (23)
- *Baʾs* / *manāfiʿ* (25)

And the central verse of the sūra (25) is the densest instance of this structure: **the same substance both destroys and builds.**

## Six: the balance repeated three times

Given above (57/29) in a table: after every distinction the sūra enters a note. It makes a grading but equalises the promise (10); it commands a race but counts the prize a bounty (21); it gives a promise but recalls whose the bounty is (29).

This is the sūra's way of keeping a check on its own measure.

## Seven: the note of "many"

Three times the sūra says **وَكَثِيرٌ مِّنْهُمْ فَٰسِقُونَ** (16, 26, 27), and once it says *"We gave those among them who believed their reward"* (27).

These notes let the text itself determine how what the sūra says about the People of the Book is to be read. The verdict is not wholesale, and the text says so by repeating it three times.

# What this says to the present

The present-day bearing of individual verses was treated in its place. Here I gather a few points that emerge from the sūra as a whole.

## One: the language of ownership

The most original contribution of the sūra is that it builds spending on **trust** rather than on **generosity** (57/7). And this changes the language itself.

Today the language of charity and donation is built largely on generosity: the giver's name is announced, the amount given is publicised, the giver is thanked. Within the frame the sūra sets up this becomes odd — no ceremony is held for the person who delivers a trust to its place.

The most practical consequence of the difference lies in **the position of the receiver.** In the language of generosity the receiver has to be grateful; in the language of trust he does not. The Qurʾān says this plainly elsewhere: *"In their properties there is a known **right** for the one who asks and the one who is deprived"* (al-Maʿārij 70/24-25).

## Two: cooling with time

57/16 is the verse of the sūra that looks most directly to the present, and the phenomenon it describes is this: **a result is produced even when nothing is done.** A bond, left untended, does not stay where it was.

Its present-day scale is far greater than that of the verse's first hearers. And the cause the verse points to — duration — is not something that can be prevented. What can be prevented is the failure to notice the effect of duration.

This is why the verse gives its warning early: **cooling with time is not noticed, because every day resembles the one before.**

And the sūra shows its remedy too, in the very next verse: dead ground comes alive (57/17). Hardening is not a sentence but a state.

## Three: the subject of the competition

Two verses apart the sūra blames the same grammatical pattern (reciprocity) once and commands it once: *tafākhur/takāthur* (20) and *sābiqū* (21).

The measure set up at al-Baqara 2/148 finds its plainest proof here: **the Qurʾān does not forbid competing, it changes the subject of the competition.** And the difference lies in whether the subject is divisible.

The present-day counterpart of this is the question of where the measure lies. In a comparative measure a point of "enough" cannot be defined, because the measure keeps moving. Without a fixed measure it is not possible to stop.

## Four: the three legs of justice

The triad set up by 57/25 — knowledge, measure, force — offers a frame, and the cases in which the frame falls short can be recognised. The most insidious is **the measure being in the hands of one of the parties.**

The word order of the verse sets an order as well: the Book, the balance, then iron. Force comes last; and it is not a purpose in itself.

This is neither the taking of a political side nor the proposal of a model of government. What the verse sets up is the description of a **relation**: force without measure is oppression, measure without force is ineffective.

## Five: between two extremes

In verse 20 the sūra criticises the world with five words; in verse 27 it criticises withdrawal from the world. And its commands stand between the two: spend, race, uphold justice.

This balance is one of the sūra's most practical contributions. For religious life has two familiar deviations: turning the world into religion, and drawing from religion something worldless. The sūra closes off both.

## Six: upon whom to read the text

This is the counterpart in al-Ḥadīd of the trap recorded in the section on al-Humaza, and here it is sharper.

The heaviest verse of the sūra (16) directs its address plainly at those who believe. And what the sūra says about the People of the Book is bounded three times with the note "many".

That is, the text has taken two separate precautions against being read outwards: on the one hand it addresses those inside, on the other it does not make its verdict about those outside wholesale.

To read the sūra while ignoring these two precautions is to fall into the very state the sūra describes — **the text ceasing to touch the one who reads it.**
