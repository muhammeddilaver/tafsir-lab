# 50. Sūrat Qāf

Forty-five verses. Meccan. It takes its name from the single letter of its first verse: **ق**.

Three sūras in the Qurʾān begin with a single letter — **Ṣād, Qāf, Nūn** — and this is one of the three. As was recorded in the treatment of al-Qalam, what the three have in common is that an oath or a qualification follows the letter. In Qāf what follows is an oath, and what is sworn by is **the Qurʾān itself**.

The subject of the sūra is single and does not change from beginning to end: **being raised after death.** But the sūra does not defend this subject as an article of creed. What it does is more interesting: it diagnoses the objection as **astonishment**, and then answers the astonishment not with an argument but with **looking**. "Have they not looked at the sky above them?" Then it comes down to the earth, then to water, then to plants, then to the reviving of dead ground — and just there, without a pause, it says: **"Such is the coming forth."**

The proof of the sūra, that is, is not a syllogism but an **image**. Seeing this settles how Qāf is to be read.

And when the sūra ends it has come back to where it started. In the first verse an oath is sworn by the Qurʾān; in the last verse it says *"remind with the Qurʾān"*. In the third verse the objectors call the resurrection "a far return"; in the thirty-first verse the Garden is brought near for the godfearing to a place *"not far off"*. In the eleventh verse the coming out of plants from the soil is called *khurūj*; in the forty-second verse the name of the day of resurrection is *yawmu'l-khurūj*.

**These three rings are the skeleton of the sūra.** This section will first make them visible.

---

## The structure of the sūra

The sūra is made of ten blocks, and the boundaries of the blocks are mostly drawn by the text's own repetitions.

| Block | Verses | Subject | What closes the block |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | 1-5 | The letter, the oath, the objection, the diagnosis of the objection | *fī amrin marīj* (5) |
| **II** | 6-11 | The proof from looking: sky, earth, plants, water, dead ground | ***kadhālika'l-khurūj*** (11) |
| **III** | 12-14 | Eight peoples — a list from history | *fa-ḥaqqa **waʿīd*** (14) |
| **IV** | 15 | The passage from the first creation to the second | *khalqin jadīd* (15) |
| **V** | 16-18 | Nearness and record: the whisper of the soul, the two angels | *raqībun ʿatīd* (18) |
| **VI** | 19-23 | The moment of death, the trumpet, the driving forward, the lifting of the veil | *hādhā mā ladayya ʿatīd* (23) |
| **VII** | 24-30 | The scene of judgement, the dispute with the *qarīn*, the question of Hell | *hal min **mazīd*** (30) |
| **VIII** | 31-35 | The Garden brought near, the description of the godfearing | *wa ladaynā **mazīd*** (35) |
| **IX** | 36-38 | History, the lesson, the six days and the absence of weariness | *mā massanā min lughūb* (38) |
| **X** | 39-45 | Patience, glorification, the call, the coming forth, the limit | *man yakhāfu **waʿīd*** (45) |

**Most of the block boundaries are marked by the repetition of a word**, and these are countable data:

- **Blocks VII and VIII both end with the word *mazīd*** — one from the mouth of Hell, the other about the Garden.
- **Blocks III and X both end with the word *waʿīd*** — and in both the final *yāʾ* of the word has dropped (*waʿīd*, not *waʿīdī*).
- **Blocks V and VI both end with the word *ʿatīd*** — one an attribute of the one who keeps the record, the other the presenting of the record kept.

### Three times "that is the day"

The sūra names the day of resurrection in three separate places, and in all three it uses the same pattern:

| Verse | Expression | The name given |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | *dhālika **yawmu'l-waʿīd*** | The day of the threat |
| 34 | *dhālika **yawmu'l-khulūd*** | The day of abiding |
| 42 | *dhālika **yawmu'l-khurūj*** | The day of coming forth |

**One day, three names.** And the order of the names points in a direction: first the threat (for the denier), then the abiding (for the godfearing), and last the coming forth — the act that is common to everyone.

The same demonstrative is used in two further places, and those two complete the pattern: *dhālika mā kunta minhu taḥīd* (19 — "this is what you were shrinking from") and *dhālika ḥashrun ʿalaynā yasīr* (44 — "that is a gathering easy for Us").

### Rings within the sūra

What follows are verifiable textual data; what is interpretation is the claim that they form a pattern, and I present that as an observation.

| Root / word | First occurrence | Its return |
|---|---|---|
| **ق-ر-أ** | *wa'l-Qurʾāni'l-majīd* (1) | *fa-dhakkir bi'l-**Qurʾān*** (45) |
| **ب-ع-د** | *rajʿun **baʿīd*** (3) | *ḍalālin **baʿīd*** (27), *ghayra **baʿīd*** (31) |
| **خ-ر-ج** | *kadhālika'l-**khurūj*** (11) | *yawmu'l-**khurūj*** (42) |
| **ح-ف-ظ** | *kitābun **ḥafīẓ*** (4) | *awwābin **ḥafīẓ*** (32) |
| **ن-و-ب** | *ʿabdin **munīb*** (8) | *qalbin **munīb*** (33) |
| **ذ-ك-ر** | *wa **dhikrā*** (8) | *la-**dhikrā*** (37), *fa-**dhakkir*** (45) |
| **ز-ي-د** | *hal min **mazīd*** (30) | *wa ladaynā **mazīd*** (35) |
| **ع-ت-د** | *raqībun **ʿatīd*** (18) | *hādhā mā ladayya **ʿatīd*** (23) |
| **ش-ه-د** | *sāʾiqun wa **shahīd*** (21) | *wa huwa **shahīd*** (37) |
| **ق-ر-ب** | *naḥnu **aqrabu*** (16) | *min makānin **qarīb*** (41) |
| **ح-ق-ق** | *kadhdhabū **bi'l-ḥaqq*** (5) | *sakratu'l-mawti **bi'l-ḥaqq*** (19), *aṣ-ṣayḥata **bi'l-ḥaqq*** (42) |
| **مَا يَقُولُونَ** | *fa'ṣbir ʿalā **mā yaqūlūn*** (39) | *naḥnu aʿlamu bi-**mā yaqūlūn*** (45) |

**The most striking of them is the root ل-ق-ي**, because it is used across the sūra for four different tasks:

| Verse | Word | What is done |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | *wa **alqaynā** fīhā rawāsiya* | Mountains were **cast** onto the earth |
| 17 | *idh **yatalaqqa'l-mutalaqqiyān*** | The two recorders **meet and take up** what is said |
| 24, 26 | ***alqiyā*** fī jahannam | **Cast** into Hell |
| 37 | *aw **alqa's-samʿ*** | He **gave / cast** his ear |

The same root: it sets a mountain in place, it catches a word, it throws a person, it turns an ear. **I record this as a pattern; I do not claim that the sūra is building a deliberate play on words.** What I record is that the root is used in this sūra with an unusual density and for tasks far removed from one another.

### The verse-ending: the common sound of forty-five verses

In the treatment of al-Qalam an observation about sound was made (all fifty-two of its verses end in *nūn* or *mīm*). In Qāf there is something similar, but of a different shape:

*majīd, ʿajīb, baʿīd, ḥafīẓ, marīj, furūj, bahīj, munīb, ḥaṣīd, naḍīd, khurūj, Thamūd, Lūṭ, waʿīd, jadīd, warīd, qaʿīd, ʿatīd, taḥīd, waʿīd, shahīd, ḥadīd, ʿatīd, ʿanīd, murīb, shadīd, baʿīd, waʿīd, ʿabīd, mazīd, baʿīd, ḥafīẓ, munīb, khulūd, mazīd, maḥīṣ, shahīd, lughūb, ghurūb, sujūd, qarīb, khurūj, maṣīr, yasīr, waʿīd.*

**All forty-five of the forty-five verses end in the same shape: a long *ī* or *ū* sound followed by a single consonant.** There is no exception. Most of the verse-endings are on the *faʿīl* pattern; the rest sit on the same sound scheme.

**I record this as a countable fact.** I build no calculation, no secret and no numerical claim on it — as was written plainly in the treatment of al-Baqara, this commentary does not enter into letter or number reckoning. What I record is this: the sūra is recited on a long breath and with a single closing sound. And that is a hint that the sūra was built to be listened to — the report mentioned below supports this as well.

### On its revelation and its recitation

There is something close to consensus that the sūra is Meccan. I transmit no report of a specific occasion of revelation.

**In the hadith collections there are two reports about the recitation of this sūra, and both are well known:**

- In Muslim's *Ṣaḥīḥ*, it is transmitted from Umm Hishām bint Ḥāritha that she learnt Sūrat Qāf by heart from the mouth of the Prophet, because he recited it every Friday in the sermon.
- Also in Muslim, it is transmitted that the Prophet recited Sūrat Qāf and Sūrat al-Qamar in the two festival prayers.

**I give these two reports with their source stated; on their details (wording, chain) I issue no ruling.** What is worth recording is this: the sūra is remembered as a text **recited aloud before a gathering**. That all forty-five verses end on a single sound pattern, and that its subject is single, agree with such a use.

The basmala was treated in earlier sections; the verses are counted without it.

## 50/1 — قٓ ۚ وَٱلْقُرْءَانِ ٱلْمَجِيدِ

*Qāf. Wa'l-Qurʾāni'l-majīd*

"Qāf. By the Qurʾān, wide in glory."

### قٓ — the letter

**In the treatment of al-Baqara 2/1 the disconnected letters were dealt with at length; I do not repeat the findings there.** Their summary was this: the meaning of these letters is **not known with certainty**; one group of scholars refers the meaning to God; among the views that seek an explanation, the one that finds most support from within the text is the explanation that the letters make the challenge meaningful by pointing to the **raw material** of the Qurʾān. And, as was written plainly there: **no numerical reckoning, no *abjad* and no letter-counting is done with these letters.**

**I set this note down here once more and in heavier type, because the letter ق is one of the places where claims of this kind are produced most often.** This commentary does not take that road.

Now I give the principal explanations transmitted for this letter in the classical commentaries. **The table is not a list of preferences; it is an inventory of what is transmitted.**

| Explanation | What it says | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Consignment (*tafwīḍ*) — referring the meaning to God** | These letters belong to the ambiguous verses; their meaning has not been made known to us, and the manner of it is not entered into | The attitude of the early generations; not a confession of ignorance but a choice to be silent where the text gives no explanation |
| **An abbreviation of a name** | ق → a pointer to a name such as *al-Qādir* or *al-Qāhir* | As recorded in the treatment of al-Baqara, this is not systematic: applied to all the letter-groups, the same method does not give consistent results |
| **An abbreviation of a sentence** | ق → the first letter of an expression such as *quḍiya'l-amr* ("the matter has been decided") | It is transmitted; but its only support is supposition, and the text does not bear it out |
| **A letter of oath** | God is swearing by this letter | Since an explicit particle of oath (*wa'l-Qurʾān*) follows immediately, this explanation is weakened: the oath is already made separately |
| **Calling attention (*tanbīh*)** | Opening with an unfamiliar sound draws the hearer into the text | A plausible function; but it does not explain **why this letter** |
| **The name of the sūra** | The letter is the name of the sūra | The sūra really is known by this name; but the same does not hold consistently for the other disconnected letters |
| **The material of the challenge** | "Here is the letter the Qurʾān is made of; go ahead" | The explanation found most plausible in al-Baqara because it rests on a pattern within the text; I take it up separately below |
| **"Mount Qāf"** | It is transmitted that this is the name of a mountain encircling the earth | **I transmit this explanation but do not adopt it.** Its source is narratives held to be *Isrāʾīliyyāt*; it has no ground in the Qurʾān or in sound transmission, and many exegetes reject it |

**And above all of them this must be written plainly: there is no certain knowledge.** None of the explanations above is enough to say "this is what this letter means". In this commentary no meaning is **manufactured** for the letter.

### But the pattern here is very plain

The pattern established in the treatment of al-Baqara needs recalling:

> *"When one looks at the verses that follow these letters a regularity appears: most of the letter-groups are followed immediately by an expression that speaks of the Book."*

**Qāf is one of the barest examples of this pattern** — because nothing at all comes in between. The letter is uttered, and immediately afterwards, within the same verse, the Qurʾān is named:

> **ق** ۚ **وَٱلْقُرْءَانِ** ٱلْمَجِيدِ

And a comparison must be added to this. Two of the three sūras that begin with a single letter continue with an oath by the Qurʾān:

| Sūra | Opening | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| **Ṣād 38/1** | صٓ | *wa'l-Qurʾāni **dhi'dh-dhikr*** — "by the Qurʾān, holder of the reminder" |
| **Qāf 50/1** | قٓ | *wa'l-Qurʾāni **'l-majīd*** — "by the Qurʾān, wide in glory" |
| **al-Qalam 68/1** | نٓ | *wa'l-**qalam**i wa mā yasṭurūn* — "by the pen and what they write" |

**In the treatment of al-Qalam this difference was dwelt on:** there the move was from the letter not to the **product** but to the **instrument** that makes the product. In Ṣād and Qāf the move is from the letter straight to the product — and in both the Qurʾān is named with an attribute.

**What is to be recorded is this:** in all three of the single-letter sūras the first thing that comes after the letter belongs to **the field of writing**. Al-Qalam names the pen; Ṣād and Qāf name the text. Letter — instrument — text. I give this as an observation.

### وَٱلْقُرْءَانِ — the oath by the Qurʾān

**That the Qurʾān swears by itself** is a situation worth dwelling on, and in this sūra its function is plain: the last verse of the sūra will say *"remind with the Qurʾān"*. The text, that is, begins its work by swearing by itself and ends it by commanding that reminding be done with itself. **The forty-three verses in between stand between these two ends.**

### ٱلْمَجِيد — root م-ج-د

**This root was treated in the section on al-Burūj.** What was recorded there was this:

> *"The sense of the root: honour, loftiness, and abundance. The lexicographers describe *majd* as 'the breadth and abundance of honour'. A pasture ample enough for a camel is called *majudat*; there is breadth and plenty in the root."*

**Here I open the concrete side of the root a little further**, because that is precisely what is at work in Qāf.

The primary usage the lexicographers give has to do with **pasture and camels**:

- **مَجُدَتِ الإِبِل** — the camel came upon ample pasture and ate its fill.
- **أَمْجَدَ الرَّجُلُ دَابَّتَهُ** — the man gave his animal fodder in plenty.
- **مَجْد** — from there: a person's honour being **broad and full**.

**The picture beneath the root, then, is this: not a little fodder in a narrow place, but enough in a wide pasture to eat one's fill.** The sense of honour is derived from that breadth. In Arabic loftiness is very often expressed by **the size of the area covered**.

**Now its use for the Qurʾān is striking.**

The Qurʾān names itself with many attributes: *ḥakīm* (wise), *karīm* (precious), *ʿaẓīm* (great), *mubīn* (clear), *ʿazīz* (unassailable). Most of these attributes convey **depth**, **worth** or **certainty**.

***Majīd*, however, conveys breadth.** What the root of the word measures is how much room a thing takes up and how far it satisfies.

**I present this as my own reading, and its support is the lexicographers' description of the root:** the Qurʾān is named here not as "glorious" but as **"wide in glory"** — large in scope, ample in the satisfaction it gives. And it has a link with the subject of the sūra: in a moment the objectors will describe the resurrection as **"far"** (verse 3), that is, with a word of **distance**. Already in the first verse the sūra sets its own measure against theirs: **breadth.**

**A note:** in al-Burūj this word occurred twice — once for the Throne (or for the owner of the Throne), once for the Qurʾān (*qurʾānun majīd*, 85/21). The attribute, then, is used of the Qurʾān in another sūra as well and is not peculiar to Qāf. What is peculiar to Qāf is that the attribute comes **inside an oath**.

### Where is the answer to the oath? — a familiar question of word order

In Arabic an oath (*qasam*) calls for an answer (*jawāb al-qasam*): "…by such and such, **it is so**." Here there is no explicit answer. The exegetes have debated this, and the principal views are these:

| View | What the answer is | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The answer has been elided (*maḥdhūf*)** | A clause such as "…surely you will be raised" is supplied | The context requires it; this is the whole subject of the sūra. In Arabic, where the indication is plain, the elision of the answer is common |
| **The answer is verse 2** | *"But they were astonished that a warner came to them from among themselves"* | The particle *bal* can come after an oath; but it is unusual for the answer-clause to come in a negative or *iḍrāb* structure |
| **The answer is verse 4** | *"We know what the earth diminishes of them"* | In sense this is the clause that best suits the oath; but two verses have come in between |
| **The answer is in verse 45** | *"You are not a tyrant over them"* | It is found weak because of the distance |

**On this disagreement I do not impose a preference.** But an observation can be recorded: all four views look for the answer of the oath around **the reality of the resurrection**. The wording of the answer is disputed; its content is not.

**And the answer's not being uttered itself produces an effect.** An oath is sworn, the sentence is not completed, and immediately afterwards the astonishment of the other side begins to be described. The reader is left standing on an unfinished sentence.

## 50/2 — بَلْ عَجِبُوٓا۟ أَن جَآءَهُم مُّنذِرٌ مِّنْهُمْ فَقَالَ ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ هَٰذَا شَىْءٌ عَجِيبٌ

*Bal ʿajibū an jāʾahum mundhirun minhum, fa-qāla'l-kāfirūna hādhā shayʾun ʿajīb*

"But they were astonished that a warner came to them from among themselves. And those who denied said: this is an astonishing thing."

### بَلْ — the particle that turns the direction of speech

**In Arabic *bal* (بل) is a particle of *iḍrāb*:** leaving the previous clause and passing to something else. It works in two ways — either it cancels what preceded, or it leaves what preceded and jumps to the real matter.

**Here it is the second.** An oath was sworn, its answer was not given, and with *bal* the subject shifts all at once to the state of the other side. The effect is this: the text does not set about defending its own claim; **it takes the objection itself as its subject.**

And note: *bal* comes twice in a row in this sūra — in verse 2 (*bal ʿajibū*) and in verse 5 (*bal kadhdhabū*). Both do the same work: putting **the real name** to the objection. First "they were astonished", then "they denied". The second *bal* rises above the first as well.

### عَجِبُوا۟ — root ع-ج-ب

**Its concrete sense:** a thing's bringing a person to a halt because its cause is not known. The lexicographers describe *ʿajab* as "the state the soul feels before something whose cause is not known". Astonishment, that is, is **a halting that arises from not knowing.**

Its derivatives show the direction of the root:

- **عَجِيب** — the thing wondered at.
- **عُجَاب** — wondered at still more; an intensive form. In the Qurʾān: *"This is truly an astonishing thing"* (Ṣād 38/5).
- **أُعْجُوبَة** — an astounding event.
- **إِعْجَاب** — a thing's pleasing someone; and **self-admiration** (*uʿjiba bi-nafsihī*).
- **إِعْجَاز** — is from a different root (ع-ج-ز); the two must not be confused.

**The most instructive side of the root is this double use:** the same root carries both "to be astonished" and "to be pleased". What links them is that in both **a person loses hold of himself before something** — in one case in a negative direction, in the other in a positive one.

**In the treatment of al-Jinn this occurrence of the root was already mentioned**, and the comparison made there is worth attention: in Sūrat al-Jinn the jinn hear the Qurʾān and say *"qurʾānan **ʿajabā**"*; the Meccans, with the same root, say *"hādhā shayʾun **ʿajīb**"*. **The same word, two opposite outcomes.** The finding in the treatment of al-Jinn was this: the word is the same, the verb that follows it is different — one believed, the other denied.

### The objection beginning not with a proof but with astonishment

**This is the most important side of this verse, and it settles the whole method of the sūra.**

In transmitting the objection of the other side the verse **transmits no proof.** What it transmits is a **reaction**: they were astonished, and they said "an astonishing thing".

And there is a difference between the two:

| | **A proof** | **Astonishment** |
|---|---|---|
| What it rests on | A premise | Habit |
| Can it be argued with | Yes — the premise can be tested | No — there is nothing to test |
| What it says | "It cannot be, for this reason" | "There is no such thing" |
| Can it be falsified | Yes | Not on its own |

**Astonishment has no probative value, because astonishment is a measure not of knowledge but of familiarity.** A person is astonished at what he is not used to. And habit says nothing whatever about whether a thing is possible.

**And because the sūra knows this, it does not answer with a proof.** What it will do from the sixth verse on is to call the addressee to look at **the things he sees every day** — that is, to step into the field of the familiar and show how astonishing the familiar thing actually is.

**I record this as my own reading**, but its support is the text's own arrangement: the objection is named "astonishment", and the answer is given by "looking". Both belong to the same field of sense.

### أَن جَآءَهُم مُّنذِرٌ مِّنْهُمْ — what were they astonished at?

The clause says two things together: a **warner came**, and that warner was **one of them**.

The exegetes differ over which of these the astonishment was directed at:

| View | What the astonishment is at | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **At the warner's coming** | At a prophet's having been sent at all | This is the primary sense of the expression *an jāʾahum mundhir* |
| **At the warner's being one of them** | Not an angel, but a person they knew | The qualification *minhum* has been set down deliberately; the Qurʾān transmits this objection explicitly elsewhere |
| **At the content of the warning** | At the resurrection; verse 3 opens this out | Verse 3 is directly an objection to the resurrection |

**The three do not exclude one another**, and the arrangement of the verse seems to give all three in order: he came → from among us → and he says this (verse 3).

**مُنذِر — root ن-ذ-ر.** This root was treated in a number of sections (al-Mulk, Nūḥ, al-Muddaththir, al-Mursalāt); I do not repeat it. Its summary: *indhār* is to give notice of an approaching danger **in time**; the root also contains *nadhr* (a vow — a word pledged in advance), so the idea of being **beforehand** stands at the centre of the root.

**مِنْهُمْ — "from among them".** The word carries the heaviest weight of the verse. That the warner is from the same community is something the Qurʾān stresses in many places, and it works in two directions: on the one hand it makes him **intelligible** (he speaks the same language, knows the same life), on the other it makes acceptance **harder** (what is familiar is not held in awe).

### فَقَالَ ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ — the change of the name

**Something in the word order is striking.** At the beginning of the sentence the subject is a pronoun: *ʿajibū* — "they were astonished". In the second clause an explicit noun comes: *fa-qāla'l-**kāfirūn***.

The same people, that is, are named in two clauses by two different names: first an indefinite "they", then **"those who deny"**.

**This is a structure called in Arabic *iẓhār fī maḥalli'l-iḍmār*:** bringing in the explicit noun where a pronoun would do. Its function is to bring forward the attribute that noun carries.

**Its effect here is this:** astonishment by itself is not denial; astonishment is a human reaction. But to turn astonishment into **a verdict** — to say "this is an astonishing thing" and close the matter — is something else. The verse names those who take the second step.

**I give this as my own reading;** its support is that the difference of naming between the two clauses stands in the text.

## 50/3 — أَءِذَا مِتْنَا وَكُنَّا تُرَابًا ۖ ذَٰلِكَ رَجْعٌۢ بَعِيدٌ

*A-idhā mitnā wa kunnā turābā? Dhālika rajʿun baʿīd*

"When we are dead and have become dust? That is a far return."

### The sentence left half-finished

**The question is not completed.** "When we are dead and have become dust — what?" There is no answer to it. In Arabic this is called *ḥadhf*, and its function here is plain: the rest of the sentence (are we to be raised?) is not thought worth saying.

**The objector, that is, will not put into words the thing he objects to.** He stops halfway through the sentence and passes straight to the verdict: *dhālika rajʿun baʿīd.*

This is one of the most economical forms mockery and belittlement take in language, and the Qurʾān transmits this objection with a similar incompleteness in many places.

### وَكُنَّا تُرَابًا — to become dust

The objection rests not on death itself but on **the state that follows death**: dispersal. While a corpse remains, being raised may perhaps be imagined; for what has mixed with the soil and become indistinguishable, imagining it grows hard.

**And the fourth verse will answer exactly this point:** *"We know what the earth diminishes of them."* The ground the objection rests on — **indistinguishability** — is aimed at directly.

### رَجْعٌ — a return

**Root: ر-ج-ع.** To return, to turn back. *Rajʿ*, as a verbal noun, is "a return". The word comes indefinite: *rajʿun* — "a return".

The Qurʾān uses this root often for the resurrection: *ilayhi turjaʿūn* (to Him you will be returned), *al-marjiʿ* (the place of return). The objector, that is, is using the other side's own word and putting an adjective in front of it.

### بَعِيدٌ — and the most important word of the sūra

**Root: ب-ع-د.** To be far. The concrete sense of the root is **distance**; *buʿd* — the gap between two points.

**Now we come to the real point of the verse.**

Which word does the objector choose to say that the resurrection **will not happen**? Not "impossible". Not "absurd". Not "contrary to reason".

**He says "far".**

**This is a word of distance.** And to express impossibility with a word of distance is a settled usage in Arabic (and in English): "a remote possibility", "a far-fetched view". But even in its figurative use the word goes on carrying its concrete sense.

**And the sūra behaves as though it has noticed this.** Because:

| Verse | Expression | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **3** | *rajʿun **baʿīd*** | The objector's verdict: the return is far |
| **16** | *naḥnu **aqrabu** ilayhi min ḥabli'l-warīd* | God is **nearer** to a person than his jugular vein |
| **27** | *ḍalālin **baʿīd*** | What is really far: error |
| **31** | *uzlifati'l-jannatu … **ghayra baʿīd*** | The Garden is brought near to the godfearing, **not far off** |
| **41** | *min makānin **qarīb*** | The caller calls from a **near** place |

**The sūra takes the word the objector chose and spreads it through the whole text.** And each time it turns its direction: what they call far turns out to be near; what is far turns out to be something else.

**I present this as my own reading**, but all five occurrences in the table stand in the text and can be counted. What is interpretation is the claim that they form an order of reply.

**This verse was mentioned in the treatment of al-Maʿārij**, and the sense given to the word there was: *"a return that will not happen"*. There too, that is, the word of distance was read as meaning impossibility. In Qāf the sūra itself keeps the sense of distance alive.

## 50/4 — قَدْ عَلِمْنَا مَا تَنقُصُ ٱلْأَرْضُ مِنْهُمْ ۖ وَعِندَنَا كِتَٰبٌ حَفِيظٌ

*Qad ʿalimnā mā tanquṣu'l-arḍu minhum, wa ʿindanā kitābun ḥafīẓ*

"We know what the earth diminishes of them. With Us there is a guarding book."

### The first answer given to the objection: knowledge

**The objection in the third verse was an objection from *indistinguishability*:** what has mixed with the soil can no longer be recognised.

**The answer does not describe how the resurrection will happen.** In its place it removes the premise the objection rests on: **indistinguishability holds for you, not for the one who keeps the record.**

This is the first example of the sūra's way of answering, and it agrees with those that follow: the objection itself is not refuted, **the ground the objection rests on** is removed.

### تَنقُصُ — root ن-ق-ص

To diminish, to lessen. The verb is in the **present-general** and **imperfect** form: *tanquṣu* — "what it is diminishing". An ongoing operation is being described.

**The subject of the verb is *al-arḍ* (the earth).** The soil, that is, is set down here not as a passive container but as an agent that **diminishes**. A working that takes the body, lessens it, dissolves it.

**This is the most concrete side of the verse, and it can be joined to the present without forcing:** the dissolving of a buried body in the soil is a process; chemical, biological, measurable. The verse gives that process a name: **being diminished.**

**How much it diminishes, however, is not measured.** The verse does not say "we know how much it diminishes"; it says ***"what it diminishes"*** — that is, not the amount but the **identity**. What has gone is known.

### The disagreement: what is it that "the earth diminishes"?

| View | What is meant | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Bodies** | The parts of the dead that dissolve in the soil | It is directly linked to *"when we have become dust"* in verse 3; this is the most widespread explanation |
| **The number of those who die** | What the earth diminishes of people: each one who dies | The expression *minhum* (of them) is open to this reading too |
| **The diminishing of deeds or of lifespan** | What is subtracted from the life lived upon the earth | It is transmitted; but it is remote from the context |

**The context brings the first reading forward**, because the previous verse speaks of mixing with the soil. The second reading is not against the text either and does not conflict with the first.

### وَعِندَنَا كِتَٰبٌ حَفِيظٌ — the guarding book

**عِندَ** — "with Him". An adverb of place, but when used of God it carries no sense of place; it states **belonging and the domain of authority**. This note lies on the same line as the note set down for *maʿiyya* (being-with) in the treatment of al-Ḥadīd, and it holds here too.

**حَفِيظ — root ح-ف-ظ.** To guard, to keep, to watch over. *Ḥifẓ* — to memorise is from this root too: **to keep a text from being lost.**

**The pattern of the word raises a grammatical question.** The *faʿīl* pattern in Arabic can carry both an active (*fāʿil*) and a passive (*mafʿūl*) sense:

| Reading | Sense | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** (in the sense of *ḥāfiẓ*) | A **guarding** book | The book keeps what is in it from being lost |
| **Passive** (in the sense of *maḥfūẓ*) | A **guarded** book | The book itself is kept from being corrupted |

**Both readings are transmitted and both are meaningful.** The first states the book's work, the second its condition. And in practice they require one another: a book that guards must itself be guarded.

**This verse was mentioned in the treatment of al-Burūj** — there it was found disputed whether expressions such as *al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ*, *umm al-kitāb* and *kitābin maknūn* are one and the same, and no ruling was issued. **That note holds here as well:** what this book is, where it is, what kind of thing it is, is not stated. The only thing stated is **its function**.

### Word order: why does *qad ʿalimnā* come first?

The sentence begins with a verb, and before it stands **قَدْ**. *Qad* plus a perfect verb conveys **confirmation** (*taḥqīq*) in Arabic: "indeed, in fact".

**And note this sequence:**

1. *Qad ʿalimnā* — **We know** (knowledge).
2. *Wa ʿindanā kitābun ḥafīẓ* — **and it is written** (record).

Knowledge first, then the record. **The record is added to the knowledge; it does not take the place of the knowledge.**

**This sequence is worth dwelling on, and I give it as my own reading:** for one who knows, keeping a record is no necessity — the one who does not forget takes no notes. The one for whom the record is kept, then, is **not the one who knows but the one about whom judgement will be given.** The record is for the sake of justice being seen.

**The same structure will be repeated in verses 17-18:** there too, immediately after it is said that God "knows the whisper of the soul", two recording angels are spoken of. **In both places the order is the same: knowledge first, then the record.** This is a repetition that can be verified within the sūra.

## 50/5 — بَلْ كَذَّبُوا۟ بِٱلْحَقِّ لَمَّا جَآءَهُمْ فَهُمْ فِىٓ أَمْرٍ مَّرِيجٍ

*Bal kadhdhabū bi'l-ḥaqqi lammā jāʾahum, fa-hum fī amrin marīj*

"But they denied the truth when it came to them. Now they are in a tangled affair."

### The second بَلْ — the diagnosis deepens

In the second verse it was said *bal ʿajibū* (they were astonished). Here a second *bal* comes and climbs a step higher: *bal kadhdhabū* (they denied).

**The difference between the two verbs matters:**

| | ***ʿajibū*** | ***kadhdhabū*** |
|---|---|---|
| What | To be astonished | To deny |
| Its kind | A reaction | A verdict |
| Responsibility | Limited | Full |
| Turning back from it | Easy | Hard |

**The sūra opens the two layers of the objection in turn:** on the surface there is astonishment, underneath it there is a verdict. And the second *bal* corrects the first: "the real matter is not their astonishment."

### لَمَّا جَآءَهُمْ — the timing

***Lammā*** — "at the moment when". The denial, that is, came **at the same instant** as the arrival of the truth. No examination, no thinking, no waiting came in between.

**This is the sharpest side of the verse.** To reject a claim is not in itself a fault; the fault is that the rejection comes **before the examination**. The verse marks the timing.

### بِٱلْحَقِّ — the three *bi'l-ḥaqq*s in the sūra

This expression occurs three times in the sūra, and all three build an order:

| Verse | Expression | What |
|---|---|---|
| **5** | *kadhdhabū **bi'l-ḥaqq*** | They denied the truth |
| **19** | *wa jāʾat sakratu'l-mawti **bi'l-ḥaqq*** | The stupor of death came with the truth |
| **42** | *yawma yasmaʿūna'ṣ-ṣayḥata **bi'l-ḥaqq*** | They will hear the cry with the truth |

**What they denied comes back, first with death and then with the cry.** The same word: what was rejected is at the door a few verses later.

**I record this as an observation;** all three occurrences stand in the text.

### مَّرِيج — root م-ر-ج

**This root was treated in the section on al-Raḥmān.** What was recorded there was this:

> *"The idea beneath the root is mixing and letting loose; the two go together, because things let loose get mixed. *Marj* — a pasture, the place where an animal is let loose. *Marīj* — mixed."*

**Here I add what belongs to Qāf.**

*Marīj* means "mixed"; but the kind of the mixing is specific: **entangled with itself, not separating out, not settling anywhere.** Like a pasture whose flock has scattered. The lexicographers use the word for indecision and instability.

**And the cause and effect the verse builds is striking:** *fa-hum fī amrin marīj* — with the particle **fāʾ**. The confusion, that is, is not the **cause** of the denial but its **consequence**.

**This is the reverse of the ordinary expectation.** The usual picture is: he was confused, and so he rejected. The verse turns this round: **he rejected, and so he was confused.**

**And it touches on something observable.** A person who rejects a thing from the outset is afterwards obliged to build a consistent account of it — and cannot. The Qurʾān lists the verdicts its addressees passed on the Prophet: a poet, a soothsayer, a madman, a sorcerer, one who has been taught. **Five separate verdicts about one person.** That scatteredness is itself *marīj*: the rejection is settled, the ground for it is not.

**I present this link as my own reading**; the verse does not enumerate the grounds, it only names the outcome.

### What this says to the present

**That rejection produces confusion is observable today as well.**

A person who rejects a claim without examining it is obliged to explain why the claim exists at all: "why do so many people believe this?" And each attempt at explanation contradicts the last. The rejection is one, the grounds are many — because the grounds were produced **after** the rejection.

What the verse says is not an accusation but a finding about **sequence**: if the verdict comes first, the reason runs along behind. This is a working that holds for everyone, without regard to which side they are on.

## From the sixth verse to the eleventh: the method of the answer

**Here we must stop and record the most important structural choice of the sūra**, because the individual verses cannot be read in full without knowing it.

In the first five verses an objection was framed: *"When we are dead and have become dust? That is a far return."*

**Now the answer comes. And the form of the answer is this:**

> *"Have they **not looked** at the sky above them?"*

**The sūra, that is, does not enter into an argument. It proposes looking.**

To see how unusual this is, it is enough to think of the other answers that might have been given:

| An answer that could have been given | Its kind |
|---|---|
| "God has power over everything" | A proof from an attribute |
| "The one who created the first time will create a second time" | A rational analogy |
| "Justice requires it" | A moral necessity |
| "The prophets have reported it so" | Transmitted report |

**The Qurʾān uses all of these proofs elsewhere** — and it will even use the second in this very sūra, in the fifteenth verse (*"were We wearied by the first creation?"*).

**But the first answer is none of them.** The first answer is **a command to look**. And the things looked at are ordinary things: the sky, the earth, mountains, plants, rain, the date palm, the greening of dead ground.

### Why looking?

**I present this as my own reading, and its support is the second verse.**

The objection was named "astonishment" (*ʿajab*). Astonishment, as was recorded above, is a measure not of knowledge but of **familiarity**: a person is astonished at what he is not used to.

**The answer to astonishment, then, is to shake familiarity itself.**

And this is exactly what the sūra does: it brings the addressee before the things **he sees every day and is never astonished at**. The sky is there every night and no one is astonished. Dry ground turns green with every rain and no one is astonished.

**The argument is this, and it is built without being stated: the measure of astonishment is working inconsistently.** The same person is not in the least astonished at a thing that has already happened (a plant coming out of dead soil), yet calls the same operation done once more "far off".

### The order of the proofs

The six-verse section follows an order that descends from above:

| Verse | Object | Where the look goes |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | The sky | Upward |
| 7 | The earth, the mountains, plants | Downward |
| **8** | ***tabṣira wa dhikrā*** | **An interposed statement: what these are for** |
| 9 | Water and gardens | Coming down from above |
| 10 | The date palm | Rising up from below |
| 11 | The reviving of a dead land | **The conclusion: *kadhālika'l-khurūj*** |

**That the eighth verse stands in the middle is striking:** the list of proofs is cut off, it is said "these are a making-to-see and a reminder", and then the list continues. The section, that is, states its own purpose in its middle.

## 50/6 — أَفَلَمْ يَنظُرُوٓا۟ إِلَى ٱلسَّمَآءِ فَوْقَهُمْ كَيْفَ بَنَيْنَٰهَا وَزَيَّنَّٰهَا وَمَا لَهَا مِن فُرُوجٍ

*A-fa-lam yanẓurū ila's-samāʾi fawqahum kayfa banaynāhā wa zayyannāhā wa mā lahā min furūj*

"Have they not looked at the sky above them — how We built it and adorned it; and it has no fissure at all."

### أَفَلَمْ يَنظُرُوا۟ — "have they not looked"

**نَظَر — root ن-ظ-ر.** To look; but in Arabic *naẓar* is the eye's **turning towards a thing and staying there**. From the same root come *intiẓār* (waiting — the gaze caught on something and waiting), *naẓīr* (a match — the product of a comparing look), *munāẓara* (looking at one another → disputation).

**At the centre of the root is not merely seeing but the duration and the direction of the look.** This is its difference from *raʾy* (to see): seeing happens, looking is done.

**And the verb comes in the form of a negative question: *a-fa-lam yanẓurū*.** The function of this pattern in Arabic is not to ask for information but **to reproach** (*tawbīkh*). "Have they not looked?" — that is, "it was standing in front of them for them to look at."

### فَوْقَهُمْ — "above them"

**In terms of word order this word looks like a redundancy.** The sky is by definition what is above; to say "the sky above them" is like saying "the earth below".

**But it is not redundant.** In Arabic a qualification of this kind stresses the **accessibility** of the object.

**What the verse says is this, and I give it as my own reading:** nothing had to be done in order to look. No journey, no instrument, no schooling. **Lifting the head was enough.** The proof was exactly above their heads.

Read together with the astonishment of the second verse, this becomes even more telling: people astonished at a far-off return have never once looked at what stands **directly above them**.

### كَيْفَ — "how"

The question is not "did they look at the sky"; it is did they look at **"how We built it"**.

**The difference matters:** to see, and to see how a thing was made, are not the same. *Kayfa* turns the look towards **the structure**. What is asked for, that is, is not to gaze at a view but **to notice the construction.**

### بَنَيْنَٰهَا — to build

**Root: ب-ن-ي.** To raise a building. *Bināʾ*, *bunyān*, *ibn* (a son — that on which a line is built) are from the same root.

**That the verb "to build" is used of the sky is a choice.** The Qurʾān uses other verbs of the sky as well: *khalq* (to create), *fatq* (to split apart), *rafʿ* (to raise). *Bināʾ*, however, calls up a structure that is **planned, load-bearing, standing.**

And this verb gains its sense when set against the verb of the seventh verse: the sky was **built**, the earth was **spread out** (*madadnāhā*). What is above is a structure, what is below is a spread cloth. **It is the picture of a house:** ceiling and floor.

### وَزَيَّنَّٰهَا — We adorned it

**Root: ز-ي-ن.** To adorn, to make beautiful.

**And note this sequence: first soundness (*bināʾ*), then beauty (*zīna*), then flawlessness (*mā lahā min furūj*).**

**Three separate claims, three separate measures:**

| Expression | What it says | Which question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| *banaynāhā* | It was built | Is there a structure? |
| *zayyannāhā* | It is beautiful | Is it merely functional? |
| *mā lahā min furūj* | It is flawless | Is there a fault in it? |

**The middle one is worth dwelling on.** A structure does not need to be adorned in order to stand. Adornment is what is **not required**. The verse says that the sky is not only sound but also **more than it had to be**.

**I give this as my own reading:** the force of the proof lies here. The necessary part of a work can be explained by accident. The unnecessary part requires intent.

### وَمَا لَهَا مِن فُرُوجٍ — it has no fissure at all

**Root: ف-ر-ج. This root was treated at length in the section on al-Mursalāt, and the verse before us was named there explicitly.** The summary of the finding there was this:

> *"Its concrete sense: to open the space between two things, to leave a gap, to form a fissure. […] The same root names both a fissure and a relief. The reason is plain — being pressed is narrowness, while relief is a space opening up in between."*

And the comparison built there was this: **the same root, the same object, the opposite verdict.** The sky of today: *mā lahā min furūj* (it has no gap at all). The sky at the resurrection: *furijat* (it was opened up — al-Mursalāt 77/9).

**I do not repeat that link. Here I add what belongs to Qāf.**

**مِن is not redundant.** *Mā lahā **min** furūj* — the *min* here is of the kind called in Arabic *ḥarf jarr zāʾid* (a redundant genitive particle), and its function is **to make the negation complete.** Not "it has no fissures", but **"it has not one fissure".** Not a single exception is left.

**And the logic of the proof lies here.** Flawlessness is the most easily falsified claim observation can make: if a single crack were found, the claim would fall. The verse calls the addressee **to look for a flaw**.

**And indeed the Qurʾān turns this into an explicit command in another sūra:**

> *"You will see no disharmony in the creation of the All-Merciful. Turn your gaze and look: can you see any crack (*fuṭūr*)? Then turn your gaze twice again; the gaze comes back to you weary and worn out."* (al-Mulk 67/3-4)

**These verses were treated at length in the section on al-Mulk**; the finding there — that the gaze returns "driven back and worn out" — is the extended form of the same claim as Qāf 50/6. **Qāf issues the verdict (there is no fissure), al-Mulk proposes the trial (look for one, you will not find it).**

### The connection with natural science — limited and qualified

**Care is needed here, because this is one of the places most open to forced interpretation.**

The verse addresses a person looking with his eyes and speaks of something seen with the eyes: no hole, no tear, no gap is visible in the sky. **This is the datum of the naked eye, and the addressee of the verse is a person looking with the naked eye.**

**I build no bridge from here to modern astronomy.** To match the verse with subjects such as the layers of the atmosphere, the structure of space or cosmic voids is the forced scientific matching that the method (`STYLE.md`) forbids; and it is unnecessary besides, because the proof of the verse already works.

**The only thing that can be recorded is this:** the attitude the verse calls for — **look, examine, search for a flaw** — agrees with the road that leads to knowledge. The verse asks for looking before it asks for belief. This is not a scientific claim but a resemblance of **method**, and it can be recorded only to that extent.

## 50/7 — وَٱلْأَرْضَ مَدَدْنَٰهَا وَأَلْقَيْنَا فِيهَا رَوَٰسِىَ وَأَنۢبَتْنَا فِيهَا مِن كُلِّ زَوْجٍۭ بَهِيجٍ

*Wa'l-arḍa madadnāhā wa alqaynā fīhā rawāsiya wa anbatnā fīhā min kulli zawjin bahīj*

"And the earth: We spread it out, cast firm mountains upon it, and made every delightful kind grow in it."

### Word order: the object brought forward

*Wa'l-**arḍa** madadnāhā* — "the earth, We spread it out". The object comes before the verb and is repeated at the end of the verb by its pronoun. In Arabic this structure is called *ishtighāl*, and it does two things: it puts **stress on the object**, and it sets up a **contrast** with the preceding clause.

**The contrast is this:** the previous verse spoke of the sky, this one of the earth. By saying "and the earth", the sentence sets the two against one another.

### مَدَدْنَٰهَا — root م-د-د

To stretch, to draw out, to spread. *Madd* — to lengthen a thing by pulling it; *imdād* — to extend support; *midād* — ink (the lengthening trace of a pen).

**A note is needed on the expression that the earth was "spread out"**, because throughout history attempts have been made to turn this expression into a proof that the earth is flat.

**The verse does not give a description of a shape; it describes a function.** *Madd* is to make a thing walkable, liveable, plantable. The word itself shows this: the same root is used for the laying of a table and the spreading of a rug.

**And this must be added:** the surface of a sphere large enough already looks spread out to one standing on it. The addressee of the verse is where his feet stand, and the verse speaks from that point of view. Elsewhere the Qurʾān uses of the same earth a root meaning *"He shaped it like an ostrich egg"* (*daḥā* — al-Nāziʿāt 79/30), and that word was treated separately in the section on al-Nāziʿāt.

**I draw no scientific miracle from this; I only record that the verse carries no claim about shape.**

### وَأَلْقَيْنَا — "We cast"

**Root: ل-ق-ي.** And, as recorded above, this is one of the most densely used roots in the sūra.

***Ilqāʾ*** — to let a thing go, to throw it, to place it. The verb describes the setting of the mountains as **casting**.

**The choice of word is striking:** not "we planted" or "we erected" but **"we cast"**. Not a verb of raising a structure but a verb of placing. Like something let go from above.

**And the same verb will be used of a human being in the twenty-fourth verse:** *alqiyā fī jahannam* — "cast into Hell". **The same root, two objects: a mountain and a person.** I record this as an observation; I do not claim that the word is building a deliberate link.

### رَوَٰسِىَ — root ر-س-و

**This root was treated in the section on al-Nāziʿāt; I do not repeat it.** The summary of the note there:

> *"To fix, to set in place, to drop anchor. […] The nautical sense of the word is decisive: *irsāʾ* is a ship's dropping of anchor. The bringing to a halt of something that moves."*

**And the link built in al-Nāziʿāt was this:** the same root is used both of the mountains (*arsāhā*, 79/32) and of the Hour's "time of anchoring" (*mursāhā*, 79/42).

**Here I add what belongs to Qāf.**

**The word here is the plural of the active participle: *rawāsī* — "those that have dropped anchor", "those that stand fixed".** The mountain, that is, is named not by its name but by **its state**. The verse does not say "mountains"; it says **"those that stand fixed"**.

**And the nautical sense of the root is particularly alive here**, because the verb comes together with *alqaynā* (we cast). Two verbs used for **dropping anchor** stand side by side: to cast, and to be made fast.

**The image is this, and it follows from the root sense the lexicographers give:** the earth is pictured as a ship with weight let down upon it. The mountains are the anchors cast into it.

**This is an image; it is not a geological claim.** I do not try to match the verse with modern accounts of the role of mountains in the earth's crust — that road is the forcing that the method (`STYLE.md`) forbids. What is recorded is the picture the word carries: **the fixing of something that could otherwise move.**

### وَأَنۢبَتْنَا فِيهَا مِن كُلِّ زَوْجٍۭ بَهِيجٍ

**أَنۢبَتْنَا — root ن-ب-ت.** To make grow, to bring plants forth. The verb is in the *ifʿāl* form: **it is God who makes grow**, not the soil. This distinction was treated in detail in the four proofs in the section on al-Wāqiʿa: the human act is "to sow", the divine act is "to make grow".

**زَوْج — root ز-و-ج.** This root was treated in several sections (al-Raḥmān, al-Nabaʾ, al-Wāqiʿa, al-Takwīr); I do not repeat it. Its summary: *zawj* is not "a pair" by itself but **one of a pair** — the one that has a mate. It does not correspond exactly to the English "pair".

**This verse was already mentioned in the section on al-Nabaʾ**, where it was given as an example of the word's use in the sense of "kind, class" (*"We made fine pairs grow from the plants"* — Luqmān 31/10; Qāf 50/7).

**What is to be added here is this: both readings are possible and both are meaningful.**

| Reading | What *zawj* means | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **A pair / a mate** | That plants are male and female | The order of reproduction; the mechanism of coming into being again after death |
| **A kind / a class** | Of every sort | The breadth of variety |

**Both are transmitted in the classical commentaries and neither cancels the other.** The context brings the second slightly forward (the expression *min kulli* — "of every one" — suits variety better), but the first is not against the text either.

**A note:** that sexual reproduction exists in plants is a fact known today, but I do not draw from the verse a "scientific fact announced in advance". The sense of "kind" for the word *zawj* is already common in Arabic, and the verse is open to both senses at once. **To pick one of a word's two senses and build a miracle on it is the procedure that the method (`STYLE.md`) forbids.**

### بَهِيج — root ب-ه-ج

**Its concrete sense: a beauty that gladdens when it is seen.** *Bahja* — joy, gladness; and the ease that the sight of a thing gives. *Ibtihāj* — rejoicing.

**Here is the distinguishing side of the word: *bahīj* describes beauty not in itself but *in its effect on the one who looks*.** It does not say "beautiful"; it says **"what gladdens a person"**.

**And this is directly connected with the next verse.** The eighth verse will say *"a making-to-see and a reminder"*. The proof, that is, has to do with the one who looks. The plant is named not for its beauty but for **what it does in the one who looks at it**.

**The Qurʾān uses this word once more in the same context:**

> *"…We sent down water; with it We made gardens of delight (*ḥadāʾiqa **dhāta bahja***) grow."* (al-Naml 27/60)

And there too the question follows immediately: *"Is there a god besides God?"* **In both verses the beauty of a plant is used as a proof.**

**And there is one further overlap, this time word for word:** the last clause of al-Ḥajj 22/5 is ***وَأَنۢبَتَتْ مِن كُلِّ زَوْجٍۭ بَهِيجٍ*** — **exactly the same wording** as the expression in Qāf 50/7 (only the subject of the verb has changed: there the earth makes grow, here "We made grow"). **And the verse in al-Ḥajj is tied to the same conclusion:** 22/6, which follows immediately, says *"…and He gives life to the dead"*.

**The same expression, that is, stands inside the same proof in both sūras.** I record this as an observation; I do not claim that there is a deliberate reference between the two sūras.

## 50/8 — تَبْصِرَةً وَذِكْرَىٰ لِكُلِّ عَبْدٍ مُّنِيبٍ

*Tabṣiratan wa dhikrā li-kulli ʿabdin munīb*

"As a making-to-see and a reminder for every servant who turns."

### The proofs are cut off and the purpose is stated

In the sixth and seventh verses objects were being listed. Here a **statement of purpose** comes all at once: what these are for.

**And in the ninth verse the listing will go on from where it left off.** This verse, that is, is a parenthesis set in the middle of the list.

**In terms of word order both words are in the accusative**, and the exegetes differ over the reason: is it *mafʿūl lah* (in order that…), *mafʿūl muṭlaq*, or *ḥāl*? **The difference in sense is not large**; all three analyses arrive at the same result: what has been made has a purpose, and the purpose is the person who looks.

### تَبْصِرَة — making-to-see

**Root: ب-ص-ر.** To see — but, as recorded in the section on al-Ḥadīd, **to see by distinguishing.** From the same root comes *baṣīra* (insight, discernment).

**The pattern of the word is decisive: *tafʿila* (تَفْعِلَة).** This is the verbal noun of the *tafʿīl* form, and in Arabic the *tafʿīl* form conveys **transitivity or causation**.

**So *tabṣira* is not "seeing" but "making to see".**

| Pattern | Sense |
|---|---|
| *baṣar* | Sight |
| *ibṣār* | Coming to see |
| ***tabṣira*** | **Making one see, bringing to notice by showing** |

**This matches exactly the command in the sixth verse:** there it was said "have they not looked". Here it is said **why** the things looked at are there: to make one see.

**And one further note:** the word names not the product of the look but the opportunity provided for it. **The making-to-see may have been done; the seeing may not have taken place.** This is exactly the situation in the second verse.

### وَذِكْرَىٰ — a reminder

**Root: ذ-ك-ر.** To remember, to mention. *Dhikrā* — a reminding; it carries both the verbal noun and an intensity.

**And now we come to the point of the two words being set side by side.**

*Tabṣira* and *dhikrā* are not synonyms. **They name two different mental operations:**

| | ***tabṣira*** | ***dhikrā*** |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | It shows | It reminds |
| To whom | To one who never noticed | To one who knew and forgot |
| What it gives | New knowledge | The return of old knowledge |
| Its time | For the first time | Again |

**The verse says, that is, that the same scene does two different things to two different people.** For the one who has never thought, the sky is a discovery; for the one who thought and forgot, it is a warning.

**I give this distinction as my own reading**, but its support is the difference in the root senses of the two words, and that difference is plain in the lexicographers.

**And this pair will return within the sūra:** in the thirty-seventh verse it will be said *"In that… there is a *dhikrā*"*, and there too two separate routes will be described — the one who has a heart, and the one who gives his ear. **In both places one single thing is offered through two separate channels.** I record this as a pattern.

### لِكُلِّ عَبْدٍ مُّنِيبٍ — a condition is set

**And here the verse draws the limit of the proofs.**

The making-to-see and the reminder are not **for everyone**; they are *"for every servant who turns"*.

**At first sight this looks as though it weakens the force of the proof.** If a proof is a proof only for the one who sees when he looks, is it a proof?

**But what is said is something else, and it must be read carefully.** The verse does not say "only a believer can see". The word *munīb* names not a belief but a **movement**.

**مُنِيب — root ن-و-ب.** Its concrete sense: **to turn, to come to a place again and again.** *Nawba* — a turn, a watch (what comes round again and again). *Nāʾiba* — a misfortune that comes round again. *Ināba* — turning towards, returning.

**At the centre of the root is repetition:** the word *nawba* — borrowed into Turkish as *nöbet*, a turn of duty — is from this root: a turn **coming round and round again.**

**So *munīb* is not "one who believes" but "one who turns".** The one who turns his gaze, who turns his face, who turns towards.

**That is the condition, and it is reasonable:** a proof shows nothing to one who does not look. The sky is closed to the person who never lifts his head — but that is not a fault in the sky.

**And it must be noted that the word will return in the thirty-third verse:** *"…and whoever comes with a heart that turns (*bi-qalbin munīb*)."* **Whatever the attribute of the person who can see the proof in the eighth verse, that is the attribute of the person who enters the Garden in the thirty-third.** The same word.

**This is a repetition that can be verified within the sūra**, and it says this: the condition at the beginning and the recompense at the end are tied to the same attribute.

### عَبْد — a servant

**That the word comes indefinite here is meaningful:** *ʿabdin munīb* — "a servant who turns". No particular community, no particular religion, no particular people is named.

**And together with *kull*: *li-kulli ʿabdin munīb* — "for every servant who turns".** The door is opened by attribute, not by identity. **This is the textual counterpart of the principle written down in the method (`STYLE.md`):** what the verse describes are attributes; whoever bears that attribute is included in it.

## 50/9 — وَنَزَّلْنَا مِنَ ٱلسَّمَآءِ مَآءً مُّبَٰرَكًا فَأَنۢبَتْنَا بِهِۦ جَنَّٰتٍ وَحَبَّ ٱلْحَصِيدِ

*Wa nazzalnā mina's-samāʾi māʾan mubārakan fa-anbatnā bihī jannātin wa ḥabba'l-ḥaṣīd*

"And We sent down blessed water from the sky; with it We made gardens grow, and grain for the harvest."

### نَزَّلْنَا — a difference of pattern

**Root: ن-ز-ل.** To come down, to send down.

**The verb is in the *tafʿīl* form: *nazzala*, not *anzala*.** In Arabic the *tafʿīl* form very often conveys **multiplicity, repetition or gradualness**.

The distinction the lexicographers record between these two patterns is this: *inzāl* states a sending down all at once, *tanzīl* a sending down piece by piece or again and again. **This distinction does not work sharply everywhere**, and the Qurʾān sometimes uses the two patterns interchangeably; but the use of the *tanzīl* pattern for rain fits the context: **rain comes down not once but again and again.**

### مَّآءً مُّبَٰرَكًا — blessed water

**بَرَكَة — root ب-ر-ك.** The root came up in the sections on al-Mulk and al-Raḥmān.

**Its concrete sense is worth dwelling on.** The lexicographers open the root thus:

- **بَرَكَ الجَمَل** — the camel knelt, folded its knees and settled on the ground.
- **بِرْكَة** — a pool, a standing body of water; the place where water **stays and remains**.
- **بَرَكَة** — good that increases and **abides**.

**At the centre of the root there are two things: settling and increasing.** Blessing is not what is much; it is **what is much and stays.** A plenty that comes and goes is not called blessing.

**And that the water is named "blessed" agrees exactly with this sense:** the water comes down, remains in the soil, and continues there by increasing. Not a thing that comes once and is over, but a thing that starts a lasting chain of production.

### فَأَنۢبَتْنَا بِهِۦ — the chain of causes

**The particle فَ** conveys sequence: We sent down, and **thereafter** We made grow. And **بِهِ** (with it) points to the means.

**The verse, that is, does not hide the intermediate cause.** In saying "We made grow" it does not skip over the water; it says **"with it We made grow"**. The existence of causes is granted, but whose hand the cause is in is also stated.

**This is the same logic as the four proofs in the section on al-Wāqiʿa**, where it was recorded thus: *"the sūra does not say 'you do nothing'. It distinguishes what it is that you do."* What is distinguished in Qāf is not the human act but the act of **the water**: the water is an instrument, not an agent.

### جَنَّٰتٍ وَحَبَّ ٱلْحَصِيدِ — two kinds of plant

**جَنَّة — root ج-ن-ن.** To cover, to conceal. *Janna* is a garden whose trees interlace and **cover the soil**. From the same root come *jinn* (the unseen), *janīn* (what is covered in the womb), *majnūn* (one whose mind is covered).

**حَبّ — grain, seed.** **ٱلْحَصِيد — root ح-ص-د:** to reap, to harvest.

**And here again the *faʿīl* pattern carries a passive sense:** *ḥaṣīd* — "what is reaped". The question of the pattern recorded for *ḥafīẓ* in the fourth verse is at work here too, but here the sense is not in dispute: grain is what is reaped, not what reaps.

**The setting of the two objects side by side draws a distinction:**

| | ***jannāt*** | ***ḥabbu'l-ḥaṣīd*** |
|---|---|---|
| What | Gardens, trees | Grain |
| Its lifetime | It stands for years | One season |
| Its end | It bears fruit and remains | **It is reaped** |
| To a person | Shade, fruit | Staple food |

**And the second is named by its end.** The grain is called neither "wheat" nor "crop" but **"what is to be reaped"**.

**I give this as my own reading, and its connection with the subject of the sūra is plain:** the sūra is about death and resurrection. And here one of the plants is named with the stress on its being **cut down**. In the next verse the date palm will be named with the stress on its **rising**. **The reaped and the rising: two directions side by side.**

I record this link without forcing it; the verse does not say that it is building such a link.

## 50/10 — وَٱلنَّخْلَ بَاسِقَٰتٍ لَّهَا طَلْعٌ نَّضِيدٌ

*Wa'n-nakhla bāsiqātin lahā ṭalʿun naḍīd*

"And the tall date palms, with clustered spathes ranged one above another."

### Why the date palm in particular?

When the Qurʾān enumerates plants it names the date palm very often, and there is a concrete reason for this: **the palm is the tree at the centre of the addressee's life.** Food, shade, timber, fibre, fuel — all come from it. In the desert a palm tree is the sign of a water source and of a settlement.

**The example, that is, is not one chosen from far away.** It is what is visible from the addressee's window.

### بَاسِقَٰت — root ب-س-ق

**Its concrete sense: to run up tall, to shoot up.** *Basaqa'n-nakhl* — the palm grew long, it rose. The lexicographers use the word especially for a height that goes upward **without branching**.

**The striking side of the word is how exactly it fits the way a palm grows.** A palm does not branch sideways; it goes up as a single trunk and its leaves gather at the top. **The word names the shape of the tree.**

**And this sets up an echo with *fawqahum* (above them) in the sixth verse:** there the head had to be lifted in order to look at the sky; here too the look goes upward. **In this section of the sūra the look keeps changing direction:** up (the sky) → down (the earth) → from above downward (the rain) → from below upward (the palm) → out of the ground (the reviving of dead soil).

**I record this movement as an observation.**

### طَلْع — the spathe cluster

**Root: ط-ل-ع.** To come out, to rise, to appear. *Ṭulūʿ* — the rising of the sun; *maṭlaʿ* — the place of rising; *iṭṭilāʿ* — to climb up onto a thing and see it, to become informed.

**طَلْع** is the palm's flower or fruit cluster in **its first emerging state**: inside its sheath, packed close, not yet opened.

**And the root of the word is exactly "what comes out".** The name of the fruit, that is, comes from the verb of coming out.

**One verse later the sūra will say: *kadhālika'l-khurūj* — "such is the coming forth".** And *khurūj* too means coming out.

**The section, that is, uses the idea of "coming out" with two different roots in two consecutive verses: ط-ل-ع and خ-ر-ج.** I record this as an observation; I do not claim that the two words are building a deliberate preparation, but the sequence stands in the text.

**Moreover, the same root will come once more at the end of the sūra:** *qabla **ṭulūʿi**'sh-shams* (39) — before the rising of the sun. **The same root: the coming out of the fruit and the rising of the sun.**

### نَّضِيد — root ن-ض-د

**Its concrete sense: to range one upon another in an ordered way.** *Naḍada* — he stacked, he ranged in order. *Naḍīd* — ranged, stacked. *Minḍada* — cushions laid one on top of another.

**Again the *faʿīl* pattern, again a passive sense:** *naḍīd* — "ranged".

**And the choice of word is botanically apt and requires no forcing:** the palm cluster called *ṭalʿ* is made of hundreds of small flowers ranged **closely and in order** inside the sheath. When the sheath splits, that order becomes visible.

**The verse, that is, describes the tree from outside (tall) and then looks inside it (ranged).** What is seen from a distance: height. What is seen on coming close: order.

**This is the same logic as the three-part sequence in the sixth verse:** structure → adornment → flawlessness. Here too: structure (height) → inner order (being ranged).

**The Qurʾān uses this word once more in a description of the Garden:** *"…and **ranged** banana trees"* (al-Wāqiʿa 56/29 — *ṭalḥin **manḍūd***; from the same root). **That word was treated in the section on al-Wāqiʿa.** The same root, that is, is used both for the cluster of a palm in this world and for the fruits of a tree in the Garden.

## 50/11 — رِّزْقًا لِّلْعِبَادِ ۖ وَأَحْيَيْنَا بِهِۦ بَلْدَةً مَّيْتًا ۚ كَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْخُرُوجُ

*Rizqan li'l-ʿibād; wa aḥyaynā bihī baldatan maytā; kadhālika'l-khurūj*

"As provision for the servants. And with it We revived a dead land. Such is the coming forth."

### The proof closes — and is tied up in a single sentence

**This verse is the conclusion of the section that has run since the sixth verse, and it closes in three steps:**

1. *Rizqan li'l-ʿibād* — **the face this whole list of plants turns towards a human being**.
2. *Wa aḥyaynā bihī baldatan maytā* — what the same water does **to what is dead**.
3. *Kadhālika'l-khurūj* — **the conclusion.**

### رِّزْقًا لِّلْعِبَادِ

**رِزْق — root ر-ز-ق.** **The giving** to a living being of what it needs and will benefit from. In the root there is not "earning" but "being given".

**The word is in the accusative (*mafʿūl lah*):** "so that it may be provision". After the purpose given in the eighth verse (*tabṣiratan wa dhikrā*), that is, a second purpose comes.

**When the two purposes are set side by side something becomes visible:**

| Verse | Purpose | For whom |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | *tabṣira wa dhikrā* | For the servant who turns |
| 11 | *rizq* | **For the servants** (without qualification) |

**In the eighth there was a condition (*munīb*), in the eleventh there is none.** Provision belongs to everyone; understanding opens to the one who turns.

**I give this distinction as my own reading**, but the difference of qualification in the two verses stands in the text: in one *li-kulli ʿabdin **munīb***, in the other only *li'l-**ʿibād***.

### وَأَحْيَيْنَا بِهِۦ بَلْدَةً مَّيْتًا

**And now the subject changes all at once.** What has been described up to here was abundance: gardens, grain, the palm, provision. In this clause the same water is applied to something **dead**.

**بَلْدَة — root ب-ل-د.** A land, a settlement, a piece of ground. In the root there is "attachment to a place, being settled".

**A point of grammar: *baldatan* is feminine, while its adjective *maytā* has come masculine.** The expected form would have been *mayyita*.

The lexicographers explain this in two ways:

| Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|
| **The *mayt* pattern is common to both genders** | In *faʿl* and similar adjective patterns the masculine-feminine distinction may not be drawn; *mayt* is used for both masculine and feminine |
| **The word supplied is masculine** | What is meant by *baldatan* is *makān* or *mawḍiʿ*; the adjective has followed the sense |

**Both explanations are transmitted and neither changes the meaning.** What should be recorded is that this is not an error but a known flexibility of Arabic.

### كَذَٰلِكَ ٱلْخُرُوجُ — the axis of the sūra

**Three words. And the whole proof of the sūra is tied here.**

**خُرُوج — root خ-ر-ج.** To come out, to go forth. From the same root come *khārij* (outside), *ikhrāj* (to bring out), *kharāj* (the produce that comes out of the soil and the tax taken on it).

**The word is *al-khurūj* — definite.** Not "a coming forth" but **"that coming forth"**. The thing the addressee has been talking about. The thing he called "far" in the third verse.

**Now let us open the structure of the proof step by step, because the force of this verse lies in its structure.**

**One. The same verb is used in two places.** The coming out of a plant from the soil and the coming out of a human being from the soil are named not with two separate words but **with the same word**. The verse does not build a comparison ("the resurrection resembles this"); **it gives the same name.**

**Two. The demonstrative looks backwards.** *Kadhālika* — "just like this". And "this" is what was described a moment ago: the greening of dead soil. **The proof, that is, is grounded in something the addressee has seen.**

**Three. Not an analogy but the showing of an instance.** A rational analogy is built like this: "if A is possible, B is possible too, because both are of the same kind." That is not what is done here. What is done is to point to an event the addressee **already accepts** and to give the second event its name.

**Four. It goes down to the objection's own ground.** The objection was: *"when we are dead and have become dust?"* What the objection rested on, that is, was **mixing with the soil.** And the answer is precisely about soil: **this is what soil does — it takes things in and then gives them out again.**

**Five. And the ordinariness is part of the proof.** The greening of dead soil is not an extraordinary event; it happens every year. **This is exactly where the force of the proof lies:** had it been presented as an extraordinary event, the addressee could have said "I have not seen that either". Because it is ordinary, it cannot be objected to.

**And the ordinariness itself raises a question:** how can a thing's happening every year put it into the category of "impossible"?

**I present these five points as my own analysis**; the verse does not enumerate these steps, it says it in one sentence.

### The Qurʾān explained by the Qurʾān

This link is one that recurs in the Qurʾān, and it is made explicitly in several places:

- *"As We revived dead soil. Such is the coming forth."* — the verse before us.
- *"…We give life to a dead land. Such is the coming forth (*khurūj*)."* (al-Zukhruf 43/11, with a similar expression: *kadhālika tukhrajūn*).
- *"It is He who sends the winds… We drive them to a dead land and with them revive the earth after its death. **Such is the raising too.**"* (Fāṭir 35/9 — *kadhālika'n-nushūr*).
- *"You see the earth barren; when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells and makes every fine kind grow"* (al-Ḥajj 22/5) — **and immediately afterwards:** *"…and He gives life to the dead"* (al-Ḥajj 22/6). **As was recorded in the treatment of the seventh verse, the last expression of al-Ḥajj 22/5 is word for word the same as Qāf 50/7.**

**This repetition shows the main form of the Qurʾān's proof of the resurrection:** not an abstract debate about possibility, but **the observation of the seasons.**

### What this says to the present

**The most serious objection this proof meets today is the objection "we now know how it happens".**

The germination of a seed is today an explicable process: water, enzymes, cell division. Nothing about it remains unknown.

**But the proof of the verse did not rest on the unknown.** The verse does not say "you do not know how this happens, therefore God exists". What it says is this: **this happens.** What was dead comes alive — whether or not the mechanism has been explained, this is what happens.

**And the objection was not about the mechanism either.** The objector was saying "there is no coming back after becoming dust"; that is, he was claiming **a categorical impossibility**. The verse answers that claim: **the category is already occupied.** A living thing coming out of a dead one is not an idea without a counterpart in nature.

**That the mechanism has been explained does not weaken this answer.** On the contrary: its having been explained confirms the reality of the event.

## 50/12-14 — كَذَّبَتْ قَبْلَهُمْ قَوْمُ نُوحٍ وَأَصْحَٰبُ ٱلرَّسِّ وَثَمُودُ ۝ وَعَادٌ وَفِرْعَوْنُ وَإِخْوَٰنُ لُوطٍ ۝ وَأَصْحَٰبُ ٱلْأَيْكَةِ وَقَوْمُ تُبَّعٍ ۚ كُلٌّ كَذَّبَ ٱلرُّسُلَ فَحَقَّ وَعِيدِ

*Kadhdhabat qablahum qawmu Nūḥin wa aṣḥābu'r-Rassi wa Thamūd — wa ʿĀdun wa Firʿawnu wa ikhwānu Lūṭ — wa aṣḥābu'l-Aykati wa qawmu Tubbaʿ; kullun kadhdhaba'r-rusula fa-ḥaqqa waʿīd*

"Before them the people of Noah (Nūḥ), the people of al-Rass and Thamūd denied. And ʿĀd, and Pharaoh, and the brethren of Lot (Lūṭ). And the people of al-Ayka and the people of Tubbaʿ. All of them denied the messengers, and My threat came true."

### What is this list doing?

**I shall not expound at length, because the verses themselves do not expound at length.**

Eight peoples are listed in three verses with no detail given at all. Not who they were, not what they did, not what befell them. **Only names.**

**And this shows the function of the list.** The Qurʾān tells the stories of these peoples at length in other sūras — al-Aʿrāf, Hūd and al-Shuʿarāʾ are full of them. **Here there is no narrative; there is a count.**

**The function of the list is this:**

**One. To show that the objection is not new.** In the second verse it was said *"an astonishing thing"*. The list shows how many times the same astonishment has been repeated. What is said to the addressee, that is: **what you are saying is not being said for the first time.**

**Two. To build multiplicity where a single instance could be called coincidence.** If one people has been destroyed, the reason can be argued about. If eight peoples have come to the same end, what is being argued about is a pattern.

**Three. To make the passage from the section of proofs to the section of verdict.** Verses six to eleven brought their proof from **nature**. Verses twelve to fourteen bring it from **history**. Both are fields of observation: one is looked at today, the other happened in the past.

**Four. Geographical and temporal breadth.** The list contains Mesopotamia (Noah), Egypt (Pharaoh), northern Arabia (Thamūd), southern Arabia (Tubbaʿ), the Jordan-Palestine line (Lot). **The examples, that is, are not from a single region.**

### An observation about the order

**The list is not chronological.** Thamūd, for instance, is named before ʿĀd — whereas the Qurʾān generally counts ʿĀd before Thamūd.

**And there may be a reason for this: the verse-ending.** The twelfth verse ends with *Thamūd* (*ū* + *d*), the thirteenth with *Lūṭ* (*ū* + *ṭ*). **Since every verse of the sūra ends with a long vowel plus a single consonant, the placing of the names has to fit that pattern of sound.**

**I record this as an observation, not as a ruling.** I cannot say for certain that the sound pattern settled the order; but that the two names at the end of the two verses sit exactly on the sound pattern stands in the text.

### إِخْوَٰنُ لُوطٍ — "the brethren of Lot"

**This expression is striking.** The Qurʾān generally names this people *qawmu Lūṭ* (the people of Lot). Here **ikhwān** (brethren) is used.

**What might the function of the word be?**

In Arabic *akh* (brother) is used not only for a blood tie but also for **membership of the same community**. The Qurʾān uses this word when it sends prophets to their peoples: *"To ʿĀd [We sent] their brother Hūd"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/65), *"to Thamūd their brother Ṣāliḥ"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/73).

**The word, that is, conveys the closeness between a messenger and those he addresses.**

**And it has a link with the second verse:** there it was stressed that the warner was *minhum* (from among them). Here the same closeness is named from the other side: **they denied one who was their brother.**

**I give this as my own reading**; other explanations of the expression are also transmitted in the classical commentaries.

### أَصْحَٰبُ ٱلرَّسِّ — the people of al-Rass

**Here honesty is required: there is no certain knowledge about this people.**

They occur in two places in the Qurʾān (here and al-Furqān 25/38), and **in both places only the name is given.** Who they were, where they lived, which prophet they denied, what befell them, is not stated.

**The lexical sense of the word is known:** *rass* — **a well**, especially one not lined with stone; and **a dug pit**. As a verb, *rassa* — to bury a thing, to put it in a pit.

**Many explanations are transmitted in the commentaries:** that they took this name because they threw their prophet into a well, that they lived in a particular region, that they were addressed by a particular prophet, and so on. **I do not transmit any of these explanations as reports**, because I cannot issue a ruling on their soundness and they conflict with one another.

**What can be said is this:** the root of the name calls up a **pit** or a **well**; the rest is unknown.

### قَوْمُ تُبَّعٍ — the people of Tubbaʿ

**Here too the same honesty is required.**

**تُبَّع** is not a proper name but a **title.** In historical records it is known as the title borne by the Ḥimyarite rulers who held sway in southern Arabia (Yemen). The word is from the root **ت-ب-ع**: to follow, to come after — that is, "the one who is followed".

**The Qurʾān names this people in two places** (here and al-Dukhān 44/37) and **gives no detail in either.** In the occurrence in al-Dukhān there is an expression comparing the people with those who came before them.

**Who Tubbaʿ was, which Tubbaʿ he was, how his people were destroyed, is not stated.** In the commentaries and the histories there are accounts about the rulers who bore the title Tubbaʿ; some of these carry favourable judgements about Tubbaʿ himself and unfavourable ones about his people. **Since I cannot vouch for the soundness of these accounts, I do not transmit them.**

**What can be recorded is this:** Yemen is the southern end of the Meccans' trade route, and the great kingdoms there are history they knew. **The list, that is, names the great power both to the north and to the south of the addressee.**

### أَصْحَٰبُ ٱلْأَيْكَةِ — the people of al-Ayka

**أَيْكَة** — a dense thicket, scrub, a grove. The Qurʾān names this people in several places (such as al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/176 and Ṣād 38/13) and associates them with the peoples to whom Shuʿayb was sent.

**Whether they are the same people as Madyan or a different one is disputed**, and I adopt no preference in this commentary. The Qurʾān sometimes names the two by separate names.

### كُلٌّ كَذَّبَ ٱلرُّسُلَ — "all of them denied the messengers"

**This clause has a point of word order, and it matters.**

Each of the eight peoples listed had denied **one** messenger: the people of Noah denied Noah, Thamūd denied Ṣāliḥ, ʿĀd denied Hūd.

**But the clause uses the plural: *ar-rusul* — "the messengers".**

**In the classical commentaries the explanation is given thus:** whoever denies one messenger has denied the institution of messengership, because all of them brought the same thing. **To reject one is to reject the measure.**

**And this has a consequence:** instead of charging the peoples separately, the verse ties them to **one common act**. Eight different places, eight different times, one single act.

### فَحَقَّ وَعِيدِ — "My threat came true"

**حَقَّ — root ح-ق-ق.** To be established, to come to pass, to be true. The verb here means "it found its place, it became real".

**وَعِيد — root و-ع-د.** To promise. *Waʿd* is generally used of something good, while ***waʿīd*** is **a word given about something bad** — a threat.

**And the final *yāʾ* of the word has dropped:** *waʿīd* instead of *waʿīdī* (my threat). This is an elision frequently seen in the Qurʾān for the sake of the verse-ending, and it is dealt with in the sources on the variant readings.

**The word occurs five times in the sūra** (*waʿīd* in 14, 20, 28 and 45; *tūʿadūn* in 32), and **its first and last occurrences come in the same form, without the *yāʾ*:**

| Verse | Expression |
|---|---|
| **14** | *fa-ḥaqqa **waʿīd*** — my threat came true |
| 20 | *dhālika yawmu'l-**waʿīd*** — the day of the threat |
| 28 | *qaddamtu ilaykum bi'l-**waʿīd*** — I sent the threat ahead to you |
| **45** | *man yakhāfu **waʿīd*** — whoever fears my threat |

**In the fourteenth verse the threat is named as something already fulfilled in the past; in the forty-fifth as something still to be feared.** The same word, once history, once future.

**I record this as an observation.**

## 50/15 — أَفَعَيِينَا بِٱلْخَلْقِ ٱلْأَوَّلِ ۚ بَلْ هُمْ فِى لَبْسٍ مِّنْ خَلْقٍ جَدِيدٍ

*A-fa-ʿayīnā bi'l-khalqi'l-awwal? Bal hum fī labsin min khalqin jadīd*

"Were We wearied by the first creation? No — they are in a confusion about a new creation."

### The second form of the proof

Verses six to eleven were a proof from **observation**. Twelve to fourteen from **history**. This verse asks, for the first time, **a question of reason**.

**And the question takes this form: can the one who was able not do it again?**

### عَيِينَا — root ع-ي-ي

**Its concrete sense: to be unable to carry out a task, to grow tired and get stuck, to be at a loss.**

- **عَيِيَ بِالأَمْرِ** — he could not manage the task, he was not equal to it.
- **أَعْيَا** — he wore out, he exhausted; and he grew tired.
- **عَيّ** — to stick in speech, to be unable to get one's tongue round it. *ʿAyiyy* — a stammerer, one who cannot make himself understood.

**At the centre of the root is *blockage*:** whether in work or in speech. Something is begun and it **will not go forward.**

**And this word is the first of two questions that will be asked in this sūra.**

### The fifteenth verse and the thirty-eighth: the same claim in two forms

**This is one of the most striking internal links of the sūra, and it can be verified:**

| Verse | Expression | Form |
|---|---|---|
| **15** | *a-fa-**ʿayīnā** bi'l-khalqi'l-awwal* | **A question** — "were We wearied?" |
| **38** | *wa mā massanā min **lughūb*** | **A statement** — "no weariness touched Us" |

**Both verses say that creating brings God no burden.** One by asking a question, the other by stating it outright. And both are built with two different roots for the same idea: **ع-ي-ي** (to be unable) and **ل-غ-ب** (to grow weary).

**The thirty-eighth verse will complete this claim.** I shall recall this link there.

### بِٱلْخَلْقِ ٱلْأَوَّلِ — the first creation

**The argument is this, and it is repeated in many places in the Qurʾān:** the first creation has already happened and no one is objecting to it. The objector too exists, has been born, is alive.

**What is under dispute, that is, is not a *possibility* but a *repetition*.** And for one who has done a work once, doing the same work a second time brings no new difficulty.

**The Qurʾān formulates this explicitly elsewhere:**

> *"It is He who begins creation, then repeats it; **and that is easier for Him**."* (al-Rūm 30/27)

**And there is a note in that verse that must be attended to:** the phrase "easier" is not a gradation from God's side but speaking **by a human measure**. The same verse continues: *"To Him belongs the highest attribute in the heavens and the earth."*

**In this commentary the following position is adopted:** no distinction between "easy" and "hard" is drawn with respect to God; such expressions speak in the addressee's measure. Indeed the forty-fourth verse of this sūra uses the same language: *"that is a gathering easy for Us"* (*ḥashrun ʿalaynā yasīr*).

### لَبْس — root ل-ب-س

**Its concrete sense: to clothe, to cover.** *Libās* — a garment; *lubs* — to put on.

**And from this the second sense arises: to make a thing indistinct by covering it over.** *Labs* — confusion, indistinctness. *Iltibās* — being mixed up together. *Talbīs* — covering something over so as to mislead.

**The link is plain:** what is clothed becomes invisible. **Indistinctness, in Arabic, is named with a verb of covering.**

**This root was treated in the section on al-Nabaʾ;** I rely on the note there and do not repeat it.

**What belongs to Qāf is this:** the verse does not call their state "denial". **It says *labs* — that is, "a state of covering they are inside".**

**And the expression is striking: *hum fī labsin* — "they are *inside* an indistinctness". The indistinctness is described not as an opinion they hold but as a place they are inside**.

**I give this as my own reading:** the word implies that the matter is not a lack of knowledge but a matter of **position**. The person inside the covering, unable to see outside, thinks there is no outside.

**And this agrees with the diagnosis of the second verse:** there too it was said that the objection rested not on a proof but on a state (astonishment).

### A third بَلْ upon the second بَلْ

The particle *bal* occurs three times in the sūra: 2 (*bal ʿajibū*), 5 (*bal kadhdhabū*), 15 (*bal hum fī labs*).

**All three do the same work: they place a diagnosis on the state of the other side.**

| Verse | Diagnosis | Layer |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | *ʿajibū* — they were astonished | Reaction |
| 5 | *kadhdhabū* — they denied | Verdict |
| 15 | *fī labs* — they are in indistinctness | State |

**Read together, the three diagnoses yield a sequence, and I give this as my own reading:** first the visible reaction, then the verdict passed, then the real state beneath the verdict. **The sūra descends from outside inward.**

**A similar movement was identified in the section on al-Qalam** (the nine attributes descending from outside inward), and there this was transmitted from al-Fajr: *"The Qurʾān generally frames a diagnosis this way: it begins with the visible act and descends to the source."* **The three *bal*s in Qāf are a shorter example of the same method.**

## 50/16 — وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ وَنَعْلَمُ مَا تُوَسْوِسُ بِهِۦ نَفْسُهُۥ ۖ وَنَحْنُ أَقْرَبُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ حَبْلِ ٱلْوَرِيدِ

*Wa laqad khalaqna'l-insāna wa naʿlamu mā tuwaswisu bihī nafsuh; wa naḥnu aqrabu ilayhi min ḥabli'l-warīd*

"We created man, and We know what his soul whispers to him. We are nearer to him than his jugular vein."

### The change of scale

**Up to the fifteenth verse the subject was large:** the heavens, the earth, peoples, history, the first creation.

**In this verse the scale drops all at once to a single human being** — and not only to a human being but to his **inside.**

**This narrowing is part of the method of the sūra**, and a similar movement was recorded in the section on al-Ḥadīd: *"the large picture is set up, and then the addressee is placed inside the picture."*

**And the narrowing here is sharper still:** from the heavens to the jugular vein.

### وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ — the link between creating and knowing

**Note how the sentence is built:** the verse says first "We created", then "We know". **The two clauses are joined by a *wāw*.**

**And the order builds a ground:** knowing is the consequence of making. The one who makes knows what he has made.

**The Qurʾān makes this link explicitly elsewhere:**

> *"Would He who created not know? He is the subtle, the aware."* (al-Mulk 67/14)

**This verse was treated in the section on al-Mulk.** Qāf 50/16 gives the same inference not as a question but as **a statement**.

### مَا تُوَسْوِسُ بِهِۦ نَفْسُهُۥ — the whisper of the soul

**وَسْوَسَة — treated at length in the section on al-Nās; I do not repeat it.** The summary of the finding there:

> *"There is a class of words in Arabic: four-lettered, built by the repetition of the first two letters, and imitating a sound that is heard. […] **وسوسة** belongs to this class. The word is itself the sound of a whisper: *was-wa-sa*. A sound repeated twice, sibilant, soft, low. The moment you say it, it does the thing it imitates."*

**And in the section on al-Nās this verse was already mentioned**, with this finding:

> *"Here the whisperer is not Satan but a person's own soul. This verse shows that the source of whispering is not a single one."*

**Here I add two things that belong to Qāf.**

**One. The transitivity of the verb.** *Tuwaswisu **bihī*** — "whispers with it" / "whispers to him". As was recorded in the section on al-Nās, this verb takes different directions with different particles (*lahumā*, *ilayhi*). The particle **بِ** here brings forward the **content** of the whisper: the **thing** the soul whispers.

**And this sharpens what the verse says:** what is known is not the existence of the whisper but **its content.**

**Two. The verb is in the imperfect.** *Tuwaswisu* — that which it keeps whispering. What is described, that is, is not an event but **a continuous working.** There is a voice speaking incessantly inside a person, and the verse records this as an ordinary state.

**Its moral consequence was treated in the section on al-Nās and holds here too:** *"a thought's arising in your mind and your adopting it are not the same thing."* The verse does not name the existence of the whisper as a fault; it says that it is **known.**

### وَنَحْنُ أَقْرَبُ إِلَيْهِ — "We are nearer to him"

**And now we come to the most quoted and most misunderstood sentence of the sūra.**

**First the note of *tanzīh* must be set down, because this sentence cannot be read without it.**

**In the section on al-Ḥadīd 57/4 — *wa huwa maʿakum ayna mā kuntum* — this question was treated in detail.** The note there holds here word for word and is worth repeating:

> *"**What the verse says:** there is no place that can lie outside God's knowledge, watch and disposal. […] **What the verse does not say:** that God is beside you in space, bodily. *Maʿiyya* (being-with) is used in Arabic for more than physical side-by-sideness — and in the Qurʾān it is used so for the most part."*

**And there the textual evidence for this finding was given:** that same verse closed the being-with with *"He sees what you do"*, that is, it placed the being-with in a frame of **knowledge and watching**.

**In Qāf 50/16 the same evidence is stronger still**, because the expression of nearness comes **directly after a clause about knowing**:

1. *wa naʿlamu mā tuwaswisu bihī nafsuh* — **We know.**
2. *wa naḥnu aqrabu ilayhi min ḥabli'l-warīd* — **We are nearer.**

**The second clause is the ground of the first.** The nearness is stated in order to explain how the knowing is possible.

**The nearness in the verse, that is, is not spatial but in respect of knowledge.** This note is set down plainly in this commentary and rests on this principle: *"There is nothing like Him"* (al-Shūrā 42/11) — the same principle was recorded in the section on al-Fajr as well.

**In the classical commentaries this is put in several ways:**

| Explanation | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Nearness in respect of knowledge** | His knowledge penetrates to a person's most hidden state; distance is no obstacle to knowledge |
| **Nearness in respect of power** | His disposal precedes a person's own disposal over his own body |
| **The nearness of the angels** | Some exegetes read the nearness through the angels of verse 17 |

**The first two explanations complete one another and are widely adopted.** The third is also transmitted; that verse 17 follows immediately gives this reading some support, but since the wording of the verse ascribes the nearness directly to God, many exegetes find it weak. **I do not impose a preference.**

### مِنْ حَبْلِ ٱلْوَرِيدِ — the jugular vein

**Now we come to the word itself.**

**حَبْل — a rope, a cord, a bond.** The Qurʾān uses this word figuratively too: *ḥablun mina'llāh* (a rope from God — Āl ʿImrān 3/112), *ḥabli'llāh* (the rope of God — Āl ʿImrān 3/103).

**وَرِيد — root و-ر-د.** And the sense of the root is interesting:

- **وَرَدَ الماءَ** — he came to water, he went down to drink.
- **وُرُود** — to arrive at a place, especially at water.
- **مَوْرِد** — a watering place, the place arrived at.
- **وَارِد** — one who goes to fetch water; the water-carrier who goes ahead of a caravan.
- **وَرِيد** — the vessel in which blood **returns**.

**At the centre of the root are *going and arriving*** — especially at water. And the vein is named from this root because blood comes and goes like a liquid.

**This root came up in the section on al-Raḥmān**; I add to the note there.

### Anatomically, what is it?

**The lexicographers generally describe the *warīd* as a vessel in the neck** and say that there are two of them, running along the two sides of the neck. Some lexicographers record that it is connected to the heart, others that it spreads through the whole body.

**If a comparison with modern anatomical terms is needed:** on the two sides of the neck there are large veins connecting the head and the brain to the heart (*vena jugularis*). This is the place the classical lexicographers describe.

**But a note must be set down:** in classical Arabic the distinction between *warīd* and *shiryān* (artery) is not as sharp as it is today, and the lexicographers differ over the exact equivalent of the word. **For this reason I do not load a definite anatomical term onto the verse.** The settled English rendering is "the jugular vein", and that is what is used here.

**And this must be written plainly: the verse is not giving a lesson in anatomy.** The word is chosen in order to name something the addressee knows, and knows to be vital.

### The construct itself: "the cord of the vein"

**There is a question of word order.** *Ḥablu'l-warīd* — "the cord of the *warīd*". How is this construct to be analysed?

| Analysis | Its sense |
|---|---|
| **An explicative construct** | "The thing called *ḥabl*, that is, the *warīd*" — the vein itself is named as a cord |
| **A true genitive** | "The cord of the vein" — the bond that carries or makes up the vein |

**The first is more widely accepted** and has parallels in Arabic (constructs that explain a thing by its own kind). On this reading *ḥabl* states **the vein's being like a cord.**

**And the choice is meaningful:** the vein is named not as a "vein" but as a **"cord"**. A cord is what is drawn taut, what binds, what lets something fall when it breaks.

### Why the comparison is so strong

**What the verse does is to make a comparison, and the term it compares with is chosen carefully.**

God's nearness is not described as being nearer to a person than **some other being**. Mother, child, friend, shadow — none of these is used.

**The comparison is made with *a part of the person's own body*.**

**And the part chosen is a particular one:** something not seen, not touched, but without which there is no life. **The furthest point at which a person could think himself nearest to himself.**

**The logic of the verse is this, and I present it as my own analysis:** it does not say "as near as you are to yourself". It says **"nearer than that"**. Even the distance between a person and himself, that is, falls short of this nearness.

**And this matches the first half of the verse exactly.** What was said in the first half? **What a person's own soul whispers to him.** That is, a region a person does not fully know even about himself, cannot put a name to, sometimes does not even notice.

**The two halves of the sentence together say this:** the place that is closed to you is not closed.

### What this says to the present

**The side of this verse that works most today is the question of the "inner voice".**

Modern people are aware of their inner speech; psychology studies it, therapy works with it. The sentences that turn endlessly in the mind — anxieties, plans, jealousies, defences — are a reality everyone knows.

**The verse says two things, and both find a counterpart today.**

**One: this voice exists and is ordinary.** The verse does not name the whisper as an illness or a sin. As was recorded in the section on al-Nās: *"the coming of a whisper is not a sign of a defect."*

**Two: this voice is not hidden.** And what follows from this is not fear but a demand for **consistency** — as was recorded in the section on al-Infiṭār: *"To be the same person alone and in a crowd."*

**Qāf 50/16 carries this one step further:** in al-Infiṭār the matter was behaviour; here the matter is **thought**. That is, just as there is no such place as "where no one sees", there is no such thing as "a thought no one knows".

**And this has a consoling face as well:** what you cannot put into words does not need to be put into words.

## 50/17 — إِذْ يَتَلَقَّى ٱلْمُتَلَقِّيَانِ عَنِ ٱلْيَمِينِ وَعَنِ ٱلشِّمَالِ قَعِيدٌ

*Idh yatalaqqa'l-mutalaqqiyāni ʿani'l-yamīni wa ʿani'sh-shimāli qaʿīd*

"When the two receivers receive — one seated on the right and one on the left."

### What does إِذْ attach to?

**We must begin with a question of word order.** *Idh* in Arabic is an adverb of time and is expected to attach to a verb. Here there is no explicit verb.

The principal solutions the exegetes give:

| Solution | How it is read |
|---|---|
| **Attached to *aqrabu* in the previous verse** | "We are nearer to him than his jugular vein — when the two receivers receive" |
| **Attached to *naʿlamu* in the previous verse** | "…We know, and all the more when the two receivers receive" |
| **Attached to an implied verb (*udhkur*)** | "Remember, when the two receivers receive…" |

**The first and second solutions are close in sense and agree with the context.** Whichever it is, the effect is the same: **the knowing of the sixteenth verse and the recording of the seventeenth are read as the continuation of one sentence.**

### The same order again: knowledge first, then the record

**The order recorded in the fourth verse is repeated here**, and this is a pattern that can be verified within the sūra:

| | **Knowledge** | **Record** |
|---|---|---|
| **Verse 4** | *qad ʿalimnā* — We know | *wa ʿindanā kitābun ḥafīẓ* |
| **Verses 16-17** | *wa naʿlamu* — We know | *idh yatalaqqa'l-mutalaqqiyān* |

**In both places the record comes after the knowledge and in addition to it.**

**And the note set down in the fourth verse holds here too:** for one who knows, keeping a record is not necessary. The one for whom the record is kept is the one about whom judgement will be given.

### يَتَلَقَّى ٱلْمُتَلَقِّيَانِ — root ل-ق-ي

**And the densest root of the sūra comes once more, this time with two of its derivatives at once.**

***Talaqqī*** — the *tafaʿʿul* form. **Its concrete sense: to meet a thing, to go out before it and take it.**

**The primary sense of the root is "to meet" (*liqāʾ*).** The *tafaʿʿul* form here adds **intent and effort**: not meeting by chance, but **standing ready to meet.**

- **تَلَقَّى الشَّيْءَ** — he took a thing (reaching out his hand), he received it.
- **مُتَلَقِّي** — one who meets and receives.
- **تَلَقِّي الرُّكْبَان** — to meet a caravan on the road before it enters the town (a well-known question in law).

**The Qurʾān uses this verb of Adam (Ādam):** *"Adam received words from his Lord (*fa-talaqqā*)"* (al-Baqara 2/37).

**And the picture here is this: the word comes out of the mouth, and the two receivers *meet it in the air*.**

**The choice of word is striking.** The verse does not say "writers" (*kātibān*) — although the Qurʾān uses that word elsewhere (*kirāman kātibīn* — al-Infiṭār 82/11). **The verb chosen here describes the step before writing: taking.**

**In the section on al-Infiṭār this verse was already mentioned and set against that one.** The finding there was this: *"Together the two verses complete the description in al-Infiṭār: position (right and left), act (taking), and scope (speech)."*

**What is to be added here is the continuousness of the verb:** *yatalaqqā* is in the imperfect — **in the act of taking.** Not a finished work but an ongoing one.

### عَنِ ٱلْيَمِينِ وَعَنِ ٱلشِّمَالِ قَعِيدٌ — right and left

**قَعِيد — root ق-ع-د.** To sit. *Qāʿid* — one who sits; *quʿūd* — sitting; *maqʿad* — the place one sits.

***Qaʿīd* is on the *faʿīl* pattern and here means *qāʿid* (one who sits).** The lexicographers also explain this pattern in the sense of "one who keeps at a task, who stays in that state".

**And the state the word chooses matters: not standing, but seated.**

**I give this as my own reading:** to sit is to have **settled into waiting.** The one standing may leave; the one seated is there to stay. The picture is not of a passing surveillance but of **a settled waiting.**

**And *ʿatīd* (ready and waiting) in the next verse completes this picture:** seated and ready.

### A point of grammar: one adjective for two

**There is a well-known question in the clause:** *ʿani'l-yamīni wa ʿani'sh-shimāli **qaʿīd*** — "on the right and on the left, **seated**". The adjective comes **singular**, although two angels are spoken of.

The exegetes and grammarians explain this as follows:

| Explanation | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Elision** | The original of the clause is *ʿani'l-yamīni **qaʿīdun** wa ʿani'sh-shimāli qaʿīd*; the first has been dropped because the second indicates it |
| **The common use of the *faʿīl* pattern** | In Arabic the *faʿīl* pattern may be used in common for singular and plural, masculine and feminine; the distinction may simply not be drawn |
| **For the sake of the verse-ending** | The verse-end is on the pattern *ī* plus a single consonant; *qaʿīdān* would not sit on that pattern |

**All three explanations are transmitted and none excludes the others.** The third is a consequence rather than a cause; but given the sound scheme of the sūra it is worth recording.

## 50/18 — مَّا يَلْفِظُ مِن قَوْلٍ إِلَّا لَدَيْهِ رَقِيبٌ عَتِيدٌ

*Mā yalfiẓu min qawlin illā ladayhi raqībun ʿatīd*

"He utters not a word but there is a watcher beside him, ready and waiting."

### يَلْفِظُ — root ل-ف-ظ, and the making of speech into an object

**The most important side of this verse is this verb.**

**The concrete sense of the root: to cast out of the mouth.**

- **لَفَظَ الشَّيْءَ** — he cast a thing out of his mouth, he spat it out, he expelled it.
- **لَفَظَتِ الأرضُ ما فيها** — the earth cast out what was inside it.
- **لَفَظَ البَحْرُ المَيْتَ** — the sea cast the dead body onto the shore. **The lexicographers make a point of this usage when explaining the root.**
- **لَفْظ** — what comes out of the mouth; and from there **the utterance**: the word as spoken, the outer face of a word.
- **لَافِظَة** — one that throws something out of its mouth; a camel, a bird.

**In Arabic, that is, an "utterance" is not a thing said but a thing *thrown out*.**

**And now let us look at what the verse does.**

The verse does not say "he does not speak". Not *yatakallamu*, but *yalfiẓu*. **Speech, that is, is described not as an act of speaking but as an *object* that comes out of the mouth.**

**And this matches exactly the verb of the preceding verse:**

| Verse | Verb | What is done |
|---|---|---|
| **18** | *yalfiẓu* | The word is **thrown** from the mouth |
| **17** | *yatalaqqā* | The two receivers **meet it and take it** |

**Together the two verbs build a single motion: a thing thrown, and those who catch it in the air.**

**I present this as my own analysis**, but its support is the concrete senses of the two roots in the lexicographers, and those senses are not in dispute.

**And what follows from it is morally heavy.** A word, once spoken, does not remain in the speaker's hand. **What has been thrown is not taken back** — the sea does not take back the dead body once it has cast it onto the shore.

**English expressions such as "to let something slip out" and "a word once out cannot be called back" carry the same intuition.** The root of the word gives that intuition in Arabic already built in.

### مِن قَوْلٍ — the breadth of the scope

***Min*** is again a *ḥarf jarr zāʾid*: it makes the negation complete. **"Not one word".**

**The verse draws no distinction of kind:** good or bad, important or trivial, serious or joking, loud or low. **Everything that comes out of the mouth.**

**In the section on al-Infiṭār this breadth was treated with a different word:** there it was recorded that *tafʿalūn* (what you do) is wider than *taʿmalūn* (the deeds you perform) — *"behaviour that was not intended, not thought important, not reflected upon is also within the scope."*

**Qāf 50/18 builds the same breadth for speech.**

### رَقِيبٌ عَتِيدٌ — two attributes

**رَقِيب — root ر-ق-ب.** **This root was treated in the sections on al-Balad and al-Mujādala; I do not repeat it.** The summary of the note there:

> *"The primary sense of the root is to watch, to observe, to wait. […] And from the same root, **raqaba** — the neck. The connection is this: the neck is the axis on which the head turns and along which the gaze is directed. One who watches cranes his neck and turns it."*

**What is to be added as belonging to Qāf:** the word states a **position** here — the watcher is not at a distance but *ladayhi* (beside him).

**عَتِيد — root ع-ت-د.** **This root was treated in the sections on al-Mulk and al-Insān.** The note in the section on al-Insān referred directly to this verse:

> *"To prepare, to keep ready, to arrange in advance. From the same root **عَتِيد** (*ʿatīd*) — ready, at hand. The Qurʾān uses this word of the recording angel."*

**The idea the root carries is *having been made ready in advance*** — as was recorded in the section on al-Insān.

### What the two attributes do together

**The two words are not synonyms, and their standing side by side says something:**

| | ***raqīb*** | ***ʿatīd*** |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | It watches | It waits ready |
| Its direction | Outward — it looks | Inward — it is in a state of readiness |
| Its time | Now | **Beforehand** |

***Raqīb* states attentiveness, *ʿatīd* readiness.** One is looking; the other took its place before the looking began.

**And this pair will return within the sūra.**

### The return of the word عَتِيد — one of the tightest links of the sūra

**In the eighteenth verse *ʿatīd* is an attribute of the one who keeps the record.**

**In the twenty-third verse the same word comes once more:**

> *wa qāla qarīnuhū **hādhā mā ladayya ʿatīd*** — "His companion said: here is what I have, ready."

**The same word, the same adverb.** In the eighteenth: *ladayhi raqībun **ʿatīd***. In the twenty-third: *hādhā mā **ladayya ʿatīd***.

**The word used while the record is being kept, that is, is repeated when the record is presented.**

**I record this link as an observation** — both occurrences stand in the text and the adverb is shared as well. **And what it says is this:** the thing that waits ready is in the end brought forward ready. The reason for the waiting becomes clear.

### What this says to the present

**Speech is recorded today more than ever.**

Every message written stays; even if deleted, it sits somewhere; everything said can turn into a screenshot; a voice recorder is carried in a pocket. **That speech is "an object thrown" has become an everyday experience through technology.**

**And this experience makes what the verse says easy to grasp, but it also carries a danger.**

**What was recorded in the section on al-Infiṭār holds here too:** *"knowing that one is recorded does not by itself correct behaviour. People get used to being recorded."*

**Qāf 50/18 adds something to this:** the verse says nothing about punishment. It only says that the word **is taken**. And the weight of what it says lies there: the matter is not being caught but the word's not coming back.

## 50/19 — وَجَآءَتْ سَكْرَةُ ٱلْمَوْتِ بِٱلْحَقِّ ۖ ذَٰلِكَ مَا كُنتَ مِنْهُ تَحِيدُ

*Wa jāʾat sakratu'l-mawti bi'l-ḥaqq; dhālika mā kunta minhu taḥīd*

"And the stupor of death came with the truth. This is what you kept shrinking from."

### The address changes all at once

**This verse makes the sharpest turn in the sūra, and it makes it with a single letter.**

Up to here the telling has been in **the third person**: *ʿajibū* (they were astonished), *kadhdhabū* (they denied), *hum* (they), *nafsuhū* (his soul).

**In the second half of this verse it passes all at once to the second person singular: *kunta* — "you".**

**In Arabic this is called *iltifāt* (a shift in the direction of address)** and it is used often in the Qurʾān. But the place of the use here is a particular one: **the address turns to you exactly at the moment of death.**

**I give this as my own reading, and its support is the word order:** the group named "they" throughout the sūra becomes singular when the scene of death is reached. **Because death is a singular event.** No one dies in company.

**A similar movement was recorded in the section on al-Wāqiʿa:** the sūra's descent *"from the widest (all humanity) to the narrowest (a single person dying in a bed)"*. **Qāf makes the same movement, but by a shorter road: with one verb form.**

### سَكْرَة — root س-ك-ر

**The root of the word comes from a different place than is supposed, and this changes how the verse is read.**

**The primary sense of the root: to close, to block, to dam.**

- **سَكَرَ النَّهْرَ** — he dammed the river, he cut off its flow.
- **سِكْر** — a dam, a barrage; a barrier set in the way of water.
- **سُكْر** — drunkenness. **That is, a dam set in the way of the mind.**
- **سَكْرَة** — the state of intoxication; a single, concentrated state (the *faʿla* pattern conveys a single occurrence).

**The Qurʾān uses this root plainly in the sense of "being closed":**

> *"They would say: our eyes have been **sealed / closed** (*sukkirat abṣārunā*); no, we are a people bewitched."* (al-Ḥijr 15/15)

**There the verb is used of the eye, and its sense is being covered, being closed.**

**So *sakra* names not a state of pleasure but a state of *closing*.** Drunkenness in Arabic is the cutting off of the mind's way.

**And this is what the verse describes:** the closing off of the mind's clarity at the moment of death, the blurring of perception, the person's passing out of himself.

**This is observable and requires no forcing.** The clouding of consciousness in a body nearing death is a medically known process.

### بِٱلْحَقِّ — "with the truth"

**And now the second move of the verse comes.**

The stupor of death comes *"with the truth"*. That is, the thing that closes consciousness brings **the reality** at the same moment.

**This looks like a contradiction, and it is probably meant to:**

| | ***sakra*** | ***bi'l-ḥaqq*** |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | It closes | It opens |
| What | Consciousness, perception | The truth |

**The same moment, that is, closes one thing while opening another.**

**And this is exactly what the twenty-second verse will say:** *"We have lifted your covering from you; today your sight is sharp."* **The moment of death is described twice in the sūra, with two different words, as "something opening from under something that closes".**

**I give this as my own reading;** its support is the structural resemblance of the two verses.

**And the expression *bi'l-ḥaqq* is also linked with the *bi'l-ḥaqq* of the fifth verse** (given in a table above): **the thing they denied comes together with death.**

### The disagreement: an anomalous reading

**In the sources on the variant readings a different reading of this verse is transmitted:**

> *wa jāʾat sakratu'l-**ḥaqqi bi'l-mawt*** — "the stupor of the truth came with death."

**This reading is not among those transmitted by mass transmission; it is counted anomalous (*shādhdh*) and is not recited in prayer.** Its transmission is of interest for showing how the placing of the words changes the sense:

| Reading | Subject | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **Mass-transmitted** | *sakratu'l-mawt* | The stupor of death brought **the truth** |
| **Anomalous** | *sakratu'l-ḥaqq* | The stupor of the truth brought **death** |

**I transmit this reading as a piece of information; I say nothing definite about whom it is ascribed to, and I build the commentary on the mass-transmitted reading.**

### تَحِيدُ — root ح-ي-د

**Its concrete sense: to turn off the road, to draw aside, to avoid.**

- **حَادَ عَنِ الطَّرِيق** — he turned off the road, he left the way.
- **حَيْدَة** — swerving, avoidance.
- **حَيْد** — the jut of a mountain or a rock; the part that leaves the line and overhangs to the side.

**At the centre of the root is *swerving aside*** — not opposing, but **going around.**

**And this is the finest side of the verse.**

To avoid death is not to deny death. No one denies death. **What is done is to swerve aside from it:** not to raise the subject, not to think about it, to say "I will think about it later".

**The verb is in the imperfect: *taḥīd* — "which you kept avoiding".** It states continuousness. Not a single flight, then, but **a swerving kept up for a lifetime.**

**And the object of the verb comes with *minhu* (from it):** *mā kunta **minhu** taḥīd*. What is avoided is death, and the verse names it with **a demonstrative**: *dhālika* — "this here".

**The scene, then, is this:** a thing unnamed for a lifetime, never looked at, always sidestepped — and now it stands before you, pointed at with a finger.

### What this says to the present

**Swerving aside from death is one of the most institutionalised habits of modern life.**

Death has been moved to the hospital, out of the home. Funerals have been professionalised. Old age is hidden. Illness is softened in speech. Children are kept away from funerals.

**None of this is denial.** No one says "human beings do not die". **What is done is exactly *ḥayd*: swerving aside.**

**And what the verse says is not a scolding but a finding:** avoiding does not prevent the meeting.

**One more note is needed:** the verse does not command one to think about death continuously either. What it says is that **fleeing from it does not work.** The difference between the two is the difference between a liveable life and a life paralysed by fear.

## 50/20 — وَنُفِخَ فِى ٱلصُّورِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ يَوْمُ ٱلْوَعِيدِ

*Wa nufikha fi'ṣ-ṣūr; dhālika yawmu'l-waʿīd*

"And the trumpet was blown. That is the day of the threat."

### From death to the resurrection in a single step

**The nineteenth verse described the death of one person. The twentieth passes to the raising of all humanity.**

**And in between there is no connective, no transitional sentence.** Only a *wāw*.

**This is a choice that recurs in the Qurʾān's account of the hereafter, and its effect is this:** the interval between death and resurrection — however long it may be — occupies no room in the telling. **For the one who dies, the two are contiguous.**

**This is not a claim about time but a choice of narration**, and I record it as my own reading.

### ٱلصُّور — the trumpet

**This word was treated in the section on al-Ḥāqqa; I do not repeat it.** The note there was this:

> There are two readings as to what the word is — **a horn or trumpet** and **the plural of "form"**. The first is the reading of the majority and agrees better with the structure of the verb: *nufikha **fī**'ṣ-ṣūr* — "it was blown **into** the trumpet".

**And the note set down there holds here too:** *"I do not speak about the manner of the thing. What the trumpet is, what kind of thing it is, who blows it, is not detailed in the Qurʾān."*

**In the section on al-Muddaththir a list of the occurrences of this expression in the Qurʾān was given**, and Qāf 50/20 was on that list.

**What is to be added as belonging to Qāf is that the verb is passive:** *nufikha* — "it was blown". **The agent is not stated.**

**And this stands out when set beside the other verbs of the sūra.** Throughout the sūra the verbs are mostly in the first person plural and the agent is explicit: *banaynāhā*, *zayyannāhā*, *madadnāhā*, *alqaynā*, *anbatnā*, *nazzalnā*, *aḥyaynā*, *khalaqnā*, *naʿlamu*, *kashafnā*.

**In this verse a passive verb comes all at once.** I record this as an observation.

### ذَٰلِكَ يَوْمُ ٱلْوَعِيدِ — the first of three names

**As was recorded above, the sūra names the day of resurrection in three separate places, and this is the first.**

**وَعِيد** — a threat; treated at the fourteenth verse. **And here it is definite: *al-waʿīd* — "that threat".** That is, the one the addressee knows, the one announced to him beforehand.

**And the twenty-eighth verse will say this plainly:** *wa qad qaddamtu ilaykum bi'l-waʿīd* — "I sent the threat ahead to you."

**The name of the day, that is, comes not from what will happen on that day but from *the word spoken before it*.**

**I give this as my own reading:** the day is named not by a surprise but by **the keeping of a word that was given.**

## 50/21 — وَجَآءَتْ كُلُّ نَفْسٍ مَّعَهَا سَآئِقٌ وَشَهِيدٌ

*Wa jāʾat kullu nafsin maʿahā sāʾiqun wa shahīd*

"And every soul came, and with it a driver and a witness."

### كُلُّ نَفْسٍ — one by one

**The scale drops to the singular again.** No crowd is described; it says **"every soul"** — that is, one by one.

**نَفْس** — a person, a life, a self. In the sixteenth verse the same word was used for "what whispers within": *mā tuwaswisu bihī **nafsuh***. **The same word at two tasks:** first what speaks inside a person, now what comes to account.

**I record this as an observation.**

### سَآئِق — the driver

**Root: س-و-ق.** To drive, to urge on ahead of oneself. *Sawq* — to drive a thing forward; *sūq* (a market) — the place to which goods are driven and dispatched; *sāq* (a leg) — what makes walking possible.

**The concrete use of the root is the driving of animals:** the shepherd urging the flock along in front of him. The word carries **a giving of direction** and **a disregard for the will** of the one driven.

### شَهِيد — the witness

**Root: ش-ه-د.** To see, to be present, to bear witness. *Shahāda* — being present and stating what one has seen; *mashhad* — a place of witness; *shahīd* — both a witness and (in other uses in the Qurʾān) one who gives his life.

**At the root of the word is *being present*** — and this stands in the same field as *qaʿīd* (seated, ready) in the seventeenth verse and *ʿatīd* (waiting ready) in the eighteenth.

### What do the two companions do?

| | ***sāʾiq*** | ***shahīd*** |
|---|---|---|
| Its work | To bring | To tell |
| Its direction | Forward — movement | Backward — the past |
| Is there compulsion | Yes | No |

**One brings the person there; the other says what he has brought with him.**

**The exegetes differ over who these two are:**

| View | Who |
|---|---|
| **Two angels** | The two receivers of verse 17; one drives, the other bears witness |
| **One angel, and a record of deeds or a limb** | The driver is an angel, the witness is the person's own deeds or his limbs |
| **One angel and a *qarīn*** | It is associated with the *qarīn* of verses 23 and 27 |

**I adopt no preference in this disagreement.** The Qurʾān also speaks of a person's own limbs bearing witness (*"On that day We seal their mouths; their hands speak to Us and their feet bear witness to what they earned"* — Yā Sīn 36/65), and this gives some support to the second reading.

**What can be recorded is this:** the verse states not an **identity** but a **function**. There is one who brings, and one who tells.

### The return of the word شَهِيد within the sūra

**The word comes once more in the thirty-seventh verse — but in an entirely different place:**

> *…aw alqa's-samʿa **wa huwa shahīd*** — "…or gives ear while he is present."

**In the twenty-first, *shahīd* is the witness beside the person. In the thirty-seventh, *shahīd* is an attribute of the person himself: present, attentive.**

**The same word, two different subjects.** And in both, the primary sense of the root — **being present** — is at work.

**I record this as an observation and add this, as my own reading:** the person who is a *shahīd* in this world (present with his attention) and the person who has a *shahīd* beside him in the hereafter are named with the same root. To the one who does not do the being-present here, a present one is given there.

## 50/22 — لَّقَدْ كُنتَ فِى غَفْلَةٍ مِّنْ هَٰذَا فَكَشَفْنَا عَنكَ غِطَآءَكَ فَبَصَرُكَ ٱلْيَوْمَ حَدِيدٌ

*Laqad kunta fī ghaflatin min hādhā, fa-kashafnā ʿanka ghiṭāʾak, fa-baṣaruka'l-yawma ḥadīd*

"You were heedless of this. Now We have lifted your covering from you; today your sight is sharp."

### One of the densest verses of the sūra

**This verse carries one of the most quoted expressions of the Qurʾān, and it was already mentioned in two other sections of this commentary:**

- **In the section on al-Ḥāqqa**, under the heading "the day when things turn out real".
- **In the section on al-Takwīr**, twice; and there this finding was made: *"The product of the stripping away is not ruin but **sight.**"*

**I rely on that finding and add to it.**

### غَفْلَة — root غ-ف-ل

**Its concrete sense: not being aware of a thing; but especially *the absence of a mark*.**

- **غَفَلَ عَنِ الشَّيْء** — he was heedless of a thing, he did not bring it to mind.
- **أَرْضٌ غُفْل** — land with no track, no mark, no road on it.
- **كِتَابٌ غُفْل** — a writing with no signature or heading on it.
- **دَابَّةٌ غُفْل** — an unbranded animal.

**Here is the most instructive side of the root: *ghafla* is not ignorance but *the absence of marks*.** On unmarked land there is no road; but the land is there. **Heedlessness is like this: the thing is there, and it has left no trace in the person.**

**And this fits exactly what the verse says.** The heedless person is not meeting something he had no knowledge of. **He is meeting something that was already there but had left no mark on him.**

***Min hādhā*** — "of this". That is, of the thing now standing before him.

### فَكَشَفْنَا عَنكَ غِطَآءَكَ — the lifting of the covering

**كَشْف — root ك-ش-ف.** To open a cover, to lift what is over a thing, to bring to light. *Kashf* — bringing to light; *munkashif* — uncovered.

**غِطَآء — root غ-ط-و / غ-ط-ي.** A cover, a lid. *Ghaṭṭā* — he covered; *ghiṭāʾ* — the cover or lid laid over a thing.

**And two things in the word order are striking.**

**One. The covering is ascribed to the person: *ghiṭāʾa-ka* — "your covering."**

The verse does not say "We lifted the covering"; it says **"your covering"**. The covering, that is, is not something that came from outside and got between; it is **the person's own covering.**

**This is the most important detail of the verse and is worth dwelling on.**

**Two. The verb comes with the particle *ʿan*: *kashafnā ʿanka*. This shows that the covering was taken off the person** — not off the scene.

**What is lifted, that is, is not the covering over what is to be seen, but the covering over the one who is to see.**

### "What changed is not you but the covering" — my own reading

**Now I come to a side of this verse that I present as my own reading. I record it not as something transmitted but as a reading I draw from the text.**

**The ordinary expectation is this:** in the hereafter a new faculty of understanding is given to a person, his capacities are increased, his sight is strengthened.

**That is not what the verse says.**

The verse does not say that the sight was strengthened. **It says that a covering was lifted.** And the attribute used of the sight after the covering is lifted is set down as though it had always been so: *fa-baṣaruka'l-yawma ḥadīd*.

**The logic, that is, is this:** the sight was sharp all along; there was something in front of it.

**And this reading has two supports within the text:**

**One.** The covering belongs to the person: *ghiṭāʾak.*

**Two.** At the beginning of the verse the word "heedlessness" is used, and as was recorded above *ghafla* is not ignorance but **the absence of marks**. What was missing, that is, was not the capacity to see but **the trace.**

**The consequence of this must be stated with care:** the verse implies that a person is in a condition to see more than he sees in this world. What prevents the seeing is not an incapacity but **something that has come in between.**

**And this is linked with the sixth verse of the sūra.** There it was said "have they not looked?" — that is, **looking was possible.** Here it is said "We have lifted your covering" — that is, **the obstacle is gone.** Two verses, the two ends of the same matter.

**One more note:** this reading has its limit. The verse does not say that perception in the hereafter is entirely the same as perception in this world; what it says is that the change taking place on that day is described with the verb **to lift**. That is the text's own word; the rest is inference.

### حَدِيد — and sharpness sharing a root with iron

**Root: ح-د-د. This root was treated at length in the section on al-Ḥadīd.** The summary of the note there:

> - **حدّ (*ḥadd*)** — a limit, an edge, a tip; and the sharp edge of a knife
> - **حديد (*ḥadīd*)** — sharp; and iron
> - **حدّاد (*ḥaddād*)** — a blacksmith; and a doorkeeper (one who holds a limit)
> - **حدود (*ḥudūd*)** — limits
>
> *"Iron, that is, takes its name from its sharpness. And the same root means a limit."*

**Here I add what belongs to Qāf, and first set down a note: *ḥadīd* here is not the name of the metal but an adjective — "sharp".** The verse does not say that the eye is made of iron.

**But the echo the word carries is at work.**

**And all three of these senses are working in the verse:**

| Sense | Its counterpart in the verse |
|---|---|
| **Sharp** | The sight has become clear |
| **Cutting** | It distinguishes; it separates what was mixed together |
| **Limit** | It settles what is what |

**The third is particularly important.** To see is in fact to separate: to see where a thing ends and where another begins. **Blurred sight is the loss of boundaries.**

**And this sets up a contrast with *labs* (indistinctness, being covered) in the fifteenth verse:**

| Verse | State | Root | What |
|---|---|---|---|
| **15** | *fī **labs*** | ل-ب-س | Covered, mixed |
| **22** | *baṣaruka … **ḥadīd*** | ح-د-د | Sharp, distinguishing |

**The sūra, that is, gives the two states of the same person with two opposite words:** first an indistinctness put on over him, then a sharpness that cuts and separates.

**I present this contrast as my own reading; both words stand in the text and their fields of meaning really are opposite.**

### What this says to the present

**The everyday counterpart of the verse is the experience of "understanding afterwards".**

What could not be seen while one was inside a relationship, a decision, a friendship becomes suddenly visible once one is outside it. And at that moment the sentence a person says is always the same: *"how did I not see it?"*

**What was not seen has not disappeared; what has gone is the thing that stood in front of the person's gaze.**

**The verse describes this experience on the scale of the hereafter**, and it does so not as a threat but as a description. **And it leaves a question:** if what prevents seeing is not an incapacity but something that has come in between — **what is coming in between now?**

---

## 50/23 — وَقَالَ قَرِينُهُۥ هَٰذَا مَا لَدَىَّ عَتِيدٌ

*Wa qāla qarīnuhū hādhā mā ladayya ʿatīd*

"His companion said: here is what I have, ready."

### قَرِين — root ق-ر-ن

**Its concrete sense: to bind two things together, to pair them.**

- **قَرَنَ بَيْنَ شَيْئَيْن** — he brought two things together, he bound them.
- **قِرَان** — joining together; it is used of the marriage contract as well.
- **قَرْن** — a horn; and **a generation** (those who live together at one time).
- **قِرْن** — an equal, a peer.
- **قَرِين** — one who is constantly together with another, an inseparable companion.

**The picture beneath the word is two animals yoked to the same beam.** A *qarīn* is not one who walks beside you; it is **one bound to the same bond.**

### Who is this *qarīn*?

**In the classical commentaries there are two main views, and the disagreement must not be concealed:**

| View | Who the *qarīn* is | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **An angel** | The recording angel of verses 17-18; he presents the record he has brought | The word *ʿatīd* was used of the angel in verse 18; the clause is built like **a presentation of the record** |
| **Satan / an accompanying jinn** | The companion that fastens on a person | In verse 27 *qarīnuhū* is plainly a being defending itself: *"Our Lord, I did not make him transgress"* |

**And the question is this: is the *qarīn* of 23 the same being as that of 27?**

| Possibility | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **The same** | Both are Satan; in 23 he presents what he has brought, in 27 he refuses responsibility |
| **Different** | 23 is an angel, 27 is Satan; the word has been used of two separate beings |

**In this disagreement I do not impose a preference.** Both readings have support: the first is supported by the repetition of the word, the second by the content of the clauses.

**One observation that can be recorded:** the clause in the twenty-third verse is a clause of **delivery** (here it is, ready), the one in the twenty-seventh a clause of **defence**. Two different tasks.

### هَٰذَا مَا لَدَىَّ عَتِيدٌ — the presentation of the record

**The clause is extremely short, and the shortness shows that the work is finished.**

*Hādhā* (here is) — *mā ladayya* (what I have) — *ʿatīd* (ready).

**And the word *ʿatīd*, as recorded above, comes back from the eighteenth verse.** The same adverb is repeated too: *ladayhi* → *ladayya*.

**The adverb *ladā* occurs four times in the sūra**, and all four have to do with the reckoning:

| Verse | Expression | Beside whom |
|---|---|---|
| 18 | *ladayhi raqībun ʿatīd* | Beside the person |
| 23 | *hādhā mā **ladayya** ʿatīd* | Beside the *qarīn* |
| 28-29 | *lā takhtaṣimū **ladayya*** / *mā yubaddalu'l-qawlu **ladayya*** | **With God** |
| 35 | *wa **ladaynā** mazīd* | **With God** |

**The adverb rises as it shifts place:** from beside the person to beside the *qarīn*, and from there into the presence.

**I record this as an observation;** all four occurrences stand in the text.

## 50/24-26 — أَلْقِيَا فِى جَهَنَّمَ كُلَّ كَفَّارٍ عَنِيدٍ ۝ مَّنَّاعٍ لِّلْخَيْرِ مُعْتَدٍ مُّرِيبٍ ۝ ٱلَّذِى جَعَلَ مَعَ ٱللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا ءَاخَرَ فَأَلْقِيَاهُ فِى ٱلْعَذَابِ ٱلشَّدِيدِ

*Alqiyā fī jahannama kulla kaffārin ʿanīd — mannāʿin li'l-khayri muʿtadin murīb — alladhī jaʿala maʿa'llāhi ilāhan ākhara fa-alqiyāhu fi'l-ʿadhābi'sh-shadīd*

"Cast into Hell every stubborn ingrate — the withholder of good, the transgressor, the sower of doubt — who set up another god beside God. Cast him into the severe punishment."

### أَلْقِيَا — the dual imperative and the disagreement of the lexicographers

**And now we come to the most debated question of word order in the sūra.**

**أَلْقِيَا** — the **dual imperative** of the verb *ilqāʾ*: **"cast, you two."**

**The question is: to whom is it addressed?**

In the classical commentaries and grammars there are three principal explanations, and **the disagreement is a real one:**

| Explanation | What it says | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Two angels** | The *sāʾiq* and the *shahīd* of verse 21 are addressed | The context suits very well: the two angels are already in the scene and have brought the person | — |
| **Addressing one person in the dual** | In Arabic, and especially in the Ḥijāzī dialect, addressing a single person with the dual form is a known usage; it conveys emphasis and reinforcement | Examples are cited from pre-Islamic poetry; the grammarians record this usage | How widespread and how far-reaching the usage is is disputed |
| **The emphatic *nūn* turning into an *alif*** | The original is *alqiyan* (with the emphatic *nūn*); in pause the *nūn* has turned into an *alif* | The turning of the light *nūn* into an *alif* in pause is a known phenomenon in Arabic | There is no trace of this reading among the other variant readings; and the form *alqiyāhu* in verse 26 makes the explanation difficult |

**There is an indication that must be recorded for the third explanation, and it matters:** the same form is repeated in the twenty-sixth verse **with a pronoun attached**: *fa-**alqiyā**hu*. The explanation from the emphatic *nūn* does not work with the pronoun attached.

**This is an observation and it strengthens the first explanation; but I do not impose a preference.** The second explanation too is a usage genuinely recorded in Arabic, and some exegetes adopt it.

**And this must be added: all three explanations arrive at the same result.** Whether two angels or a pattern of emphasis, the content and the force of the command do not change.

### The form of the command: the verb first

**The clause begins with the imperative and the object comes afterwards.** In Arabic this is the normal order, but here its effect is sharp: **the command is given before it is said whom it concerns.**

*Alqiyā fī jahannama* — "cast into Hell" — **whom?** The answer takes two and a half verses.

**The clause, that is, states the verdict first and the ground afterwards.** And the ground is given in five attributes.

### كُلَّ — not a person but a type

**The word *kull* was treated in the sections on al-Humaza and al-Qalam.** The note there was this: *"kull is singular in wording and plural in sense; and it indicates not a person but a type."*

**And the warning given in the section on al-Qalam must be repeated here**, because a list of five attributes carries the same risk:

> *"to take up the text, place other people inside the description and pass judgement on them, is to commit the very act the sūra describes."*

**And all the attributes are indefinite:** *kaffārin, ʿanīdin, mannāʿin, muʿtadin, murībin*. **That is, not "that man" but "someone like this".**

### Five attributes

**كَفَّار — root ك-ف-ر.** To cover. The *faʿʿāl* pattern — as recorded in the section on al-Burūj — conveys **multiplicity and continuity**. Not "one who covers", then, but **"one who has made covering his trade".**

**عَنِيد — root ع-ن-د. Treated in the section on al-Muddaththir; I do not repeat it.** The summary of the note there: *"to turn off the road, to draw aside; and to do so in resistance. […] the word states not a single fault but a fault that has become permanent."*

**And in the section on al-Muddaththir this verse was already mentioned**, with this finding: the word occurs in three further places in the Qurʾān, and in all three it stands beside another attribute — *jabbārin ʿanīd* (Hūd 11/59; Ibrāhīm 14/15) and *kaffārin ʿanīd* (Qāf 50/24).

**Now I add to this an observation belonging to Qāf, and I record it as my own reading.**

In the Qurʾān the attribute that most often accompanies *ʿanīd* is **jabbār**. And **this sūra will use exactly that word in its forty-fifth verse:**

> *wa mā anta ʿalayhim bi-**jabbār*** — "you are not a **jabbār** over them." (50/45)

**Two words the Qurʾān uses side by side elsewhere are thus separated in this sūra:**

| Word | Of whom | Verse |
|---|---|---|
| ***ʿanīd*** | Of the denier | 24 |
| ***jabbār*** | **Negated** — of the messenger | 45 |

**I present this link as my own reading**; the places of the two words in the text are fixed, but the claim that a link is made between them is mine.

**مَّنَّاع لِّلْخَيْر — root م-ن-ع.** To withhold, to refuse. Again the *faʿʿāl* pattern: **one who has made withholding a habit.**

**And this expression occurs once more in the Qurʾān, in the same form:**

> *…**mannāʿin li'l-khayri muʿtadin** athīm* (al-Qalam 68/12)

**In the section on al-Qalam this expression was treated within the portrait of nine attributes.** And what was recorded there for the word *khayr* was: *"The use of the word in the Qurʾān is broad and includes the sense of wealth."*

**The overlap between the two verses is notable and can be verified:**

| | **al-Qalam 68/12** | **Qāf 50/25** |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *mannāʿin li'l-khayr* | *mannāʿin li'l-khayr* |
| 2 | *muʿtadin* | *muʿtadin* |
| 3 | ***athīm*** (sinful) | ***murīb*** (one who casts doubt) |

**The first two attributes are word for word the same; the third is different.**

**I record this as an observation and do not claim that there is a deliberate reference between the two sūras.** But the difference is meaningful: the portrait in al-Qalam is a portrait of **conduct** and goes on with *athīm* (sinful); the list in Qāf is a list of **denial** and goes on with *murīb*.

**مُعْتَد — root ع-د-و.** To exceed a limit, to attack. *Iʿtidāʾ* — to cross a boundary; *ʿaduww* — an enemy; *ʿudwān* — aggression.

**The word is in the *iftiʿāl* form and states the crossing of a limit *knowingly*.**

**مُّرِيب — root ر-ي-ب.** **This root was treated in the section on al-Baqara.** The note there was this:

> *"**Rayb** — a doubt that unsettles. There is unease and suspicion in it. From the same root comes *rība*: an ill opinion held against someone."*

**And the pattern of the word is decisive here: *murīb* is the active participle of the *ifʿāl* form — that is, transitive.**

| Pattern | Sense |
|---|---|
| *murtāb* | One who **feels** doubt |
| ***murīb*** | One who **casts doubt, who puts others into doubt** |

**The word can therefore be read in two directions:** one who is himself in doubt, and/or **one who puts others into doubt.** Because of the transitivity of the pattern the lexicographers bring the second forward; the first sense is also transmitted.

**And the second sense is linked with the fifteenth verse:** there they were *fī labs* (inside an indistinctness). Here *murīb* — one who **spreads** the indistinctness.

**I give this link as my own reading.**

### The order of the five attributes

**There is a movement in the sequence, and I present this as my own analysis:**

| # | Attribute | Domain |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *kaffār* | **Inner** — belief: he covers |
| 2 | *ʿanīd* | **Inner** — will: he resists |
| 3 | *mannāʿ li'l-khayr* | **Outer** — the hand: he does not give |
| 4 | *muʿtad* | **Outer** — the hand: he takes |
| 5 | *murīb* | **Outer** — the tongue: he spreads |

**And the twenty-sixth verse closes the list with a single clause:** *alladhī jaʿala maʿa'llāhi ilāhan ākhar* — "who set up another god beside God."

**This states the source of the five attributes.** The method recorded in the section on al-Qalam is at work here too: *"The Qurʾān generally frames a diagnosis this way: it begins with the visible act and descends to the source."*

### فَأَلْقِيَاهُ — the repetition of the command

**The command was given in the twenty-fourth verse. In the twenty-sixth it is repeated.**

**In Arabic this is called the repetition made when a long series of attributes has come in between** — the verdict at the head of the sentence is said again once the grounds have been enumerated.

**And there is a change in the repetition:**

| Verse | Expression | To where |
|---|---|---|
| 24 | *alqiyā **fī jahannam*** | Into Hell |
| 26 | *fa-alqiyāhu **fi'l-ʿadhābi'sh-shadīd*** | **Into the severe punishment** |

**The first states the place, the second the state.** It passes from the name of a place to a quality.

**I record this as an observation.**

## 50/27-29 — قَالَ قَرِينُهُۥ رَبَّنَا مَآ أَطْغَيْتُهُۥ وَلَٰكِن كَانَ فِى ضَلَٰلٍۭ بَعِيدٍ ۝ قَالَ لَا تَخْتَصِمُوا۟ لَدَىَّ وَقَدْ قَدَّمْتُ إِلَيْكُم بِٱلْوَعِيدِ ۝ مَا يُبَدَّلُ ٱلْقَوْلُ لَدَىَّ وَمَآ أَنَا۠ بِظَلَّٰمٍ لِّلْعَبِيدِ

*Qāla qarīnuhū rabbanā mā aṭghaytuhū wa lākin kāna fī ḍalālin baʿīd — qāla lā takhtaṣimū ladayya wa qad qaddamtu ilaykum bi'l-waʿīd — mā yubaddalu'l-qawlu ladayya wa mā anā bi-ẓallāmin li'l-ʿabīd*

"His companion said: Our Lord, I did not make him transgress; he was himself in a far-gone error. — He said: do not quarrel in My presence; I sent the threat ahead to you. — With Me the word is not changed, and I am no wronger of the servants."

### The defence and its rejection

**Three verses build one scene: accusation, defence, verdict.**

**And the form of the defence is striking:** the *qarīn* does not say that he did nothing, but that **his influence was not decisive.**

### مَآ أَطْغَيْتُهُۥ — "I did not make him transgress"

**طُغْيَان — root ط-غ-ي.** Its concrete sense: **to overflow, to spread past a limit.** The overflowing of water from its vessel. The Qurʾān uses this plainly: *"When the water overflowed (*lammā ṭagha'l-māʾu*) We carried you in the ship"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/11).

***Aṭghā* — the *ifʿāl* form: to make overflow.** The accusation, that is, is: "you made him overflow."

**And the defence does not deny the overflowing; it denies being the one who caused it.**

### The Qurʾān explained by the Qurʾān

**The same defence is transmitted in another place in the Qurʾān, at much greater length:**

> *"When the matter was decided Satan said: God promised you what is true; I promised you too, and I broke my word. **I had no compelling power over you; I only called, and you answered me.** So do not blame me, blame yourselves."* (Ibrāhīm 14/22)

**In the section on al-Nās the principle this verse carries was recorded:** *"the whisperer has no compelling power in his hand. The only thing he has is a call — an invitation, a word, a whisper. Since there is no compulsion, the responsibility stays with the person."*

**Qāf 50/27 is the shortest form of the same defence.**

**And a note is needed:** these verses transmit Satan's defence not in order to **confirm** it but in order to show where responsibility lies. Indeed the next verse cuts the dispute off — that is, the defence is neither accepted nor argued with.

### فِى ضَلَٰلٍۭ بَعِيدٍ — the third *baʿīd*

**And the key word of the sūra comes for the third time.**

| Verse | Expression | Who says it |
|---|---|---|
| **3** | *rajʿun **baʿīd*** | The deniers |
| **27** | *ḍalālin **baʿīd*** | The *qarīn* |
| **31** | *ghayra **baʿīd*** | God |

**In the third verse what was "far" was the resurrection. In the twenty-seventh what is "far" is the error.**

**And this is one of the clearest corrections the sūra makes by way of a word, and I give it as my own reading:** what was far was not the resurrection; **what was far was the place they had fallen into.**

***Ḍalāl* — root ض-ل-ل.** To lose the way, to stray. The concrete sense of the root is losing the track in the desert. **And the adjective *baʿīd* brings that concrete sense to life:** having strayed **far** from the road.

### لَا تَخْتَصِمُوا۟ لَدَىَّ — "do not quarrel in My presence"

**اِخْتِصَام — root خ-ص-م.** To go to law, to contend. *Khaṣm* — an adversary, a litigant; *khuṣūma* — contention.

**The verb is plural: *takhtaṣimū* — "do not quarrel (you, a group)".** Yet there are two parties in the scene. The plural either takes in the crowd both parties represent, or is the general form of prohibition used in Arabic for more than two.

**And the place in which the prohibition is set matters: *ladayya* — "in My presence".** What is forbidden, that is, is not quarrelling itself but **quarrelling there.**

**I give this as my own reading:** the dispute had its place and its time; that place was this world. At the moment of judgement dispute is pointless, because it does not change the judgement.

### وَقَدْ قَدَّمْتُ إِلَيْكُم بِٱلْوَعِيدِ — announcing in advance

**قَدَّمْتُ — root ق-د-م.** To bring forward, to send ahead. *Taqdīm* — to put forward; *qadīm* — what comes before; *qadam* — a foot (what is put forward).

**And the verb is in the *tafʿīl* form: to send ahead, to convey beforehand.**

**This clause is the ground of the justice of the verdict.** The punishment is not a surprise; **the announcement had been made.**

**And it has a link with the fourteenth verse:** there it was said *"my threat came true"* — of the peoples of the past. Here it is said that the threat had been announced **beforehand**. **The two together: notice was given, and then it came to pass.**

### مَا يُبَدَّلُ ٱلْقَوْلُ لَدَىَّ

**بَدَّلَ — root ب-د-ل.** To change, to put another thing in the place of a thing. *Badal* — what takes a thing's place.

**The verb is passive and in the imperfect: *yubaddalu* — "is not changed".** It states a general ruling.

**ٱلْقَوْل — the word.** Definite: **"that word"** — that is, the one announced beforehand.

**And this sets up an interesting echo with the eighteenth verse.** There a person's word *yalfiẓu* (is thrown from his mouth) and is taken. Here God's word *lā yubaddalu* (is not changed).

**Two words, two fates:** a person's word is thrown out and recorded; God's word does not change.

**I record this as my own reading.**

### وَمَآ أَنَا۠ بِظَلَّٰمٍ لِّلْعَبِيدِ — the question of *ẓallām*

**Here there is a question the grammarians and exegetes dwell on at length.**

**ظَلَّام** is on the *faʿʿāl* pattern and conveys **intensity**: "one who wrongs much". The verse negates this: *"I am no **great wronger** of the servants."*

**The objection is plain:** does "I am no great wronger" mean "I wrong a little"?

**The classical answers are these:**

| Answer | How |
|---|---|
| **Because of the multiplicity of the object** | The servants are many (*al-ʿabīd* — plural). Even a small wrong done to a great number is "much" in the total; the intensity lies not in the act but in **the scope** |
| **The intensity of the negation** | In Arabic, negating an intensive form strengthens the negation: "not even the smallest wrong exists" |
| **The attribute of one who never wrongs at all** | Denying an attribute in its intensive form denies the attribute itself as well; for one who never does a thing cannot do it much either |

**These three explanations are widely transmitted in the classical sources and complete one another.** The first is the closest to the text, because it rests on the word's coming together with *li'l-**ʿabīd*** (of the servants — plural).

**And this principle is established by the Qurʾān's own words:** *"God does not wrong anyone by so much as a speck"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/40).

**عَبِيد — a servant.** And something in the word order is striking: the clause ends with *li'l-**ʿabīd***.

**The one who gives the verdict, that is, names those he addresses not as "the guilty" or "the deniers" but as *servants*.** The same word was used in the eighth verse (*ʿabdin munīb*) and in the eleventh (*li'l-ʿibād*).

**I record this as an observation:** even at the moment of judgement the sūra keeps the common name.

## 50/30 — يَوْمَ نَقُولُ لِجَهَنَّمَ هَلِ ٱمْتَلَأْتِ وَتَقُولُ هَلْ مِن مَّزِيدٍ

*Yawma naqūlu li-jahannama hali'mtalaʾti wa taqūlu hal min mazīd*

"On that day We shall say to Hell: are you full? And it will say: is there any more?"

### The scene itself

**This is the most striking scene of the sūra, and its force lies less in what it describes than in how it describes it.**

**Two clauses. A question, and a question.** And one of the two parties is **Hell.**

### Personification

**In Arabic rhetoric this is called *tashkhīṣ* (giving personality to what is inanimate)** and it is used in several places in the Qurʾān: the weeping of heaven and earth (al-Dukhān 44/29), the heavens and the earth saying "we come willingly" (Fuṣṣilat 41/11), the speaking of the skins (Fuṣṣilat 41/21).

**Here what is personified is Hell. And the only property given to it is *appetite*.**

**Now let us look at exactly what the personification does here, because the matter is not an ornament of style.**

**One. It removes number.** The verse does not say "Hell is very wide"; it gives no measure, no figure, no volume. **Instead it shows something that is never sated.** Had a measure been given, the mind would have grasped and bounded it. Insatiability cannot be grasped.

**Two. It turns the scene into a dialogue.** The verdict is not narrated; **it is spoken.** And the other party to the speech is the **place** of the punishment. The punishment, that is, ceases to be a passive place and becomes a **party.**

**Three. It turns the question round.** The one who asks is God: "are you full?" The expected answer is "I am full" or "I am not". **What comes instead is a question.** No answer is given; **a want is stated.**

**Four. And just here the word builds a bridge to the other half of the sūra.** I shall come back to this five verses later.

**I present these four points as my own analysis.**

### The disagreement: what does *hal min mazīd* mean?

**And here there is a real disagreement about the sense; it must not be concealed.**

**هَلْ** is an interrogative particle in Arabic, but in Arabic interrogative particles do not always ask a real question; sometimes they convey **denial** or **rejection** (*istifhām inkārī*).

| Reading | What *hal min mazīd* means | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **A real question / a request** | "Is there any more?" — Hell **wants more** | Insatiability; the capacity is not exhausted |
| **A question conveying denial** | "Is there room for more?" — that is, **I am full, no room is left** | Fullness; the capacity is exhausted |

**Both readings are transmitted in the classical commentaries and both lie within the bounds the language allows.**

**I can record an observation, not as a preference:** the second reading answers the question directly ("are you full?" — "I am full"). The first gives no answer but states a demand, and this explains better why the question was asked.

**Moreover the Qurʾān uses this expression in another place:** *"I shall surely **fill** Hell with jinn and men together"* (Hūd 11/119; a similar expression at al-Sajda 32/13). **These verses say that the filling will take place**; but they do not settle at which moment the question in Qāf is asked.

**I do not impose a preference.** And this must be stated: **the link that will be built with the thirty-fifth verse is sharper on the first reading and weaker on the second, but it holds either way** — because what makes the link is the word itself, not its sense.

### ٱمْتَلَأْتِ — root م-ل-أ

To be full. *Malaʾa* — he filled; *malʾān* — full; *malaʾ* — the leading men of a people (those who fill an assembly).

**The verb is in the *iftiʿāl* form and in the feminine of the second person:** *imtalaʾti* — "are you full (O Hell)". **The word *jahannam* is treated as feminine in Arabic.**

**And the *iftiʿāl* form adds something here: that the filling has taken place of itself and is complete.**

### مَزِيد — root ز-ي-د

To increase, to grow more. *Ziyāda* — an increase; *mazīd* — increase, more, a greater amount.

**The word may be a *mīm*-verbal noun or a passive participle:** "increasing" or "what is increased". **Both analyses come out at the same place: more.**

### هَلْ مِن — the two questions in the sūra

**This pattern occurs twice in the sūra, and both times as the last words of a verse:**

| Verse | Expression | Who asks | What it seeks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **30** | *hal min **mazīd*** | Hell | **More** |
| **36** | *hal min **maḥīṣ*** | (Of the peoples destroyed) | **A place to escape to** |

**The two questions are each other's opposite:** one wants to take in more, the other looks for a way out.

**I record this as an observation;** both patterns stand in the text and both are in the position of the verse-ending.

## 50/31 — وَأُزْلِفَتِ ٱلْجَنَّةُ لِلْمُتَّقِينَ غَيْرَ بَعِيدٍ

*Wa uzlifati'l-jannatu li'l-muttaqīna ghayra baʿīd*

"And the Garden was brought near to the godfearing, not far off."

### The ring closes

**And the clearest internal link of the sūra is completed here.**

**In the third verse the deniers had said:**

> *dhālika **rajʿun baʿīd*** — "this is a **far** return."

**In the thirty-first verse the sūra says:**

> *uzlifati'l-jannatu li'l-muttaqīna **ghayra baʿīd*** — "…**not far off.**"

**The same word. Once as an objection, once as an answer.**

**And the answer uses the measure the objection used.** The denier had spoken in the measure of distance; the sūra replies in the same measure. **The dispute is carried on not on the plane of "is it possible or not" but on the plane of "is it far or near"** — because the other side had chosen the ground.

**I present this link as my own reading**, but the presence of the two words in the text and their being from the same root are beyond dispute.

**And there is one more thing:** in the third verse *baʿīd* came as **a predicate** (this is a far return). Here *ghayra baʿīd* — in **negated** form.

### أُزْلِفَتْ — root ز-ل-ف

**This root was treated in the sections on al-Mulk and al-Takwīr; I do not repeat it.** Its summary:

> *"Nearness, drawing near. *Zulfa* — nearness, a rank. […] And from this root **مُزْدَلِفَة** — the name of a place in the rites of the pilgrimage; the place of drawing near. […] In the root there is the closing of a distance."*

**And what was recorded for the pattern in the section on al-Takwīr holds here too:**

> *"The pattern: the passive of *ifʿāl* — 'it was brought near'. The Garden, that is, does not come near of itself; **it is brought near.**"*

**What is to be added as belonging to Qāf is this:** in al-Takwīr this verb came in the same scene as the "kindling" of Hell. **In Qāf the bringing near of the Garden comes immediately after Hell's question "is there any more?".**

**The two verses stand one after the other with no transition between them:**

| Verse | Scene | Last word |
|---|---|---|
| **30** | Hell | *hal min **mazīd*** |
| **31** | The Garden | *ghayra **baʿīd*** |

**The sūra sets the two scenes side by side and takes the reader from one to the other in a single step.**

### غَيْرَ بَعِيدٍ — word order

**The word is in the accusative and the grammarians analyse it in different ways:** as the *ḥāl* of *janna*, or as the absolute object of the verb *uzlifat*. **It makes no difference to the sense.**

**And the expression is itself a repetition:** *uzlifat* already means "it was brought near"; *ghayra baʿīd* (not far off) says it once more.

**This repetition is not idle.** In Arabic, saying an idea both positively and negatively (*it was brought near* + *not far*) makes for reinforcement.

**But here it has a second task as well, and I give this as my own reading:** the second expression appears to be placed there in order to bring back the word of the third verse. The repetition, that is, alongside the reinforcement also does the work of **a reference.**

### لِلْمُتَّقِينَ — the godfearing

**Root: و-ق-ي.** To guard, to keep oneself from. **This root was treated in the section on al-Baqara**; I rely on the note there.

**And the word order is striking: the Garden is brought near *to the godfearing*** — not the godfearing to the Garden.

**The direction of the movement makes a contrast with the movement in the scene of Hell:**

| | **Hell (24, 26)** | **The Garden (31)** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *alqiyā* — cast | *uzlifat* — it was brought near |
| Who moves | **The person** — he is cast | **The Garden** — it is brought near |
| Direction | Inward, downward | Towards, face to face |

**I record this contrast as an observation;** the subjects of the two verbs are explicit in the text.

## 50/32-33 — هَٰذَا مَا تُوعَدُونَ لِكُلِّ أَوَّابٍ حَفِيظٍ ۝ مَّنْ خَشِىَ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنَ بِٱلْغَيْبِ وَجَآءَ بِقَلْبٍ مُّنِيبٍ

*Hādhā mā tūʿadūna li-kulli awwābin ḥafīẓ — man khashiya'r-Raḥmāna bi'l-ghaybi wa jāʾa bi-qalbin munīb*

"This is what you were promised: for everyone who turns back often and who keeps guard. — For the one who feared al-Raḥmān unseen and came with a heart that turns."

### هَٰذَا مَا تُوعَدُونَ — the promise fulfilled

**تُوعَدُونَ — root و-ع-د.** And it is the same root as the sūra's chain of *waʿīd*.

**But here the verb is on the positive side:** *tūʿadūn* — "what you are promised". **The same root is used for a threat in the fourteenth and twentieth verses and for a promise here.**

**This is the natural doubleness of the root in Arabic:** *waʿd* (a good promise) and *waʿīd* (a threat) come from the same root. **By naming both with the same root the Qurʾān says something:** a word given, in whichever direction, is a bond of the same kind.

### أَوَّاب — root أ-و-ب

**To turn, to come back.** *Awb* — a return; *iyāb* — a return (in the Qurʾān: *inna ilaynā **iyābahum*** — al-Ghāshiya 88/25).

***Awwāb* is on the *faʿʿāl* pattern: one who turns back much.** As was recorded in the section on al-Burūj, this pattern conveys **multiplicity and continuity**.

**The word describes, that is, not a single return but the making of returning into a habit.**

**This root was treated in the section on al-Ghāshiya**; I rely on the note there.

### حَفِيظ — and the return of the fourth verse

**And now we come to one of the finest links in the sūra.**

**In the fourth verse it was said:** *wa ʿindanā **kitābun ḥafīẓ*** — "with Us there is a **guarding** book."

**In the thirty-second verse the same word, this time of a person:** *li-kulli awwābin **ḥafīẓ*** — "for everyone who turns and who **guards**."

**The same attribute, two subjects:**

| Verse | Who / what | What it guards |
|---|---|---|
| **4** | The book | The record |
| **32** | A person | (See below) |

**And what is it that a person guards?**

The exegetes differ over this, and **the disagreement is a real one:**

| View | What he guards |
|---|---|
| **God's limits** | He observes the commands and prohibitions |
| **His covenant** | He keeps the word he has given |
| **His own sins** | He does not forget what he has done, he keeps its account |
| **His own soul** | He guards himself from what is forbidden |

**All four are transmitted in the classical sources and none excludes the others.**

**And I want to add an observation, as my own reading:** the third view agrees in an interesting way with the repetition from the fourth verse. **There there was a book keeping a record; here a person keeping a record.** Both are *ḥafīẓ*.

**I do not impose this as an interpretation**; the repetition of the word stands in the text, the building of the link is mine.

### خَشِىَ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنَ بِٱلْغَيْبِ

**خَشْيَة — root خ-ش-ي.** To fear; but in Arabic a fear different from *khawf*.

**The distinction the lexicographers record is this:**

| | ***khawf*** | ***khashya*** |
|---|---|---|
| Its source | The danger itself | The **greatness** of what is feared |
| Its kind | A reaction of flight | An awe mingled with reverence |
| Its relation to knowledge | Not required | **It requires knowledge** |

**The Qurʾān uses an expression that supports this distinction:** *"Of His servants only **those who know** feel awe of God"* (Fāṭir 35/28).

**And the name chosen in the verse is itself striking: *al-Raḥmān*.**

**What is held in awe, that is, is not a name of severity but a name of mercy.**

**This is a detail I record as my own reading**, and what it says is this: the state described here is not a fear of punishment. **It is the diffidence felt before a generosity.**

**بِٱلْغَيْب** — "in absence", "though he does not see". **Root: غ-ي-ب.** To be hidden, to pass out of sight. *Ghayba* — absence; *ghība* — speaking behind someone's back (in his absence).

**This verse was already mentioned in the section on al-Infiṭār**, and it was used there in this context: the removal of the situation in which behaviour is unseen, and the demand for **consistency** that arises from it.

**And what the expression says is plain:** it is easy to behave when one is seen. The measure is what is done **when one is not seen.**

### وَجَآءَ بِقَلْبٍ مُّنِيبٍ — and the return of the eighth verse

**مُنِيب — root ن-و-ب.** **It was treated at the eighth verse, and it was recorded there:** *"at the centre of the root is repetition […] *munīb* is not 'one who believes' but 'one who turns'."*

**And the link noted there is completed here:**

| Verse | Expression | What |
|---|---|---|
| **8** | *li-kulli ʿabdin **munīb*** | The condition of the person who can see the proof |
| **33** | *bi-qalbin **munīb*** | The attribute of the person who comes to the Garden |

**The same word: at the beginning a condition, at the end a recompense.**

**And there is a difference between them:** in the eighth verse what was *munīb* was **the servant** (*ʿabdin munīb*); in the thirty-third what is *munīb* is **the heart** (*qalbin munīb*).

**I give this as my own reading:** the turning has descended from a behaviour of the person to an attribute of the heart. **The sūra's movement from outside inward is at work here too.**

### وَجَآءَ — "he came"

**The verb is striking:** the person does not enter the Garden, he **comes** — and he comes with something: *bi-qalbin munīb.*

**What is brought, that is, is not a list of deeds but a heart.**

**And this sets up an echo with the particle *bāʾ* earlier in the sūra:** in the sixteenth verse *tuwaswisu **bi**hī nafsuh* — what the soul whispers. **In both places the particle *bāʾ* points to a content that is carried.**

**I record this echo without forcing it.**

## 50/34 — ٱدْخُلُوهَا بِسَلَٰمٍ ۖ ذَٰلِكَ يَوْمُ ٱلْخُلُودِ

*Udkhulūhā bi-salām; dhālika yawmu'l-khulūd*

"Enter it in peace. That is the day of abiding."

### The command turning to the plural

**The thirty-third verse was singular:** *man khashiya* — "whoever fears". **The thirty-fourth passes to the plural:** *udkhulū* — "enter".

**The description, that is, is singular and the invitation plural.** The condition is set one by one; the door opens to all together.

**I record this as an observation.**

### بِسَلَٰمٍ — root س-ل-م

**Its concrete sense: to be flawless, complete, sound.** *Salāma* — a thing's being unharmed; *salīm* — sound; *islām* — to submit; *silm* — peace.

**At the centre of the root is *completeness*** — and the sense of peace comes from there: peace is the state in which the parties are unharmed.

**And the expression *bi-salām* states a circumstance: "in peace", that is, unharmed.**

**An observation:** in the scenes described throughout the sūra there was always a **diminishing** — what the earth takes away from the body (4), what death closes (19), what the covering hides (22). **Here for the first time completeness is named.**

### ذَٰلِكَ يَوْمُ ٱلْخُلُودِ — the second of three names

**خُلُود — root خ-ل-د.** Abiding, permanence.

**This root was treated in the sections on al-Fajr and al-Humaza.** What was recorded in al-Fajr was this: the same root is used both for the permanence ʿĀd expected from their buildings (*laʿallakum takhludūn* — al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/129) and for the permanence the man in al-Humaza expected from his wealth (*akhladahū* — 104/3).

**And the conclusion reached there was this:** *"what is written on stone and what is written on wealth are the same claim."*

**Where this root is placed in Qāf is striking:** *al-khulūd* is named for the first time not as a **claim** but as a **fact** — and its owner is not a person but a day.

**I give this as my own reading:** what a person wants from stone and from wealth is here the name of a day.

## 50/35 — لَهُم مَّا يَشَآءُونَ فِيهَا وَلَدَيْنَا مَزِيدٌ

*Lahum mā yashāʾūna fīhā wa ladaynā mazīd*

"In it they have whatever they wish; and with Us there is more."

### Two *mazīd*s — the sharpest point of word order in the sūra

**And now we come to the most striking repetition of a word in the sūra.**

**The thirtieth verse ended thus:**

> *wa taqūlu **hal min mazīd*** — Hell says: **is there any more?**

**The thirty-fifth verse ends thus:**

> *wa **ladaynā mazīd*** — with Us there is **more.**

**The same word. Five verses apart. In two opposite scenes.**

**This is a verifiable textual datum.** Now let us look at what it does — and this section is **my own reading.**

### What the word does in the two scenes

| | **Verse 30** | **Verse 35** |
|---|---|---|
| Who says it | Hell | **God** |
| Its place in the clause | A question | A statement |
| Its form | *Hal min* — an interrogative particle | *Ladaynā* — an adverb stating possession |
| What it states | **A want** | **What is possessed** |
| Its consequence | Insatiability | Inexhaustibility |

**And the difference lies exactly here:**

**Hell's *mazīd* is a lack.** Never sated, always wanting, never complete. **Wanting is felt for what one does not have.**

**God's *mazīd* is a surplus.** Unasked for, unrequested, unearned. **And note: the people of the Garden are not asking for it.** The first half of the verse already says: *lahum mā yashāʾūna fīhā* — **whatever they wish is theirs.**

**Wanting, that is, is already met. *Mazīd* comes where wanting *ends*.**

**And this is the finest side of the verse:** *mazīd* is not something asked for. It is something standing **beyond** the capacity to ask.

**The comparison that follows is this:**

- **In Hell: there is demand, and no satisfaction.**
- **In the Garden: there is satisfaction, and more on top of it.**

**Both scenes are built on the idea of "not enough" — but in two opposite directions.** In one the vessel does not fill; in the other what is given does not run out.

**I record this reading as my own reading.** What stands in the text is that the same word is used in the position of the verse-ending in two opposite scenes; the interpretation is mine.

### لَهُم مَّا يَشَآءُونَ فِيهَا

**In the clause *lahum* has been brought forward:** "theirs, in it, is what they wish". In Arabic this fronting establishes **restriction** (*takhṣīṣ*): **it is theirs alone.**

**مَا يَشَآءُونَ** — "what they wish". **The object is indefinite and without limit.** The verse does not enumerate what is given.

**And this is a different choice from sūras such as al-Wāqiʿa that describe the Garden in detail.** Qāf gives no description; it states **capacity**.

**I record this as an observation:** the sūra did not describe Hell either (only a single question), and it does not describe the Garden. **In both cases what is described is not a place but a measure.**

### وَلَدَيْنَا — the last stop of the adverb

**As was recorded above (at verse 23), the adverb *ladā* occurs four times in the sūra, and this is the last.**

**The journey of the adverb is completed thus:** a watcher beside the person (18) → a record beside the *qarīn* (23) → a word that does not change in the presence (28-29) → **a surplus held in store (35).**

**That is, the same adverb is used first for surveillance, then for evidence, then for judgement, and at the last for bounty.**

**I record this as an observation;** all four occurrences stand in the text.

## 50/36 — وَكَمْ أَهْلَكْنَا قَبْلَهُم مِّن قَرْنٍ هُمْ أَشَدُّ مِنْهُم بَطْشًا فَنَقَّبُوا۟ فِى ٱلْبِلَٰدِ هَلْ مِن مَّحِيصٍ

*Wa kam ahlaknā qablahum min qarnin hum ashaddu minhum baṭshan fa-naqqabū fi'l-bilād; hal min maḥīṣ*

"How many a generation We destroyed before them, whose grip was stronger than theirs. They bored through the lands. Is there any place of escape?"

### The second return to history

**Verses twelve to fourteen were a list of names. Here there are no names; there is a measure.**

**كَمْ** — "how many", "how very many". In Arabic *kam khabariyya*: it states multiplicity without giving a number.

### قَرْن — a generation

**Root: ق-ر-ن** — the same root as *qarīn* in the twenty-third verse.

***Qarn* is at the same time a body of people living together** — "those yoked side by side". The word is used both for a generation and for an approximate span of time.

**That the same root is used for two different tasks in the sūra is an observation:** one is the companion beside a person (23, 27), the other the community living in the same age (36).

### بَطْش — root ب-ط-ش

**Its concrete sense: to grasp a thing violently and tightly.** *Baṭasha* — to seize hard; *bāṭish* — one who grips tightly.

**This root was treated in the section on al-Burūj**; I rely on the note there.

**The choice of word matters: power is named not as "strength" (*quwwa*) but as *grip*.** Not an abstract power, that is, but **the capacity to lay hold with the hand.**

**And *ashaddu minhum baṭshan* — "stronger than they in grip".** What is said to the addressee, then, is: **there were those stronger than you.**

### فَنَقَّبُوا۟ فِى ٱلْبِلَٰدِ — root ن-ق-ب

**And this is the most concrete word in the verse.**

**The primary sense of the root: to bore, to make a hole.**

- **نَقَبَ الجِدَار** — he bored through the wall.
- **نَقْب** — a hole made in a wall; a tunnel.
- **نَقِيبَة** — one who penetrates into a group; *naqīb* — the chief of a people, the one who knows their affairs.
- **مِنْقَب** — a boring tool.
- **نِقَاب** — a veil that covers the face; but from the same root, because **it leaves a hole for the eye.**

**And the verb is in the *tafʿīl* form: *naqqabū* — to bore much, again and again.**

**The exegetes differ over what they did:**

| View | What they did |
|---|---|
| **They roamed the earth** | They travelled from land to land, they opened up roads; here *naqb* is "to open a way, to pass through" |
| **They riddled the lands** | Conquest and destruction; they broke into cities |
| **They searched for a way out** | The question at the end of the verse supports this reading: they bored, but did they find a place to escape to? |

**All three readings are transmitted.** The third makes the tightest link with the verse's last clause, and this is an observation, not a preference.

### And now a link — my own reading

**This verb has a counterpart elsewhere in the sūra, and I present it as my own reading.**

| Verse | Who | What is done | Root |
|---|---|---|---|
| **36** | **Human beings** | They **bore through** the earth | ن-ق-ب |
| **44** | **The earth** | It **splits open** from them | ش-ق-ق |

**A person bores through the earth; in the end the earth splits open from the person.**

**The text does not build this link; I build it, and I record it as such.** But the object and the subject of the two verbs stand in the text and have changed places exactly.

### هَلْ مِن مَّحِيصٍ — a place of escape

**Root: ح-ي-ص.** Its concrete sense: **to slip away sideways, to shun, to turn and flee.** *Ḥāṣa ʿan* — he turned away from it and fled.

**And the root is very close in sense to *taḥīd* (ح-ي-د) in the nineteenth verse:** both state **swerving aside.**

**That the two roots are sometimes named together in the Qurʾān has drawn the attention of the lexicographers as well.** Both are present in Qāf:

| Verse | Word | Root | Escape from what |
|---|---|---|---|
| **19** | *taḥīd* | ح-ي-د | From death |
| **36** | *maḥīṣ* | ح-ي-ص | From the reckoning |

**I record this as an observation; I do not claim that the two roots are the same.** Their closeness is at the level of meaning, not of derivation. **A similar caution was set down in the section on al-Insān:** derivation is not to be inferred from resemblance of sound.

***Maḥīṣ* may be a noun of place or a *mīm*-verbal noun:** "a place of escape" or "escape". **Both analyses give the same result.**

**And the question is left unanswered.** In the thirtieth verse Hell's question was not answered; here too this question is not answered. **The sūra leaves both questions hanging.**

## 50/37 — إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَذِكْرَىٰ لِمَن كَانَ لَهُۥ قَلْبٌ أَوْ أَلْقَى ٱلسَّمْعَ وَهُوَ شَهِيدٌ

*Inna fī dhālika la-dhikrā li-man kāna lahū qalbun aw alqa's-samʿa wa huwa shahīd*

"In that there is a reminder for whoever has a heart, or gives ear while he is present."

### Two separate roads

**This verse is one of the Qurʾān's plainest statements about understanding, and it is built on a single particle: أَوْ (or).**

**Two conditions are named, and between them there is not "and" but "or":**

1. *Man kāna lahū **qalb*** — one who has a heart.
2. *Aw **alqa's-samʿa** wa huwa shahīd* — or one who gives his ear while present.

**Two separate doors, that is. Entering by one is enough.**

### قَلْب — and what the root carries

**Root: ق-ل-ب. This root was treated in the sections on al-Mulk and al-Inshiqāq.** The note in al-Mulk was this:

> *"This is one of the most heavily laden roots in Arabic: to turn a thing over, to overturn it, to change its face. From the same root comes **قَلْب (*qalb*)** — and the heart is given this name because it is the thing that turns, changes, passes from state to state. Arabic has named the inner centre of a human being not as 'what is fixed' but as 'what is turned'."*

**And now let us look at what this does in this verse.**

**The expression "whoever has a heart" is odd at first sight.** There is no human being without a heart.

**But the word describes not the organ but the function the root describes: *the thing that can turn*.**

**The condition, then, is this, and I give it as my own reading:** in order to understand, an inside that **can be turned** is required. The capacity of something to turn you.

**And this is directly linked with the word *munīb* in the sūra:** the root *nawb* also stated turning (8, 33). **Three verses build the same idea with two different roots:**

| Verse | Word | Root | What |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | *ʿabdin **munīb*** | ن-و-ب | A servant who turns |
| 33 | ***qalbin munīb*** | ق-ل-ب + ن-و-ب | **The two together** |
| 37 | *lahū **qalb*** | ق-ل-ب | An inside that can turn |

**And that the thirty-third verse joins the two roots is striking:** *qalbin munīb* — "a turning heart", that is, "a turning thing that turns".

**I record this as an observation;** all three occurrences stand in the text.

### أَلْقَى ٱلسَّمْعَ — to give the ear

**And the root ل-ق-ي comes for the fourth and last time in the sūra.**

***Ilqāʾ* — to throw, to let go, to place.** And here its object is **hearing**: *alqa's-samʿa* — "he threw / laid down his hearing".

**The English "to give ear" is an exact counterpart, and it too is a verb of giving.**

**The picture in the word is this:** the faculty of hearing is taken up and **set down** somewhere. Not hearing, that is, but **directing one's hearing to a place.**

**And read together with the root's other uses in the sūra, the verb says something:**

| Verse | Who throws | What is thrown |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | God | Mountains |
| 24, 26 | The angels | A human being |
| **37** | **A human being** | **His own hearing** |

**The only human being in the sūra who throws something with this root is in the thirty-seventh verse.** And what he throws is his own attention.

**I record this as my own reading;** the four occurrences stand in the text, the comparison is mine.

### وَهُوَ شَهِيدٌ — "while present"

**And the condition is completed: giving the ear is not enough; one must be *shahīd*.**

**As was recorded above (at verse 21), at the centre of the root ش-ه-د is *being present*.**

**The expression says, that is: a person who has given his ear but whose mind is elsewhere does not meet this condition.**

**The exegetes explain *shahīd* here as follows:** present in heart, attentive, not heedless. **And this makes a direct contrast with *ghafla* in the twenty-second verse.**

| Verse | State | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **22** | *fī **ghafla*** | He did not see |
| **37** | *wa huwa **shahīd*** | He heard |

**Both words stand on the axis of "being present":** *ghafla* is having left no trace; *shahāda* is being there.

### The distinction between the two roads — my own reading

**Now I come to the most important side of the verse, and I present this plainly as my own reading.**

**The two conditions named describe two different kinds of person:**

| | **The first road** | **The second road** |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | *Lahū qalb* | *Alqa's-samʿa wa huwa shahīd* |
| Source | **From within** | **From without** |
| How | He turns of himself, he grasps | He takes it in by listening |
| What it requires | Understanding | **Attention** |
| What is active | The inner centre | The ear |

**And what the verse says is this: either one suffices.**

**It is worth saying why this matters.**

Understanding is not the same in everyone. Before a text, a proof, a scene in nature, not everyone sees the same thing. **If the only road were "whoever has a heart", understanding would be a matter of aptitude.**

**The second road sets aptitude aside and puts *attention* in its place.** Whoever can give his ear and be there — whatever his understanding — is inside the door.

**And attention, unlike aptitude, is something everyone can do.** It is not something given to you but something you give.

**I record this reading as my own reading.** What stands in the text is the particle *aw* (or), and its showing that each of the two conditions suffices on its own.

### ذِكْرَىٰ — the return of the eighth verse

**And one more ring of the sūra closes.**

**In the eighth verse it was said of the plants and the sky:** *tabṣiratan wa **dhikrā** li-kulli ʿabdin munīb.*

**In the thirty-seventh verse the same word is used of history:** *la-**dhikrā** li-man kāna lahū qalb.*

**The two sections of proof in the sūra — nature (6-11) and history (12-14, 36) — are thus concluded with the same word.**

| Verse | Proof | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **8** | Sky, earth, plants | *tabṣira* + *dhikrā* |
| **37** | The generations destroyed | *dhikrā* |

**And there is a difference:** the eighth verse had **two** words (making-to-see + reminder), the thirty-seventh **one** (reminder).

**I give this as my own reading, and there is a reasonable ground for it:** nature **is seen**, history **is not.** What befell past generations can only be told and remembered. **That *tabṣira* is in the eighth verse and not here, then, agrees with the kind of the proofs.**

## 50/38 — وَلَقَدْ خَلَقْنَا ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضَ وَمَا بَيْنَهُمَا فِى سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ وَمَا مَسَّنَا مِن لُّغُوبٍ

*Wa laqad khalaqna's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍa wa mā baynahumā fī sittati ayyāmin wa mā massanā min lughūb*

"We created the heavens and the earth and what is between them in six days, and no weariness touched Us."

### فِى سِتَّةِ أَيَّامٍ — six days

**This expression was treated at length in the section on al-Ḥadīd 57/4; I do not repeat it.** The summary of the findings there:

> *"The word **يَوْم** in Arabic does not mean only 'a day of twenty-four hours'. The primary sense of the root is a span of time, a period. […] how long the six 'days' lasted has not been stated and is not known. To close this indeterminacy and try to match it one to one with the periods of modern cosmology or geology is the forced scientific matching that the method (`STYLE.md`) forbids."*

**That note holds here too and is not repeated.**

**What belongs to Qāf is this: the clause that follows the expression here is different.** In al-Ḥadīd the "six days" were followed by the *istiwāʾ*, the being-with, and the watching. **Here what follows is the denial of weariness.**

### لُغُوب — root ل-غ-ب

**Its concrete sense: to grow tired, to be worn out, to have one's strength give out.**

- **لَغَبَ / لَغِبَ** — he grew tired, he was worn out.
- **لُغُوب** — weariness; especially **the exhaustion that comes after a task.**
- **لَاغِب** — weary.

**The lexicographers compare the word with *taʿab* (growing tired) and explain *lughūb* as its consequence: *taʿab* is the strain within the work itself, *lughūb* the exhaustion left after** the work.

**This distinction is not firm and the lexicographers do not draw it the same way everywhere;** but it is transmitted and it agrees with the verse: **the state after the work is done** is what is named.

**And the negation is again complete:** *mā massanā **min** lughūb* — here too *min* completes the negation: **not any weariness at all.**

**مَسّ — root م-س-س.** To touch. The choice of word is striking: it does not say *"We did not grow tired"*, it says **"weariness did not touch Us"**. **Weariness is described as something that comes from outside and makes contact, and that contact does not happen.**

### The link with the fifteenth verse is completed

**The link recorded above (at verse 15) closes here, and it can be verified:**

| Verse | Expression | Root | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **15** | *a-fa-**ʿayīnā** bi'l-khalqi'l-awwal* | ع-ي-ي | A question |
| **38** | *wa mā massanā min **lughūb*** | ل-غ-ب | A statement |

**Both verses say the same thing: creating brought God no burden.**

**And the difference between the two agrees with their places in the sūra:** the fifteenth verse stands **in the section of proofs**, in the form of a question; the thirty-eighth **in the closing section**, in the form of a verdict.

**The sūra asks the same claim first and answers it afterwards.** I record this as an observation.

### Historical background — careful and without a claim

**In the classical commentaries a background is recorded for the expression at the end of this verse, and it would be a defect to pass it by without transmitting it. But how it is transmitted matters.**

**What is recorded is this:** some exegetes say that the expression *"no weariness touched Us"* is an answer to an understanding according to which God rested after the creation.

**Now several things need to be said in order to put this note in its place, and I say each of them separately, marking its measure.**

**One. In the creation narrative of the Torah, rest on the seventh day is spoken of.** At Genesis 2:2-3 it is related that God finished His work on the seventh day, rested, and hallowed that day. **This is the text's own statement and is verifiable information.**

**Two. How this expression is to be understood has been separately debated within the traditions that hold that text sacred**, and those traditions have their own views interpreting "rest". **In this commentary no blanket ruling is issued about the text or the tradition of another religion** — this is the principle written in the method (`STYLE.md`). **I therefore frame no sentence of the form "such and such a religion believes thus".**

**Three. It is not certain that the verse is answering a particular text.** The Qurʾān uses this expression not in a context of debate but within its own chain of proofs: it completes the question of the fifteenth verse. **I transmit no report as an occasion of revelation**, because I cannot vouch for its soundness.

**Four. And this distinction must be recorded:** "resting" and "growing tired" are not the same thing. That a text speaks of rest does not necessarily mean that it speaks of weariness.

**Five. What the verse does within itself is plain and not disputed:** the denial of **human limits** with respect to God. To grow tired, to have one's strength give out, to need to recover after a task — these are the states of finite beings.

**And this agrees with a principle the Qurʾān repeats:** *"There is nothing like Him"* (al-Shūrā 42/11). **The same principle was recorded in the section on al-Fajr.**

**I give these five points to this extent and in this order, because the historical side of the matter is real but what can be claimed is limited.**

### And the work the verse does in the sūra

**This verse takes aim at one of the oldest objections to the resurrection.**

The objection is this: creating is a hard work; it has been done once; doing it a second time requires fresh power.

**The verse removes the premise:** the first creation entailed no burden. **There is therefore no question of gathering power to repeat it.**

**This is the last link of the chain of proofs the sūra has been building from the beginning:**

| Verses | Proof | Its kind |
|---|---|---|
| 6-11 | Sky, earth, plants, dead ground | **Observation** |
| 12-14, 36 | The peoples destroyed | **History** |
| 15 | The first creation | **Reasoning (a question)** |
| **38** | **The six days and the absence of weariness** | **Reasoning (a statement)** |

**And from the thirty-ninth verse the proofs end; the commands begin.**

## 50/39-40 — فَٱصْبِرْ عَلَىٰ مَا يَقُولُونَ وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ قَبْلَ طُلُوعِ ٱلشَّمْسِ وَقَبْلَ ٱلْغُرُوبِ ۝ وَمِنَ ٱلَّيْلِ فَسَبِّحْهُ وَأَدْبَٰرَ ٱلسُّجُودِ

*Fa'ṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūna wa sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika qabla ṭulūʿi'sh-shamsi wa qabla'l-ghurūb — wa mina'l-layli fa-sabbiḥhu wa adbāra's-sujūd*

"So be patient with what they say; and glorify your Lord with praise before the rising of the sun and before its setting. — And glorify Him in part of the night, and after the prostrations."

### فَ — the particle that ties the consequence

**The verse begins with *fāʾ*: "so".** Everything recounted up to the thirty-eighth verse, that is, issues in what follows.

**And the consequence that issues is striking.** After the chain of proofs is complete, the command given to the Prophet is not **to go on arguing**; it is **to be patient and to glorify.**

**I record this as my own reading:** the sūra puts into practice here what it will say plainly in the forty-fifth verse. The proof has been presented; making it accepted is not the task.

### ٱصْبِرْ — root ص-ب-ر

**Its concrete sense: to hold a thing in a place, to confine it, to tie it.** *Ṣabara'd-dābba* — he tethered the animal in a place. *Ṣabr* — to hold the self in a place. *Ṣabbāra* — hard, enduring rock.

**Patience in Arabic, then, is not a "bearing" but an act of holding.** A person holds himself in a place.

**And the object of the command is specified: *ʿalā mā yaqūlūn* — "with what they say".** What is to be borne, that is, is not an affliction but **speech.**

### مَا يَقُولُونَ — and the link with the forty-fifth verse

**This expression occurs twice in the sūra, with five verses between them:**

| Verse | Expression | Command / statement |
|---|---|---|
| **39** | *fa'ṣbir ʿalā **mā yaqūlūn*** | **You:** be patient |
| **45** | *naḥnu aʿlamu bi-**mā yaqūlūn*** | **We:** We know |

**The same expression, with two different subjects.** What falls to you is patience; the knowing is Ours.

**This is a repetition that can be verified, and it prepares the close of the sūra.**

### وَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ — glorification

**سُبْحَان — root س-ب-ح.** Its concrete sense: **to swim in water or in air, to move swiftly along.** *Sabaḥa fi'l-māʾ* — he swam in the water. The Qurʾān uses this root of the heavenly bodies: *"each swimming in an orbit"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/33 — *yasbaḥūn*).

**And *tasbīḥ* derives from this sense of the root: to make a thing *flow away* from what does not befit it, to declare it free of it.**

**Glorification, that is, is before being praise an act of setting apart: keeping God clear of everything that does not befit Him.**

**And its place in this sūra is meaningful.** What did the preceding verse say? *"No weariness touched Us."* — that is, a sentence of *tanzīh*. **And immediately afterwards comes the command to glorify.**

**I record this link as my own reading:** the thirty-eighth verse gives the declaring-free as a statement, the thirty-ninth commands the same work as an **act**.

**بِحَمْدِ — root ح-م-د.** To praise; and, **as was treated in the sections on al-Baqara and al-Fātiḥa**, *ḥamd* is the praise given for a good done by will.

**The expression "glorify with praise" joins two movements:** a putting at a distance (*tanzīh*) and a drawing near (praise). **One says "He is not so", the other "He is thus".**

### The times, and the disagreement

**Four times are named in the verses:**

1. *Qabla ṭulūʿi'sh-shams* — before the rising of the sun.
2. *Wa qabla'l-ghurūb* — before its setting.
3. *Wa mina'l-layl* — in part of the night.
4. *Wa adbāra's-sujūd* — after the prostrations.

**In the classical commentaries it is widely said that these times point to the prayers**, and the following correspondences are transmitted:

| Time | The counterpart transmitted |
|---|---|
| Before the rising of the sun | The dawn prayer |
| Before its setting | The noon and afternoon prayers; or the afternoon prayer alone |
| In part of the night | The sunset and night prayers; or the night vigil |
| After the prostrations | The supererogatory prayers said after the obligatory ones; or the *witr* |

**These correspondences are transmitted in the classical sources and the exegetes differ among themselves.** In this commentary **no legal ruling is issued**; what is transmitted is passed on.

**And this note is necessary:** the wording of the verse does not say prayer, it says **glorification**. Prayer is the most comprehensive form of glorification, but the wording of the command is wider.

### أَدْبَٰرَ ٱلسُّجُودِ — a variant reading

**Here there is a genuine variant reading:**

| Reading | Analysis | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **أَدْبَٰرَ** (with *fatḥa*) | The plural of *dubur* | "The **backs of / after** the prostrations" |
| **إِدْبَٰرَ** (with *kasra*) | The verbal noun *idbār* | "At the **ending / conclusion** of the prostrations" |

**Both readings are transmitted in the sources on the variant readings. I make no definite ascription as to which imam held which reading.**

**The difference in sense is small and both point to the same time:** after the end of the prostration or of the prayer.

**A comparison must be recorded:** at al-Ṭūr 52/49 a similar expression comes in the form with *kasra*: *wa **idbāra**'n-nujūm* — "at the withdrawing of the stars". **This expression was treated in the section on al-Ṭūr.**

**Root: د-ب-ر.** The back, the end. *Dubur* — the back; *idbār* — to turn one's back, to withdraw; *tadbīr* — to think out the end of a matter; *mudabbir* — the one who orders its end.

## 50/41 — وَٱسْتَمِعْ يَوْمَ يُنَادِ ٱلْمُنَادِ مِن مَّكَانٍ قَرِيبٍ

*Wa'stamiʿ yawma yunādi'l-munādi min makānin qarīb*

"And listen: on the day the caller calls from a near place."

### وَٱسْتَمِعْ — giving ear to something not yet heard

**اِسْتِمَاع — root س-م-ع, the *istifʿāl* form.** And this form adds **intent and effort** here: *samʿ* is to hear, *istimāʿ* is **to give ear**.

**And the object of the command is unusual: something that will happen in the future.**

*Wa'stamiʿ **yawma** yunādi'l-munādi* — "listen: on the day…"

**A sound that has not yet happened, that is, is what he is asked to listen for.**

**The grammarians resolve this as follows:** the object of the verb *istamiʿ* has been elided ("listen to this") and *yawma* is an adverb of time. **That is, "listen to this: on the day…"** The sense is to draw attention to what is about to be related.

**But its effect is striking all the same, and I give this as my own reading:** the command is framed as though it asked not for listening to a report but **for waiting for a sound.**

**And it has a link with the thirty-seventh verse:** there it was said *alqa's-samʿ* (he gave his ear). **Here the same root comes in the imperative.** What was described in the thirty-seventh is commanded in the forty-first.

| Verse | Expression | Root | Mood |
|---|---|---|---|
| **37** | *alqa's-**samʿa*** | س-م-ع | Statement |
| **41** | *wa'**stamiʿ*** | س-م-ع | **Command** |

**I record this as an observation.**

### يُنَادِ ٱلْمُنَادِ — the caller

**Root: ن-د-ي / ن-د-و.** To call out, to summon — **especially from a distance.** *Nidāʾ* — calling from afar; *nādī* — a meeting place (where people call to one another); *nadwa* — an assembly (the *Dār al-Nadwa* in Mecca is from this root).

**At the centre of the root is *calling from a distance*.**

**And what the verse says turns exactly that round:** calling is a verb that requires distance, but the call comes **from a near place.**

**I record this contrast as an observation;** the tension between the root sense of the word and its qualification in the verse stands in the text.

**A note on the spelling:** the final *yāʾ* has dropped from both *yunādi* and *al-munādi* (not *yunādī* / *al-munādī*). **This is an elision made for the sake of the verse-ending** and is the same case as the one recorded for *waʿīd* in the fourteenth verse.

### مِن مَّكَانٍ قَرِيبٍ — from a near place

**And the sūra's axis of distance works for the last time.**

| Verse | Expression | Who / what |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | *rajʿun **baʿīd*** | The resurrection (a claim) |
| 16 | *naḥnu **aqrabu** ilayhi* | God — to a person |
| 27 | *ḍalālin **baʿīd*** | Error |
| 31 | *ghayra **baʿīd*** | The Garden |
| **41** | *min makānin **qarīb*** | **The call** |

**The sūra opened with an objection that began with "far" and closes with a scene that ends with "near".**

**This is a verifiable sequence of words; the claim that it forms an order of reply is my own reading.**

### Where is this "near place"?

**In the commentaries there are reports naming particular places.**

**I do not transmit these reports,** because I cannot vouch for their soundness and the Qurʾān gives no detail on the matter.

**The only thing the text says is: near.** And this qualification implies that the distance is near **for everyone** — because the call comes from a single place and everyone hears it.

**And this is again linked with the sixteenth verse:** there the nearness reached down **into the person's own inside.** Here the place the call comes from is near. **Read together, the two verses yield this: there is no distance anywhere.**

**I record this as my own reading.**

## 50/42 — يَوْمَ يَسْمَعُونَ ٱلصَّيْحَةَ بِٱلْحَقِّ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ يَوْمُ ٱلْخُرُوجِ

*Yawma yasmaʿūna'ṣ-ṣayḥata bi'l-ḥaqq; dhālika yawmu'l-khurūj*

"On that day they will hear the cry with the truth. That is the day of coming forth."

### ٱلصَّيْحَة — root ص-ي-ح

**Its concrete sense: to shout aloud, to cry out.** *Ṣayḥa* — a single cry (the *faʿla* pattern conveys a single occurrence); *ṣiyāḥ* — shouting; *ṣāḥa'd-dīk* — the cock crowed.

**The Qurʾān uses this word in scenes of destruction:** of Thamūd, Madyan and other peoples it is said *"the cry seized them"* (as at Hūd 11/67 and 94).

**The usual use of the word in the Qurʾān, that is, is a word of destruction.**

**And here the same word is used for the call of the resurrection.**

**I record this as an observation:** the same sound put an end to peoples in history; here it makes a beginning.

### بِٱلْحَقِّ — for the third time

**The last of the three *bi'l-ḥaqq*s set out in a table above:**

| Verse | Expression |
|---|---|
| 5 | *kadhdhabū **bi'l-ḥaqq*** |
| 19 | *jāʾat sakratu'l-mawti **bi'l-ḥaqq*** |
| **42** | *yasmaʿūna'ṣ-ṣayḥata **bi'l-ḥaqq*** |

**What was denied in the fifth verse comes back with death in the nineteenth and with the cry in the forty-second.**

**And the expression is striking: the cry is heard "with the truth".** Hearing, that is, is no longer something open to interpretation. **No dispute remains as to what is heard.**

### ذَٰلِكَ يَوْمُ ٱلْخُرُوجِ — the ring closes

**And the most important internal link of the sūra is completed here.**

**In the eleventh verse, after the coming out of a plant from dead ground had been described, it was said:**

> *kadhālika'l-**khurūj*** — "such is the **coming forth**."

**In the forty-second verse the same word comes, this time as the name of a day:**

> *dhālika yawmu'l-**khurūj*** — "that is the day of **coming forth**."

**The same word, the same definiteness, the same pattern of demonstrative (*kadhālika* / *dhālika*).**

**And thirty-one verses lie between them.**

**The sūra gave a definition in the eleventh verse and in the forty-second placed that definition as the name of a day.**

**The structure of the proof is thus completed:**

| Step | Verses | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6-10 | The things to be looked at are listed |
| 2 | 11 | Dead ground revives → ***kadhālika'l-khurūj*** |
| 3 | 12-38 | History, death, the reckoning, the Garden and Hell |
| 4 | **42** | ***yawmu'l-khurūj*** — the day is given its name |

**And this completes what the sūra has been building from the start:** the addressee finds the name of the thing he called "far" in the name of what happens in his own field.

**I present this as my own reading;** the places of the two words in the text and their being from the same root are beyond dispute.

**One more note:** the word *khurūj* has been chosen instead of "resurrection" (*baʿth*) or "spreading out" (*nashr*). **And *khurūj* is more concrete than either: to come out.** The sūra names the most abstract event with the most concrete verb.

## 50/43 — إِنَّا نَحْنُ نُحْىِۦ وَنُمِيتُ وَإِلَيْنَا ٱلْمَصِيرُ

*Innā naḥnu nuḥyī wa numītu wa ilayna'l-maṣīr*

"It is We, We who give life and give death; and to Us is the journey's end."

### A threefold reinforcement

**The clause is built with one of the densest structures of emphasis in Arabic:**

1. **إِنَّ** — the particle of emphasis.
2. **نَا** — "we" (the noun of *inna*).
3. **نَحْنُ** — a further pronoun (the *ḍamīr al-faṣl*, or a pronoun of emphasis).
4. **نُحْيِي** — the verb, again in the first person plural.

**"We", that is, is said three times.** In Arabic this structure establishes **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*, "only"): **giving life and giving death belong to Us alone.**

### The order: giving life first

**And something in the word order is striking: *nuḥyī* (We give life) comes first, *numīt* (We give death) second.**

**This is also the natural order of life** — first to live, then to die. But the Qurʾān sometimes uses these two verbs in the reverse order as well (al-Mulk 67/2: *alladhī **khalaqa'l-mawta** wa'l-ḥayāt* — who created death and life; death first).

**That ordering was dwelt on in the section on al-Mulk.**

**In Qāf the order runs in the natural direction and agrees with the context:** the sūra is at this moment describing **the resurrection**. **In the forty-second verse people came out of the earth; the forty-third says "giving life is Our work".**

### ٱلْمَصِير — root ص-ي-ر

**Its concrete sense: to pass from one state into another, to become.** *Ṣāra* — he became, he came to be. *Ṣayrūra* — becoming. *Maṣīr* — the place arrived at, the end returned to.

**Here is the interesting side of the word:** the root states not a place but **a change of state**. *Maṣīr* is, before "the place one goes to", **"the state one will come to be in"**.

**And there is a fronting in the word order: *ilaynā* has come before the verb (or the predicate).** This again establishes **restriction**: **the returning is to Us alone.**

## 50/44 — يَوْمَ تَشَقَّقُ ٱلْأَرْضُ عَنْهُمْ سِرَاعًا ۚ ذَٰلِكَ حَشْرٌ عَلَيْنَا يَسِيرٌ

*Yawma tashaqqaqu'l-arḍu ʿanhum sirāʿā; dhālika ḥashrun ʿalaynā yasīr*

"On that day the earth splits open from them, and they come forth in haste. That is a gathering easy for Us."

### تَشَقَّقُ — root ش-ق-ق

**This root was treated in the section on al-Inshiqāq, and the verse before us was taken into a table there.** The note there was this:

> *"In this root there is neither birth nor building. What happens here is **the breaking apart of a whole**. The other branches of the root — *mashaqqa* (wearing hardship), *shiqāq* (division) — all face the same way: something was holding together, and it holds no longer."*

**And in the table in al-Inshiqāq this note was set down for this verse:** *"the earth splits open from them → **to the coming forth of the dead**."*

**Here I add what belongs to Qāf.**

**The verb is in the *tafaʿʿul* form and carries a reflexivity close to a passive: *tashaqqaqu* — "it keeps splitting", "it splits piece by piece".** The *tafaʿʿul* form may add **gradualness** here as well: not all at once, but splitting and splitting.

**And the particle matters: *tashaqqaqu'l-arḍu ʿanhum* — "the earth splits from them".**

**The particle *ʿan* states separation.** The earth, that is, opens **off them** — as though a covering over them were being drawn away.

**And this sets up an echo with the twenty-second verse:**

| Verse | Expression | Particle | What is lifted |
|---|---|---|---|
| **22** | *kashafnā **ʿanka** ghiṭāʾak* | *ʿan* | The covering before the gaze |
| **44** | *tashaqqaqu'l-arḍu **ʿanhum*** | *ʿan* | The soil over the body |

**The same particle, the same movement: an opening from above.**

**I present this as my own reading;** the particle and the verb structure in the two verses stand in the text.

### سِرَاعًا — in haste

**Root: س-ر-ع.** To be swift. *Surʿa* — speed; *sarīʿ* — swift; *sirāʿ* — the plural of *sarīʿ*.

**The word is in the accusative as a circumstantial and in the plural: "as swift ones".** It states the condition of those who come out.

**And it sets up a contrast:** death and lying in the earth are a **motionless** state; the coming forth is **swift**.

**The Qurʾān records this haste in other places too:** *"They come out of their graves running"* (expressions such as al-Maʿārij 70/43). **That scene was treated in the section on al-Maʿārij.**

### حَشْرٌ عَلَيْنَا يَسِيرٌ

**حَشْر — root ح-ش-ر.** To gather into a place, to drive together. In the concrete sense of the root there is **the driving together of what is scattered**.

**يَسِير — root ي-س-ر.** To be easy. *Yusr* — ease; *maysara* — abundance, means; *yasār* — the left hand (the less used hand in Arabic).

**And here the line built in the fifteenth and thirty-eighth verses is completed:**

| Verse | Expression | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **15** | *a-fa-ʿayīnā* | Were We wearied? |
| **38** | *mā massanā min lughūb* | No weariness touched Us |
| **44** | *ḥashrun **ʿalaynā yasīr*** | **Easy for Us** |

**Three verses say the same idea with three different words in three different places: a question, a negation, an affirmation.**

**And a note is needed** — it was set down at the fifteenth verse too: **no distinction between "easy" and "hard" is drawn with respect to God.** Such expressions speak in the addressee's measure. The note set down for *"that is easier for Him"* at al-Rūm 30/27 holds here as well.

**And there is a fronting in the word order: *ʿalaynā* (for Us) has been brought forward.** What is stressed, that is, is **for whom** it is easy: not for you, but **for Us**.

## 50/45 — نَّحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَقُولُونَ ۖ وَمَآ أَنتَ عَلَيْهِم بِجَبَّارٍ ۖ فَذَكِّرْ بِٱلْقُرْءَانِ مَن يَخَافُ وَعِيدِ

*Naḥnu aʿlamu bimā yaqūlūn; wa mā anta ʿalayhim bi-jabbār; fa-dhakkir bi'l-Qurʾāni man yakhāfu waʿīd*

"We know best what they say. You are not a tyrant over them. So remind with the Qurʾān whoever fears My threat."

### The way the sūra ends

**This verse shows a choice about how the sūra ends, and the choice is striking.**

**For forty-four verses the sūra has spoken of resurrection, reckoning, Hell and the Garden.** One would expect such a sūra to end with a warning, a threat or a summons.

**But the last verse is not a warning; it draws a *limit*.** And the limit is not the addressee's — it is **the messenger's.**

**I record this as my own reading**, but the one addressed in the verse is explicit in the text: *wa mā **anta**…* — "you".

### نَّحْنُ أَعْلَمُ بِمَا يَقُولُونَ

**The link with the thirty-ninth verse was recorded above and is completed here:**

| Verse | Expression | Whose part |
|---|---|---|
| **39** | *fa'ṣbir ʿalā **mā yaqūlūn*** | You: **be patient** |
| **45** | *naḥnu aʿlamu bi-**mā yaqūlūn*** | We: **We know** |

**And the division of work between the two clauses is plain:** what you are to do about what they say is to be patient; **assessing what they say is not your task.**

**This is a sentence of consolation, and there are others like it in the Qurʾān.** The finding transmitted from al-Baqara in the section on al-Ghāshiya is at work here too: *"your task is to convey, not to produce the outcome."*

### وَمَآ أَنتَ عَلَيْهِم بِجَبَّارٍ — the use of the word *jabbār* of a human being

**Root: ج-ب-ر. This root was treated at length in the section on al-Ḥashr** (in the treatment of *al-Jabbār* among the names of God) **and I do not repeat the findings there.** Their summary:

> *"The root sense the lexicographers give is twofold and both senses are alive in Arabic: **1. To set a break, to mend.** […] **2. To compel, to force into submission.** […] The link between the two senses is this: to set a broken bone is also to force the bone back into its place."*

**And this was also recorded in the section on al-Ḥashr, and it is directly at work here:**

> *"**The use of the word of a human being is always negative in the Qurʾān.** At Maryam 19/32, *wa lam yajʿalnī **jabbāran** shaqiyyā*; at al-Qaṣaṣ 28/19, *in turīdu illā an takūna **jabbāran** fi'l-arḍ*. […] The same word is blamed in a human being and appears as praise in God. This is not a coincidence but a rule of the language: the worth of an attribute depends on whether the one who bears it has a right to it."*

**Now I add what belongs to Qāf.**

**One. The word is negated here — and the person of whom it is negated is the Prophet.**

The verse does not blame the word and turn it against others. **To the messenger, the one addressed in the sūra, it says "you are not this".**

**And the form of the negation is emphatic: *mā anta … bi-jabbār*. The بِ at the head is again a particle of emphasis: you are certainly not.**

**Two. The link with the twenty-fourth verse — recorded above and to be recalled here.**

The Qurʾān uses the word *ʿanīd* together with *jabbār* in two places (*jabbārin ʿanīd* — Hūd 11/59; Ibrāhīm 14/15). **This sūra separates the two words:** *ʿanīd* is an attribute of the denier (24), while *jabbār* is the attribute **denied** of the messenger (45).

**I present this link as my own reading.**

**Three. A comparison with al-Ghāshiya 88/22.**

**This verse was already mentioned in the section on al-Ghāshiya**, where the word *musayṭir* was treated. The finding there:

> *"To be a **musayṭir** is a position the Qurʾān grants to no one. Neither are the addressees in that position (al-Ṭūr), nor the messenger (al-Ghāshiya)."*

**And the distinction drawn there holds here too:**

> *"a finding states a state of affairs, while the negation of an attribute states an **authority**. Al-Ghāshiya says not that the outcome cannot be produced but that the **authority** to produce it has not been given."*

**Qāf 50/45 does the same work as al-Ghāshiya 88/22, and the two words complete one another:**

| Sūra | Word | Root | The position denied |
|---|---|---|---|
| al-Ghāshiya 88/22 | *musayṭir* | س-ط-ر | **One who supervises, who stands over** |
| **Qāf 50/45** | ***jabbār*** | ج-ب-ر | **One who compels, who forces into submission** |

**Together the two remove two distinct authorities:** the authority to supervise and the authority to **compel**.

### The rejection of compulsion in the name of religion

**And now we come to the plainest consequence of this verse.**

**The verse says that the messenger has no authority to compel.** And this is not a sentence standing alone in the Qurʾān; **it is part of a line:**

- *"There is no compulsion in religion"* (al-Baqara 2/256).
- *"You are not a *musayṭir* over them"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/22).
- *"Had your Lord willed, everyone on earth would have believed, all together. Will you then **compel** people until they become believers?"* (Yūnus 10/99).
- *"You cannot guide those you love"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/56).
- *"Remind; you are only one who reminds"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/21).

**In the section on al-Ghāshiya the logic of the verse from Yūnus was recorded thus:**

> *"if faith were wanted by way of compulsion, that could have been done directly. Since it was not done, what is wanted is not the kind of thing that can be obtained by compulsion."*

**And the conclusion reached:** *"what can be obtained by force is not faith. Compulsion can produce behaviour; it cannot produce conviction."*

**Qāf 50/45 is the last link of this line and adds one more thing: the choice of word.**

***Jabbār*, as was recorded in the section on al-Ḥashr, also carries the sense of mending: one who forces a break back into place.**

**And this is the finest side of the verse, and I give it as my own reading:** because the two senses of the word stand together, the negation takes in two things at once. **You are neither one who compels nor one who corrects by force.**

**What is rejected, that is, is not only ill-intentioned pressure; *well-intentioned* pressure too.** The intention to set a broken bone does not wait for the consent of the bone's owner — and the verse removes exactly this position from the messenger.

**I record this reading as my own reading;** its support is the twofold sense of the root recorded in the section on al-Ḥashr.

### فَذَكِّرْ بِٱلْقُرْءَانِ — the sūra returns to where it began

**And the ring closes.**

| Verse | Expression |
|---|---|
| **1** | *wa'l-**Qurʾān**i'l-majīd* — **an oath** by the Qurʾān |
| **45** | *fa-dhakkir bi'l-**Qurʾān*** — **remind** with the Qurʾān |

**The sūra began by swearing by itself and ends by commanding that reminding be done with itself.**

**And the particle *bāʾ* matters: *bi'l-Qurʾān* — "*with* the Qurʾān".** The **instrument** of the reminding, that is, is specified.

**Compulsion is taken out of the messenger's hand, and something is put in its place: a text.**

**I give this as my own reading, and its support is the word order of the sentence:** the negation (*mā anta… bi-jabbār*) and the command (*fa-dhakkir bi'l-Qurʾān*) come one after the other and are joined by a **fāʾ**. **The second, that is, is the consequence of the first:** since you are not one who compels, **then remind.**

**ذَكِّرْ — root ذ-ك-ر.** And this is the sūra's third occurrence of *dhikr*:

| Verse | Word | What |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | *dhikrā* | The function of the proof from nature |
| 37 | *dhikrā* | The function of the proof from history |
| **45** | ***dhakkir*** | **A command: remind** |

**Twice a noun, once a verb.** The sūra turns the name it gave to the function of its two sections of proof into a command at the end.

**And the verb is in the *tafʿīl* form: *dhakkara* — to remind (transitive).** Not "remember", that is, but **"remind".**

**This verse was mentioned in the section on al-Aʿlā**, where findings were made about the limits of the command; I rely on that note.

### مَن يَخَافُ وَعِيدِ — to whom the reminding is addressed

**And there is one further qualification in the last clause.**

The command is not "remind everyone": *man **yakhāfu waʿīd*** — **"whoever fears My threat".**

**How is this qualification to be read?**

| Reading | What it says | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **This is the one who will benefit** | The reminding is given to everyone, but its effect is seen only in the one who fears | It agrees with the general line of the Qurʾān: *"You only warn…"* (Fāṭir 35/18; Yā Sīn 36/11) |
| **The addressee is being limited** | Remind only those who fear | It is hard to reconcile with the Qurʾān's verses on the universality of the message |

**The first reading is widely adopted** and agrees better with the context: the clause frames not a prohibition but **a consolation**. Once the limit of the authority has been drawn, where the outcome will be seen is stated.

**And the word *waʿīd* comes for the last time — again without the *yāʾ*, exactly as in the fourteenth verse.**

**The sūra's chain of *waʿīd* closes thus:** the threat came true in the past (14) → the threat became the name of a day (20) → the threat had been announced beforehand (28) → **the one who fears the threat is the one to whom the reminding is addressed (45).**

### On the ending of the sūra

**And one last observation.**

**The sūra does not end with a threat.** The threat is named in the last clause, but the subject of the clause is not the threat; it is **the reminding.**

**And the last command comes immediately after a limit:** once what you cannot do has been said, what you can do is given.

**I record this as my own reading:** the sūra tells the one it addresses what he will not do before it tells him what he will do. And this order fits the whole method of the sūra. This is what it had been doing from the beginning: showing what the objection is not (not a proof but astonishment), and then showing what is to be done (not argument but looking).

## Close: where the sūra leaves us

**Sūrat Qāf treats a single subject across forty-five verses and in a single sound pattern.** And when it is done, it has brought back every one of the words it began with.

**The verifiable rings of the sūra — set out one by one above:**

| Opening | Close |
|---|---|
| *wa'l-**Qurʾān**i'l-majīd* (1) | *fa-dhakkir bi'l-**Qurʾān*** (45) |
| *rajʿun **baʿīd*** (3) | *ghayra **baʿīd*** (31), *min makānin **qarīb*** (41) |
| *kadhālika'l-**khurūj*** (11) | *dhālika yawmu'l-**khurūj*** (42) |
| *ʿabdin **munīb*** (8) | *bi-qalbin **munīb*** (33) |
| *kitābun **ḥafīẓ*** (4) | *awwābin **ḥafīẓ*** (32) |
| *hal min **mazīd*** (30) | *wa ladaynā **mazīd*** (35) |
| *fa'ṣbir ʿalā **mā yaqūlūn*** (39) | *naḥnu aʿlamu bi-**mā yaqūlūn*** (45) |
| *a-fa-**ʿayīnā*** (15) | *mā massanā min **lughūb*** (38), *ʿalaynā **yasīr*** (44) |

**And the method of the sūra may be summarised in this commentary as follows:**

**An objection began not with a proof but with astonishment (2).** The sūra answered it not with an argument but **with a call to look** (6). What it made them look at was ordinary: sky, earth, water, the date palm, dead ground. And it joined the name of the ordinary thing to the name of the thing in dispute: ***kadhālika'l-khurūj*** (11).

**Then the scale narrowed:** from history (12-14) to a single person (16), from there to the **inside** of a person (16), from there to a single word (18), from there to a single moment of death (19).

**Then the covering was lifted (22)** and the scene of judgement was set (24-30). Two scenes were placed side by side, and the passage between them took a single verse (30→31).

**Then the sūra drew back:** the proofs ended (38), the commands began (39), and the last verse set a limit to the messenger's authority (45).

**What the sūra leaves with the one it addresses is not a verdict but a question.** The twenty-second verse said: *"We have lifted your covering; today your sight is sharp."* And on the reading recorded above, what changed was not the sight itself but what stood in front of it.

**The question the sūra leaves, then, is this — and the text itself does not ask it; I ask it:** if what prevents seeing is not an incapacity but something that has come in between, **what is coming in between now?**

And the sixth verse of the sūra had already given the shortest answer that can be given to this question: *a-fa-lam yanẓurū ila's-samāʾi **fawqahum*** — have they not looked at the sky **above them**?

The proof was above their heads all along.
