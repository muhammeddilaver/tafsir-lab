# 110. Sūrat al-Naṣr

Three verses. One of the shortest sūras in the Qurʾān, and probably one of the latest to be revealed.

Seen from outside, the sūra is a proclamation of victory. Looked at from within, it turns out not to be one. There is no celebration in the text, no boasting, not a single sentence directed at the defeated. A text of three verses begins by speaking of something won and ends by **commanding that pardon be sought**.

This is the single tension that explains the whole sūra: **what is asked for after victory is not celebration but apology.** The rest of this chapter will consist largely of investigating what that choice means.

A note on structure: the three verses are, grammatically, **a single sentence.** The first two verses are the condition (*"when…"*), the third is the answer to that condition (*"then do this"*). This is why the sūra cannot be divided; read on their own, the first two verses leave the sentence unfinished.

### The name of the sūra

It takes its name from the word *naṣr* in the first verse. It is also referred to by its opening words, *"Idhā jāʾa naṣru'llāh"*.

In the tradition the sūra has a third name as well: **al-Tawdīʿ** — "the farewell". This name arises from an interpretation not found in the text of the sūra: some of the Companions read in it the announcement of the Prophet's departure. That a sūra should take its name not from its content but from a meaning laid upon it is worth noticing; the ground of this reading is treated separately below.

### The time of revelation

The sūra is counted as Medinan. In terms of the order of revelation it stands near the end of the lists.

**The report that it is "the last sūra revealed".** In a report transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās in the Ṣaḥīḥ of Muslim, the last sūra to be revealed is said to be *"Idhā jāʾa naṣru'llāhi wa'l-fatḥ"*. This report is found in the source and is widely transmitted.

But "the last revealed" is not a question with a single answer in the Qurʾānic sciences. Other texts too are pointed to in the reports as the last verse revealed — al-Nisāʾ 4/176, on inheritance law, and al-Baqara 2/281 are the most often named among them. The tradition generally reconciles this difference as follows: **the last complete sūra** revealed is al-Naṣr, **the last verse** is another. This reconciliation is reasonable but not certain; the reports come from different Companions, and each may have been speaking of what he himself knew.

Let us keep this clear: there is no serious hesitation about the sūra's being **one of the last revealed**. To say "it is definitely the last" is to say more than the reports carry.

**Where it was revealed.** One report transmits that the sūra was revealed at Minā during the Farewell Pilgrimage. This detail is disputed; it is not decisive for understanding the sūra.

### The historical frame

To understand what the sūra is speaking of, the twenty years behind it must be briefly recalled. What follows is the frame given not by the Qurʾān but by **the biographical and historical sources**; it is accepted in its broad lines, and disputed in its details.

- The call began secretly in Mecca; with the open call it met with harsh opposition.
- A group of Muslims migrated to Abyssinia. A social and commercial boycott was applied to the Banū Hāshim for a period.
- After the Hijra the centre moved to Medina. Badr, Uḥud, the Trench — a period weighted towards defence.
- In year 6 of the Hijra the treaty of al-Ḥudaybiya was made. To all appearances a step backward, and for some Muslims even a disappointment: that year they returned without performing the ʿumra, and the terms appeared to favour the other side.
- In year 8 of the Hijra Mecca was entered. The sources agree that this entry took place with so little fighting that it may be called bloodless.
- Year 9 of the Hijra is called in the biographical sources **"the year of the delegations"** (*sanat al-wufūd*): representatives of tribes from every part of Arabia began coming to Medina.
- In year 10 of the Hijra the Farewell Pilgrimage was performed; a short time afterwards the Prophet died.

The scene in the second verse of the sūra — people entering in throngs — corresponds historically to these last two or three years. That is, the sūra speaks at the point where **the outcome of a long process becomes visible**.

The basmala was treated in earlier sūras and is not taken up again here; the verses are numbered without counting it.

## 110/1 — إِذَا جَآءَ نَصْرُ ٱللَّهِ وَٱلْفَتْحُ

*Idhā jāʾa naṣru'llāhi wa'l-fatḥ*
"When the help of God and the opening come,"

### إِذَا — "when", not "if"

Arabic has two basic particles of condition, and the difference between them is meaningful here.

**إِنْ (in)** is used for things whose occurrence is doubtful, merely possible: *"if he comes"*. **إِذَا (idhā)** is used for things about whose occurrence there is no doubt, and only **the time** of which is unknown: *"when he comes"*. This distinction is one of the standard entries in the classical books of grammar.

The verse does not say *in jāʾa*, it says *idhā jāʾa*. That is, the sentence carries this sense: this will come — the question is not whether it will come, but what is to be done when it does.

If the sūra was revealed in Medina, at a time close to the actual realisation of the victory, this choice of particle is self-explanatory. But the particle also conveys an attitude: the Qurʾān does not open the outcome itself to debate, it shifts the debate to what will happen after the outcome.

### جَآءَ — "came"

The verb is in the past tense but its sense is future. In Arabic, future events whose certainty is being stressed may be told in the past tense — because they are as certain as done, they are spoken of as done. The Qurʾān does this openly: *"The command of God has come; do not seek to hasten it"* (al-Naḥl 16/1). The rest of the verse shows that the command has not yet come; the verb is nevertheless in the perfect.

The verb itself is also a choice. Victory could have been spoken of as being "seized", "won", "taken". It is not. Victory is something that **comes**; something that arrives at the door, that reaches you, that moves of itself.

And in this verse there is **no human act at all.** All the labour, the travelling, the loss and the endurance of twenty years are left outside this sentence. The subject of the sentence is one thing only: *naṣru'llāh*.

### نَصْر — naṣr

Root **n-ṣ-r**: to help someone, to stand behind him, to support him.

One of the concrete uses of the root has to do with rain: the Arabic dictionaries record usages such as *naṣara'l-ghaythu'l-arḍa* (the rain helped the earth, that is, it fell abundantly); it is also noted that the word *naṣr* carries the sense of "rain". The image is clear: dry earth that can do nothing for itself, and something that comes down from above, revives it and makes it green. Help comes not by the effort of what is below but by the descent of what is above.

**Derivatives.** *Nāṣir* (one who helps), *naṣīr* (helper), *nuṣra* (help), *anṣār* (helpers), *istinṣār* (asking for help), *tanāṣur* (helping one another), *manṣūr* (one who is helped, who is brought to victory). That the Muslims of Medina are called the **Anṣār** comes from this root: their attribute is not the winning of victory but the taking in of someone who seeks refuge.

In the Qurʾān, the disciples' answer *"We are the helpers of God"* to Jesus's (ʿĪsā) question *"Who are my helpers in the way of God?"* is also built on this root (Āl ʿImrān 3/52; al-Ṣaff 61/14).

**Naṣr and "victory" are not the same thing.** In English this sūra is often called the sūra of "Victory", and that loses the direction of the word. *Naṣr* is not the victory itself but **the support that produces the victory.** Victory is the outcome; naṣr is the act that produces the outcome. And the act has an agent.

### نَصْرُ ٱللَّه — "the help of God"

This construct is the first ruling of the sūra.

The sentence could have been built as *"naṣruka"* (your victory), *"naṣrunā"* (our victory), or without naming the agent at all. Instead the Qurʾān attributes the help directly to God. In grammatical terms the construct is **an annexation to the agent**: the one who does the helping is God.

This is an emphasis the Qurʾān sustains throughout:

- *"Help is only from God"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/126; the same expression occurs at al-Anfāl 8/10 as well). This sentence comes in a context that speaks of being supported by angels at Badr — that is, in the very place where the most concrete, most visible causes of the victory are enumerated, the causes themselves are not counted as the owners of the victory.
- *"If God helps you, none can overcome you"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/160).
- *"On that day the believers will rejoice at the help of God. He helps whom He will"* (al-Rūm 30/4-5).

And there is one further place where the Qurʾān uses this combination that throws direct light on Sūrat al-Naṣr: *"a help from God and a near opening"* (**al-Ṣaff 61/13**). There *naṣr* and *fatḥ* stand side by side as a **promise**. In Sūrat al-Naṣr the same two words stand side by side as a **fulfilment**. The words that were promised come back in the same order.

Another link runs backwards. Describing the moment of hardship of earlier communities, the Qurʾān frames this sentence: *"…the messenger and those who believed with him were so shaken that they said, 'When will the help of God come?' Know well that the help of God is near"* (al-Baqara 2/214). There *naṣru'llāh* is the subject of a question — asked, not yet seen. Sūrat al-Naṣr is the moment at which the same combination has ceased to be a question.

### ٱلْفَتْح — fatḥ

Root **f-t-ḥ**: **to open.** The opening of something closed; of a door, a lock, a knot, a road that has been shut.

**Derivatives.** *Miftāḥ* (key — the instrument that opens), *fātiḥa* (the opening part of a thing), *iftitāḥ* (inauguration), *istiftāḥ* (seeking an opening, seeking a judgement), *al-Fattāḥ* (one of the names of God: the one who opens abundantly, who judges — *"He is al-Fattāḥ, al-ʿAlīm"*, Sabaʾ 34/26).

The root has two basic directions in Arabic, and both are found in the Qurʾān:

**1. To open.** *"Even if We opened to them a gate from the sky and they kept ascending through it…"* (al-Ḥijr 15/14). *"With Him are the keys of the unseen"* (al-Anʿām 6/59).

**2. To judge, to decide between two parties.** *"Our Lord! Judge between us and our people with truth; You are the best of those who judge"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/89). The connection is this: to give judgement is to open a matter that has stayed closed; a dispute is a knot, and the judge unties it.

The military sense arises from these two directions. The taking of a city is **the opening of the gate** of a walled place; and it is **the settling by judgement** of a long dispute between two sides. The Qurʾān uses this sense in an interesting way in the context of Badr: addressing the polytheists, *"If you were seeking a decision, the decision has come to you"* (al-Anfāl 8/19) — that is, "you asked for a judgement, and the judgement has come". It is used not of the winning side but of the losing one.

**Victory being called an "opening".** The real point here is this: Arabic had other words for a military outcome — *ghalaba* (overcoming), *ẓafar* (obtaining), not *fatḥ*. The Qurʾān chooses not "overcoming" but "opening".

The meaning of this lies in the logic of the word itself. **To open is not to create.** What is opened is already there; it is merely shut. The one who opens the door does not produce what is behind the door, he only makes it accessible. With this choice of word the sūra describes what happened thus: nothing new was founded; what was closed was opened.

The second consequence follows from the first: a door is for **entering**. The point of a door's being opened is that someone will be able to come in. And indeed in the next verse people are entering. The first two verses of the sūra are built on a single spatial image: **the door was opened, people are coming in.** That the verb of *dukhūl* (entering) follows the opening is no coincidence; the word of the first verse prepares the verb of the second.

**The definite article.** *Al-fatḥ* — a particular opening. The majority of the exegetes understand this as **the conquest of Mecca**; the Medinan character of the sūra and the historical context support this. Some take the article as generic and read it as an opening in general. The two readings do not conflict in practice: the conquest of Mecca was itself the gateway to a wider opening.

This should be noticed: the most famous use of the word *fatḥ* in the Qurʾān, the verse *"We have granted you a clear opening"* (al-Fatḥ 48/1), is widely said in the classical commentaries to have been revealed regarding **the treaty of al-Ḥudaybiya**. No city was taken there; indeed that year they returned without even performing the ʿumra. It is nevertheless called an **opening**. This is the clearest proof that the word means not "taking a city" but "a door's being opened" — because what was opened at al-Ḥudaybiya was not territory but contact: the halting of war between the two sides, people becoming able to speak with one another.

### وَ — the work of the conjunction

*Naṣru'llāhi wa'l-fatḥ* — two elements joined by a conjunction. In the classical analyses this connection is read in two ways:

| Reading | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Explicative connection** (*ʿaṭf tafsīrī*) | "The opening" is the unfolding of "the help"; the two are two names for one event. What is the help? It is the opening. |
| **Two distinct elements** | *Naṣr* is the process (the support that comes along the way), *fatḥ* is the outcome (the opening of the door). First the help, then the opening. |

The second reading is supported by the order: the help is named first, the opening after. Cause first, outcome after.

And there is this too: *naṣr* is bound to God by annexation, *fatḥ* is given with the article alone. An element joined by a conjunction shares the ruling of what it is joined to; that is, the opening too is implicitly attributed to the same source. But at the level of language the difference is preserved: the owner of the help is stated openly, while the opening is left open. This accords with the fact that where the opened door opens onto is to be stated in the next verse.

## 110/2 — وَرَأَيْتَ ٱلنَّاسَ يَدْخُلُونَ فِى دِينِ ٱللَّهِ أَفْوَاجًا

*Wa raʾayta'l-nāsa yadkhulūna fī dīni'llāhi afwājā*
"and you see people entering the religion of God in throngs,"

### رَأَيْتَ — "when you see"

The second part of the condition. And what is striking is this: **an act of witnessing is placed inside the condition.**

The sentence could have said "when people enter". It does not; it says "when **you** see them entering". The one addressed is singular, and he is the Prophet. The occurrence of the event and the seeing of it thus become two distinct conditions.

This has two consequences:

**First**, the measure of victory is redefined. The opening was mentioned in the first verse; but the condition did not close there. For the condition to be completed, something further must be seen: people entering. That is, the taking of the city is not yet an outcome by the measure of the sūra. The outcome is the opening not of the city but of people.

**Second**, it carries the character of good news. In struggles that last a long time most people do not see the outcome. Here the one addressed is told that he will see the outcome **with his own eyes**.

The root **r-ʾ-y** in Arabic covers both seeing with the eye and grasping with the mind (from the same root, *raʾy* — opinion, view). That the object here is a concrete event brings seeing with the eye to the fore.

### ٱلنَّاس — people

With the definite article: **al-nās** — people, the crowd, the populace. Not restricted, not reduced to a tribe or a group.

The scholars of the language differ about the root of the word — it is said to derive from *ʾ-n-s* (closeness, familiarity) or from *n-w-s* (to move, to sway). This debate will be taken up in its place in Sūrat al-Nās; the real point here is the word's **scope**.

The comparison is this: this call had begun with two or three people. In the early years those who believed could be counted one by one; the sources record their names individually. The word now used is not "*a group of people*" but, straightforwardly, **"people"**. The move from counting individuals to a collective noun is the summary of the process.

### يَدْخُلُونَ — "they are entering"

Root **d-kh-l**: to go in. From the same root *dākhil*, *madkhal* (place of entry), *idkhāl* (bringing in).

The verb is in the **imperfect**. It does not say "they entered" (*dakhalū*) but "they are entering". The imperfect here forms a **circumstantial clause**: what is seen is not a completed event but a movement flowing before the eye. What is described is not a finished statistic but an ongoing stream.

### فِى دِينِ ٱللَّه — "into the religion of God"

The particle **فى (fī)** means "into", and here it completes the spatial image. Religion is depicted as a space that is entered — a place that has a door, that one goes into, that one is inside. In the previous verse a door was opened; here people are going in.

The root analysis of the word **دِين** was carried out in Sūrat al-Māʿūn (*d-y-n*: debt, submission, receiving what is due, an order in operation). Here the word means "religion in operation, the order that is followed".

The construct **دِينِ ٱللَّه** also draws a boundary: what is entered is **the religion of God**; not the religion of a person, of a tribe, or of the winning side. This distinction matters in the context of a conquest, because it is usual in history for the religion of the victor to be called by the victor's name. The verse names the owner of what is entered.

### أَفْوَاجًا — in throngs

The most visual word in the sūra.

Root **f-w-j**. **Fawj**: a body of people moving together, a company, a cluster. Its plural is **afwāj**. The word can also be understood like a military unit, but in Arabic it carries a more general sense of "group".

Its other uses in the Qurʾān are all in the context of **movement in a mass**:

- *"On the day the trumpet is blown, you will come in throngs"* (al-Nabaʾ 78/18).
- *"We shall gather from every community a throng"* (al-Naml 27/83).
- *"Whenever a throng is cast into it…"* (al-Mulk 67/8).

**The word is plural and indefinite.** These two features together give this sense: not one company, but an indefinite number of companies coming one after another. The English renderings "in throngs", "in waves", "in droves" catch this repetition.

**Its place in the grammar.** The word is in the accusative as a *ḥāl* (an element expressing state); that is, it tells **how** the act of entering took place. What is described is not how many entered but **the manner of the entering.** No number is given; a rhythm is given.

### From entering one by one to entering in throngs

This word is the one-word summary of a twenty-year process, and its force comes exactly from this contrast.

In the first period of the call, to believe was an **individual and costly** decision. A person set himself against his own tribe, and often against his own family; what he lost was concrete — protection, trade, standing, sometimes safety of life. Under these conditions people came not in floods but **one by one**, and generally in secret.

*Afwājan* reverses this picture. Coming no longer requires standing alone; people come as a group. That the biographical sources call the ninth year of the Hijra "the year of the delegations" is the historical counterpart of this picture: tribes joined not individually but collectively, through their representatives.

**But this word is not merely a declaration of success.** Mass participation has another face as well, and the Qurʾān does not cover that face. A verse revealed in the same period says precisely this to some of those who joined in a mass:

> *"The Bedouin say, 'We believe.' Say: You do not believe; rather say, 'We have submitted.' For faith has not yet entered your hearts."* (al-Ḥujurāt 49/14)

Read inside the picture of *afwājan*, this verse establishes a balance: **participation in crowds is no guarantee of depth.** To come through the door and to be inside are not the same thing; as the number of those entering grows, a diversification of the reasons for entering is unavoidable.

This is one of the grounds needed for understanding the command to seek forgiveness in the third verse. The growth of numbers is the most gladdening and at the same time the most perilous moment for a community.

## 110/3 — فَسَبِّحْ بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ وَٱسْتَغْفِرْهُ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ تَوَّابًۢا

*Fa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika wa'staghfirh, innahu kāna tawwābā*
"then glorify your Lord with praise and ask forgiveness of Him. He is indeed *tawwāb* (the one who accepts returning again and again)."

### فَ — the particle of the answer

The hinge of the sūra. This particle signals that the first two verses are the condition and this verse the answer. The sentence is thus completed: *"…when it comes and when you see it, then do this."*

The structural conclusion that follows is important: the sūra is **not a report about a victory but an instruction given after a victory.** The report stands only as the condition of the instruction.

### The content of the command: three verbs

Three things are asked for in the answer — and all three are turned not outward but inward.

| What is asked | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Glorification** | To hold Him free of every deficiency (the negative direction) |
| **Praise** | To acknowledge His perfection, to direct praise to Him (the positive direction) |
| **Seeking forgiveness** | To ask that one's own fault be covered (the direction turned upon oneself) |

Let us now take these three one by one, and then return to why it is these three.

### سَبِّحْ — glorification

Root **s-b-ḥ**. The concrete and surprising original sense of the root: **to swim in water or in air, to flow onward, to move swiftly through a medium.**

The Qurʾān preserves this concrete sense: *"each swimming in an orbit"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/33; the same expression occurs at Yāsīn 36/40 as well) — this is the verb used of the sun, the moon and the heavenly bodies.

When it moves into Form II (سَبَّحَ) the word takes on the sense of **tanzīh**: holding God free of everything unbefitting Him. In the lexicographical tradition the two senses are linked thus: to swim is to **move away swiftly through** a medium; and glorification is to hold God far from deficiencies. The connection is not forced, but neither is it certain; the relation between the two senses of the root is the lexicographers' interpretation.

**The function of glorification is negative.** It does not say "He is such"; it says "He is far from such". It is the same method we saw in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ: drawing a boundary instead of giving a description.

Why glorification at the moment of victory? Because victory is a moment at which it becomes easy to think wrongly about God. The winning side is inclined to turn its own winning into a certificate of divine approval: "if we won we were right, and if we were right everything we did was correct." Glorification cuts this chain — because to glorify is to say that God cannot be made part of any human reckoning.

### بِحَمْدِ رَبِّكَ — "your Lord, with praise"

The particle **بِ** here conveys accompaniment or state: the glorification is to be done together with praise. The two together form a complete expression in two directions: *"He is far from deficiencies"* + *"praise belongs to Him."*

The detailed analysis of the word **حَمْد** was carried out in the section on al-Fātiḥa. Its function here is this: **to change the direction of praise.**

At the moment of victory praise circulates in abundance and naturally turns towards the winner. At exactly that moment the verse transfers the praise elsewhere. This is not only a theological correction but a practical safeguard: a person who accepts the praise directed at himself makes his next decision under the weight of that praise.

**رَبِّكَ — "your Lord."** That "Lord" is used here rather than "God" is meaningful. *Rabb* means the one who nurtures, who brings up, who leads a thing stage by stage to maturity (the analysis of this root was carried out in Sūrat al-Fātiḥa and Sūrat al-Fīl). When this name is chosen in the aftermath of victory, what has happened is thereby named not a "gain" but **the outcome of a long process of nurture**.

The pronoun *"…ka"* (your) also establishes a personal bond. This sets the tone of the whole sūra: the text addresses not a state, not an army, not a community, but **a single person**.

### وَٱسْتَغْفِرْهُ — "and ask forgiveness of Him"

Here is the heart of the sūra.

**Root gh-f-r**: **to cover, to close over, to put a cover on top.** The concrete residue of the root survives in the Turkish loanword **miğfer**, the helmet that covers the head in battle, which comes from this root. *Ghifāra* means a case drawn over something.

This concrete sense matters for understanding the notion of forgiveness correctly: forgiveness is not **counting what happened as not having happened**, it is **covering it.** The act does not disappear; its visibility, its consequence and its reckoning are lifted. And to cover is at the same time to protect — a helmet protects what it covers.

**Form X** (اسْتَغْفَرَ): **to seek** something. *Istighfār* is to request to be covered. The servant is not doing something here; he is asking that something be done.

### Asking pardon after victory

Now we have come to the most extraordinary point of the sūra.

This command lies outside everything one expects. When a struggle is won, the expected sequence is: celebration, thanks, a reckoning of accounts, the announcement of the next step. The text does none of these. At the end of a twenty-year process, at the moment the goal is reached, the one addressed is told to **apologise**.

Why? Several explanations are worked through in the classical commentaries and in the later literature. These are less alternatives to one another than different faces of the same command.

**1. Seeking forgiveness is placed at points of ending in the Qurʾān.** This is the most concrete explanation and the one that rests least on interpretation, because it is a pattern within the Qurʾān itself:

- After the main rites of the pilgrimage are completed: *"Then flow on from where the people flow on, and ask forgiveness of God"* (al-Baqara 2/199).
- After the night vigil, in the hours before dawn: *"And in the hours before dawn they would ask forgiveness"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/18).

The pattern is this: **when a work is finished, the covering of the faults that occurred during it is asked for.** Seeking forgiveness here is not a confession of sin but **a closing movement** — the giving up of the claim that what was done was done without deficiency.

On this reading, the seeking of forgiveness in Sūrat al-Naṣr is the largest-scale instance of that same movement, performed at the end of a twenty-year task.

**2. Victory is the moment of highest moral risk.** This is an inference, and I record it as my own interpretation, not as a transmitted report.

While power is in hand, injustice is cheap; there is no one left to call it to account. For the winning side, the defeated side is no longer a threat but an object. What follows victories in history is most often revenge, and revenge appears at that moment even morally legitimate — the sentence "they deserved it" is never said more easily than on the day of victory.

The command to seek forgiveness places a caveat upon this moment: **the winning side has not closed its account.** The person who asks for pardon is at the same moment closer to becoming the person who grants it.

The biographical sources widely transmit that a general amnesty was declared upon the entry into Mecca; the term *ṭulaqāʾ* ("those set free") used in the sources for the Meccans is also a historical trace of that amnesty. The details of these reports vary, but the fact of a general amnesty appears extensively in the early sources.

**3. The question of the Prophet's seeking forgiveness.** There is a theological debate here, and it should be recorded honestly.

The Qurʾān commands the Prophet to seek forgiveness in more than one place: al-Muʾmin (Ghāfir) 40/55; Muḥammad 47/19; and here. The main views on how this is to be understood:

| View | Ground |
|---|---|
| On behalf of the community / to teach the community | Though the one addressed by the command is singular, the exemplarity is general; at Muḥammad 47/19 forgiveness is also asked for believing men and women |
| As what servanthood requires, without depending on the condition of sin | Seeking forgiveness is not a confession of a deficiency but an acknowledgement of the distance between servant and Lord |
| The measure of fault growing finer as the station rises | A saying widespread in the tradition puts this as: "the good deeds of the pious are the faults of those brought near" — this is a saying, not a hadith, and should be known as such |

I take no side in this debate. What the sūra shows us stands independently of the views: **self-criticism has been placed as the natural response to success.**

**4. Its counterpart in the reports.** According to a report transmitted from ʿĀʾisha and found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, after this sūra was revealed the Prophet began often to repeat these words in his bowing and prostration: *"Subḥānaka'llāhumma rabbanā wa bi-ḥamdika, allāhumma'ghfir lī"* — "O God, our Lord, glory be to You with Your praise; O God, forgive me." In the report, the reason given for his doing this is that he was putting this command of the Qurʾān into practice.

The striking side of the report is that the three verbs of the sūra (glorification, praise, seeking forgiveness) are found in that sentence in the same order. That is, the command was put into practice not as an abstract attitude but as **a direct utterance**.

### إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ تَوَّابًۢا

**إِنَّ** — the particle of emphasis. It signals that what is about to be said is being stood behind.

**كَانَ** — "was". The past tense here does not convey time; according to the classical explanation it conveys **the fixedness of the attribute**. That is, not "He was once so" but "**He has always been and always is so**". The Qurʾān frequently gives God's attributes in this pattern (*kāna ghafūran raḥīmā*, *kāna ʿalīman ḥakīmā*).

**تَوَّابًا — tawwāb.**

Root **t-w-b**: **to turn, to come back.** The original sense of the root is not moral but spatial — to leave a place and come back to where one came from. From the same root *tawba* (a returning), *matāb* (the place returned to), *tāʾib* (one who returns).

In English the usual rendering, "repentance", has drifted towards remorse and the promise not to do a thing again. The core sense in Arabic is simpler and stronger: **to change direction and come back.** Turning back is not going to a new place; it is returning to a place that had been left.

**The pattern: فَعَّال (faʿʿāl).** One of the intensive forms in Arabic; it conveys abundance, repetition and continuity. Among the names of God, *ghaffār* (much-forgiving), *qahhār*, *razzāq* are on the same pattern. *Tawwāb* therefore means not "one who accepts once" but **one who responds to returning again and again, continuously**.

### The turning goes both ways

The most striking side of this word is this: **the same verb is used both of the servant and of God**, and what determines the direction is the particle.

| Construction | Who | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *tāba **ilā**'llāh* | The servant | He turned to God — he gave up the error and turned his direction round |
| *tāba'llāhu **ʿalā** ʿabdih* | God | He turned to the servant — He inclined towards him, accepted him, bent over him with His mercy |

The same movement is made from both sides. And the Qurʾān brings the two together in a single verse:

> *"Then He **turned** to them so that they too might **turn**. God is indeed tawwāb, raḥīm."* (al-Tawba 9/118)

The order in this sentence establishes at the level of language a point much debated in theology: **God turns first, and then the servant is able to turn.** The servant's turning is not a beginning but a response.

The first place where the Qurʾān uses this name shows the same structure: in the account of Adam (Ādam), immediately after the error, it says *"Then his Lord turned to him (accepted his turning); He is indeed tawwāb, raḥīm"* (al-Baqara 2/37). This is the name placed immediately after the first error in the history of humankind.

**The name standing alone here.** In the Qurʾān *tawwāb* generally comes together with *raḥīm* (al-Baqara 2/37, 54, 160; al-Ḥujurāt 49/12). In Sūrat al-Naṣr it stands by itself. One reason for this is the harmony of the verse-endings — *afwājā / tawwābā* close on the same sound. But the result affects the meaning too: the whole weight of the sentence gathers on the idea of responding to a return.

### The function of the sentence: taʿlīl

The last sentence is a ground: *seek forgiveness, **because** He is tawwāb.*

That is, the command is tied not to the possibility of punishment but to a promise of acceptance. Seeking forgiveness here is not a defence arising from fear but a movement arising from knowing that the door is open.

And there is one more harmony of language: across the sūra, words of **movement and direction** have accumulated — *jāʾa* (came), *yadkhulūna* (they are entering), *fatḥ* (opening). The last word of the sūra is a word of movement too: *tawwāb* (the one who turns, who turns towards the one who turns). People are entering the religion, the servant is turning to his Lord, the Lord is turning to His servant. The whole of a three-verse text is built out of movements that change direction.

## The sūra as a whole

### A summary of the structure

| Part | Verse | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Condition 1** | 1 | The help and the opening come |
| **Condition 2** | 2 | People are seen entering in throngs |
| **Answer** | 3 | Glorification, praise, seeking forgiveness — and its ground |

The point to notice: neither part of the condition is **an act of the one addressed.** The help comes, people enter — in both, the agent is someone else. The only act that falls to the one addressed is in the answer, and that too is an inward movement.

That is, the sūra, while telling of a man's twenty years of labour, never mentions that labour; it attributes the outcome entirely elsewhere; and the only thing it asks of the one addressed is a precaution against the damage this outcome might do to him.

### The sūra's neighbours in the muṣḥaf

The sūra stands between al-Kāfirūn (109) and al-Masad (111). This sequence is the order of arrangement, not the order of revelation; even so, the picture that emerges is striking.

**Al-Kāfirūn**, in Mecca, is the sharpest expression of separation: *"Your religion is yours, and my religion is mine."* That sūra closes the door.

**Al-Naṣr**, in the same city, tells of the same people entering in throngs. This sūra opens the door.

Between the two lie twenty years, and the body of people addressed is largely the same. This juxtaposition in the muṣḥaf shows that separation is not a final ruling: roads that part decisively at one point can later join.

### "This is the announcement of the parting" — the report from Ibn ʿAbbās

This is the most famous transmitted report about this sūra, and it is found in al-Bukhārī.

According to the report, ʿUmar, during his caliphate, used to admit Ibn ʿAbbās — despite his being very young — into the assembly of the elder Companions who had fought at Badr. This did not please some of them, and it was said, "we too have sons of his age". One day ʿUmar gathered them and asked what they thought about *"Idhā jāʾa naṣru'llāhi wa'l-fatḥ"*. Some said, "we are commanded, when God gives us victory, to praise Him and to ask forgiveness"; some kept silent. ʿUmar turned to Ibn ʿAbbās: "Do you say this too?" Ibn ʿAbbās said: "No. This is the appointed term of God's Messenger, which He announced to him: when the help and the opening come, that is the sign of your term; so glorify Him and ask forgiveness." ʿUmar said: "I know nothing of it other than what you know."

**Notes on the report.** The report is found in a sound source and shows that this reading existed in the early period. But this must be kept distinct: **it is an interpretation, not the plain statement of the text.** There is not a single word in the sūra that speaks of death. The report itself already presents it as one view — some of those in the assembly understood it otherwise, and ʿUmar's question is precisely aimed at bringing out that difference.

The giving of the name "the sūra of farewell" to the sūra arises from the spread of this reading.

### The completion of a task and the completion of a life

The logic behind Ibn ʿAbbās's reading is this: **if a person's life is built upon a task, then the announcement that the task is complete is the announcement that the life is complete as well.**

This logic finds a support in the language of the sūra. The commands in the third verse are not a forward-looking programme. It does not say "now establish this", "organise that", "march there". All three things asked for are **closing movements**: acknowledging, returning the praise, asking that the fault be covered. These are things done at the end of a work.

A similar reading has been made in the tradition for the verse *"This day I have perfected your religion for you"* (al-Māʾida 5/3); it is transmitted that this verse was revealed at the Farewell Pilgrimage, and it is transmitted that some Companions, on hearing it, did not rejoice but on the contrary grew sorrowful. The ground is the same: to what has been completed there is nothing further to add.

I do not present this reading as a settled ruling. But this stands as a separate question, and independently of the sūra: **if the meaning of a person's life is bound to a work, what happens when that work is finished?** The answer the sūra gives is that what is to be done when the work is finished is not to find a new work but to turn one's direction upon oneself.

### The sound texture of the sūra

The three verses close on two different sounds: *al-fatḥ* (the first), *afwājā* and *tawwābā* (the second and third). The closing of the last two verses on a long *-ā* gives the sūra a widening and a slowing at the end. The first verse ends on a hard close, the last two are drawn out on an open syllable.

I record this not as a claim of miracle but as an example of the general sound character of the short sūras.

## What this says to the present

**One. A person's turning upon himself at the moment of success.**

This is the most directly practical ruling of the sūra, and it requires no religious context. What does a person do when he has accomplished something? The common answer: he celebrates, he recounts it, he plans the next one. The movement the sūra proposes is another — **taking stock**. The moment of winning is the moment at which the losses are counted: who was done what to along this road, which corner was cut, which fault was deferred with "it will be put right once we get there".

The practical value of this is as follows: success tends to legitimate retrospectively the methods that led to it. When the outcome turns out well, everything done along the way is counted as correct. Seeking forgiveness is precisely the movement that cancels this automatic approval.

**Two. To whom the outcome belongs.**

The sūra names the victory *naṣru'llāh* and mentions no human act at all in that verse. This is not a denial of the labour — the twenty years of labour are plain to see. What it says is finer: **success is greater than the sum of the labour spent on it.**

This has a counterpart valid for everyone. The outcome of a work depends as much on a great number of conditions that are not ours as on the labour we put into it: timing, health, where we were born, the people who crossed our path, the accidents that kept us from making mistakes we did not make. To claim success entirely for oneself is not only a theological error but **a statistical one** — it counts the controllable variables as more numerous than they actually are. The most concrete cost of this error is this: trusting too much that the same method will work next time as well.

**Three. The difference between number and depth.**

*Afwājan* is a picture of success; but, as al-Ḥujurāt 49/14 reminds us, the number of those entering says nothing about the quality of those entering. This distinction holds for every structure that takes the number of participants as the measure of success — a community, an institution, a movement, a platform.

As the number rises, the reasons for participating diversify; as they diversify, average commitment falls. This is not a corruption but a natural consequence of growth in scale. That the sūra pairs this picture with the seeking of forgiveness is apt for exactly this reason: growing into a crowd is itself a risk, and being aware of the risk requires not giving up the crowd but putting the measure somewhere other than number.

**Four. If an opening is not a "taking" but an "opening".**

The political ring the word has taken on in its usual English rendering, "conquest", has largely covered over the meaning in its root. Yet *fatḥ* is the opening of what was closed, and the worth of an opened door depends on what passes through it. Whoever opens a door and passes oppression through it has turned the meaning of the word inside out.

This measure works at the personal level too. Every opportunity before a person is an "opening": a position, a door, an access, a right to speak. By the sūra's measure none of these is a gain in itself; the gain is **what passes through it.**

**Five. The way endings are closed.**

We have seen that the Qurʾān places the seeking of forgiveness at points of ending: at the end of the pilgrimage, at the end of the night, at the end of a task. The idea underlying this can be stated plainly for today as well: **no work has been done as well as it is claimed to have been done.**

This is not pessimism but measure. To say "it turned out flawless" while finishing a work means that the work will never be looked over again. To say "I did what I could; let its deficiencies be covered" is to close the work without locking the door completely. This is why the last word of the sūra is *tawwāb*: a return is always possible — both for the servant and, by the symmetry the language establishes, for the other side.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

- The report that the sūra is "the last sūra revealed" is found in the source, but it must be read together with the differing reports about "the last verse revealed". No definite ordering has been claimed.
- The report that the sūra was revealed at Minā during the Farewell Pilgrimage was transmitted; it was noted that it is disputed.
- Ibn ʿAbbās's reading, "this is the announcement of the appointed term", rests on the report in al-Bukhārī; but it was separately recorded that this is an **interpretation**, and that there is no expression concerning death in the text.
- The observation that "victory is the moment of highest moral risk" is my own inference; it is not a transmitted report, and it was marked as such in the text.
- The views on how the Prophet's seeking of forgiveness is to be understood were transmitted; no preference is adopted.
- The link between the senses "to swim" and "to hold free of deficiency" in the root *s-b-ḥ* is a relation established by the lexicographers; it was not presented as a settled etymological ruling.
- The details from the biographical sources concerning the conquest of Mecca (the general amnesty, the term *ṭulaqāʾ*, the year of the delegations) were transmitted from the historical sources and kept apart from the text of the Qurʾān. In this sūra the Qurʾān gives neither the name of a city nor the name of a person nor a date.
