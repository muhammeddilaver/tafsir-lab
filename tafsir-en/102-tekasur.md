# 102. Sūrat al-Takāthur

Eight verses; twenty-eight words, depending on the method of counting. It takes its name from the word **التكاثر** in the first verse.

The sūra opens not with an address but with a **diagnosis**. There is no "O mankind", no "Say", no question. The first word is a verb fastened straight onto the one addressed: *it distracted you.* By the time the sentence is formed the thing has already happened and finished — the verb is in the perfect. The one addressed hears that his situation has been determined before any proposal is put to him.

The skeleton of the sūra is five steps:

1. **Diagnosis** (1-2): there is an occupation, and that occupation lasts until death.
2. **Threat** (3-4): "soon you will know" — twice.
3. **Condition** (5): "if only you knew" — a conditional sentence whose answering clause is not spoken.
4. **Oath** (6-7): you will see, and see with the eye.
5. **Reckoning** (8): and you will be asked.

What is striking is this: nowhere in the sūra is a way out shown. There is no exception as in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr, no counter-example as in al-Māʿūn. The text makes the diagnosis, states the outcome, and closes. We shall come back at the end of the sūra to what this gap means.

## Meccan or Medinan

The majority say it is Meccan. There is also a view that it is Medinan, and that view rests on some of the reports about the occasion of revelation that we shall take up below — the dispute of two communities in Medina over superiority in number. The style fits the general character of the Meccan sūras: short verse-endings, hard direct address, a closing scene from the hereafter, the absence of legal content laying down rulings. I state no firm preference; the distinction does not change how the sūra is understood.

The basmala was treated in earlier sections and is not taken up again here; the verse numbers are counted without it.

## 102/1 — أَلْهَاكُمُ ٱلتَّكَاثُرُ

*Alhākumu't-takāthur*
"The rivalry for increase distracted you."

The whole weight of the sūra is gathered into two words. Both have to be opened up one by one, because the usual English renderings carry neither of them fully.

### أَلْهَىٰ — to distract

**Root: ل-ه-و.** Its primary sense in the lexicon: to turn one's face away from a thing, to move off from it, to be occupied with something else. From this comes the word **lahw**.

**What is lahw?** It is generally rendered "amusement", and that rendering misleads, because in English amusement is an innocent and bounded thing — a break in the middle of work. The Arabic *lahw* describes a function of another kind: **an occupation that holds a person back from his real business, that takes his attention from there and fastens it elsewhere.** At the centre of the word stands not merriment but **displacement**. Attention is lifted from one place and set down in another.

Some of the lexicographers draw this distinction between *lahw* and *laʿb* (play): *laʿb* is aimless play — a child's game; *lahw* is what draws a person away from what matters. The two come side by side often in the Qurʾān: *"The life of this world is only play and diversion"* (al-Anʿām 6/32; also Muḥammad 47/36, al-ʿAnkabūt 29/64).

The verb in the verse is of **Form IV** (*lahā* → *alhā*): not "he was distracted" but **"it distracted"** — transitive. That is, there is a subject at work, and that subject is distracting man. Man stands here in the position of the one acted upon: not distracting himself, but having been distracted.

**The root's usage in the Qurʾān confirms this reading.** The verb and its derivatives occur almost everywhere in the context of "holding back from remembrance, from the real business":

- *"O you who believe! Let not your wealth and your children distract you from the remembrance of God"* (al-Munāfiqūn 63/9). The same form of the verb, the same structure. And what does the holding back is here named openly: wealth and children.
- *"Men whom neither trade nor sale distracts from the remembrance of God"* (al-Nūr 24/37). The same verb, negated — that is, a description of those who are not held back.
- *"Leave them be; let them eat, let them take their enjoyment, let long expectation distract them. Soon they will know."* (al-Ḥijr 15/3).

**This third one deserves a pause.** Al-Ḥijr 15/3 builds, in a single verse, the skeleton of the first three verses of Sūrat al-Takāthur: the same root (*yulhihim*), and after it a threat cast in the same pattern (*fa-sawfa yaʿlamūn* — "soon they will know"). The difference lies at one point only: in al-Takāthur what distracts is *takāthur*, in al-Ḥijr it is *al-amal* (long expectation, a picture of the future built as though it would never end). The two sūras name one mechanism by two different names. The Qurʾān's explaining of itself is very clearly visible here.

- *"while their hearts are at play and diversion"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/3). The word *lāhiya* here is from the same root, and it gives an important piece of information: *lahw* is the work of the heart. The body may be in one place; the question is where the attention is.

**That the verb comes in the perfect.** Not "it will distract you" or "it is distracting you", but **"it distracted you."** In Arabic the perfect (*māḍī*) reports what has occurred and been completed. The sentence is not a warning but a **record of proceedings**. No debate is opened; what has happened is entered on the record.

There is a further consequence of this: a diagnosis built with a perfect verb does not put the one addressed on the defensive, because there is no proposal to defend against. He stands not before a demand he might object to, but before a picture shown to him.

### ٱلتَّكَاثُر — rivalry in abundance

The name of the sūra and its key word. It cannot be understood without knowing its pattern.

**Root: ك-ث-ر.** Abundance. From the same root: *kathīr* (much), *kathra* (abundance), *akthar* (more, the majority), *kawthar*.

**Pattern: تَفَاعُل (*tafāʿul*).** This is the vital point of the word. In Arabic the *tafāʿul* form conveys **reciprocity** (*mushāraka*) — it shows the act passing back and forth between two or more parties:

- *qātala* (he killed) → *taqātala* (they killed one another)
- *ʿārafa* (he knew) → *taʿārafa* (they came to know one another)
- *fākhara* (he boasted) → *tafākhara* (they boasted against one another)
- *kaththara* (he multiplied) → **takāthara** (they vied with one another in abundance)

So *takāthur* does **not** mean "to multiply". To multiply is *takthīr*, a separate pattern. *Takāthur* is **to claim superiority over one another by abundance, to compete over who has more**.

This difference changes the whole sūra. The pattern tells us this: **what is criticised is not accumulation itself, but accumulation's becoming relational.** The value of abundance lies not in itself but in the comparison. Not what is in the hand, but how much more it is than what is in another's.

The practical consequence of this is heavy: **in the reciprocal pattern there is no finishing line.** An absolute threshold can be crossed — one can say "this much is enough" and arrive there. But a relative measure has no threshold, because the measure is moving; as the other side advances, the target advances too. This is why the natural end of *takāthur* is the place named in the second verse: the grave. What ends the race is not satisfaction but death.

**The Qurʾān's own definition.** This word occurs in one other place in the Qurʾān, and there inside a definition:

*"Know that the life of this world is only play, and diversion, and adornment, and boasting among yourselves, and rivalry in abundance of wealth and children."* (al-Ḥadīd 57/20)

This verse is the lexicon of Sūrat al-Takāthur. In it there is *laʿb*, there is **lahw**, there is *zīna*, there is *tafākhur* and there is **takāthur**. Two of them are of the same pattern (*tafākhur* and *takāthur*) — both, that is, are reciprocal. And the verse says something Sūrat al-Takāthur does not say: abundance of what. *"Of wealth and children."*

**And here the most striking choice of the sūra comes into view: Sūrat al-Takāthur does not say this.**

### Abundance of what? What the silence means

The subject of the verb in the verse is *al-takāthur*, and the word comes **absolute**: annexed to nothing, bounded by nothing. It does not say "rivalry in abundance of wealth", nor "of children", nor "of number".

In Arabic, leaving a word without its complement in this way is called **ibhām** (leaving indefinite), and it is not a random omission; it has a recognised function in rhetoric: to keep the scope of the word open-ended. What is left unsaid contains all of what might have been said.

The consequences of this:

**One.** The sūra is not a criticism of wealth. Had it been a criticism of wealth it would have said wealth — as al-Ḥadīd 57/20 does say it, as al-Humaza 104/2 does say it. That it is not said here shows that the target is **not the material but the pattern**.

**Two.** The material of the rivalry can change; the pattern does not. The man who competes by the number of his tribe, the man who competes by his flock, the man who competes by his title are all inside the same act. Nor does the moral value of the material change this: **to compete in the abundance of a good thing is also *takāthur*.** The sūra has not closed this door; it has left it open.

**Three.** The indefiniteness requires the one addressed to draw up his own list. The question "what was it that distracted you" is handed over to the reader.

### Word order

The order of the sentence is: *alhā* (verb) + *kum* (object) + *al-takāthur* (subject).

In Arabic the ordinary order is verb–subject–object. Here the object appears to have come forward, but it would be wrong to count this a point of rhetoric: the object is an **attached pronoun** (*kum*), and an attached pronoun must in any case cling to the verb. The word order is therefore obligatory, not a choice.

Against that, there are things here that really are choices:

**That it opens with a verbal sentence.** The sūra opens not with a nominal sentence (something like "man has been distracted") but with a verb. In Arabic a verbal sentence conveys *ḥudūth* (coming to be, occurrence, renewal); a nominal sentence conveys fixity. What is described here is not a state; what is recorded is **an event that has happened**.

**That no introduction is made.** In the Qurʾān sūras mostly open with an address, a command, an oath, or with the disjoined letters. Sūras that open directly with a verb of accusation are few. Near examples: *"The command of God has come"* (al-Naḥl 16/1), *"The reckoning of mankind has drawn near"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/1), *"The Hour has drawn near"* (al-Qamar 54/1). What all of these have in common is that the introduction is skipped and the reader is left in the middle of the scene.

**Address in the second person plural.** Not "man was distracted" but "it distracted **you**". The sūra does not choose third-person narration; it turns to the one addressed. And this address runs to the end of the sūra: *taʿlamūn, taʿlamūn, taʿlamūn, la-tarawunna, la-tarawunnahā, la-tusʾalunna.* Through eight verses it does not once pass into the third person. From beginning to end the sūra speaks looking into the face of the one addressed.

## 102/2 — حَتَّىٰ زُرْتُمُ ٱلْمَقَابِرَ

*Ḥattā zurtumu'l-maqābir*
"Until you visited the graves."

One of the most discussed short verses in the Qurʾān. And that because of the choice of a single word.

### حَتَّىٰ — the particle that draws the limit

**حتى** is the particle that conveys *ghāya* (a limit, a point of arrival): "as far as", "until". It shows how far an act runs on.

Here it gives two pieces of information:

**One.** The distraction has an end — so it was not unbounded.
**Two.** That end is not an end set by man. The race finishes not by anyone saying "enough" but by striking a limit from outside.

So the diagnosis of the first verse is given in the second together with its duration: **a whole lifetime.** The occupation is not a period but a life.

### زُرْتُمْ — you visited

**Root: ز-و-ر.** The lexicographers say that beneath the root lies the sense of **inclining, turning towards a direction**; *ziyāra* is to turn towards someone. *Zūr* (falsehood, crooked speech), which comes from the same root, carries this core too — speech bent away from the truth: *"Shun false speech"* (al-Ḥajj 22/30).

But the whole point of the verse lies not in the lexical sense of the word but in **the implication the word necessarily carries**:

**A visit is by definition temporary.** A visitor is not a resident of the place he goes to. He comes, stays a while, goes. Whoever uses the word "visit" has excluded permanence from the outset.

The verse does not say "you died" (*mittum*), does not say "you entered the grave", does not say "you took up residence in the graves". It says **"you visited"**. The choice of word takes the grave out of being a destination and makes it a stopping place.

This point is often voiced in the classical commentaries, and it agrees exactly with what follows in the sūra: verses 3-8 describe what comes after the grave. The word, that is, announces in advance what is to come. At the end of a visit the visitor has to go somewhere else; and the sūra is about to say where.

A side note: some exegetes have counted this word among the text-internal indications of the hereafter. This is an inference and a possibility carried by the language; it is right to record it not as a ruling but as a horizon the word opens.

### ٱلْمَقَابِر — the graves

**Root: ق-ب-ر.** *Qabr* — the place where one is buried. **مَقْبَرَة** is a noun of place: a place of burial, a graveyard. Its plural is *maqābir*.

There is one interesting place where the Qurʾān uses this root: *"Then He caused him to die and had him placed in the grave"* (ʿAbasa 80/21). There the burial of man is mentioned as one of the things done for him — as a kind of honouring. This takes the grave out of being merely a pit and turns it into a treatment particular to man.

That the word comes in the **plural** is also striking. Not "you visited the grave" but "the graves". It may have come in the plural because the one addressed is plural; but there is a further reading, and it is connected with the reports: each community its own graveyard, its own dead. The competing parties carried the competition as far as their graveyards.

### The disagreement: what does "visiting the graves" mean?

The exegetes read this expression in two main directions. Both have been defended in the sources.

| Reading | What it means | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **To die** (the widespread one) | "You were distracted until you died and were laid in the grave." *Visiting* is a figure for death | What follows in the sūra describes what is beyond death; *ḥattā* closes off the span of a life; the temporariness of a visit agrees with the resurrection | In Arabic "to visit the grave" is not an established idiom for dying; the assumption of a figure is required |
| **Actually going to the graveyard** | The rivalry went so far that, counting the living no longer sufficing, they went to the graveyard and counted the dead | The reports transmitted as the occasion of revelation; the preservation of the word's literal (*ḥaqīqa*) sense | Since verses 3-8 of the sūra describe what is after death, the second verse is expected to touch on death |

**The two readings do not exclude one another**, and this has to be said. The text can set up the scene of an actual going to the graveyard and at the same time imply death. Indeed, even on the second reading the picture is this: **the dead are being counted as material for the rivalry** — that is, not even death has been left outside the race; it has been drawn into it. This is a heavier picture than the first reading gives.

If I am to state my own preference: the first reading sits better with the structure of the sūra, because *ḥattā* marks the end of a span, and that the span should be a lifetime agrees with the rest of the text. But this is a preference and it is not binding; the second reading has a force of its own and rests on the ground of the transmitted reports.

### Historical background and the reports about the occasion of revelation

The common core of the reports transmitted as the occasion of revelation is this: **two communities disputed over superiority in number and honour; they counted their living, and when no result came of it they went to the graveyard and counted their dead as well.**

The reports differ as to which communities these were: some mention two branches of Quraysh, some two Anṣār tribes in Medina. I do not settle these reports by giving names, because the versions do not agree with one another and the state of the chains is disputed. But the **behaviour** the reports describe fits the social structure of the period exactly, and that part of it is historically sound:

In a tribal society, **number is directly security and standing.** In a structure with no central state, no regular army, no functioning court, the only thing that protects a man's right is the number of men behind him. In a blood feud, deterrence is measured by number; in negotiations over alliance, weight is determined by number. This is why one of the most widespread themes of Arabic poetry has been *mufākhara* — boasting of the abundance of one's tribe, of the crowdedness of one's lineage.

The logic of the practice of counting the dead comes out of this as well: the crowdedness of a lineage is measured not only by the number alive today but by depth in the past. A long and crowded lineage means a deep-rooted tribe.

So what the sūra takes as its target was not, for that society, a deviance but **a measure of value counted normal and reasonable.** What the Qurʾān targets here is not a crime but **a unit of measurement itself.**

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

The clearest portrait in the Qurʾān of this same cast of mind is the story of the owner of the two gardens in Sūrat al-Kahf:

*"He had fruit. And he said to his companion, while talking with him: I am greater than you in wealth and mightier in men."* (al-Kahf 18/34)

The sentence is exactly the definition of *takāthur*: the word **akthar** (more) is from the same root as *takāthur*, and the comparison is made openly — "than you". What is spoken of is not the wealth itself but the difference. And two items are counted: wealth and the number of men; that is, the pair "of wealth and children" of al-Ḥadīd 57/20.

And what follows shows what *takāthur* does to a man:

*"He entered his garden, wronging himself, and said: I do not think that this will ever perish. Nor do I think that the Hour will come."* (al-Kahf 18/35-36)

The end of the counting-race is the supposition of permanence. This sentence binds us straight to Sūrat al-Humaza — there the same supposition will be put in another word: *"he supposes that his wealth has made him immortal"*.

Other connections:

- *"Wealth and sons are the adornment of the life of this world"* (al-Kahf 18/46). This verse does not disparage wealth; it states its **category**: adornment. Adornment is the domain not of function but of appearance.
- *"Then he hopes that I will give him more"* (al-Muddaththir 74/15). This is the shortest description in the Qurʾān of the race that does not end. Before it, that man is said to have been given "extended wealth" and "sons before his eyes" (al-Muddaththir 74/12-13) — that is, the one who already has much is hoping for more. The matter of the threshold that cannot be found.

## 102/3 — كَلَّا سَوْفَ تَعْلَمُونَ

*Kallā sawfa taʿlamūn*
"No! Soon you will know."

The breaking point of the sūra. The two-verse picture is finished; now the picture is intervened in.

### كَلَّا — the particle that cuts

One of the hardest particles in Arabic, and used with a frequency peculiar to the Qurʾān.

**On its function the lexicographers gather into two main views:**

**The first (the majority) — rejection and restraint (*radd* and *zajr*).** *Kallā* both **rejects** the preceding state ("no, it is not so") and **restrains** the one addressed from it ("give it up, stop"). It does two jobs at once: it erases a judgement and it halts a behaviour. No single English word carries it; the pair "No! Enough." is an approximate rendering.

**The second — affirmation, or opening (*taṣdīq* / *istiftāḥ*).** Some lexicographers say that in certain places the word may be an opening particle meaning "truly, indeed". This view is a minority one and it is not convincing for the usage in al-Takāthur, because here there plainly is something to be rejected — the state of distraction in the first verse.

**An observation about the word's distribution.** *Kallā* occurs in the Qurʾān overwhelmingly in **the short, hard Meccan sūras**, and almost every time it cuts off a claim or a state. Its usage in sūras such as al-ʿAlaq, al-Muddaththir, al-Maʿārij and al-Muṭaffifīn is always of this kind. The nature of the word is built upon cutting, not upon argument.

**But what exactly is being rejected here?** In the first two verses there was an accusation, but no *claim* had been openly voiced. What is *kallā* cutting off?

What it cuts is the assumption that was never spoken: **the assumption that abundance is a gain.** Those competing supposed that they were winning. *Kallā* erases that supposition — although it was never put into words. The particle answers a claim the one addressed has not stated but is living.

This is how *kallā* typically works in the Qurʾān: it takes aim at claims that have been built not as speech but as behaviour.

### سَوْفَ — "soon"

In Arabic there are two instruments for conveying the future: the **سـ (*sīn*)** attached to the head of the verb, and the separately written **سَوْفَ (*sawfa*)**.

According to the majority of the lexicographers the difference between them is this: *sīn* conveys the near future, *sawfa* a more distant and wider one (*tarākhī* — the placing of a distance in between).

**In the context of a threat this distance is not a lightening but an aggravation.** Between a threat that comes "right now" and one that comes "one day, without fail", the second is generally the more wearing: its coming is certain, its time is not. Uncertainty lengthens the life of a threat.

*Sawfa* also shows that a respite is being given. The punishment is not being carried out at this moment — there is a stretch of time in between. What that time is for is not said; but that the sūra repeats the same sentence in the fourth verse suggests this gap has not been left idly.

### تَعْلَمُونَ — "you will know"

The **object of the verb is not spoken.** What will they know?

This is the same technique as in the first verse. There, "abundance of what" was not said; here, "knowledge of what" is not said. At two critical points the sūra leaves the one addressed alone with a gap.

In rhetoric this is one of the known ways of enlarging a threat: **the punishment not named is greater than the one named**, because it has no limit. When it is said "you will see such and such", the mind of the one addressed draws a boundary. When it is said "you will see", it cannot.

That the verb has been chosen from the root of **knowledge** is meaningful too. The sūra does not say "you will be punished" or "you will be sorry". It says **"you will know"**. The name of the problem is thus set down from the outset: this is a problem of knowledge. Man is not merely doing something wrong; he **knows** something wrong — or rather, he does not know the right of it. The fifth verse of the sūra will carry this diagnosis on openly: *"If only you knew with the knowledge of certainty."*

This is what separates the sūra from a moral sermon. Al-Takāthur does not diagnose idleness or wickedness; it diagnoses **a deficiency of knowledge**.

## 102/4 — ثُمَّ كَلَّا سَوْفَ تَعْلَمُونَ

*Thumma kallā sawfa taʿlamūn*
"Then again, no! Soon you will know."

The same sentence, with a connective at its head, a second time.

### Why is there a repetition?

In the Qurʾān it is not frequent for a sentence to be repeated word for word one directly after the other. The exegetes read this repetition in two ways:

**The first reading — emphasis (*taʾkīd*).** The same threat is said a second time, because once is not counted enough. In Arabic, verbatim repetition is one of the strongest means of emphasis. On this reading *thumma* conveys no order in time; it conveys **an order of degree** (the usage the lexicographers call *tarākhī fi'l-rutba*): the second warning is heavier than the first.

**The second reading — two separate times.** The first "you will know" points to the moment of death or to the grave, the second to the resurrection and the raising. On this reading *thumma* conveys a real order in time, and there is a long interval between the two knowings. This view too is widespread in the classical commentaries, and it agrees with the second verse of the sūra: a visit to the graves had been spoken of, so something happens in the grave.

Both readings can be defended. The second appears to sit a little better with the flow of the sūra, because the text is already setting up a timeline: the race → the grave → ? → the seeing of *al-Jaḥīm* → the questioning. But this is a preference; there is no proof that weakens the first reading.

### The work the repetition does

The repetition has a further effect, independent of meaning, and it has to do with the rhythm of the text.

The first two verses are fast and dense: a diagnosis, a limit. The third verse cuts. The fourth verse, **by repeating the cut, brings things to a stop.** The text is not advancing; it is standing in the same place. The reader waits for the advance and the advance does not come.

This is the text keeping the one addressed waiting. And waiting is what is needed for the threat to work — because what is threatened has already been thrown forward into the future by *sawfa*. The sūra does with its form what it says in its words: while postponement is being described, it is postponing.

One further observation: *kallā* occurs three times in the sūra (verses 3, 4 and 5). Three verses of an eight-verse text begin with this particle. The middle of the sūra consists of three rejections laid one on top of another.

## 102/5 — كَلَّا لَوْ تَعْلَمُونَ عِلْمَ ٱلْيَقِينِ

*Kallā law taʿlamūna ʿilma'l-yaqīn*
"No! If only you knew with the knowledge of certainty."

The densest verse of the sūra. The third *kallā*, and after it a conditional sentence whose answering clause is not spoken.

### لَوْ — a condition that did not come about

**لو** in Arabic is of the kind called the *imtināʿ* condition: it supposes a condition that did not come about, and reports that the result depending on it did not come about either. "Had it been thus, it would have been so — but it was not."

The verse, that is, registers at the level of language that the one addressed does **not** possess certain knowledge. Had they known, the situation would not have been this.

### The answer that is not spoken

The answering clause of the conditional (*jawāb al-law*) **is not spoken.** "If only you knew with the knowledge of certainty…" — and there is no more.

This is a recognised manner of speech in Arabic and the lexicographers treat it under the heading of *ḥadhf* (ellipsis). Its function is this: when the answer is not spoken, the one addressed completes it himself, and the answer a man completes himself is always more effective than the one spoken. Besides which, the answer is not reduced to a single thing.

Possible completions: *…you would not have entered that race. …you would not have been distracted for a single moment. …you would have been seized with horror. …you would have let go of what you had gathered.* All of them may be right; the text chooses none of them.

This gap is the third instance of something the sūra has already done twice: **abundance of what** was not said, **what you will know** was not said, **what will happen** is not said. In a text of eight verses, three deliberate gaps. The sūra keeps the one addressed continually at work in his own mind.

### عِلْمَ ٱلْيَقِين — *ʿilm al-yaqīn*

**The root of يَقِين: ي-ق-ن.** The complete removal of doubt; knowledge settling, becoming fixed. Some of the lexicographers record that beneath the root lies the sense of **clearing, settling, coming to rest** — as muddy water clears and settles to the bottom. *Yaqīn* is supposition settling out into knowledge.

In the Qurʾān the word occurs both in the sense of knowledge and in the sense of death:

- *"and of the hereafter they are certain"* (al-Baqara 2/4) — the verbal form.
- *"because they were patient and were certain of Our signs…"* (al-Sajda 32/24).
- *"And serve your Lord until the certainty comes to you"* (al-Ḥijr 15/99). According to the majority of the exegetes the *yaqīn* here is **death** — because death is the moment about which no doubt at all remains. This usage of the word makes a silent link with the second verse of Sūrat al-Takāthur.

**The grammatical analysis of the construct.** *ʿIlm al-yaqīn* is resolved in two ways:

- **An annexation that is adjectival in form:** originally *al-ʿilm al-yaqīn* (the knowledge that is certain), turned into a construct. That is, "certain knowledge".
- **An absolute object:** on the structure *taʿlamūna ʿilman* (to know with a knowing), the verbal noun being annexed: "to know with the knowing of certainty".

The two come out in the same place and make no difference of meaning. The emphasis is this: the **kind** of the knowledge is being specified. The matter is not knowing but **how one knows.**

### The degrees of certainty

Three forms of this construct occur in the Qurʾān:

| Construct | Where | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| **علم اليقين** (*ʿilm al-yaqīn*) | al-Takāthur 102/5 | Certain knowledge; knowledge reached by report, by proof, by reason |
| **عين اليقين** (*ʿayn al-yaqīn*) | al-Takāthur 102/7 | The certainty of seeing with the eye |
| **حق اليقين** (*ḥaqq al-yaqīn*) | al-Wāqiʿa 56/95; al-Ḥāqqa 69/51 | The certainty of being joined to the thing itself |

**Honesty is required here.** In later periods — particularly in the Sufi literature — this trio was turned into a systematic order of stages: to know by report → to see → to live, or to be. This systematisation **does not occur, gathered together and defined, in the Qurʾān itself.** The Qurʾān uses these three constructs, but it does not present them as a teaching in three steps; nor do the three ever come side by side in one place.

This does not mean that the systematisation is wrong. That the first two constructs come **in the same sūra, in the right order** and in verses following one another shows that the idea of a gradation has a ground in the text. But to present it as a system of terms set up by the Qurʾān goes beyond the data we have.

There is an example widely repeated in this literature, and I transmit it because it is instructive, without ascribing it to any one person: *When someone tells you that a fire exists, you know it — this is ʿilm al-yaqīn. When you see the flame with your own eye, it becomes ʿayn al-yaqīn. And when your hand touches the fire, you have arrived at ḥaqq al-yaqīn.*

**Where the sūra stands within this trio is important.** Al-Takāthur contains the first two and does not contain the third. The text passes from knowledge to seeing and stops there. I read this not as a deficiency but as a limit — and I must state that this is my own reading: the sūra does not describe the moment of contact to the one addressed; it carries him only as far as the moment of seeing. The place left undescribed is exactly the place where the unspoken answer of the conditional in verse 5 lies.

### The central claim of the verse: not knowing

Here the sūra brings its diagnosis to its final form.

Man does not enter into *takāthur* because he is wicked. **He enters it because he does not know.** More exactly: he enters it although he supposes that he knows, because the knowledge is not of a kind that works. Almost every man knows that he will die; there is nobody who does not know it. But this knowledge does not change the behaviour.

This is exactly what the verse says: *you have knowledge, but not of the kind of certainty.* The difference between the two knowledges is not a difference of truth — it is a difference of **power to affect**. If you do not live according to what you know to be true, that knowledge is of another kind.

## 102/6 — لَتَرَوُنَّ ٱلْجَحِيمَ

*La-tarawunna'l-jaḥīm*
"Truly, you will surely see the Hellfire."

### A three-layered reinforcement

The **لَ** at the head of the verse is not a simple particle of emphasis. In Arabic it is the *lām al-qasam*: **the lām of an oath that is not spoken.** There is, that is, an elided (*maḥdhūf*) oath, and the sentence is the answer to that oath. Its open form is roughly this: *"By God, you will surely see."*

The **ـنَّ** at the end of the verb bears the name *nūn al-taʾkīd al-thaqīla* (the heavy nūn of emphasis). It is the strongest instrument of emphasis that can be attached to a verb in Arabic.

Three layers are thus laid one on top of another:

1. The unspoken oath
2. The lām of the oath
3. The heavy nūn of emphasis

For comparison: in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr, treated in the preceding section, there was also a three-layered reinforcement (oath + *inna* + lām). The two sūras use the same rhetorical density, and by the measure of rhetoric that density shows one thing only: **the one addressed is in the position of a denier.** The speech is being made in front of someone who will not believe what is said.

**A note on the variant readings.** In some readings the verb is read in the passive: *la-turawunna* — "you will surely be made to see". The direction of the meaning changes (being shown instead of seeing), the ruling stays the same. Since I cannot say with certainty which imām it belongs to, I give no name; that the reading is transmitted is recorded.

### ٱلْجَحِيم — *al-jaḥīm*

**Root: ج-ح-م.** The fierce blazing of fire, the flaring of embers. *Jaḥmat al-nār* — the fierceness of the flame.

The Qurʾān uses several names for Hell, and the emphasis of each name is different: *jahannam*, *nār* (fire), *saqar*, *laẓā*, *hāwiya*, *saʿīr* and **ḥuṭama**. This last will be used in al-Humaza, which comes two sūras later. Two neighbouring sūras name the same place by two different properties: al-Takāthur by **the fierceness of the flame**, al-Humaza by **the function of breaking and crushing to pieces**.

The **al-** at the head of the word conveys definiteness: the known, the already described. Those addressed are not hearing this word for the first time.

### The passage from "knowing" to "seeing"

A threshold is crossed here in the flow of the sūra.

- Verse 3: **you will know**
- Verse 4: **you will know**
- Verse 5: **if only you knew**
- Verse 6: **you will see**

Through three verses the verb of knowing was repeated; in the fourth the text passes to the verb of seeing. This is the gradation set up in verse 5 being applied within the text: *ʿilm al-yaqīn* is turning into *ʿayn al-yaqīn*.

And the direction of the change is one-way. **Knowledge depends on choice; seeing does not.** A man can choose not to know a thing, can set his knowledge aside, can forget it, can postpone it. But he cannot choose not to see what is placed before his eyes. The sūra turns the knowledge the one addressed is fleeing into a form that cannot be fled.

From this comes the central irony of the sūra: **certainty will come, but it will come at the moment when it is no longer of any use.** The wish "if only you knew" in verse 5 is precisely for this — had the knowledge come in its time, something would have changed; the seeing will come in its time and will change nothing.

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

*"There is not one of you but will come to it; this is a decree settled upon your Lord."* (Maryam 19/71)

This verse carries the same all-inclusiveness as *la-tarawunna'l-jaḥīm*: the address admits of no exception. The exegetes differ over what the word *wurūd* (coming to) there means — passing over it, seeing it, or entering it. The disagreement has not been settled and I state no preference here. But the point the two verses share is beyond dispute: the seeing of Hell is a scene that comes before the division between believer and unbeliever.

## 102/7 — ثُمَّ لَتَرَوُنَّهَا عَيْنَ ٱلْيَقِينِ

*Thumma la-tarawunnahā ʿayna'l-yaqīn*
"Then you will surely see it with the certainty of seeing with the eye."

### عَيْنَ ٱلْيَقِين — *ʿayn al-yaqīn*

**عَيْن** carries two basic senses in Arabic, and both of them are at work here:

**One. The eye.** The organ of sight. On this reading the construct means "the certainty of the eye", that is, direct witnessing. Not what is heard but what is seen.

**Two. The thing itself, its very self.** In Arabic *ʿayn al-shayʾ* means "the very thing itself"; the words *aynen* ("exactly") and *aynı* ("the same") used in Turkish today come from this sense as well. On this reading the construct means "certainty itself" — not a degree of certainty, that is, but certainty in person.

The two senses do not conflict; they complete one another. What is seen with the eye is the thing itself.

Grammatically *ʿayn al-yaqīn* is in the accusative and is analysed either as an absolute object or as a circumstantial qualifier. The structure is exactly the same as *ʿilm al-yaqīn* in the preceding verse — which shows that the two constructs have been deliberately paired.

### ثُمَّ — seeing upon seeing

In the preceding verse it had already been said "you will see". Why again?

The principal explanations the exegetes give:

**One — two different seeings.** The first from afar, the second from close by. What is seen in the place of the gathering and what is seen when one has entered it are not the same thing. Here *thumma* conveys a real interval of time.

**Two — emphasis.** The same judgement is said a second time, this time with its quality specified. *Thumma* conveys distance in respect of degree.

**Three — the specifying of the kind of seeing.** The sixth verse announced the seeing; the seventh says **in what degree of certainty** that seeing will be. On this reading the second verse is like an adjective to the first.

All three can be defended. The third is supported by the deliberate repetition here of the *ʿilm al-yaqīn* pattern of verse 5: the sūra had named a kind of knowledge; now it names a kind of seeing.

### The difference between knowing and seeing

This is the most fruitful place in the sūra, and it is worth dwelling on.

The text describes the gulf between a man's possessing a piece of knowledge and his living according to that knowledge. It sets this down not as a moral defect but as **a matter of the kind of knowledge**: if the knowledge in your hand is not working, the problem lies not in its truth but in its kind.

The counterpart of this in the social sciences is real and is not forced. It is a phenomenon long discussed in psychology and the behavioural sciences: people's having correct knowledge on a subject is generally not sufficient for them to behave in accordance with that knowledge. This gap is called by various names — the knowledge–behaviour gap, the value–action gap. Its best-known examples are health behaviours: it is not an unusual situation for those who know the risks best to be the ones who take those risks most.

Various explanations are put forward as to why this should be so: that abstract knowledge carries no emotional weight; that distant consequences lose their value against near gains (the phenomenon called "temporal discounting" in economics); that risk needs to be perceived personally rather than statistically. I transmit these as attempts at explanation; none of them is settled.

But the observation itself is sound, and the description in the verse overlaps with it: **that knowledge does not change behaviour until it turns into seeing.** The difference of the verse is this: the modern literature defines this gap as a problem and looks for ways of closing it. The sūra says that the gap **will necessarily close** — and that at the moment it closes it will be too late. This is the real problem the sūra sets up.

## 102/8 — ثُمَّ لَتُسْـَٔلُنَّ يَوْمَئِذٍ عَنِ ٱلنَّعِيمِ

*Thumma la-tusʾalunna yawmaʾidhin ʿani'n-naʿīm*
"Then on that day you will surely be asked about the bounty."

The last verse of the sūra, and the verse that changes the picture entirely.

### لَتُسْـَٔلُنَّ — the passive form

The verb is reinforced with the lām and the heavy nūn of emphasis, as in the two preceding verses. But there is a difference: this verb is in the **passive**. "You will be asked" — who does the asking is not said.

This choice does two things:

**One.** The emphasis falls not on the questioner but on **the questioning**. What matters is not who asks, but that the question cannot be escaped.

**Two.** The questioner is known already; saying it is unnecessary. In Arabic, when the passive form leaves the agent unnamed because he is known, it produces a kind of veiled magnification.

And there is this too: throughout the sūra the one addressed had stood in the position of subject — *you were distracted, you visited, you will know, you will see.* In the last verse he ceases to be subject and becomes **object**. The man who counted for a whole lifetime becomes at the end the party who is asked about. This fall in grammatical position is the very fall the sūra describes.

### يَوْمَئِذٍ — "on that day"

An adverb formed of *yawm* (day) joined with *idh* (at that time). The tanwīn at its end is, according to the lexicographers, **compensatory** (*ʿiwaḍ*): it stands in the place of an elided clause. Its expansion is therefore roughly this: *"on the day when you see al-Jaḥīm with your own eye."*

This binds the time of the questioning to the preceding verse. The questioning comes after the seeing. First it will be seen, then it will be asked.

### عَنِ ٱلنَّعِيم — bounty

**Root: ن-ع-م.** The concrete core of the root is **softness**. *Nāʿim* — soft. *Naʿma* — the softness of life, its ease, its comfort. From this are derived *niʿma* (a good thing given) and *naʿīm* (a state of abundance and ease).

The Qurʾān uses this word **mostly for the Garden**: *jannāt al-naʿīm* — the gardens of bounty (Yūnus 10/9, Luqmān 31/8, al-Wāqiʿa 56/12 and other places). *"The righteous are surely in bounty"* (al-Infiṭār 82/13; al-Muṭaffifīn 83/22). And from the same root: *"On that day there are faces which shine with bounty"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/8).

**This piece of information sets the tone of the verse.** *Naʿīm* is not a word with a negative resonance; it is the word the Qurʾān chooses in order to describe the Garden. The verse does not say "you will be asked about the forbidden", does not say "you will be asked about waste". It says that you will be asked about **the bounty** — that is, about what is good, what has been given, what is pleasant.

The subject of the questioning is not evil but **good**. This completes the whole logic of the sūra: the problem was not wealth itself but the relationship formed with wealth. And now, likewise, the problem is not the bounty itself; it is what has been done with the bounty.

### What is meant by "bounty"? The disagreement

The early generations and the exegetes have differed over the scope of this word. The principal views:

| View | Its scope |
|---|---|
| All the bounties of this world | Everything given: health, security, food, time, reason |
| The surplus above necessary need | Not what is required in order to live, but the ease beyond that |
| Health, security and free time | Bounties consumed without being noticed, whose cost is not felt |
| The bounty of Islam and faith | Religion itself, as the greatest bounty |

I do not give the names of the holders of these views one by one, because I cannot speak with certainty about the ascriptions. But the variety of views is itself worth recording: this wide spread of opinion about the scope of the word arises from the *al-* in the verse conveying the genus — that is, from its covering the whole of bounty.

**Two widely mentioned reports.** In a famous hadith found in al-Bukhārī it is transmitted that the Prophet said: *"There are two bounties in which many people are cheated: health and free time."* This hadith points to the two most easily overlooked items of the concept of "bounty"; both are bounties that cannot be counted, cannot be accumulated, and are not noticed as they are consumed.

In another report found in the hadith collections it is related that the Prophet and two of his companions went out from hunger, ate dates and drank cool water in the house of one of the Companions, and that the Prophet then said that this too was among the bounties that would be asked about. Since I cannot say with certainty in which collection this report stands, I do not write the name of a source. The idea the report carries is plain: the threshold of the questioning is not high but very low.

**A dispute of interpretation.** In the classical period an anxiety was voiced about this verse: if one is to be asked about every bounty, is benefiting from bounty itself an evil? The answer given has generally been this: the verse brings up not the bounty but **the account of the bounty**; what is asked is not "why did you benefit from it" but "what did you do" and "were you thankful". I transmit this answer as a preference; it agrees with the sūra's own logic, because from the beginning the sūra has taken as its target not wealth but the relationship formed with wealth.

### The reversal that closes the sūra

The eight-verse text closes with a single movement, and to see this movement is to understand the sūra.

**At the beginning:** man is counting. His wealth, his children, his men, his dead — whatever it may be. The one who counts is the subject; what is counted is the object. The relation of ownership runs one way.

**At the end:** man is the party who is asked about. The things he counted are no longer his property but items on which an account will be given.

The sūra, that is, **turns ownership into a trust.** What is in your hand does not count as yours because it is in your hand; because it was given to you, it becomes a record about which you will be asked. What one has is not a possession but a **file**.

And if one looks closely, the sūra does not do this by way of a command. It does not say "do not give, do not accumulate, do not compete". It only states **the real nature of the relationship**. It issues no order; it corrects a definition.

## The sūra as a whole

### The sound structure

The verse-endings of the eight verses run as follows: *takāthur — maqābir — taʿlamūn — taʿlamūn — al-yaqīn — al-jaḥīm — al-yaqīn — al-naʿīm.*

A break can be seen. The first two verses close with the sound **r**. From the third onwards the verse-ending changes and begins to close with a long vowel plus a nasal sound (**n** or **m**).

The place of the change is meaningful: **the sound changes exactly where the subject changes.** The first two verses describe this world — the race and the graveyard. From the third verse to the end, the hereafter is described. As the text changes its subject it changes its sound as well.

I record this not as a claim of a miracle but as an example of the dense sound-weave of the short Meccan sūras. In these sūras of the Qurʾān, sound and content often move together.

### The structure of the sūra, and the thing that is missing

| Verse | What it does | Verb form |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Diagnosis | Perfect |
| 2 | The duration of the diagnosis | Perfect |
| 3 | Rejection + threat | Future |
| 4 | Rejection + threat (repeated) | Future |
| 5 | Rejection + a condition that did not come about | Conditional, no answering clause |
| 6 | An announcement under oath | Future |
| 7 | The same announcement, graded | Future |
| 8 | Reckoning | Future, passive |

The most striking thing in the table is that one of the columns is empty: **there is no command in the sūra, no prohibition, no way out.**

Let us make a comparison. Sūrat al-ʿAṣr lays down the ruling and then gives the exception. Sūrat al-Māʿūn describes, and implies the contrary of what it describes. Sūrat al-Balad speaks of the "steep ascent" and counts out how it is to be climbed. Al-Takāthur does none of these. It makes the diagnosis, announces the outcome, closes.

Is this a deficiency? The sūra's place in the codex answers this question, and we come to it below.

### The trio: al-Takāthur — al-ʿAṣr — al-Humaza

In the arrangement of the codex these three sūras stand side by side, and they take hold of three different faces of the same matter. This link was set up in the section on al-ʿAṣr; here I complete it.

First a note: **this is an observation about the arrangement of the muṣḥaf, not about the order of revelation.** The relation of the sūras to one another (*tanāsub al-suwar*) is a recognised subject in exegesis, but some scholars have preferred to remain cautious against the forcing of such links. The table below has to be read with that note in mind.

| | **al-Takāthur (102)** | **al-ʿAṣr (103)** | **al-Humaza (104)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Address** | Second person plural: "it distracted you" | Third person singular, the name of the species: "man" | Third person singular, an attribute: "whoever" |
| **Scale** | Social — rivalry, comparison | Belonging to the species — everyone | Individual — the portrait of a single character |
| **Direction** | Horizontal: competing with the one beside you | Not vertical but temporal | Vertical: crushing the one below you |
| **Counting** | *Takāthur* — rivalry in abundance | *Khusr* — a negative balance | *ʿAddadahu* — counting over and over |
| **Death** | "Visiting the graves" | Time itself | The refutation of the supposition "it made him immortal" |
| **A way out** | None | **There is one** (the exception: four conditions) | None |

The last row is decisive. **Only in the middle one of the three sūras is there a door out.** Al-Takāthur makes the diagnosis and closes with the outcome; al-Humaza makes the diagnosis and closes with the outcome. Al-ʿAṣr stands between the two and shows the single way out: faith, righteous deeds, enjoining the truth, enjoining patience.

There is a logic to this arrangement. It was stated in the section on al-ʿAṣr that that sūra changes the unit of measure: what has to be counted is not wealth but time. Al-Takāthur shows the social form of using the wrong unit, al-Humaza the individual form; al-ʿAṣr states the right unit and what is to be done with it.

One further observation: **the endings of the three sūras are of different kinds.** Al-Takāthur ends with a question ("you will be asked"); al-ʿAṣr ends with an answer (the four conditions); al-Humaza ends with a closed door ("it is closed over upon them"). Question — answer — closure. I record this not as a claim about structure but as an order visible in the arrangement of the muṣḥaf.

## The connection with social science

The linguistic analysis of the sūra points to a place that genuinely overlaps with certain concepts of modern social science. In this section I set that overlap out, and what I must state at the outset is this: **the sūra does not announce these concepts in advance.** The sūra describes a phenomenon; the modern concepts describe the same phenomenon in another language. The agreement between the two descriptions does not mean that one of them foretold the other.

### Conspicuous consumption

The economist and sociologist Thorstein Veblen put forward the concept of **"conspicuous consumption"** in *The Theory of the Leisure Class*, published in 1899. His thesis was this: above a certain level of prosperity, consumption is undertaken not to meet a need but **to display social position**. The function of the goods is not to be used but to be seen. Veblen set a further concept beside this one: comparative evaluation — people's measuring their own situation not absolutely but by those around them.

The point at which this concept overlaps with the word *takāthur* is very clear: **both of them say that value is relational.** In Veblen's thesis the meaning of consumption is born of comparison; in the *tafāʿul* pattern likewise the meaning of the verb is born of reciprocity. Arabic morphology has written into the structure of the word what a concept of social science states.

The difference between the two descriptions should be recorded as well. Veblen examines the phenomenon as the behaviour of a class; the sūra diagnoses it as a loss of attention. Veblen says "this is how it is"; the sūra says "this distracted you".

### The race that does not end: why there is no point of "enough"

The conclusion that came out of the analysis of the pattern was this: in a measure of reciprocity the threshold is undefined.

This is a phenomenon that has been observed independently in economics and in psychology, and it goes by several names.

**Hedonic adaptation.** In psychology, the name given to the phenomenon of people adapting relatively quickly to improvements in their conditions of life, their subjective well-being returning after a time to its former level. The comparison of the "hedonic treadmill" is widespread in the literature for describing this: the belt speeds up, you speed up too, but you stay where you are. Debate continues over the extent and the limits of the phenomenon — it has been shown that for some life events the adaptation remains partial.

**Relative income.** In economics there are findings that the level of happiness people report is related less to absolute income than to **income relative to those around them**. This debate goes by the name of the "Easterlin paradox" and **it is not settled**; there are contrary findings and criticisms of method. For that reason I mention it here not as a thesis but as a disputed literature.

**Positional goods.** The name given to goods whose value comes not from themselves but from their not being found in the hands of others. When everyone's share of a positional good increases, nobody's situation improves, because the good was already taking its value from the comparison.

The common conclusion of these three is what the second verse of the sūra says: **the race has no natural point of ending.** Had there been an absolute threshold ("this much wealth, this much security") it could have been arrived at. A relative threshold is not a place that can be arrived at, because the place does not stand still. The sūra says this in a single phrase: *ḥattā zurtumu'l-maqābir.* What ends the race is not satisfaction.

### Lahw and attention

The sūra's diagnosis is not a criticism of consumption but a diagnosis about **attention**. The verb is not "it forbade", "it corrupted", "it defiled"; it is **"it distracted"**.

This distinction is the soundest point at which the sūra connects with the present. For the most widespread form of loss in modern life is not the doing of an evil but the holding of attention continually somewhere else. In recent years a concept called **"the attention economy"** has been in use in economics and in communication studies: it rests on the thesis that the scarce resource is not information but attention, and that economic competition takes place not in the production of information but in the gathering of attention. Though the concept has its disputed sides, the situation it describes is familiar.

Let us recall that the core of the word *lahw* is **displacement**: attention is lifted from one place and set down in another. The word the sūra uses names exactly this operation.

And the usage in al-Anbiyāʾ 21/3 completes it: *"while their hearts are in lahw."* That is, *lahw* has to do not with where the body is but with where the heart is. A man can be present in the right place and be in the wrong place.

## What this says to the present

**First: the material of the rivalry changes, its pattern does not.**

That the sūra leaves the question "abundance of what" empty is its most serviceable side for today. The number of one's tribe is nobody's measure now; but the measure has not disappeared, it has changed its item. Today anything that can be counted and compared can be material for the same pattern: income, title, followers, publications, certificates, countries travelled to, books read, distance run, the grade a child obtains.

That the pattern is indifferent to the material brings a warning with it: **to compete in the number of a good thing is also *takāthur*.** The sūra does not sort the material according to its moral value, because its target is not the material but the manner of measuring. The number of one's works of charity, of one's acts of worship, of one's pieces of knowledge can also turn into an item in a race; and when it does, the act the sūra describes works exactly as before.

The distinguishing question is this: *would the quantity of this thing carry the same meaning for me if no comparison were being made?* If the answer is no, then that quantity is already being kept not for itself but for the comparison.

**Second: spending a life without doing evil.**

The sūra's diagnosis is not a moral violation but a loss of attention. This comes out at the same place as al-ʿAṣr's measure of "the cost of doing nothing", and the two together step outside the widespread understanding that defines morality solely as "not doing bad things".

For a man's balance sheet to be negative he does not have to have harmed anyone. It is enough that he be busy, productive, even content — provided only that his occupation has made him forget his real business. This is exactly the verb of the sūra: *alhā*. And the object of the verb is the man himself.

**Third: the removal of death from the field of vision.**

The sūra sets the limit of the occupation at the graveyard. The second verse is not a reminder of death but a **measuring rod**: it is used to show how long the race lasts.

One of the distinguishing features of modern life is that it has taken death out of the daily field of vision. Death mostly takes place in an institutional space, funeral arrangements have been professionalised, graveyards have been moved outside the settlement. There are practical reasons for this change and all of them are reasonable. But it has a side-effect as well: **the measuring rod has been let go of.**

A man who races without knowing how long the race will last cannot measure whether the race is reasonable either.

**Fourth: the distance between knowing and seeing.**

The most practical measure of the sūra is here. A man who says he knows something but does not live according to it is not lying; he possesses knowledge of a different kind.

The concrete counterpart of this is everywhere in daily life: knowing the health data and not changing the habit; seeing that a relationship has broken down and not intervening; noticing that an account does not add up and continuing not to look at it. What is common to all of them is not ignorance but the **coldness** of the knowledge.

The verse sets this difference down not as an accusation but as a diagnosis — and also as a wish: *if only you had known with the knowing of certainty.* The answer to the wish was not spoken; the gap still belongs to the reader.

**Fifth: from ownership to trust.**

The reversal of the last verse touches the idea of ownership. The sentence "it is my property, I do what I like with it" does not lose its validity before the sūra — it loses its scope. The property is yours; but its being yours does not mean that it will not be asked about.

This measure has a practical consequence, and it comes out at a different place from a criticism of wealth: **the keeping of records.** The owner of a file is obliged to know the contents of the file. The sūra requires not the abandoning of wealth but the looking at what the wealth is: where it came from, where it goes, what it makes possible.

And that the subject of the questioning is **bounty** — that is, what is good, what has been given — is the real subtlety here. The sūra does not ask about what is bad; it asks about what is good. Health, time, security, opportunity. These are bounties that cannot be accumulated and cannot be counted; and precisely for that reason they never enter the list of the race. The sūra closes by saying that at the end of a life ending with things that are counted, the questioning will be about **the things that are not counted**.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

At several points through the text a definite language was deliberately not used; the reader needs to know them:

- Whether the sūra is Meccan or Medinan is disputed; no preference was stated.
- The versions of the reports transmitted as the occasion of revelation differ as to who the disputing parties were. For that reason no name of a tribe or a person was given; the common core of the reports was transmitted.
- The two readings of the expression *"you visited the graves"* were given in a table. A preference was stated, and it was recorded that it is not binding.
- In what sense the repetition in verses 3 and 4 (and the *thumma*) is to be taken is disputed; both readings were given, and neither was presented as certainly correct.
- The passive variant reading in verse 6 (*la-turawunna*) is transmitted, but since I cannot say with certainty which imām it belongs to, no name was written.
- That the making of the three degrees of certainty into a systematic teaching **belongs to later periods** was stated separately; the Qurʾān does not present this trio as a defined system.
- The example of fire, smoke and burning is an example widely repeated in this literature; it was not ascribed to any one person.
- The views about the scope of "bounty" in verse 8 were given in a table, but the views were not ascribed by name to their holders.
- The Prophet's words about the dates and the cool water are transmitted in the hadith collections; since I cannot say with certainty in which collection they stand, no name of a source was written. For the hadith about health and free time, al-Bukhārī was recorded.
- Of the concepts in the social-science section, Veblen's "conspicuous consumption" is an established concept; the "Easterlin paradox" and the extent of hedonic adaptation are disputed in the literature, and this was stated openly. None of these concepts was presented as knowledge foretold in advance by the verse.
- The link between the three sūras (102-103-104) is an observation about the arrangement of the muṣḥaf; it is not a claim about the order of revelation.
