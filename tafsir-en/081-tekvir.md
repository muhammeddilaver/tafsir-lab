# 81. Sūrat al-Takwīr

Twenty-nine verses. It is counted among the early sūras of the Meccan period, and there is no disagreement worth noting on the point. It takes its name from the verb in the first verse: **kuwwirat** — "was rolled up".

The sūra consists of two unequal parts, and the link between them is not visible at first sight. The first part (1–14) recounts the dismantling of the universe. The second part (15–29) recounts where the text you are holding came from. The link between them will be shown in the section on the sūra as a whole.

## What the sūra is doing

**The first part: twelve conditions, one answer.**

The sūra lines up twelve clauses each beginning *"When…"*. The sun is rolled up, the stars fall, the mountains are set moving, the pregnant camels are left untended, the wild animals are gathered, the seas are set ablaze, the souls are paired, the girl buried alive is questioned, the pages are spread open, the sky is stripped away, the Fire is set blazing, the Garden is brought near.

Twelve conditional clauses. And then a single answer: **"A soul will know what it has brought."**

That is: the whole universe is being dismantled, and the destination of all this dismantling is one person's coming to know something.

**The second part: four oaths, one report.**

An oath is sworn by the stars, by the night and by the morning; then a report follows: this speech is speech carried by a strong and trustworthy messenger. Then three objections are refused — he is not possessed, he does not withhold, this is not the speech of a satan. And the sūra closes with a question and with a qualification.

**The sūra has a single subject: the source of knowledge and the place knowledge arrives at.** The first part says where knowledge finally arrives (one person will know what he himself has brought). The second part says where that knowledge comes from now (a chain, and the qualities of every link in the chain). The sūra gives the **source** and the **destination** of the report in one and the same text.

## The names of the sūra

It is transmitted that in the sources it is called **al-Takwīr**, and, by its opening clause, **Idhā'sh-shamsu kuwwirat**. Naming short sūras by their first clause is a widespread practice.

## A report

It is reported that the Prophet said that whoever wishes to see the Resurrection as though with his own eyes should read this sūra and the sūras al-Infiṭār and al-Inshiqāq. The report is found in sources that include al-Tirmidhī, and it is ascribed to Ibn ʿUmar; its chain of transmission is disputed. I do not quote its wording verbatim.

I transmit the report not as material for exegesis but to show how these three sūras were perceived as a cluster in the early period. All three open with the same pattern (*idhā* + noun + verb), and all three give the Resurrection as a sequence of events.

## The basmala

The word-by-word analysis of the basmala was done in Sūrat al-Fātiḥa; I do not repeat it here. A note particular to this sūra: the first thirteen verses of a text that opens with the names al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm recount the dismantling of everything that exists. And in the middle of that dismantling, in the eighth verse, a child whose account no one ever called for is given a voice. The sūra's understanding of mercy stands there.

## The twelve conditions — the common structure (verses 1–13)

Before the verses are treated one by one, three structural features that belong to all twelve at once must be seen. If these are not seen, the individual verses will not be read correctly.

### One: not one of the twelve clauses has a subject

Set the verbs out in order:

| Verse | Verb | Form |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **كُوِّرَتْ** | Passive (Form II) |
| 2 | **ٱنكَدَرَتْ** | Form VII — happening of itself (*muṭāwaʿa*) |
| 3 | **سُيِّرَتْ** | Passive (Form II) |
| 4 | **عُطِّلَتْ** | Passive (Form II) |
| 5 | **حُشِرَتْ** | Passive |
| 6 | **سُجِّرَتْ** | Passive (Form II) |
| 7 | **زُوِّجَتْ** | Passive (Form II) |
| 8 | **سُئِلَتْ** | Passive |
| 10 | **نُشِرَتْ** | Passive |
| 11 | **كُشِطَتْ** | Passive |
| 12 | **سُعِّرَتْ** | Passive (Form II) |
| 13 | **أُزْلِفَتْ** | Passive (Form IV) |

**In eleven of them the verb is passive; in one (the stars) it is on the pattern of happening of itself.** That is: in none of the twelve clauses is the one who does the act named.

This is no accident. The last two words of the sūra are **رَبُّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ** — "the Lord of all the worlds". The subject is kept hidden across twenty-nine verses and disclosed **at the very end**.

So the sūra builds an architecture: everything that happens is the work of one, but the name of that one is held back to the end. For twelve verses the reader carries the question "who?", and receives the answer in the very last word. This will be gathered up again in the section on the sūra as a whole.

### Two: six of them are on the doubled pattern

*Kuwwirat, suyyirat, ʿuṭṭilat, sujjirat, zuwwijat, suʿʿirat* — six of them are the passive of **Form II**. In Arabic the doubling (*shadda*) of this form conveys **repetition, multiplicity and comprehensiveness**.

These verbs, then, do not say "it was done once"; they say **"it was done to all of them, from end to end, over and over"**. It is not one single sun that is rolled up; the operation of rolling up is carried through without remainder.

The undoubled ones — *ḥushirat, suʾilat, nushirat, kushiṭat* — are rather single, completed acts: gathered, questioned, spread open, stripped away.

### Three: the logic of the sequence

The twelve conditions divide into two groups of six, and the division is sharp:

| | **1–6: The dismantling of the visible world** | **7–13: The setting up of the reckoning** |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Sun, stars, mountains, camels, animals, seas | Souls, the buried girl, the pages, the sky, the Fire, the Garden |
| What happens | The existing order breaks apart | A new order is set up |
| Direction | **Dismantling** | **Building** |

**Within the first group there is an order of its own as well: from far to near.**

The sun (the farthest, the largest) → the stars → the mountains (the most fixed thing on earth) → **the camels** (a man's property) → the wild animals → the seas.

The scale comes down from the sky to the earth, and from there to the pen. And the fourth item of the series, in the middle of the whole cosmic list, is **a man's own herd**. This is another instance of the technique of the "drop in scale" treated in the sections on al-Māʿūn and ʿAbasa: the largest claim is tied to the nearest object.

Now one by one.

## 81/1 — إِذَا ٱلشَّمْسُ كُوِّرَتْ

*Idhā'sh-shamsu kuwwirat*
"When the sun is rolled up."

### كَوَّرَ — to roll up, to wind

**Root: ك-و-ر.** The concrete sense: **to wind something round upon itself, to wrap it, to roll it up.**

The commonest concrete use of the word is seen with the turban: **كَوْر العمامة** — one winding of the turban, each single turn wound about the head. *Kāra'l-ʿimāmata ʿalā raʾsihī* — he wound the turban about his head.

Other uses:
- **كَارَة (*kāra*)** — a bundle, a pack; a cloth in which things are wrapped up and gathered together.
- **كَوَّرَهُ فَٱنْكَارَ** — he rolled it up and flung it, and away it rolled.

Two movements thus stand together beneath the root: **to wind** and **to gather up and set aside.**

### The Qurʾān's own usage settles the matter

This root occurs in one other place in the Qurʾān, and there its sense is too clear to leave room for dispute:

> **يُكَوِّرُ ٱلَّيْلَ عَلَى ٱلنَّهَارِ وَيُكَوِّرُ ٱلنَّهَارَ عَلَى ٱلَّيْلِ**
> *"He winds the night upon the day, and He winds the day upon the night."* (al-Zumar 39/5)

The same verb, the same form. And what is described there is plain: **one thing being wound upon another.** The night is wound over the day like a turban; then the reverse.

This fixes the sense of al-Takwīr 81/1. The sun is **not exploded, not shattered, not put out** — it is **wound and rolled up.** Its light is gathered in upon itself, the way a turban is wound.

### A caution: the claim that "the sun is a sphere"

In some modern writings the claim is advanced, on the strength of the word *takwīr*, that "the Qurʾān is announcing that the sun is a sphere". This claim is linguistically baseless, and it needs to be stated plainly:

- The word **كُرَة** (sphere, ball) is from the root **ك-ر-و / ك-ر-ي**; it is not the same root as **ك-و-ر**. The order of the letters differs, and in Arabic the order of the letters determines the root.
- The verb of the verse conveys not a **shape** but an **operation**: being rolled up.
- The usage in al-Zumar 39/5 shows that the word is the name of a movement, not of a shape.

Per the method (`STYLE.md`), claims of this kind are not made in this commentary. What the verse says is strong enough as it stands; it has no need of anything added to it.

### The sūra's first image: not destruction but gathering up

**And here is the real point.**

The most expected way of recounting the Resurrection would be explosion, collision, breakage. The sūra does not choose it. The first verb it chooses is a verb of **putting in order**: the rolling up of a cloth, the winding of a turban, the tying of a bundle.

The Qurʾān uses this register elsewhere too:

> *"On that Day We shall roll up the sky as the pages of a book are rolled up."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/104)

There too there is the verb *ṭayy* (to roll up) and the comparison with a **register**. The universe is handled like a cloth or a written scroll: unrolled, used, and rolled up when its work is done.

**The idea this image carries is this:** what is rolled up is not annihilated; it is **put away.** A cloth that is rolled up does not cease to exist, it goes out of use. The first word of the sūra pictures the end of the universe not as a destruction but as a **closing.**

### And within the sūra stands the exact opposite of this

The tenth verse: **وَإِذَا ٱلصُّحُفُ نُشِرَتْ** — *"When the pages are spread open."*

*Nashr* is the exact opposite of *ṭayy* (rolling up): to **spread open** what has been rolled up.

**This is the central image of the sūra:**

| | What happens |
|---|---|
| The sun (1) | **Is rolled up** |
| The sky (11) | **Is stripped away** |
| The pages (10) | **Are spread open** |

**What is open closes, what is closed opens.** The universe is rolled up, the file is opened. These are the two directions of a single movement, and the whole architecture of the sūra is built upon it.

**And the link with ʿAbasa:** in ʿAbasa 80/22 the word used for the raising of the dead was **أَنشَرَهُ** — the same root. In the Qurʾān's lexicon, then, the raising of a dead man and the opening of the register are one and the same verb: **to spread open what has been rolled up.** The two sūras use this root so that each completes the other.

### الشَّمْس — the sun

Root **ش-م-س**. Why does the sūra begin with the sun?

Because the sun is the most unchanging thing a human being knows. The measure of time, the measure of direction, the source of life. A society's calendar, its sowing season, the times of prayer, the planning of a journey — all of them depend on it.

**The list of dismantlings begins with the one whose dismantling looks most impossible.** And it goes on: the stars (the means of finding one's direction at night), the mountains (the fixed points of the earth). In three verses every fixed point a human being has is gone.

## 81/2 — وَإِذَا ٱلنُّجُومُ ٱنكَدَرَتْ

*Wa idhā'n-nujūmu'nkadarat*
"When the stars fall."

### كَدَرَ — to grow turbid

**Root: ك-د-ر.** The concrete sense: **to grow turbid, to lose one's clearness.** *Kadir* — turbid; its opposite *ṣāfī* — clear. In Arabic it is used of water growing muddy: *kadira'l-māʾu*.

From this comes the metaphor: *kadar* — the clouding of one's peace, the darkening of the heart. English has no word descended from this root; Turkish took the word over directly, and there *keder* means grief.

**ٱنْكَدَرَ** — Form VII (*muṭāwaʿa*): "it grew turbid, it clouded over of itself". In Arabic this pattern conveys that something **happens of its own accord**.

### Two senses, and how they join

The classical lexicons record two senses for *inkadara*:

1. **It grew turbid, its light went, it darkened.**
2. **It poured down, scattered, fell.** *Inkadarati'n-nujūm* — the stars scattered. Also *inkadara'ṭ-ṭāʾir* — the bird swooped down, came flocking.

**How do the two stand together?** For a star, "to grow turbid" and "to fall" come to the same thing: whether it stays in place and goes out, or whether it falls, it has vanished from the sky. What the observer sees is the same result. The word names the result seen by the eye, not the physical mechanism.

**The Qurʾān's parallel:** *"When the stars are scattered"* (al-Infiṭār 82/2: *wa idhā'l-kawākibu'ntatharat*). There *intithār* (scattering) is used — which supports the sense of falling.

### Why does the pattern change in this verse?

Of the sūra's twelve verbs **this one alone is not passive.** The sun is rolled up (someone rolls it); the stars simply cloud over (of themselves).

This is a point about the word order and it is worth recording. As a possible reading — my own reading: **when the source of the light is taken away, the lights go out of themselves.** An operation is applied to the sun; the falling of the stars comes as the result of that operation. The difference of pattern lodges the relation of cause and effect inside the grammar itself.

I do not present this as a certain intention. The verb's coming on the Form VII pattern may also have to do with the harmony of the verse-endings.

## 81/3 — وَإِذَا ٱلْجِبَالُ سُيِّرَتْ

*Wa idhā'l-jibālu suyyirat*
"When the mountains are set moving."

**Root: س-ي-ر.** To walk, to move, to travel. *Sayr* — going, travel; *sīra* — the course a person takes, his way through life; *sayyāra* — a caravan, a company on the move. *Tasyīr* (Form II) — to set moving.

**The choice of the word.** The verse does not say "the mountains were shattered" or "collapsed". It says **"were set moving"**. What is done to the mountains, then, is not breaking but **being made to move**.

Why is this the more shattering? Because in the Qurʾān's lexicon the mountain is the emblem of **fixity**:

- *"And He set firm mountains (*rawāsī*) on the earth, lest it shake with you"* (al-Naḥl 16/15).
- *"And the mountains as pegs"* (al-Nabaʾ 78/7: *wa'l-jibāla awtādā*).

*Rawāsī* (anchored, fixed) and *awtād* (pegs) — both convey immobility. The mountain, then, is by definition the thing that does not move. To say of it that it "moved" is to cancel the word's own definition.

**The Qurʾān's other descriptions of the mountains** give the same event in different images, and some of them were treated in this commentary:

| Place | Image |
|---|---|
| al-Takwīr 81/3 | *Suyyirat* — set moving |
| al-Qāriʿa 101/5 | *Ka'l-ʿihni'l-manfūsh* — like carded wool (treated in the section on al-Qāriʿa) |
| al-Muzzammil 73/14 | *Kathīban mahīlā* — a heap of sand pouring away |
| al-Wāqiʿa 56/5 | *Bussat bassā* — crumbled to powder |
| al-Naml 27/88 | *Taḥsabuhā jāmidatan wa hiya tamurru marra's-saḥāb* — you suppose them motionless, yet they pass by as the clouds pass |

al-Naml 27/88 is especially striking, because it says that **the mountain's looking fixed is a mistake of ours.** But to read this verse as "the Qurʾān is announcing the movements of the earth's crust" is a forcing of the kind the method (`STYLE.md`) forbids: the context of the verse is the Day of Resurrection, and the description belongs to that Day. I record it as a possible reading, not as a claim.

## 81/4 — وَإِذَا ٱلْعِشَارُ عُطِّلَتْ

*Wa idhā'l-ʿishāru ʿuṭṭilat*
"When the pregnant camels are left untended."

The most concrete, the most local and the most telling verse of the sūra.

### ٱلْعِشَار — the she-camel close to giving birth

**The word.** *ʿIshār* is the plural of **عُشَرَاء** (*ʿusharāʾ*). *ʿUsharāʾ*: **a she-camel that has entered the tenth month of her pregnancy, close to giving birth.**

Its name comes straight from the numeral **عَشَرَة** (ten): the one that has reached the tenth month.

### Why the most valuable property?

To understand this one has to know that economy.

In a desert society the camel was an economy in itself: transport, the carrying of loads, milk, meat, hide, wool, and the unit in which wealth was measured. A man's riches were stated in the number of his camels.

**And the pregnant camel was the most valuable part of the herd.** The reasons pile one upon another:

1. **Two living things.** For the price of one camel you have acquired two.
2. **Milk.** A camel gives milk after she has given birth. A pregnant camel means milk in the near future — and milk, in that country, is a staple food.
3. **An investment spread over years.** A camel's pregnancy lasts about a year. A camel that has reached the tenth month means the last two months of a year's waiting.
4. **Fragility.** A camel near her time needs tending; if she is left untended, both she and her young are lost.

The *ʿusharāʾ*, then, was in that society **the thing a man got up in the night to look at.** The most watched over, the most talked about, the most calculated over part of the herd.

### عُطِّلَتْ — left idle

**Root: ع-ط-ل.** Its sense: **to leave empty, to leave without work, to take off what was worn.**

The derivatives show the web of the root:
- **عَاطِل (*ʿāṭil*)** — out of work, standing idle. English has no descendant of this root; Turkish borrowed the word, and *atıl* lives on there in the sense of "idle, inert".
- **تَعَطَّلَتِ ٱلْمَرْأَة** — the woman took off her ornaments, was left unadorned.
- **عُطْلَة** — a holiday, the stopping of work.
- **بِئْرٌ مُعَطَّلَة** — an abandoned well; it occurs in the Qurʾān (al-Ḥajj 22/45).

**And this is exactly what the verse says: the camel has not died.**

The verse does not say "the camels perished". It says **"they were left untended"**. The camel is there, still pregnant, still valuable — but **there is no one who attends to her.**

### The verse's real claim: the collapse of a system of value

This is the finest observation in the sūra.

The easy way of recounting the end of a world is to say that the valuable things were destroyed. The verse does not do this. **It says that the valuable thing is standing where it was, and has lost its value.**

And this is a far more exact account. For the value of a thing lies not in the thing but in **the attention given to it.** If no one is looking, the most valuable property is no different from a lump of rock. Value is not a property of the object; it is a relation between the object and a human being.

**The verse cuts that relation.** And the moment it is cut, all the calculations a man has given his whole life to are void at once.

**Why this stands here, in the middle of the cosmic list, is plain as well.** The sun, the stars, the mountains — these are nobody's property. When they are lost no one suffers a personal loss. The fourth verse puts **the reader's own property** into the list. In a universal list of twelve items, this one item alone **comes out of somebody's pocket.**

This is also why the word is so narrow and so technical. It could have said "possessions"; it does not. Exactly what will rise in the hearer's mind has been reckoned.

**What this says to the present.** The word's counterpart today can be asked outright: *what is the thing you would go back into a fire for?* Whatever the answer, the verse is speaking about it. And what the verse says is not that the thing will be destroyed; it is that **you will not be in a state to give it a thought.**

In its thirty-seventh verse ʿAbasa said the same thing about people: *"every man will have concern enough of his own."* al-Takwīr says the same thing about property.

## 81/5 — وَإِذَا ٱلْوُحُوشُ حُشِرَتْ

*Wa idhā'l-wuḥūshu ḥushirat*
"When the wild animals are gathered."

### وَحْش — the wild

**Root: و-ح-ش.** *Waḥsh* — an untamed animal, a wild one. Its plural is *wuḥūsh*.

**And from the same root comes وَحْشَة (*waḥsha*): loneliness, desolation, solitude.** In Arabic *istawḥasha* — he felt lonely, he took fright.

The connection is plain: the wild animal is the one that flees from man, the one found in empty places. **Beneath the root lies the idea of "keeping away from man".** English has no descendant of this root; the Turkish *vahşi* ("wild, savage") and *vahşet* ("savagery") are borrowed straight from it.

### حَشْر — to gather

**Root: ح-ش-ر.** To drive together into one place and collect; to bring together what is scattered. *Ḥashr* — the gathering at the Resurrection; *maḥshar* — the place of gathering.

In the concrete sense of the word there is a **compulsion**: not coming of oneself, but being driven and brought.

### A violation of category

**Here is what makes the verse shattering:** the wild animal is by definition the animal that does not gather. It flees from its own kind and from man; it forms no herd, accepts no stall, comes to no call.

And read together with the fourth verse, a crossing appears:

| | The camel (verse 4) | The wild animal (verse 5) |
|---|---|---|
| Whose | A man's property, domestic | Nobody's, wild |
| What happens | **It is left** | **It is gathered** |

**The animal a man owns is abandoned; the animal he does not own is gathered.** The two verses do the reverse of one another.

I record this as my own reading. But the two verses standing side by side, and the verbs being exact opposites, gives one to think that the arrangement is deliberate.

### Are the animals too called to account?

This verse opens a question in the classical commentaries, and the question is a real one.

**The Qurʾān's own datum is this:**

> *"There is no creature that walks on the earth, and no bird that flies with its two wings, but they are communities like you. We have left nothing out of the Book. Then to their Lord they will be gathered (**يُحْشَرُونَ**)."* (al-Anʿām 6/38)

**The same verb.** And the verse says plainly that the animals too will be gathered.

**The views:**

| View | What it says | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **They are gathered, the claims between them are settled, and then they become dust** | There is a reckoning, but no requital (Garden or Fire) | In the hadith collections reports are transmitted to the effect that on the Day of Resurrection the hornless sheep will take its due from the horned one; it is stated that this is found in Muslim. I do not quote the wording verbatim |
| **They are merely gathered; it is a part of the terror** | The verse speaks of no reckoning; it speaks of a scene | Context: the twelve items are drawing a tableau |
| **The gathering is a general return, as in al-Anʿām 6/38** | Not entering into detail | Cautious |

No preference is adopted in this commentary; all three have been defended, and the Qurʾān does not open the matter up.

**But there is something in the context, and it must be recorded.**

The fifth verse speaks of the gathering of the animals. **In the eighth verse a murdered child is questioned.** The two verses in between are the seas set boiling and the souls paired.

The sūra, then, sets up a sequence that widens the scope of the reckoning: **if even the animals are gathered, then a child whose account no one ever called for will certainly be asked after.**

The sūra does not say this; I am the one constructing it, and I record it as my own reading. But the order of the verses allows the reading.

## 81/6 — وَإِذَا ٱلْبِحَارُ سُجِّرَتْ

*Wa idhā'l-biḥāru sujjirat*
"When the seas are set ablaze."

### سَجَرَ — two senses

**Root: س-ج-ر.** The lexicons record two senses:

1. **To fill an oven and light it, to set it ablaze.** *Sajara't-tannūra* — he lit the oven. **مَسْجُور** — kindled, made red-hot. *Sujūr* — the fuel thrown into an oven.
2. **To fill, to make overflow.** *Sajara'l-ināʾa* — he filled the vessel.

**How do the two stand together?** They join in the image of the oven: a *tannūr* is first **filled** (with fuel), then **lit**. The idea beneath the root may be the filling of a vessel to its capacity with something — whether fuel or water.

### The other usage in the Qurʾān

> *"And by the filled sea (**وَٱلْبَحْرِ ٱلْمَسْجُورِ**)"* (al-Ṭūr 52/6)

There too there is the same word and the same dispute: is *masjūr* "filled" or "set ablaze"? In both places the word comes together with the sea, and in both places both senses are possible.

### The views

| View | How it is understood | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **They were set ablaze, they became fire** | The seas catch fire | The commonest sense of the root; the usage "to light the oven" supports it |
| **They filled, overflowed, mingled together** | The barriers between the seas are lifted and the waters join | *al-Infiṭār 82/3*: *"When the seas are made to burst forth"* (*fujjirat*) supports this direction |
| **Their water went, they dried up** | It is rested on a usage of the word connected with emptying | Weak; this is not the principal sense of the root |

**No preference is adopted.** The word carries both senses and the Qurʾān does not explain. This much, however, can be recorded: the first and the second view do not exclude one another — what fills overflows, what overflows mingles, and in the image of the oven the two are together already.

**And a caution:** on the strength of this verse claims are advanced that rest on the composition of sea water, on the flammability of hydrogen and oxygen, and the like. These are things the verse does not say, and per the method (`STYLE.md`) they are not made in this commentary. The image of the verse is an oven; an oven the hearer knows.

**Its place in the context.** The seas are the last item of the first group. And it is a striking close: the sūra sets the dry thing moving (the mountains) and sets the wet thing ablaze (the seas). Everything turns into the contrary of its own nature. **The first group closes with the suspension of the laws of nature.**

## 81/7 — وَإِذَا ٱلنُّفُوسُ زُوِّجَتْ

*Wa idhā'n-nufūsu zuwwijat*
"When the souls are paired."

The second half of the sūra — the setting up of the reckoning — begins here. The subject is no longer objects but persons.

### زَوَّجَ — to pair

**Root: ز-و-ج.** *Zawj* — a pair, a mate; the like of a thing, or what completes it. *Tazwīj* — to make into a pair, to pair up.

**An important note about the language:** the Arabic *zawj* does not, as the English "spouse" does, convey marriage alone. **It is used of everything that comes in twos.** The Qurʾān: *"of every kind two, a pair (*zawjayni'thnayn*)"* (Hūd 11/40); *"noble pairs of plants"* (Luqmān 31/10: *zawjin karīm*).

The verb, then, need not mean "were married"; it means **"were brought together with their like"**.

### The views

| View | What it says | Ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Like was brought together with like** | People are grouped according to their deeds and their states | *al-Ṣāffāt 37/22*: *"Gather those who did wrong and **their kinds** (*azwājahum*)"* — there *azwāj* has the sense of "their likes". *al-Wāqiʿa 56/7*: *"You will be three classes (**azwājan thalātha**)"* — three groups, three "pairs" | **The strongest.** The parallel within the Qurʾān is direct, and in both places the same word carries this sense |
| **The spirit was paired with the body** | The resurrection is being described | Reasonable as a description of the resurrection | Possible; but the Qurʾān does not use this expression in this sense anywhere else |
| **Each was paired with his deed** | A person is joined to what he has done | In accord with the fourteenth verse (*mā aḥḍarat*) | Possible |
| **The believer was paired with a houri, the denier with a satan** | The distribution of requitals | It is transmitted in some commentaries | Weak: at this point of the sūra the requitals are not yet being distributed; the Garden and the Fire come in verses 12–13 |

**Preference:** the first view looks the stronger. Its ground is that in al-Ṣāffāt 37/22 and al-Wāqiʿa 56/7 the same word is used in the sense of **"like / class"**, and that the context is a context of **sorting**. It is not binding.

### What the sorting means

On this reading the verse says: **what stands mixed in this world is sorted out on that Day.**

And mixture is a basic feature of life in this world. A house, a market, a quarter, a family — all of them hold together people who are not in the least alike. People come together by place, by kinship, by work; **not by their likeness.**

The verse describes a moment at which the measure of grouping changes: the reason for being together is no longer nearness but **likeness.**

**And note the verse that follows.** The seventh verse says "like joins with like"; the eighth verse separates the murdered girl from her father. The sorting, then, comes down **as far as the inside of the family.** The list of flight in ʿAbasa 80/34-36 recounted the same dissolution from the other side.

## 81/8-9 — وَإِذَا ٱلْمَوْءُۥدَةُ سُئِلَتْ ۝ بِأَىِّ ذَنۢبٍ قُتِلَتْ

*Wa idhā'l-mawʾūdatu suʾilat ۝ Bi-ayyi dhanbin qutilat*
"When the girl buried alive is questioned: for what sin were you killed?"

The centre of the sūra is here.

### وَأْد — to bring a weight down upon

**Root: و-أ-د.** And the concrete sense of the root shows the scene the word carries: **weight, bearing down, a load coming down on top of something.**

- **الوَئِيد** — a heavy, rumbling sound; the noise a thing makes by its own weight.
- **التُّؤَدَة (*tuʾada*)** — acting deliberately, not hurrying, circumspection. **From the same root.** *ʿAla't-tuʾada* — slowly, in a measured way.
- **وَأَدَ الجَارِيَة** — he buried the girl alive; that is, **he brought the weight of the earth down upon her.**

**The word, then, names the killing as a verb of "weight".** Not strangling, not striking, not cutting — **pressing down upon.**

**المَوْءُودَة** is a passive participle: **the one upon whom a weight has been brought down.** The word, in the passive pattern, speaks from the position of the victim.

And its being of the same root as *tuʾada* (acting with circumspection) is an unnerving coincidence: the root's idea of weight opens in one place onto a virtue (gravity of bearing), and in another onto this act.

### The Qurʾān's other word for this practice

This practice was treated in the section on al-Shams 91/10, and this verse was pointed to there. I complete the connection here.

In Sūrat al-Shams the verb **دَسَّى** (*dassā*) was used of the man who buries his own soul; the root is **د-س-س**, "to slip something secretly inside a thing, to hide it". And the only other use of that root in the Qurʾān was this:

> *"When one of them is given the news of a girl, his face turns dark and he is filled with rage. He hides himself from the people because of the bad news he has been given: is he to keep her in humiliation, or **bury her in the earth** (*yadussuhū fi't-turāb*)? Look — how evil is the judgement they make!"* (al-Naḥl 16/58-59)

**Now set the two words side by side:**

| | **al-Naḥl 16/59** | **al-Takwīr 81/8** |
|---|---|---|
| The word | **دَسَّ** (*dassa*) | **وَأَدَ** (*waʾada*) |
| Its concrete sense | To slip in secretly, to hide | To bring a weight down upon |
| From whose side it is seen | **The father's**: what he does and why — he hides out of shame | **The child's**: what has come down upon her |
| Form | A verb, the father its subject | A passive participle, the victim's name |

**The same event, recounted from two different places.**

al-Naḥl's verb recounts the father's **motive**: to hide. The verse already says that the father hides himself from the people — *yatawārā mina'l-qawmi min sūʾi mā bushshira bih*. Verb and motive are the same: concealment. The father is burying his shame.

al-Takwīr's word says nothing at all about the father. **It looks at the body of the victim:** the one upon whom a weight has come down.

This distinction shows what each of the two sūras is doing. al-Naḥl makes a **diagnosis**: this thing is done out of shame. al-Takwīr opens an **account**: and this has an owner.

### The historical frame: what we know and what we do not

Care is needed here, because the subject lends itself to exaggeration in either direction.

**The Qurʾān's own data.** The Qurʾān comes at this subject from several places, and names **two distinct motives**:

| Verse | What it says | Motive |
|---|---|---|
| *al-Naḥl 16/58-59* | The father who is given the news of a girl darkens in the face, hides himself, considers burying her | **Shame, disgrace** |
| *al-Isrāʾ 17/31* | *"Do not kill your children for fear of poverty"* | **Fear of poverty** (*imlāq*) |
| *al-Anʿām 6/151* | *"Do not kill your children out of poverty"* | **Poverty** |
| *al-Anʿām 6/137* | *"Their associates made the killing of their children seem fair to many of the mushrikūn"* | Religious and social sanction |
| *al-Anʿām 6/140* | *"Lost indeed are those who kill their children in folly, without knowledge"* | General condemnation |

**The point to note:** al-Isrāʾ 17/31 and al-Anʿām 6/151 say *"your children"* — they do not specify sex. The Qurʾān thus appears to be separating two distinct things: the killing of **a child** out of poverty, and the killing of **a girl** out of shame. The *mawʾūda* of al-Takwīr is feminine, and names the second.

**On how widespread it was.** Two exaggerations must be avoided here:

- **The exaggeration that "everyone did it".** This is not supported in the sources, and it is demographically incoherent as well. A society that killed its girls on a wide scale would stop reproducing within a generation or two. The reports name particular tribes (Tamīm, for instance) and particular individuals; this gives one to think that the practice was **not widespread, but known and able to be accepted.**
- **The exaggeration that "it never happened".** This is not defensible either. The Qurʾān is addressing the very society in which the thing took place, and it refers to a practice its hearers knew. A text does not invent, to its own disadvantage, a practice about which its hearers would say "there is no such thing". The hearers brought a great many objections against these verses; that no objection of the form "we do not do such a thing" was recorded is meaningful.

**The wider frame.** Infanticide and exposure are not a practice peculiar to pre-Islamic Arabia. In the Roman world the exposure of the newborn (*expositio*) was for a long time a practice tolerated in law; comparable instances are documented in other societies as well. This information is recorded not in order to make the pre-Islamic practice lighter but **in order to take the subject out of being the characteristic of one people and make it a human matter** — and indeed, per the method (`STYLE.md`), no wholesale ruling is issued about any community in this commentary.

**And today.** Sex-selective practices have produced measurable imbalances of population in some countries where the means of determination before birth became widespread, and legal measures have been taken on the matter. I write this not as a "scientific miracle of the verse" but as a record that the motive the verse describes — a girl being counted a burden and a disgrace — has not been buried in the past.

### And the real point: why is the victim questioned and not the doer?

The sūra's most important choice is here, and it needs to be dwelt on.

The verse could have run: *"And when the one who buried her is questioned: by what right did you kill this girl?"* That is the expected sentence. Whoever calls to account, questions the doer.

It is not framed. **The question is put to the victim**, and its content too is about her: *"for what sin were you killed?"*

What this carries must be unfolded in order.

**One. The question is the heaviest question that can be put to the killer — only its addressee has changed.**

This point is treated in the classical commentaries, and it is correct: to question the victim in his presence is the harshest form of questioning the doer. For the doer cannot answer. And the answer the victim will give is testimony against him, and cannot be refused.

There is a counterpart in a court of law: giving the floor to the victim's kin in front of the accused is a heavier procedure than putting a question to the accused directly. The one who is questioned answers; **the one who is not questioned has not been questioned because he has no answer.**

**Two. The question has no answer, and the question itself announces this.**

*"For what sin?"* — this question contains a presupposition: **to kill, a sin is required.** The moment the question is asked, that presupposition is established. And the one thing the girl can do — be silent — shows that the presupposition has nothing to answer to it.

The verse, then, frames no sentence of judgement ("she was innocent"); **it lodges the judgement in the structure of the question.** A question that has no answer is more decisive than a sentence that says there is no answer.

**ذَنْب — root ذ-ن-ب.** The concrete sense of the word is **tail**: *dhanab* — a tail; *dhunāba* — the hinder part of a thing, its tip. Some of the lexicographers make a connection between *dhanb* (sin) and *dhanab* (tail): **a sin is the consequence it drags behind it.** This derivation is not undisputed; I transmit it with a note of caution.

If the connection holds, the irony of the question is doubled: the girl had nothing at all that she "dragged behind her" — because she had not yet had a life.

**Three. And the most important: the girl is given a legal personality.**

To see this one has to know her position in that society. The murdered girl had:

- no name,
- no inheritance,
- no one who held the claim to her blood (the mechanism of the blood-feud protects only a person who has someone behind him to call for an account — the same mechanism treated for the orphan in the section on al-Māʿūn),
- and the one who killed her was already her guardian. That is: the killer and the holder of the claim were one and the same person. In this case the system, by its own definition, cannot work.

**And the sūra gives her the first word in the greatest scene of the Resurrection.**

Look at the list of twelve items: the sun, the stars, the mountains, the camels, the animals, the seas, the souls — all of them named collectively. **The eighth item is the only item that names a single person.** Beside the sun and the mountains, a girl.

**This is a declaration of standing.** The sūra writes a death of which no one kept a record into the same list as the dismantling of the universe.

**Four. Here too the doer is not named — and here that means something different.**

None of the sūra's twelve verbs had a subject. Nor has this verse one: *qutilat* — "was killed". Who killed her is not said.

But the silence here does a different work from the others. In the others the subject was God, held back to the end. Here the subject is **among the listeners.** His name is not given because it does not need to be: the one who did it knows who is being spoken of.

This is another instance of the principle *al-ʿibratu bi-ʿumūmi'l-lafẓ* treated in the section on al-Māʿūn. The verse gives no name; it gives a **position.**

### The variant reading

Two readings are transmitted, and they genuinely change the sense:

| Reading | How it is read | Its sense |
|---|---|---|
| **سُئِلَتْ ... قُتِلَتْ** | Both passive, third person | "She was asked: for what sin were **you killed**?" The question is put to her |
| **سَأَلَتْ ... قُتِلْتُ** | The first active, the second first person | "She asked: for what sin was **I killed**?" **She** is the one asking |

I do not give the name of an imam. The common reading is the first.

**Note what the second reading does:** the girl is no longer the one questioned but the one who **asks**. Not a silent witness but a plaintiff demanding an account.

The two readings arrive at the same place, but with different force: in the first she **is given** the floor, in the second she **takes** it. In the classical tradition it is counted not a defect but a richness that two sound readings should both yield a correct sense; that rule works here exactly.

### What this says to the present

**The Qurʾān handles this matter not with a ruling but with a scene.**

Laying down a prohibition is easy, and the Qurʾān does lay one down elsewhere (al-Isrāʾ 17/31; al-Anʿām 6/151). But al-Takwīr does something else: **it makes the victim speak.**

Why this matters has to do with the way moral change works. Prohibiting a practice mostly remains ineffective if the victim of that practice **is not counted as a person.** Until who counts changes, what may be done does not change either.

The verse intervenes at exactly this point: **it gives the floor, in the most crowded assembly in the universe, to someone with no legal personality.**

And what this says to the present is not confined to the historical practice itself. In every society there are losses that go unseen because there is no one to call for an account of them. The verse's measure is this: **the recording of a wrong does not depend on the victim's being able to claim it.**

The exact counterpart of this was recorded in the section on ʿAbasa: the blind man had not seen the face darken — and that movement was recorded all the same. The two sūras state the same principle on two scales: **what goes unnoticed is entered in the record too.**

## 81/10 — وَإِذَا ٱلصُّحُفُ نُشِرَتْ

*Wa idhā'ṣ-ṣuḥufu nushirat*
"When the pages are spread open."

### صُحُف — pages

This word was treated in the section on al-Bayyina 98/2, and this verse was pointed to there; I do not repeat it here. In summary: the root **ص-ح-ف**, a flat surface written upon; *muṣḥaf* is from the same root — that whose pages have been gathered together.

**The difference here: the definite article is present.** In al-Bayyina *ṣuḥufan* (indefinite), in ʿAbasa *fī ṣuḥufin* (indefinite); here **ٱلصُّحُف** — definite. That is: the pages the hearer knows, that have been spoken of, that are expected: **the registers of deeds.**

The Qurʾān mentions this record in many places; the plainest is this:

> *"We have fastened every man's deed about his neck. On the Day of Resurrection We shall bring out for him a book that he will find **spread open** (*manshūrā*): 'Read your book! Today your own soul suffices as a reckoner against you.'"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/13-14)

The same root (*manshūrā*), the same scene. And that verse is also the unfolding of the sentence in the fourteenth verse, *"a soul will know what it has brought"*: the reckoner is the person himself.

### نَشَرَ — to open out, to spread

**Root: ن-ش-ر.** The concrete sense: **to open out what has been rolled up, to lay it out, to spread it.** *Nashara'th-thawba* — he spread out the cloth. Its opposite is **طَوَى** (*ṭayy*) — to roll up.

Two important derivatives from this root:
- **نُشُور** — the resurrection; the "opening out" of the dead. *"To Him is the return"* (al-Mulk 67/15: *ilayhi'n-nushūr*).
- **أَنْشَرَ** — to raise to life. **ʿAbasa 80/22**: *"Then, when He wills, He raises him up."*

### The sūra's central opposition

Now go back to the first verse.

| Verse | What | Verb | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The sun | *kuwwirat* — **rolled up** | Open → closed |
| 11 | The sky | *kushiṭat* — **stripped away** | Closed → open |
| 10 | The pages | *nushirat* — **spread open** | Closed → open |

**The whole movement of the sūra is in these three verbs:** the universe closes, the record opens.

And this is not merely a play of images; it is the sūra's argument. For in the life of this world the case is the reverse: **the universe is open, the record is closed.** A man sees the sky, sees the sun, sees the mountains; but he does not see where what he does is written down. The sūra describes the moment at which these two change places.

To put it another way: **the curtain between what is seen and what is recorded is lifted.**

### A detail in the sequence

In the eighth verse the girl is questioned. **In the tenth verse the registers are opened.**

The girl's question, then, comes **before** the files are opened.

I record this as my own reading, but the sequence itself stands in the text: her case does not wait for the file to be opened. It does not need to be in the record — because there was no system that entered her into the record in the first place.

## 81/11 — وَإِذَا ٱلسَّمَآءُ كُشِطَتْ

*Wa idhā's-samāʾu kushiṭat*
"When the sky is stripped away."

### كَشَطَ — to flay a hide

**Root: ك-ش-ط.** And its concrete sense shows how local the images the Qurʾān chooses can be:

**كَشَطَ ٱلْجِلْدَ عَنِ ٱلْجَزُور** — to flay the hide from a slaughtered camel, to strip it off.

The general sense of the word: **to pull away the covering on top, to strip it off, to lift it.**

**The sky, then, is stripped away like the hide of an animal.** The image is taken from the language of the herdsman and the butcher; an operation the hearer saw every day.

The harshness of this must not be missed. An animal whose hide is flayed is no longer alive; flaying is the separating off of what will not be used again.

### The sky as a covering

**The verb of the verse says something about what the sky is: the sky is a covering.**

The Qurʾān says this plainly:

> *"And We made the sky a guarded roof; yet they turn away from its signs."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/32)

*Saqfan maḥfūẓā* — a guarded roof. The sky, then, is the roof above us.

**And all the Resurrection verbs the Qurʾān uses of the sky recount the lifting of a covering:**

| Place | Verb | What |
|---|---|---|
| al-Takwīr 81/11 | *kushiṭat* | Stripped away, its hide flayed |
| al-Anbiyāʾ 21/104 | *naṭwī* | We roll up — like a register |
| al-Infiṭār 82/1 | *infaṭarat* | Cracked, split |
| al-Inshiqāq 84/1 | *inshaqqat* | Split |
| al-Raḥmān 55/37 | *inshaqqat… wardatan ka'd-dihān* | Split, and became red like oil |

**Five different verbs, one idea: the lid on top comes off.**

### And the real point: the curtain that is lifted

**The Qurʾān explains this idea exactly in one place**, and that verse changes how this sūra is read:

> **لَّقَدْ كُنتَ فِى غَفْلَةٍ مِّنْ هَٰذَا فَكَشَفْنَا عَنكَ غِطَآءَكَ فَبَصَرُكَ ٱلْيَوْمَ حَدِيدٌ**
> *"You were heedless of this; now We have lifted your veil from you — so your sight today is sharp."* (Qāf 50/22)

*Kashafnā ʿanka ghiṭāʾaka* — "We have lifted your covering from you". And the result: **the sight grows sharp.**

So all the covering-lifting verbs used in the recounting of the Resurrection look to a single result: **what could not be seen becomes visible.**

And this explains the sūra as a whole:

- The sun is rolled up — the source of the light is taken away.
- The sky is stripped away — the lid on top is removed.
- The pages are spread open — the record becomes visible.
- And the fourteenth verse: **"a soul will know."**

**The single product of thirteen verses of dismantling is knowledge.** The sūra is not recounting a destruction; it is recounting a **coming to light.**

This looks to the same place as Sūrat ʿAbasa's turning about the root **س-ف-ر** (to bring to light). The two sūras think the Resurrection under the same category: the lifting of the covering.

## 81/12-13 — وَإِذَا ٱلْجَحِيمُ سُعِّرَتْ ۝ وَإِذَا ٱلْجَنَّةُ أُزْلِفَتْ

*Wa idhā'l-jaḥīmu suʿʿirat ۝ Wa idhā'l-jannatu uzlifat*
"When the Fire is set blazing, and when the Garden is brought near."

### ٱلْجَحِيم — *jaḥīm*

**Root: ج-ح-م.** *Jaḥmatu'n-nār* — fire at its fiercest heat, the violence of the flame. *Jāḥim* — fire grown hot, fire that dazzles the eye.

The Qurʾān uses several names for hell, and each name brings one aspect forward: *jahannam*, *nār* (fire), *saʿīr* (blaze), *saqar*, *ḥuṭama* (treated in the section on al-Humaza), *laẓā*, *hāwiya* (treated in the section on al-Qāriʿa), *jaḥīm*. **Jaḥīm** is the name that brings **the heat** forward.

### سَعَّرَ — to set blazing

**Root: س-ع-ر.** *Saʿara'n-nāra* — he kindled the fire, set it blazing. **سَعِير** — a blazing fire; one of the names of hell.

The root has an interesting relative: **سِعْر** — price. In Arabic the rising of prices is expressed with the same word as the flaring up of a fire (*ishtaʿalati'l-asʿār*). The lexicographers tie the connection to the idea of "rising, growing hot". I record this as an observation about a family of words; I attach no ruling about the verse to it.

**The pattern: the passive of Form II**, with the doubled consonant. Repetition and violence: it was set blazing and set blazing again.

### أُزْلِفَتْ — brought near

**Root: ز-ل-ف.** To draw near, to be near. *Zulfa* — nearness, rank. *Zulfā* — nearness: *"And indeed he has a nearness with Us"* (Ṣād 38/25).

And from this root **مُزْدَلِفَة** — the place-name among the rites of the pilgrimage; the place of drawing near.

**The pattern: the passive of Form IV** — "was brought near". The Garden, that is, does not draw near of itself; it **is brought near.**

### And the asymmetry here

The two verses stand side by side, but the work they do is not symmetrical:

| | **The Fire** | **The Garden** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *suʿʿirat* — set blazing | *uzlifat* — brought near |
| Where the operation falls | **On itself** — it is being made ready | **On its position** — it is being moved |
| Who moves | Man goes to it | It comes to man |

**The Fire is prepared; the Garden is brought.**

The Qurʾān keeps this difference elsewhere too: *"And the Garden is brought near to the godfearing"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/90; Qāf 50/31 — *wa uzlifati'l-jannatu li'l-muttaqīn*). The same verb in both places, and in both places **it is said to whom it is brought near.**

**In al-Takwīr, by contrast, it is not said to whom it is brought near.** Neither for whom the Fire is set blazing, nor to whom the Garden is brought near. The rule the sūra has applied from the beginning holds here as well: **in no clause is either the subject or the addressee named.**

This gap will be filled in the next verse: *"a soul will know"*.

### ٱلْجَنَّة and concealment

**Root: ج-ن-ن.** This root was treated in the section on al-Baqara 2/25; I do not repeat it here. In summary: the sense of the root is **to cover, to conceal**, and all its derivatives turn about invisibility — *janīn* (covered in the womb), *jinn* (the unseen), *majnūn* (whose mind is covered over), *mijann* (a shield), *janna* (the garden whose trees cover the ground).

**And the fine point here is this:** the whole movement of the sūra was the lifting of coverings — the sky was stripped away, the pages were spread open, the light of the sun was rolled up. And the last item of this series of openings is the thing whose very name amounts to **"the hidden one"**: *al-janna*.

The lifting of the coverings, then, is completed by the most hidden thing of all **becoming visible.** It was recorded in the section on al-Baqara: *"What is promised is, by definition, what cannot be seen now."* The thirteenth verse is the moment at which that unseeable thing becomes visible.

I record this as my own reading; it is not visible without the analysis of the root made in al-Baqara.

## 81/14 — عَلِمَتْ نَفْسٌ مَّآ أَحْضَرَتْ

*ʿAlimat nafsun mā aḥḍarat*
"A soul will know what it has brought."

The single answer to thirteen verses of condition. The axis of the sūra.

### Scale: one sentence against thirteen verses

The proportion has to be seen first.

The sun was rolled up, the stars fell, the mountains moved, the property was left behind, the animals were gathered, the seas caught fire, the souls were sorted, a girl was questioned, the registers were opened, the sky was stripped away, the Fire blazed up, the Garden drew near.

**All of this was the parts of one condition. The answer is three words: a soul knows.**

The dismantling of the universe is set up so that one person may come to know something. The difference of scale is deliberate, and it carries the sūra's whole claim: **the destination of an event this large is the knowledge of individual human beings.**

This is the same movement as the architecture of Sūrat al-Māʿūn — from a cosmic claim to a cooking pot — and the same movement as ʿAbasa's table of foods. A technique the Qurʾān repeats: **the large is measured in the small.**

### نَفْسٌ — why singular and indefinite?

**This is the most discussed point in the verse.**

Look at the subjects of the twelve conditional clauses: *ash-shams* (definite), *an-nujūm* (plural, definite), *al-jibāl*, *al-ʿishār*, *al-wuḥūsh*, *al-biḥār*, **an-nufūs** (plural!), *al-mawʾūda*, *aṣ-ṣuḥuf*, *as-samāʾ*, *al-jaḥīm*, *al-janna*.

All of them are either definite or plural or both. In the seventh verse it was **souls** (plural) that were paired.

**And the subject of the answering clause: نَفْسٌ — singular, indefinite.**

| | The conditional clauses | The answering clause |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Plural and definite | **Singular and indefinite** |
| Scope | Collective, mass | **One by one** |
| What happens | The world is dismantled | One person knows |

**The views:**

| View | What it says | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Generality** (*ʿumūm*) — "every soul" | The indefinite here is inclusive by force of the context | Common; correct as to sense |
| **Individuation** (*tafrīd*) — one by one | The plurality dissolves, and each is left alone | More in accord with the word order |
| **Magnification** (*tahwīl*) | "Such a soul as this…" | Possible |

**The second reading accords best with the structure of the sūra.** For in the seventh verse the souls were **plural** and **being grouped**; in the fourteenth verse they are **singular** and **alone**. The sūra first classifies, then dissolves the class as well.

And this is the same as what ʿAbasa 80/37 says: *"every man will have concern enough of his own."* The two sūras frame the same solitude in two different sentences.

### عَلِمَتْ — a perfect verb

The verb is in the **perfect**: "knew". Yet what is recounted lies in the future.

In Arabic, recounting a future event with the perfect conveys **certainty**: it is so certain to happen that it is spoken of as having happened. The Qurʾān does this often in its descriptions of the Resurrection.

**And all the conditional clauses are in the perfect as well.** *Kuwwirat, inkadarat, suyyirat…* — all of them on the pattern of the past. So the sūra's fourteen verses are recounted entirely in the past tense, while the *idhā* at their head carries them into the future.

This tension — recounting the future in the past tense — sets the tone of the sūra: what is recounted is not being argued, it is being **recalled.**

### مَآ أَحْضَرَتْ — "what it has brought"

**The most carefully chosen word in the verse.**

The verse does not say *"mā ʿamilat"* (what it did). Nor does it say *"mā qaddamat"* (what it sent ahead) — an expression the Qurʾān does use. It says **"mā aḥḍarat"**: **what it has brought, what it has made ready.**

**Root: ح-ض-ر.** To be present, to be there, to come into someone's presence. *Ḥāḍir* — present, the one who is there; its opposite *ghāʾib*. *Ḥuḍūr* — being present. **إِحْضَار** (Form IV) — to bring, to bring into someone's presence.

**To see the difference one must look at the Qurʾān's parallel:**

> *"On the Day when every soul will **find present** before it whatever good it has done…"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/30: *tajidu kullu nafsin mā ʿamilat min khayrin muḥḍarā*)

The same root (*muḥḍarā*), and there the verb is **to find** (*tajidu*). The deeds, that is, are found already set out before the person.

**al-Takwīr, by contrast, gives the verb to the person: he is the one who brings.**

The idea this difference carries is this: deeds are not things recorded somewhere far off and then set before us; they are **things a person carries with him.** A man does not go there empty-handed; he has brought something, and it is there that he learns what it is.

An image of a journey is built: everyone is carrying a load, and what the load is is opened at the destination.

### And the sūra's question of knowledge

**The verb is "knows".** The product of thirteen verses of dismantling is not punishment, not fear, not regret — **knowledge.**

And this explains the second half of the sūra directly. For verses 15–29 are entirely about **the source of knowledge**: whom did this report come from, through whose hand did it pass, is its carrier trustworthy, did he conceal any of it, is its source corrupt.

**This is the link between the two halves:**

| | The first half (1–14) | The second half (15–29) |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Where knowledge **arrives** | Where knowledge **comes from** |
| When | That Day | Now |
| Result | *ʿAlimat nafsun* — a soul knows | *In huwa illā dhikr* — this is a reminder |

The sūra builds a chain of knowledge and shows both its ends: **at its source a trustworthy messenger, at its end a person left alone.**

## 81/15-16 — فَلَآ أُقْسِمُ بِٱلْخُنَّسِ ۝ ٱلْجَوَارِ ٱلْكُنَّسِ

*Fa-lā uqsimu bi'l-khunnas ۝ Al-jawāri'l-kunnas*
"I swear by those that withdraw — that run their course, that go into their lairs."

### فَلَا أُقْسِمُ — the لا at the head

How widespread this pattern is in the Qurʾān, and the grammatical dispute over it, were treated in tabular form in the section on al-Balad 90/1; this verse appears in the list there. I do not repeat it here.

**The only thing to be added here is the letter فَـ at the head.**

In the other places the pattern is mostly *lā uqsimu* or *wa lā uqsimu*. Here there is a **fāʾ**: *fa-lā uqsimu*.

In Arabic the *fāʾ* conveys consequence and connection. The oath, then, is not a new beginning; it is **the consequence of the preceding fourteen verses.** "Since it is to be so — then I swear that…"

This is the grammatical tie that binds the two halves together. The sūra is not changing its subject; it **moves to taking up the same subject from the other end.**

### ٱلْخُنَّس — those that withdraw

**Root: خ-ن-س.** This root was treated in the section on al-Nās 114/4 (*al-khannās*), and this verse was mentioned there; I do not repeat it here. In summary: the concrete sense of the root is **to draw back, to shrink away, to hide, to withdraw and vanish.** *Khanas* — the nose being set back; *al-Khansāʾ* — the name of a pre-Islamic poet, from the same root.

**الخُنَّس** is the plural of *khānis* and is on the pattern **فُعَّل** — the plural of the active participle, conveying continuity and multiplicity.

The *al-khannās* of Sūrat al-Nās, by contrast, was on the pattern **فَعَّال** — the intensive form. Two different patterns, one root: the one "constantly shrinking away" (the satan), the other "those that withdraw and vanish" (the stars).

### ٱلْجَوَارِ — those that run

**Root: ج-ر-ي.** To flow, to run, to go swiftly. *Jārī* — flowing; its plural *jawārī*.

The Qurʾān uses this word of ships as well: *"His are the running ships, raised up like mountains upon the sea"* (al-Raḥmān 55/24: *al-jawāri'l-munshaʾāt*). The same word.

### ٱلْكُنَّس — those that go into their lairs

**Root: ك-ن-س.** And in this root two senses stand together:

1. **كَنَسَ** — to sweep. *Kanasa'l-bayta* — he swept the house. *Miknasa* — a broom.
2. **كِنَاس** — the lair of the gazelle and of the wild animal in the thicket, its shelter. **كَانِس** — the one that goes into its lair.

**How do the two join?** The lexicographers explain *kinās* as "the hollow the animal opens for itself by sweeping" — the lair, that is, is a cleared hollow. The idea beneath the root: **to open a place and to go into it.**

**كُنَّس**, like *al-khunnas*, is on the *fuʿʿal* pattern; both are on the same pattern and end on the same sound.

### Three words, one movement

Set the three attributes one upon another and a single behaviour comes out:

| Word | What it does |
|---|---|
| **الخُنَّس** | Draws back, shrinks away, vanishes |
| **الجَوَارِ** | Runs, advances, makes its way |
| **الكُنَّس** | Goes into its lair, hides |

**Appearing → going → hiding.** A cycle.

### What is meant? — the disagreement

| View | Ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **The stars (in general)** | They are visible by night and vanish by day. *Khunnas* — those that draw back; *kunnas* — those that vanish | The common view. Simple and consistent |
| **The planets** — the five planets visible to the naked eye | As they advance across the sky they slow for a while, appear to be going backwards, then advance again. **This answers to all three words exactly:** withdrawing, advancing, vanishing | Frequently mentioned in the classical commentaries. It finds a counterpart for all three words |
| **Gazelles / wild oxen** | The proper use of the word *kinās* is an animal's lair. The next verse speaks of the night and the morning; the animal image fits that tableau as well | A minority view; but strong on the linguistic side |

**And a caution is needed here.**

The attraction of the second view is real: the "appearing to go backwards" of heavenly bodies is a phenomenon **visible to the naked eye**, and requires no knowledge of modern astronomy. An observer who watches the sky over weeks notices it; it is known that this movement was recorded in the older traditions of observation.

**But this cannot be said:** "This verse is announcing the orbital motion of the planets." It cannot be said, because:

- The verse describes an **appearance**, not a mechanism. The words name what the observer sees.
- The classical exegetes drew this interpretation not from any astronomical knowledge but from **the lexical sense of the words**.
- And the verse's function is not to give information; it is **to swear an oath.** The things sworn by are things the hearer knows — one does not swear by something he does not know.

Per the method (`STYLE.md`), no forced connection with natural science is made in this commentary. **What can be recorded is this:** the three words name a behaviour seen in the sky — appearing, advancing, vanishing — with three separate verbs; and the description fits what is seen with the naked eye.

### And the oath's link with the context

**The things sworn by are things that hide.**

And the report that follows is about a message that comes **from an unseen source**. The twenty-fourth verse will say *al-ghayb* (the unseen) outright.

The sūra, that is, swears by **things that hide and come back into view**, and so defends the truth of a report that comes from what is hidden.

I record this connection as my own reading. But that the Qurʾān's oaths are generally related to their subjects has been observed in other sections of this commentary as well — night and day in al-Ḍuḥā, coming to light in al-Shams, splitting open in al-Falaq.

## 81/17-18 — وَٱلَّيْلِ إِذَا عَسْعَسَ ۝ وَٱلصُّبْحِ إِذَا تَنَفَّسَ

*Wa'l-layli idhā ʿasʿas ۝ Wa'ṣ-ṣubḥi idhā tanaffas*
"And by the night, when it turns away; and by the morning, when it breathes."

The two most beautiful verses of the sūra.

### عَسْعَسَ — a word of two opposite senses

**Root: ع-س-س**, a quadriliteral reduplicated form (four letters, the pair repeated): *ʿas-ʿas*.

**And the word is one of the *aḍdād*** — that is, it conveys both a thing and its exact opposite:

| Sense | What it means |
|---|---|
| **It came, it drew on** | The beginning of the night, the falling of the darkness |
| **It went, it withdrew, it turned its back** | The ending of the night, the drawing back of the darkness |

This class of words, called *aḍdād* in Arabic, is interesting, and it is not rare. But in this word the opposition is no accident — **it follows from the logic of the root.**

### The resolution of the opposition: thinning

At the centre of the root lie **thinning, growing sparse, lightness.** And the night has two thin ends:

- **At its beginning:** the darkness has not yet settled; it is thin and new.
- **At its end:** the darkness has already dissolved; it is thin and about to be over.

***ʿAsʿasa*, then, names not the thick middle of the night but its thin edge.** And because the night has two thin edges, the word can look both ways at once.

This reading takes the opposition out of being an oddity of language and makes it the appearance of a single sense from its two ends. **What the word describes is not a direction but a consistency.** I offer this as the explanation I find strongest; the preference is not binding.

**The root's relatives support this too:**
- **عَسَّ** — to go about by night, to patrol.
- **العَسَس** — the night watchmen, the patrol. Beneath the root lie **the night, and movement in the night.**

### Which of the two? — the context decides

Both views have been defended in the classical commentaries. But the next verse makes the choice easier:

**وَٱلصُّبْحِ إِذَا تَنَفَّسَ** — "and by the morning, when it breathes."

If what were meant were the night **coming on**, the two oaths would be sworn at two moments far apart: evening and morning. If what is meant is the night **going**, then **both oaths are sworn at one and the same moment:** the last edge of the night and the first instant of the morning.

And that moment is a single moment — the seam itself. As the night draws back, the morning breathes.

This is why most of the exegetes stand in this direction. **My preference is in the same direction**, this is its ground, and it is not binding.

In the section on al-Ḍuḥā the Qurʾān's oaths by the night were given in tabular form; there, for this verse, the note "when it draws on / when it withdraws" was entered — that indeterminacy was deliberate, and it is explained here.

### تَنَفَّسَ — the morning's breathing

**Root: ن-ف-س.** This root was treated in the section on al-Shams 91/7 (*nafs*), and this verse was mentioned there; I do not repeat it here. In summary: beneath the root lies **breath** — *nafas* (the air breathed), *tanaffus* (breathing in and out), *nafīs* (precious), *munāfasa* (vying for something precious).

**What is to be added here:**

**One. The pattern is Form V.** This form conveys **gradualness** (coming about slowly, by degrees) and **reflexivity**. The morning, that is, is not lit from outside; **by itself, little by little, it lets out its breath.**

This fits what the morning actually looks like: the light does not come all at once; the horizon opens by degrees. The pattern itself carries this gradualness.

**Two. Why "breath"?**

Because breath is **the sign of being alive**. That a thing breathes shows that it has not died. The sūra's first thirteen verses recounted death, dismantling, going out; the eighteenth verse answers with **a drawing of breath.**

And breath comes **from within**. The morning is not lit by a source outside it; **it releases what it has held inside.**

**Three. Two images of the same event.**

The Qurʾān uses two different images for the morning, and the two look from opposite directions:

| Place | Verb | Image | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| al-Anʿām 6/96 | **فَالِقُ ٱلْإِصْبَاح** | The one who **splits open** the morning | From outside, by an agent |
| al-Takwīr 81/18 | **تَنَفَّسَ** | The morning **breathes** | From within, of itself |

In the section on al-Falaq *fāliqu'l-iṣbāḥ* was dwelt on: the night is closed, it cracks at some point of the horizon and the light comes out from within. Here the same event is given **not as a splitting but as a drawing of breath.**

The two images do not contradict one another; they look at the same event from two sides. And they show **that the word the Qurʾān chooses changes according to the work of the place it is chosen for**: Sūrat al-Falaq is a sūra of taking refuge, and there the **splitting** of the darkness was what was wanted; al-Takwīr is a sūra of report, and here the **aliveness** of the morning is what is wanted.

**Four. And a return of a root inside the sūra.**

The fourteenth verse: **عَلِمَتْ نَفْسٌ** — a *nafs* knows.
The eighteenth verse: **تَنَفَّسَ** — the morning drew *breath*.

**The same root.** The word the sūra uses for the human being and the word it uses for the morning come from one root: **breath.**

A *nafs* is a breath. And the morning is the breath at the end of the night. The sūra names the person and the morning with the same word.

I record this as my own reading; it is not visible without the analysis of the root made in al-Shams.

### Where the two verses stand in the sūra

**And now look at where these two verses stand.**

Before them: across fourteen verses the sun was rolled up, the stars fell, the sky was stripped away, the seas caught fire. **The ending of a world.**

After them: *"Truly it is the speech of a noble messenger"* — **the arrival of a report.**

**And exactly in the middle, two verses: the night withdraws, the morning breathes.**

In passing from the ending to the report, the sūra sets in between **the image of what comes after an ending.** Morning follows night; and this is a fact repeated every day, and disputed by no one.

I offer this as a possible reading: these two oaths of the sūra do more than swear — **they carry the argument itself in the form of an image.** Darkness is not the last word. The same observation was made in the section on al-Falaq: *"darkness is not permanent"*, and there too the ground was the splitting open of the morning.

## 81/19-21 — إِنَّهُۥ لَقَوْلُ رَسُولٍ كَرِيمٍ ۝ ذِى قُوَّةٍ عِندَ ذِى ٱلْعَرْشِ مَكِينٍ ۝ مُّطَاعٍ ثَمَّ أَمِينٍ

*Innahū la-qawlu rasūlin karīm ۝ Dhī quwwatin ʿinda dhi'l-ʿarshi makīn ۝ Muṭāʿin thamma amīn*
"Truly it is the speech of a noble messenger — possessed of strength, of firm standing with the Owner of the Throne; obeyed there, trustworthy."

### The answer to the oath, and a threefold reinforcement

The answer to the four oaths is here. And the construction of the sentence lays three separate means of reinforcement one upon another:

1. **إِنَّ** — the particle of emphasis.
2. **لَـ** (the *lām* of inception) — a second emphasis.
3. **A nominal sentence** — in Arabic this conveys fixity and continuance; unlike a verbal sentence, not a passing condition but a settled state.

**A threefold emphasis is no accident.** For opposite it stands a threefold denial, and the sūra will count them out in order: he is not possessed (22), he does not withhold (24), it is not the speech of a satan (25).

### قَوْلُ رَسُولٍ — "the speech of a messenger"

**There is a point here that must be attended to.**

At first sight the expression can be read as "this Qurʾān is the messenger's own speech". It is not so. The construct *qawlu rasūl* means **the speech the messenger carries** — not the speech the messenger composed.

This follows from the logic of the word *rasūl* itself. It was treated in the section on al-Bayyina 98/2: **the root ر-س-ل**, to send; *risāla* — the thing sent; *rasūl* — the person sent. The definition of a messenger is **that he carries speech which is not his own.** If the speech were his, he would not be a messenger.

And indeed the verse begins to enumerate the messenger's qualities — and the qualities it enumerates are exactly those to be looked for in a **carrier**.

### The same expression, two different messengers

**This construct occurs in two places in the Qurʾān**, and in the two places it points to two different persons. The comparison is instructive for what it shows about how the Qurʾān is read:

| | **al-Takwīr 81/19** | **al-Ḥāqqa 69/40** |
|---|---|---|
| The expression | *innahū la-qawlu rasūlin karīm* | *innahū la-qawlu rasūlin karīm* |
| What follows | *"of firm standing with the Owner of the Throne, obeyed there"* (20-21) | *"It is not the speech of a poet… not the speech of a soothsayer… it is a sending down from the Lord of all the worlds"* (41-43) |
| Who is meant | **The angel** (Gabriel, Jibrīl) | **The Prophet** |
| Why | Being present beside the Throne, being obeyed there — these are not expressions used of a human being. Moreover verse 22 mentions the Prophet separately, saying *ṣāḥibukum* (your companion) | The charges of poet and soothsayer had been directed at the Prophet; the context points to him |

**The same sentence in two places, two different persons.** And what makes the distinction is not the sentence itself but **what comes after it.**

This shows a basic rule of reading the Qurʾān: the meaning of an expression is found not inside the expression but in the place where it stands.

### Six qualities: what is looked for in a carrier?

Now set out in order what is enumerated in verses 19–24. Everything enumerated is a condition necessary for **a report to arrive sound**:

| Verse | Quality | Which question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 19 | **كَرِيم** — noble, honoured | Who is carrying it? Not just anyone |
| 20 | **ذِى قُوَّة** — possessed of strength | Can he carry it? Has he the power to bear the load |
| 20 | **عِندَ ذِى ٱلْعَرْشِ مَكِين** — of firm standing with the source | Has he access to the source? Did he receive it directly |
| 21 | **مُطَاع** — obeyed | Has he authority in the chain? Does he command the intermediate links |
| 21 | **أَمِين** — trustworthy | Will he alter it? Will he deliver the trust as it was |
| 24 | **غَيْرُ ضَنِين** — not withholding | Will he diminish it? Will he keep part of it back |

**This is an argument from a chain of transmission.**

The sūra does not say "this is true, believe it". It gives the answer to the question of how you check whether a report has come through sound: **you examine the carrier.** And the conditions the classical science of hadith looks for in a transmitter are exactly like those enumerated here — *ḍabṭ* (retentiveness), *ʿadāla* (probity), *ittiṣāl* (connection to the source).

I record this resemblance as an inference. But the structure of the argument really is this: **the truth of the content is defended by the soundness of the channel.**

### كَرِيم

**Root: ك-ر-م.** It was treated in the section on ʿAbasa 80/13 and 80/16: *karam* — generosity, nobility, worth.

**And there is a link between the two sūras:** in ʿAbasa 80/13-16 the pages were *mukarrama* (made honoured) and their bearers *kirām* (the honoured ones). In al-Takwīr 81/19 the messenger is *karīm*. **The two sūras qualify the bearer of the revelation with the same root.**

### ذِى قُوَّةٍ

**Root: ق-و-ي.** Strength, power, endurance.

**And the Qurʾān's parallel is decisive:**

> *"He was taught it by one mighty in powers (**شَدِيدُ ٱلْقُوَىٰ**)."* (al-Najm 53/5)

In Sūrat al-Najm the same notion is used in the same context and of the same being. This is one of the strongest grounds for the view that the *rasūl* of al-Takwīr is the angel.

### عِندَ ذِى ٱلْعَرْشِ مَكِينٍ

**مَكِين — root م-ك-ن.** *Makān* — place; *makāna* — position, standing. *Makīn* — **one whose place is firm, whose position is settled.**

**And here there is a very fine parallel from within the Qurʾān itself:**

> *"The king said: Bring him to me, that I may take him for myself. And when he had spoken with him he said: **Today you are with us of firm standing and trustworthy** (إِنَّكَ ٱلْيَوْمَ لَدَيْنَا مَكِينٌ أَمِينٌ)."* (Yūsuf 12/54)

**مَكِين أَمِين** — the same two words, side by side. And there they describe an official whom a king trusts in his court.

**al-Takwīr, then, uses the language of the court in describing the angel's position.** The Owner of the Throne, and with Him an official of firm standing, obeyed and trustworthy. The most concrete frame the hearer could grasp: **a sovereign's most trusted envoy.**

**ذِى ٱلْعَرْش — the Owner of the Throne.** And the choice of name is meaningful: it does not say "Allāh", it does not say "al-Raḥmān". **The title that conveys sovereignty** is chosen — because the subject is a chain of authority.

**العَرْش — root ع-ر-ش.** Its concrete sense: an arbour, a high roofed structure, a ceiling; and from this, a throne. *ʿArīsh* — a vine arbour. In the Qurʾān: *"gardens trellised and untrellised"* (al-Anʿām 6/141: *maʿrūshāt*).

Beneath the root, then, lies **a raised structure**. It looks to the same idea as *marfūʿa* (raised) in ʿAbasa 80/14.

### مُّطَاعٍ ثَمَّ أَمِينٍ

**مُطَاع — root ط-و-ع.** To obey, to comply willingly. *Ṭāʿa* — obedience; *ṭawʿan* — willingly, of one's own accord (the opposite of *karhan*).

A passive participle: **"the one who is obeyed".** The messenger, that is, has others under his command; he stands high in the chain.

**ثَمَّ — and here there is a mistake in reading that is often made.**

The word is read **ثَمَّ** (with *fatḥa*), not **ثُمَّ** (with *ḍamma*). The two are entirely different words:

| | **ثُمَّ (*thumma*)** | **ثَمَّ (*thamma*)** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A conjunction: "then" | An adverb of place: **"there"** |
| Its function | Sequence | Indicating place |

In the verse it is **ثَمَّ**: **"obeyed there".** That is: not *"he is obeyed, then he is trustworthy"*, but *"there — that is, with the Throne — his word is heeded; and he is trustworthy."*

**And the idea this qualification carries is important.** The angel is obeyed not everywhere but **there.** His authority comes not from himself but from the position he occupies. The messenger's power is the sender's power.

**أَمِين — root أ-م-ن.** This root was treated in the section on al-Baqara 2/3 (*yuʾminūn*); I do not repeat it here. In summary: the root's proper sense is **trust and safety** — *amān* (a guarantee of safety), *amāna* (a trust), *amīn* (trustworthy), *al-Muʾmin* (the giver of safety).

**What is to be added here:** an *amāna* is what a person holds in his hand but which is not his own, and which **must be delivered exactly as it was.** The attribute *amīn*, then, is the most fitting of all attributes for a carrier: one who does not alter what he carries.

**And a detail:** it is widely reported that before his prophethood the Prophet was called **al-Amīn** by the Meccans. This is a piece of information amply present in the sources; it does not rest on a single report.

Its link with the next verse is direct: the twenty-second verse will say *"your companion is not possessed"*. **Those who called him al-Amīn are now calling him possessed.** This is what stands silently between the two verses.

## 81/22 — وَمَا صَاحِبُكُم بِمَجْنُونٍ

*Wa mā ṣāḥibukum bi-majnūn*
"And your companion is not possessed."

### صَاحِبُكُم — "your companion"

**The whole force of the verse is in this word.**

The verse does not say "our messenger", does not say "the prophet", and does not give his name. **صَاحِبُكُم** — "your companion", "the one from among you, who lives with you".

**Root: ص-ح-ب.** To be together, to keep company, to accompany. *Ṣāḥib* — a companion; *ṣuḥba* — companionship; *aṣḥāb* — those who are together.

**And the pronoun: كُم — "your".** The verse, that is, appeals to the hearer's own testimony.

This is a way of offering evidence, and it is extremely practical: **you know him.** He has been among you for forty years. You know how he speaks, how he behaves, how he trades, how he keeps a trust. To tell whether a man is possessed no learning is needed; **closeness is enough.**

And the verse uses the strongest evidence the hearer has — his own observation — against him.

**And the link with ʿAbasa:** in ʿAbasa 80/36 the same root was used, in the list of flight, of the wife: *wa ṣāḥibatihī*. The two sūras use this root in two opposite directions — in one, being together is evidence; in the other, being together dissolves. I record this parallel as my own reading.

### مَجْنُون — one whose mind is covered over

**Root: ج-ن-ن.** This root was treated in the section on al-Baqara 2/25; I do not repeat it here. In summary: the sense of the root is **to cover, to conceal**, and all its derivatives turn about invisibility — *janīn* (covered in the womb), *jinn* (the unseen being), **majnūn (whose mind is covered over)**, *mijann* (a shield), *janna* (the garden that covers).

**And now attend to what the word meant in that culture.**

*Majnūn* does not coincide exactly with the modern "mentally ill". The form of the word is a passive participle: **"struck by a jinn"** — that is, one who has come under the influence of a jinn, whose speech comes not from himself but from it.

**This changes what the charge is:**

- To say "mad" in the modern sense: *this man's mind is disordered, what he says is meaningless.*
- To say *majnūn* in that culture: **this man's speech is not his own; its source is not where he claims it is.**

The accusation, then, is not about soundness of mind but about **the source of the speech.**

**And precisely for this reason the sūra's answer is not the expected answer.**

The sūra does not say "he is sane". It does not say "his speech is coherent". Instead:

- 19-21: it recounts **who the real carrier is.**
- 23: it says that **he saw him.**
- 25: it says that **it is not the speech of a satan.**

**If the charge is about the source, then, the answer is about the source too.** The sūra meets the accusation on its own ground.

It was recorded in the section on al-Inshirāḥ: labels such as *majnūn*, *shāʿir*, *sāḥir*, *kāhin* are not random insults; **they are that culture's standard ways of invalidating a man's speech** — classifying the speaker instead of arguing with what is said. al-Takwīr refuses the classification and pulls the argument back to what is said.

**A note on the grammar.** The construction *mā … bi-*: the particle of negation + a **redundant *bāʾ***. This *bāʾ* adds nothing to the meaning; it **reinforces**. So not "is not" but **"is in no way"**.

## 81/23 — وَلَقَدْ رَءَاهُ بِٱلْأُفُقِ ٱلْمُبِينِ

*Wa laqad raʾāhu bi'l-ufuqi'l-mubīn*
"Assuredly he saw him on the clear horizon."

### The last link in the chain: eyewitness

Verses 19–21 described the carrier. This verse adds one thing more: **the receiver saw the carrier.**

The report, that is, did not arrive anonymously. The link between is not an indirect intuition or an inner voice; it is **an encounter that was seen.**

With this the argument from a chain of transmission is completed: the source (the Owner of the Throne) → the carrier (the messenger whose qualities were enumerated) → **a delivery that was seen** → the receiver (your companion).

**The grammar.** *Wa laqad* — *wāw* + *lām* + *qad*: the *lām* of the oath and the particle of verification. It conveys a threefold certainty.

### Who saw whom?

**The view of the majority:** the Prophet saw Gabriel. Its ground is directly the context — the three preceding verses describe the angel, the following verse speaks of the Prophet; the pronouns are distributed between these two.

**And Sūrat al-Najm confirms this:**

> *"He was taught it by one mighty in powers. Endowed with sound judgement; he stood erect — **and he was on the highest horizon** (وَهُوَ بِٱلْأُفُقِ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ). Then he drew near and came down."* (al-Najm 53/5-8)

**The same word: الأفق.** And in the same context: an event of seeing, and a teaching.

The two sūras are looking at the same event. al-Takwīr says *al-ufuqi'l-mubīn* (the clear horizon), al-Najm *al-ufuqi'l-aʿlā* (the highest horizon).

**A qualification.** Whom the event of seeing in Sūrat al-Najm points to — Gabriel, or God — has been disputed in the history of exegesis, and differing views are transmitted among the Companions as well. That dispute belongs to Sūrat al-Najm and should be treated there. **In al-Takwīr the dispute is narrower**, because the three verses immediately before it plainly describe a messenger.

### ٱلْأُفُق — the horizon

**Root: أ-ف-ق.** The horizon, the line where sky and earth meet; *āfāq* — its plural, the quarters of the sky, far countries. In the Qurʾān: *"We shall show them Our signs on the horizons and in themselves"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/53: *fi'l-āfāqi wa fī anfusihim*).

### ٱلْمُبِين — clear

**Root: ب-ي-ن.** This root was treated in detail in the section on al-Bayyina 98/1; I do not repeat it here. In summary: beneath the root lie **separating, opening a space between** — *bayn* (the space between), *bayān* (explanation, distinguishing), *bayyina* (the clear proof that distinguishes), *mubīn* (clear, distinguishing).

**And the word's placing here is fine:** the *horizon* is the line at which sky and earth are **separated**. The root's sense of "separating" and the horizon itself carry the same idea.

**And its function:** *mubīn* says that the seeing took place in conditions that **leave no room for doubt.** This is the first question to be asked of an eyewitness: how did you see, what was the weather, what was the distance. The verse answers that question in advance.

## 81/24 — وَمَا هُوَ عَلَى ٱلْغَيْبِ بِضَنِينٍ

*Wa mā huwa ʿala'l-ghaybi bi-ḍanīn*
"He is not niggardly with the unseen."

The best-known variant reading of the sūra is here, and it genuinely changes the sense.

### Two readings

The difference lies at a single point: **ض** or **ظ**.

| Reading | Root | Its sense | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ضَنِين** (with *ḍād*) | **ض-ن-ن** | Niggardly, withholding | "He does not withhold the unseen from you, does not hide it, does not give it short" |
| **ظَنِين** (with *ẓāʾ*) | **ظ-ن-ن** | Under suspicion, one who is doubted | "He is not under suspicion in the matter of the unseen, not someone to be accused" |

**Both are sound readings, and both are recorded in the sources.** Since I am not certain which reading belongs to which imam, I give no name.

**A note:** this is not a matter of copying. In Arabic script **ض** and **ظ** are separate letters, and they were distinguished from one another in the early period as well. What we have before us, then, is a genuine variant reading.

### The words

**ضَنِين — root ض-ن-ن.** *Ḍanna bi'sh-shayʾ* — he withheld a thing, could not bring himself to give it, did not want it out of his hand. *Ḍinn* — what a person counts most precious and gives to no one. *Ḍanāna* — niggardliness.

What is peculiar to the word: not an ordinary miserliness, but **withholding because one finds a thing precious.** A man will give his property but not his secret; this is what *ḍanīn* is.

**ظَنِين — root ظ-ن-ن.** *Ẓann* — to suppose, to conjecture; and *ẓanīn* (the *faʿīl* pattern, with the sense of a passive participle) — **one who is suspected, accused, held untrustworthy.** In Arabic *shāhidun ẓanīn* — a suspect witness; a person whose testimony is not accepted. A legal term.

### Which fits the context better? — both

**And this is what is interesting.**

| | **The ضَنِين reading** | **The ظَنِين reading** |
|---|---|---|
| Which objection it answers | "He is not telling us everything; he hides a part, he gives selectively" | "He is making it up; he cannot be trusted" |
| Its ground in the context | The preceding word **أَمِين** (21). One who is *amīn* delivers his trust **without diminution**. *Ḍanīn* is the exact opposite of *amīn* | Before it the charge of **مَجْنُون** (22), after it the refusal of **قَوْلِ شَيْطَانٍ** (25). Both are imputations |
| What kind of claim | **The completeness of the transmission** | **The honesty of the transmitter** |

**The two readings meet two different objections.** And both are present in the context.

In the classical tradition it is counted not a defect but **a richness** that two sound readings should each yield a solid sense: as much meaning is found in one place as in two verses. This is an approach widely voiced in the literature on the variant readings; I ascribe it to no particular person.

**My own preference** — it is not binding — is towards *ضَنِين*, and its ground is the word order: the word stands in the same rhyme and in the same field of ideas as *amīn* three verses earlier. *Amīn* (one who can be entrusted) and *ḍanīn* (one who withholds) set up a natural opposition: he who takes a trust does not diminish it.

But the *ظَنِين* reading has a strong ground too: verses 22, 24 and 25 together form a series refusing three imputations, and *ẓanīn* fits that series exactly.

### The heavy idea the ضَنِين reading carries

When this reading is accepted, the verse turns out to have said something striking.

**Knowledge of the unseen is valuable in proportion as it is withheld.**

The claim of access to the unseen has been a source of power throughout history — soothsaying, divination, esoteric knowledge. And this power arises precisely from **the knowledge not being distributed.** If knowledge is open to everyone it produces no privilege. The soothsayer's position depends on his giving what he knows selectively and in part.

What the verse says is this: **the carrier does not do this.** He gives what he has entire, gives it to everyone, gives it to his opponents too.

**And this is behaviour contrary to the carrier's own interest.** Someone who wants to build a position does not distribute what he has received in this way.

I record this as an **observation**, not as proof. A similar observation was made in the section on ʿAbasa, and the same qualification was entered there too: the text contains elements that do not work in its carrier's interest. The reader decides for himself where to place this in his own reasoning.

### عَلَى ٱلْغَيْب — the word order and the particle

**Fronting (*taqdīm*).** The phrase *ʿala'l-ghaybi* comes **before** *bi-ḍanīn*. The normal order would be *wa mā huwa bi-ḍanīnin ʿala'l-ghayb*. Its being brought forward conveys **specification (*ikhtiṣāṣ*)**: "in the matter of the unseen precisely".

**The particle عَلَى.** The usage is not *ḍanna bi-* but *ḍanna ʿalā*. *ʿAlā* conveys being over something and having the mastery of it (*istīlāʾ*). The unseen, that is, is pictured as something he holds under his hand — and he does not withhold it.

**ٱلْغَيْب — root غ-ي-ب.** This root was treated in the section on al-Baqara 2/3; I do not repeat it here. In summary: **to vanish from sight, to be covered over.** The unseen is **not what does not exist but what is not seen** — this distinction is critical, and it is often confused.

**And it settles into place in the weave of the sūra:** in the fifteenth verse an oath was sworn by **the stars that hide**; in the twenty-fourth verse **a report that comes from what is hidden** is defended. An oath by things that vanish and come back, on behalf of a report that comes from the place of vanishing.

## 81/25 — وَمَا هُوَ بِقَوْلِ شَيْطَٰنٍ رَّجِيمٍ

*Wa mā huwa bi-qawli shayṭānin rajīm*
"Nor is it the speech of an accursed satan."

### The third refusal

The last of the three refusals:

| Verse | What is refused | Where it looks |
|---|---|---|
| 22 | *majnūn* | **The receiver** — your companion |
| 24 | *ḍanīn / ẓanīn* | **The transmission** — diminution, imputation |
| 25 | *qawlu shayṭān* | **The source** — where it comes from |

Together the three close off every point at which a report can be questioned: from whom did you receive it, how did you transmit it, whom did it come from.

### رَجِيم — driven off, stoned

**Root: ر-ج-م.** And the two senses of this root come together here in an interesting way:

1. **To stone, to throw stones; to drive off, to send away.** *Rajīm* — stoned, driven off. This is the word in the formula *aʿūdhu bi'llāhi mina'sh-shayṭāni'r-rajīm*.
2. **To speak without knowledge, to make a stab at it, to conjecture.** It occurs plainly in the Qurʾān:

> *"…**stoning at the unseen** (رَجْمًۢا بِٱلْغَيْبِ)…"* (al-Kahf 18/22)

*Rajman bi'l-ghayb* — "throwing stones at what is not seen", that is, **guessing blindly.**

**And now set the two verses side by side:**

- Verse 24: *"He is not withholding / not under suspicion in the matter of **al-ghayb**."*
- Verse 25: *"It is not the speech of a **rajīm** satan."*

**The two verses carry the two words that stand together in al-Kahf 18/22: *ghayb* and *rajm*.** And *rajman bi'l-ghayb* means exactly "shooting off about the unseen".

Verses 24–25 together, then, say this: **this is not a stone thrown at the unseen.**

I record this connection as my own reading; it rests on the use of the words within the Qurʾān and it is not forced.

### Historical background: soothsaying

What the verse refuses is not an abstract possibility; it was an institution present in the hearer's everyday life.

The **kāhin** was, in pre-Islamic Arabia, the person taken to give news of the unseen. The widespread understanding of how it worked was this: the soothsayer has a jinn or a satan for a helper; the helper brings what it has heard from the sky; and the soothsayer passes this on in rhymed, cadenced, obscure speech.

**The hearer, that is, had a ready-made category in which to place the Qurʾān.** Someone who speaks in rhyme and gives news of the unseen — is a soothsayer.

Verses 19–25 of the sūra refuse exactly this category. And the method of refusal is worth noting: **it does not deny the category, it enumerates the qualities of the channel.** The soothsayer's channel rests on a hidden, unsupervised, driven-off being. The channel here: its position stated, its authority stated, its trustworthiness stated, and seen.

**The Qurʾān sets this distinction out plainly in another place** — it was mentioned in the section on al-Bayyina as well:

> *"The satans did not bring it down. It is not fitting for them, nor are they able to. For they are kept far from the hearing."* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/210-212)

**عَنِ ٱلسَّمْعِ لَمَعْزُولُون** — "removed from the hearing". The channel, that is, is closed.

## 81/26 — فَأَيْنَ تَذْهَبُونَ

*Fa-ayna tadhhabūn*
"Where, then, are you going?"

### One question after seven verses of report

Verses 19–25 were entirely declarative sentences: it is this, it is so, it is not. The twenty-sixth verse turns suddenly to the hearer and asks.

**And this is the only question in the sūra's second half.**

A technique the Qurʾān repeats: a thesis is set up, the evidence is arrayed, then a single question is put to the hearer and no answer is given. al-Māʿūn opened with *a-raʾayta*; ʿAbasa 80/3 asked *wa mā yudrīka* and gave no answer. Here too there is no answer.

**The withholding of the answer is the technique itself:** a question that is answered closes; a question that is not answered goes away with the hearer.

### Two readings

**The grammar.** *Ayna* (whither) is an adverb of place and has been brought in front of the verb — **fronting**. This makes the question emphatic and rhetorical: it is not merely information that is asked for; **astonishment and reproach** are conveyed.

| Reading | How it is understood |
|---|---|
| **"Where are you going?"** | After all this explanation, which road are you taking? Is there anywhere else to go? |
| **"Where is your mind going?"** | In Arabic there is an established usage, *ayna yudhhabu bika*, in the sense of "where is your mind running off to" |

Both readings have been defended in the sources, and they do not exclude one another. Both arrive at the same place: **no other place to go is pointed out.**

**And the question works by recalling the sūra's first half.** For the first thirteen verses had recounted the dismantling of every place there is to go: the sun, the mountains, the seas, the sky. For someone who has read the sūra's first half the question already has no answer — **there is no place left to go.**

I record this connection as my own reading, but it may be the most direct link between the sūra's two halves.

## 81/27 — إِنْ هُوَ إِلَّا ذِكْرٌ لِّلْعَٰلَمِينَ

*In huwa illā dhikrun li'l-ʿālamīn*
"It is nothing but a reminder for all the worlds."

### إِنْ … إِلَّا — restriction

The pattern **negation + exception**: the strongest form of restriction in Arabic. *In* is here a particle of negation (in the sense of *mā*), and *illā* the exception.

Not "it is a reminder", but **"it is nothing but a reminder."** All other definitions are shut out: not poetry, not divination, not sorcery, not one man's claim.

**And this too is left outside:** not an instrument of power, not a certificate of privilege. It looks to the same place as the refusal of *ḍanīn* in the twenty-fourth verse.

### ذِكْر — a reminder

**Root: ذ-ك-ر.** To mention, to remember, to call to mind.

**And here the most direct link with ʿAbasa is made:**

| | **ʿAbasa 80/11** | **al-Takwīr 81/27** |
|---|---|---|
| The expression | *kallā innahā **تَذْكِرَة*** | *in huwa illā **ذِكْر*** |
| The pattern | No restriction, but emphasis (*inna*) | **Restriction** (*in … illā*) |
| Its place | After the recounting of an event, a return to the text | After the recounting of the source, a return to the text |
| What follows | *fa-man shāʾa dhakarah* — whoever wills, remembers | *li-man shāʾa minkum an yastaqīm* — whoever wills, goes straight |

**The two sūras take the same two steps:** first they define the text as a "reminder", and immediately afterwards leave it to a person's willing.

And the definition itself carries a claim. **A reminder is the waking of something already present in the one addressed.** One who does not know is given information; one who knows is reminded. This definition assumes that the text will find an answering echo in the one addressed. The expression in al-Shams 91/8, *"and He inspired it with its wickedness and its godfearing"*, looks to the same place.

### لِّلْعَٰلَمِين — for all the worlds

**Root: ع-ل-م.** *ʿĀlam* has the sense of "what is known, that by which something is known" — from the same root come *ʿilm* (knowledge) and *ʿalāma* (a mark). An *ʿālam*, then, is a domain of being that is itself a sign. This word was treated at al-Fātiḥa 1/2 (*rabbu'l-ʿālamīn*).

**And the work the word order does is this:** across fifteen verses an extremely particular chain has been recounted — the Throne, a chosen messenger, an act of seeing, a single receiver. And the conclusion is tied up as **"for all the worlds"**.

**The chain is narrow, the destination wide.** That the channel is selective does not mean that the content is selective.

## 81/28 — لِمَن شَآءَ مِنكُمْ أَن يَسْتَقِيمَ

*Li-man shāʾa minkum an yastaqīm*
"For whoever among you wills to go straight."

### Word order: an appositive substitute

This verse is an **appositive substitute** for *li'l-ʿālamīn* in the preceding verse. The sentence, that is, works like this:

> "for all the worlds — [that is] for whoever among you wills to go straight."

**The substitute does not cancel what came before it; it defines it.**

What this does must be seen:

- **The address is universal:** the text has been sent to all the worlds.
- **The benefit is conditional:** the one who profits by it is the one who wills.

The two do not contradict. A door may be open to everyone, and still only those who enter get inside. The verse preserves the breadth of the opening while stating whom the benefit reaches.

**مِنكُم — "among you".** *Min* is partitive (marking off a part): not all of you, but some among you.

### يَسْتَقِيم — to go straight

**Root: ق-و-م.** This root was treated in several places in this commentary: *mustaqīm* at al-Fātiḥa 1/6, *iqāma* at al-Baqara 2/3, *qayyima* at al-Bayyina 98/5. I do not repeat it here.

In summary: the sense of the root is **to stand, to be set upright, to straighten**, and *mustaqīm* says two things at once — **straightness** and **an upright stance**.

**And what is to be added here is that in this place the word is a *verb*.**

| Place | Word | Its kind | Whose |
|---|---|---|---|
| al-Fātiḥa 1/6 | *aṣ-ṣirāṭa'l-mustaqīm* | **An adjective** | **The path's** |
| al-Baqara 2/3 | *yuqīmūna'ṣ-ṣalāh* | A verb | The servant's (of the prayer) |
| al-Bayyina 98/5 | *dīnu'l-qayyima* | An adjective | The religion's |
| **al-Takwīr 81/28** | *an **yastaqīm*** | **A verb** | **The person's own** |

**What is an attribute of the path in al-Fātiḥa becomes the person's act in al-Takwīr.** What was asked for there as "guide us to the straight path" is described here as "whoever wills to go straight". The same root at two ends, as asking and as doing.

**The pattern: Form X.** One of the functions of this form is **seeking** (*istaghfara* — he sought forgiveness). So *yastaqīm* is not "he became straight"; it is **"he sought to be straight, looked for it, turned towards it"**. The pattern itself conveys an effort.

And read together with *shāʾa* (willed), a doubled wanting comes out: to will and to seek. The verse describes not a single decision but a sustained orientation.

## 81/29 — وَمَا تَشَآءُونَ إِلَّآ أَن يَشَآءَ ٱللَّهُ رَبُّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ

*Wa mā tashāʾūna illā an yashāʾa'llāhu rabbu'l-ʿālamīn*
"And you do not will unless God, the Lord of all the worlds, wills."

The sūra's last verse, and its most discussed sentence.

### Word order: a second restriction

The twenty-seventh verse set up a restriction with *in … illā*. This verse repeats the same pattern: *mā … illā*.

**And the two do the same work in opposite directions:**

- 27: what the text is is bounded — *only* a reminder.
- 29: a person's willing is bounded — *only* if He wills.

In two sentences the sūra draws the limit of two things: **the text's claim** and **the human being's power.**

### The apparent contradiction

The twenty-eighth verse: *"for whoever wills."*
The twenty-ninth verse: *"you cannot will."*

The two verses stand one after the other, and at first sight they look to cancel one another. This lies at the centre of one of the longest debates in the history of *kalām*.

I take no side in this commentary. The frame of this debate was given in the section on al-Baqara 2/7 (the sealing of hearts), and this was recorded there: **not one of the parties has held that a human being is without responsibility; the debate is about *how* responsibility is constituted.** That qualification holds here too, and I do not repeat it.

**The frame of the views:**

| Reading | What it says | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute determination** | A human being has no real willing; everything is the divine willing | It makes verse 28 meaningless. A text does not address as "whoever wills" someone who has no willing |
| **The independence of the human will** | The verse says only that the **possibility** is from God; the willing is the human being's | It strains the wording of verse 29: the verse speaks not of possibility but of willing directly |
| **Two layers** | 28: the act is the human being's and is ascribed to him; the responsibility is his. 29: the possibility of that act, its ground and its coming about are God's | How the two layers join is the theological debate itself; the verse does not explain it |

### The verse's placing says something

I want to add an observation — **as my own reading, not as a theological preference.**

**The qualification comes after the invitation.** The order is this:

1. This is a reminder (27).
2. **For whoever among you wills** (28).
3. And your willing too depends on Him (29).

**Had the order been reversed, the text would be incoherent.** A text saying "you cannot will — so will" would contradict itself. Whereas a text saying "will — and know that your willing too rests on something" is saying something else.

The sequence itself, then, gives a direction of reading: **the qualification was placed there not to cancel the invitation but to show what the invitation rests on.**

And the verse's practical function fits this too. The twenty-eighth verse strengthens the person's hand: you can will. The twenty-ninth verse keeps him from claiming the source of that strength as his own: **that you are able to will is itself something given.**

This also speaks silently with the verse of *al-mawʾūda* in the sūra's first half. There, the right of someone with no power at all was delivered up. Here, someone who supposes he has power is told where his power comes from. At both ends the sūra does the same thing: **no one holds a power that originates in himself.**

### رَبُّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِين — and the subject the sūra hides

**And now the whole architecture of the sūra closes.**

It was recorded in the first section: **not one** of the twelve conditional clauses has a subject. The sun was rolled up — who rolled it is not said. The sky was stripped away — who stripped it is not said. The Fire was set blazing, the Garden was brought near — all of them passive.

**And in the twenty-ninth verse, in the sūra's last two words, the subject's name is given for the first and only time: ٱللَّهُ رَبُّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ.**

The question held in suspension across twenty-nine verses — *who is doing all this?* — is answered in the very last word.

This is the sūra's strongest structural feature. And it produces this effect: for thirteen verses the reader looks for the subject; in the second half a chain is described (the messenger, the Owner of the Throne, the carrier), but the head of the chain is still referred to by a title (*dhi'l-ʿarsh*); and only in the last sentence is the name set down.

**Moreover the last two words also make a rhyme with the verse two before it:** verse 27 ended with *li'l-ʿālamīn*, verse 29 ends with *rabbu'l-ʿālamīn*. **The text is for all the worlds, and the worlds have a Lord.** The same word, one of them the destination, one the source.

**And one link more:** *rabbu'l-ʿālamīn* is the second verse of al-Fātiḥa. al-Takwīr ends where al-Fātiḥa began. I record this as a possible reading — the order of the codex and the order of revelation are different, and connections of this kind are not proof but a possible reading.

## The sūra as a whole

### The architecture

| Verses | Section | What it does | Verse-ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-6 | **Dismantling** | The breaking apart of the visible world: sun, stars, mountains, camels, animals, seas | *-at* |
| 7-13 | **Building** | The setting up of the reckoning: the sorting, the questioning of the girl, the registers, the sky, the Fire, the Garden | *-at* |
| 14 | **The answer** | *ʿAlimat nafsun mā aḥḍarat* — a soul knows | *-at* |
| 15-18 | **The oath** | The stars, the night, the morning | *-s* |
| 19-25 | **The report** | The carrier's qualities and the three refusals | *-īn / -ūn* |
| 26-29 | **The close** | A question, a definition, an invitation, a qualification | *-ūn / -īn* |

**Three blocks of rhyme, three blocks of subject.** When the sound changes the subject changes too; the ear notices the boundaries of the sections before the sense does.

### The link between the two halves

At first sight the sūra stands like two separate sūras: one the Resurrection, the other revelation. The link is this:

| | The first half (1–14) | The second half (15–29) |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Where knowledge **arrives** | Where knowledge **comes from** |
| Time | That Day | Now |
| Key verb | *ʿalimat* — knew | *innahū la-qawlu* — it is the speech of… |
| Result | A soul knows what it has brought | This is a reminder, for whoever wills |

**The sūra shows the two ends of a chain of knowledge.** The *fāʾ* of the fifteenth verse, standing in the middle (*fa-lā uqsimu*), lodges this link inside the grammar: the second half begins as the consequence of the first.

And the two verses at the very centre — the night withdraws, the morning breathes — carry the image of the passage from the ending to the report.

### The sūra's central image: what closes and what opens

| What | Verb | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| The sun (1) | *kuwwirat* — rolled up | **Closes** |
| The sky (11) | *kushiṭat* — stripped away | **Opens** |
| The pages (10) | *nushirat* — spread open | **Opens** |
| The Garden (13) — *j-n-n*: the hidden one | *uzlifat* — brought near | **Becomes visible** |

In the life of this world the universe is open and the record is closed. The sūra recounts the moment at which these two change places.

And Qāf 50/22 gives it its name: *"We have lifted your veil from you — your sight today is sharp."* The product of the dismantling is not destruction but **sight.**

### The hidden subject

Across twenty-nine verses the subject of no verb is named. Eleven passive verbs, one Form VII. Even in the second half the head of the chain is referred to by a title: *dhi'l-ʿarsh*.

**And in the last two words the name is set down: ٱللَّهُ رَبُّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ.**

The sūra's whole tension is built on this deferral.

### The drops in scale

Twice the sūra makes a sheer descent from the large to the small:

1. **Verses 1-4:** the sun → the stars → the mountains → **the pregnant camels.** From the cosmos to the pen.
2. **Verses 1-14:** thirteen verses of the universe being dismantled → **a soul knows.** From the universe to a single person.

This is the same technique as al-Māʿūn's architecture (from the Day of Reckoning to a bucket) and as ʿAbasa's table of foods (from the resurrection to a single meal): **the large claim is measured in the small.**

### Links with ʿAbasa

The two sūras stand side by side in the codex, and they genuinely complete one another. An inventory of the links made within the text:

| Subject | ʿAbasa | al-Takwīr |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening / raising to life** — ن-ش-ر | 80/22 *ansharah* (He raises to life) | 81/10 *nushirat* (the pages were spread open) |
| **Pages** — ص-ح-ف | 80/13 *fī ṣuḥufin mukarrama* | 81/10 *aṣ-ṣuḥuf* |
| **The definition of the text** — ذ-ك-ر | 80/11 *innahā tadhkira* | 81/27 *in huwa illā dhikr* |
| **The human being's willing** | 80/12 *fa-man shāʾa dhakarah* | 81/28-29 *li-man shāʾa… wa mā tashāʾūna illā* |
| **Companionship** — ص-ح-ب | 80/36 *ṣāḥibatihī* (he flees from his wife) | 81/22 *ṣāḥibukum* (you know him) |
| **Being left alone** | 80/37 *shaʾnun yughnīh* | 81/14 *ʿalimat **nafsun*** (singular) |
| **Noble bearers** — ك-ر-م | 80/16 *kirāmin barara* | 81/19 *rasūlin karīm* |
| **Burying alive** | — | 81/8-9 (together with al-Shams 91/10) |
| **The lifting of the covering** | 80/15, 38 — the root س-ف-ر | 81/11 *kushiṭat* |
| **The recording of what goes unnoticed** | The blind man did not see the face darken | The girl had no one to call for an account of her |

**The last row is the deepest thing the two sūras share:** both of them say that **a right no one claimed is entered in the record.**

### What it does not say: two wrong readings

**The first wrong reading: using the sūra as a source of natural science.**

There are three places in the sūra that look open to this, and at all three one must stop, per the method (`STYLE.md`):

- **كُوِّرَتْ** — the claim that "the sun is a sphere". *Kura* (ك-ر-و) and *kawr* (ك-و-ر) are **separate roots**; the verb conveys not a shape but an operation.
- **سُجِّرَتْ** — claims resting on the composition of sea water. The image of the verse is an oven; an oven the hearer knows.
- **ٱلْخُنَّس ٱلْجَوَارِ ٱلْكُنَّس** — the claim about the orbits of the planets. The words name an **appearance**, not a mechanism; and the classical exegetes drew this interpretation not from astronomy but from the lexicon.

The problem common to all three is this: the verse's force lies not in its announcing some unknown piece of information but in **its naming a known thing correctly.** To add a "miracle" to it is in fact to weaken it — for the claim becomes refutable, and the verse has been tied to that claim.

**The second wrong reading: reading verse 29 as a sentence that removes responsibility.**

The sūra itself does not permit this: across twenty-eight verses it makes a call, arrays evidence, puts a question to the hearer. The twenty-ninth verse does not make all of that unnecessary; **it shows the ground the invitation rests on.** The sequence itself says so: the qualification comes *after* the invitation.

### What this says to the present

**One. Value is not a property of the object.**

The fourth verse's observation — the pregnant camels being left untended — speaks not of a destruction but of **a severing of attention.** The camel is there; its value is gone.

This is an observation about where value stands: **the value of a thing lies not in the thing but in the attention given to it.** And attention is something that can be withdrawn.

The verse's question to the present is direct: *which of the things in your hand takes its value from the fact that you are looking at it?* And when that looking stops, what is left?

**Two. The right no one claimed.**

The eighth and ninth verses are among the clearest expressions of the Qurʾān's understanding of justice. The girl had:

- no legal personality,
- no one to claim her blood,
- and the one who killed her was already her guardian.

No system, that is, could have opened her case. **The sūra places her case in the middle of the list that recounts the dismantling of the universe.**

What this says to the present goes far beyond the historical practice. In every order there are losses that go unrecorded because there is no one to call for an account of them. The verse's measure is this: **that a wrong is real does not depend on someone's being able to claim it.**

**Three. Giving the victim the floor.**

And the verse's **method** is as important as its content. On this matter the Qurʾān does not merely lay down a prohibition (there are places where it does: al-Isrāʾ 17/31; al-Anʿām 6/151). al-Takwīr does something else: **it makes the victim speak.**

Prohibiting a practice is mostly not enough if the victim is not counted as a person. What changes what may be done is a change in **who counts.** The verse intervenes there.

**Four. A report is assessed by its channel.**

The argument verses 19–25 build is a piece of reasoning still valid today: if the truth of a report is in question, the questions to be asked are settled. **Who carried it? Did he have access to the source? Could he have altered it? Did he keep part of it back? By sight, or by hearsay?**

The sūra answers every one of these questions in turn. And this carries a method for how knowledge is to be verified — in an age when knowledge is in everyone's hands and its source is often uncertain, the questions have not grown old.

**Five. The power of withholding, and letting it go.**

The *ضَنِين* reading of the twenty-fourth verse touches on a very practical mechanism: **knowledge produces power in proportion as it is withheld.**

In an institution, in a profession, in a relationship, the person who holds the information gains standing precisely from holding it. The moment it is shared, that standing dissolves. This is why not sharing information mostly arises not from ill will but from **the natural working of interest.**

The verse qualifies a carrier with the reverse of this: he gives what he has entire, to everyone, and to his opponents as well.

**Six. The morning's breathing.**

The eighteenth verse is the sūra's calmest sentence, and it comes precisely after its darkest passage.

The night withdraws, the morning breathes. The image of what comes after an ending. And this is a fact repeated every day, and disputed by no one.

The same observation was made in the section on al-Falaq: **darkness is not permanent**, and its evidence is repeated every morning. al-Takwīr says it not as a splitting open but as **a drawing of breath** — and the two images look to the same place.

**Seven. Knowing what you have brought.**

The fourteenth verse's verb is not "sees" but "knows". And its object is not "what you did" but **"what you brought".**

The difference has to do with how responsibility is constituted: deeds are not things recorded somewhere far off and then set before us; they are **things a person carries with him.**

The question this puts to the present follows from that, and it is an everyday one: *did you add anything today, and is what you added coming with you?*

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

Per the method (`STYLE.md`), I list explicitly the places throughout the text where definite language was deliberately not used:

- **The report about reciting the sūra.** I stated that it is transmitted in sources that include al-Tirmidhī and with an ascription to Ibn ʿUmar; I recorded that its chain of transmission is disputed, and I did not quote its wording verbatim.
- **The exact sense of كُوِّرَتْ.** The root's two senses (to wind / to gather up and set aside) were given, and on the strength of al-Zumar 39/5 "to wind" was brought forward; but it was not fixed to a single sense.
- **The claim that "the sun is a sphere".** It was refused outright and its ground (that the roots are different) was given.
- **ٱنكَدَرَتْ.** Two senses were given; neither was preferred. The interpretation of the difference of pattern (the one Form VII verb) was offered as **my own reading**, and it was recorded that it may have to do with the verse-endings.
- **سُجِّرَتْ.** Three views were given in tabular form; **no preference was adopted**, because the word carries both senses and the Qurʾān does not explain.
- **زُوِّجَتْ.** Four views were given; I preferred the first (like with like) and wrote out its ground (al-Ṣāffāt 37/22, al-Wāqiʿa 56/7). **It is not binding.**
- **The calling of the animals to account.** Three views were given; **no preference was adopted.** It was stated that the report of the hornless sheep is found in Muslim; its wording was not quoted verbatim.
- **How widespread burying alive was.** Exaggeration in both directions was avoided, and why each exaggeration is indefensible was written out separately. The tribal name (Tamīm) was given with the note that it occurs in the reports, and not as a settled finding.
- **The derivational link between ذَنْب and ذَنَب (tail).** It was ascribed to some of the lexicographers, and the qualification "it is not undisputed" was entered.
- **The سُئِلَتْ / سَأَلَتْ variant reading.** The two readings were given in tabular form; **no imam's name was given.**
- **The ضَنِين / ظَنِين variant reading.** The two readings were given in tabular form and it was shown that each has a ground in the context. I stated my own preference (*ضَنِين*) with its ground; **it is not binding**, and the ground of the other reading was written out plainly as well. **No imam's name was given.**
- **What الخُنَّس / الكُنَّس are.** Three views were given in tabular form; **no preference was adopted.** The attraction of the planetary interpretation was recorded, but the claim that "the verse announces orbital motion" was refused outright and the ground was written out.
- **The direction of عَسْعَسَ.** It was stated that it is one of the *aḍdād*; I preferred the reading "when it withdraws" and wrote out its ground (that it falls at the same moment as verse 18). **It is not binding.** The "thinning" solution was offered as the explanation I find strongest, not as a transmitted report.
- **Whom the event of seeing in al-Najm 53/5-8 points to.** The existence of the dispute was recorded and it was stated that the dispute belongs to Sūrat al-Najm; it was not settled here.
- **That the رَسُول is the angel.** It was grounded in the context and compared with al-Ḥāqqa 69/40; but it was stated plainly that this is an **inference**, and that the verse gives no name.
- **The Prophet's being called al-Amīn.** It was given with the qualification "it is amply present in the sources, it does not rest on a single report".
- **The theological interpretation of verse 29.** Three readings were given in tabular form; **no side was taken.** Reference was made to the frame at al-Baqara 2/7 and it was not repeated. The observation about the verse's placing was offered as **my own reading**, with the statement that it is not a theological preference.
- **My own inferences.** The following are my reading, are not transmitted reports, and are not binding: the interpretation of the subject's being hidden throughout the sūra and disclosed at the end; the central image of "what closes and what opens"; the crossing of the domestic animal being abandoned and the wild animal being gathered; the reading of the order between the gathering of the animals and the questioning of the girl; the girl's question coming before the opening of the registers; the link between the *j-n-n* root of *janna* and the series of openings; the return of the root between *nafs* and *tanaffus*; the interpretation of the position of verses 17-18 in the middle of the sūra; the link of verses 24-25 with al-Kahf 18/22; the link of the question of verse 26 with the sūra's first half; the parallel between *ṣāḥibukum* and ʿAbasa 80/36; the closing link with al-Fātiḥa; and the whole of the section "What this says to the present". These distinctions were stated within the text.
