# 112. Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ

Four verses, fifteen words. One of the shortest sūras in the Qurʾān and probably its most memorised text. There is no command in it, no prohibition, no story, no promise and no threat. From beginning to end the sūra does one thing: **it describes what God is by saying what He is not.**

Three of the four verses are negative sentences. This is not chance but the method of the sūra; we shall return to it in the sections that follow.

### The name of the sūra

The word *ikhlāṣ* does not occur in the text of the sūra. This is a common situation in the Qurʾān — the word *fātiḥa* does not occur in al-Fātiḥa either. Sūra names are most often taken from a prominent word inside the text; here the source of the name is not a word but the subject.

Root **kh-l-ṣ**: a thing's being cleared of what is mixed into it, its becoming pure. *Khāliṣ* milk is milk with no water added to it; *khulāṣa* is the essence of a thing, the part separated from its sediment. **Ikhlāṣ**, derived from this, means making a work unmixed. The Qurʾān uses this root in the context of service: *"mukhliṣīna lahu'l-dīn"* — reserving religion for Him alone (al-Bayyina 98/5; also al-Zumar 39/2, 11, 14).

Two grounds are given for the sūra's bearing this name, and both are reasonable: the sūra expresses *tawḥīd* **in a pure state**, mixed with no addition; and the sūra **clears** its reader of the mixtures in his conception of God. The name thus states both the quality of the text and the work the text does in its reader.

The sūra has other names in the tradition as well: *al-Tawḥīd*, *Qul huwa'llāhu aḥad*, *al-Asās*. These have been in use from the earliest centuries, but the one that became most widespread is al-Ikhlāṣ.

### Meccan or Medinan

The majority say it is Meccan; there is also a view that it is Medinan, and that view rests on some of the reports about the occasion of revelation that we shall take up below. Content and style — short verse-endings, direct address, a focus on the core of belief — fit the general character of the Meccan sūras. I do not issue a definite ruling; this distinction does not change how the sūra is understood.

## 112/1 — قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ

*Qul huwa'llāhu aḥad*
"Say: He is God, *aḥad* (one, unique)."

### قُلْ — "Say"

The first word of the sūra is an imperative, and this imperative is directed to the Prophet. Before it begins to expound what it describes, the text **declares its own source.**

To see what this means, imagine a version with the word *qul* taken out: *"He is God, aḥad."* In meaning almost the same. But now it is unclear who is saying this sentence. It could be a human being's finding about God, it could be a philosopher's conclusion, it could be a poet's inspiration.

*Qul* closes that indeterminacy. The sentence becomes: *I am saying this to you; you in turn say it to them.* The word, that is, is not the Prophet's own word; it is a word given to him, which he has been charged with passing on. That the imperative itself has been left inside the text is further worth noticing — *qul* could have been deleted and only its content transmitted. It was not deleted. **The revelation has carved into the text itself the fact that it is a word being passed on.**

This is particularly meaningful in the place where *tawḥīd* is stated most densely. A human being cannot on his own describe what sort of God God is; the description too must have its source in Him. Where a person begins to speak about God of his own accord, what he describes sooner or later begins to resemble himself. The first word of the sūra closes this door.

**The sūras that begin with "Qul".** Five sūras in the Qurʾān open with this imperative: al-Jinn (72), al-Kāfirūn (109), al-Ikhlāṣ (112), al-Falaq (113), al-Nās (114). The last three are consecutive and are named together in the tradition. What these sūras have in common is that each of them **states a position**: in al-Kāfirūn the boundary of belief is drawn, in al-Ikhlāṣ the one believed in is described, in al-Falaq and al-Nās the taking of refuge is declared. They are texts not of pronouncement but of avowal; and that the one avowing did not take his words from himself is marked from the outset by "qul".

The word itself occurs in hundreds of places in the Qurʾān. Most of these are an answer to a question or an objection: "They ask you about such and such; say…" The prevalence of this pattern shows that the Qurʾān came down largely inside a living conversation.

### هُوَ — "He"

One of the most abstract words in Arabic: the third person singular pronoun. It carries neither an attribute nor a description. It only points.

The grammarians divide in two over the function of this pronoun here, and both readings are meaningful:

**The first reading — the pronoun refers back to a question.** The addressees have asked something about God or have made some claim; *huwa* points to that subject in their minds. The sentence is read thus: *"That one you are asking about — He is God, aḥad."* This reading positions the sūra as an answer.

**The second reading — *ḍamīr al-shaʾn* (the pronoun of the matter).** In Arabic a pronoun may stand at the head of a sentence that refers to nothing at all and is placed there only to magnify the sentence that follows it. Its approximate equivalent in English is this: *"The matter is this: God is aḥad."* Here the pronoun works as a door; it announces in advance the weight of the sentence that comes after it.

Both readings have been defended in the classical commentaries. The first ties the sūra to history, the second exalts the sentence. That the text can carry both at once is not a vagueness but a gain.

This too must be noted: for the servant, the one instrument of language that can point directly at the Essence is the pronoun, because the pronoun loads on no quality. The sūra begins to describe by pointing at what cannot be described.

### ٱللَّه — Allāh

The detailed analysis of this name was carried out in the section on al-Fātiḥa; here let us note only the weight it carries in this context.

This name is the first filling of the sentence opened with *huwa*. What the pronoun points to is met first by a **proper name**: He — is God. The attributes will come after that. The sequence establishes this order: first who He is, then how He is.

That Arabic does not permit this name to be made plural, to be made indefinite ("a god"), or to have the article at its head stripped off — this is a singularity registered in the language itself. The rest of the sūra turns that registration of the language into an explicit declaration.

### أَحَد — aḥad

The key word of the sūra, and the most debated word in it.

**The root.** *Aḥad* comes originally from the root **w-ḥ-d**; the *wāw* at its head turned in time into a *hamza* (from the same root: *wāḥid*, *waḥda*, *tawḥīd*, *ittiḥād*). *Aḥad* and *wāḥid* are thus kin — but in Arabic kindred words are not the same word, and the difference here carries the whole weight of the sūra.

**The difference between *aḥad* and *wāḥid*.** The distinctions dwelt on by the classical lexicographers and the exegetes may be gathered as follows:

| | **wāḥid (واحد)** | **aḥad (أحد)** |
|---|---|---|
| Place in number | The beginning of the number series; two and three follow after it | Does not enter the number series; a second to it cannot be conceived |
| Division | It has no partner in its kind, but its essence may be conceived as divisible into parts | It has neither a partner in kind nor a part in its essence |
| Use of it for others | Used freely of people and objects: *rajulun wāḥid* (one man) | Cannot be used absolutely and as an attribute |
| Its plural | Can be made plural (*āḥād*, *wuḥdān*) | When used of God it has no plural |
| Typical context | Affirmative sentences | Negative, interrogative and conditional sentences |

The most critical row is the last. In Arabic *aḥad* is overwhelmingly used **in a negative context**, and there it means "no one, anyone at all": *"mā jāʾanī aḥad"* — no one came to me. Examples from the Qurʾān: *"We make no distinction between any of His messengers"* (al-Baqara 2/285); *"Not one of you could have held Us off from him"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/47). Where it occurs in an affirmative sentence it is almost always inside a construct: *"one of the two said"* (Yūsuf 12/36), *"you are not like any one of the women"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/32).

This is why the use in this verse is an exception in the language. Here *aḥad* comes in an affirmative sentence, without a construct, directly as the predicate. To an Arab ear this is unaccustomed — and because it is unaccustomed it draws attention. The negative resonance the word carries in its sense of "no one" also stays inside the sentence: to say *He is aḥad* comes close at the same time to saying *there is no one who could be set beside Him*.

**Why was it not said "Allāhu wāḥid"?** It could have been, and the Qurʾān says something of the kind elsewhere: *"Your God is one God"* (al-Baqara 2/163), *"Are separate lords better, or God, the One, the Overwhelming?"* (Yūsuf 12/39), *"He is the One, the Overwhelming"* (al-Raʿd 13/16; also Ṣād 38/65, al-Zumar 39/4).

But *wāḥid* expresses a numerical oneness, and numerical oneness is always open to a comparison: to say "one of them" keeps alive, at the level of language, the possibility "there could have been two". A tribe could say "we have one lord", and this did not deny the neighbouring tribe its own lord. In the Arabia of the pre-Islamic period (*jāhiliyya*) it was exactly so.

*Aḥad* closes off that possibility. *Aḥad* does not say "there is one of Him"; it says **"He is the one beside whom no other can be conceived"**. It is the expression not of number but of uniqueness. And the sense of indivisibility is the second half of the matter: what is *aḥad* has no part, no kind, no instance; nothing can be taken out of it.

**The word's coming indefinite.** In the verse *aḥad* comes without the article "al-" at its head, with tanwīn. Normally indefiniteness conveys indeterminacy; but in some places in Arabic the tanwīn conveys **exaltation** (*taʿẓīm*) — the word is left indefinite in order to show that its scope is too great to be stated. In the exegetical tradition the tanwīn in this verse has mostly been understood in this way. Besides, *aḥad* is already unique in itself by its very meaning; it has no need of the article of definition.

That *al-Ṣamad* in the next verse comes with the article makes this difference more visible; we shall return to it when we get there.

**The sūra opens with *aḥad* and closes with *aḥad*.** The word occurs in verses 1 and 4. But the two occurrences are not identical: the first is in an affirmative sentence and is an attribute of God; the second is in a negative sentence and means "no one". The sūra thus closes by using the same word once **of Him** and once **of anything that might be set over against Him**. This is a very tight frame to have been built in a text of four verses.

### Word order

There are several possibilities in the grammatical analysis of the sentence, and they differentiate the meaning somewhat:

- *huwa* is the subject, *Allāhu* the first predicate, *aḥad* the second predicate → "He is God; He is aḥad." Two independent statements.
- *huwa* is the subject, *Allāhu* an appositive substitute (explanatory), *aḥad* the predicate → "He — that is, God — is aḥad." A single statement; the name Allāh explains the pronoun.
- *huwa* is the pronoun of the matter, *Allāhu aḥad* an independent nominal sentence → "The matter is this: God is aḥad."

All three have been defended in the classical sources. In practice the meaning is close; the difference lies in whether the emphasis falls on the name *Allāh* or on the attribute *aḥad*.

One further point to note: the sentence is a **nominal sentence**; there is no verb. The same choice we saw in the first verse of al-Fātiḥa holds here too — a nominal sentence conveys fixity and continuity. Not "God became aḥad" or "God becomes aḥad"; He is so, just so and always. Being *aḥad* is not an event but a state.

## 112/2 — ٱللَّهُ ٱلصَّمَدُ

*Allāhu'ṣ-Ṣamad*
"God is *al-Ṣamad* (the one who needs nothing, to whom everything turns)."

### Two points: no connective, and no pronoun either

**There is no connective particle** at the head of this verse. After the preceding verse it could have said "wa Allāhu'ṣ-Ṣamad"; it does not. In Arabic the dropping of the connective between two sentences (*faṣl*) shows that the sentences stand in a relation too tight for them to be joined — the second is the explanation of the first, its completion or its answer. "Allāhu'ṣ-Ṣamad", then, is not a second piece of information set alongside "Allāhu aḥad"; it is its unfolding.

By contrast verses 3 and 4 are **joined by *wāw*** to one another and to what comes before. In four verses the sūra uses two different modes of connection: the first two verses interlock, the last two are set in sequence.

The second point: the verse does not say "huwa'ṣ-Ṣamad" (He is al-Ṣamad); it **repeats the name Allāh.** In Arabic the repetition of the name where a pronoun would have served conveys exaltation and fixing. Moreover, both verses beginning with the same name establishes a parallel: *Allāhu aḥad / Allāhu'ṣ-Ṣamad.* Two attributes are predicated separately of the same subject.

### ٱلصَّمَد — al-Ṣamad

This word **occurs only here in the Qurʾān.** A hapax legomenon — a word with a single occurrence. While almost all the names the Qurʾān uses of God (al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm, al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm, al-Ghanī, al-ʿAlīm…) are repeated many times over, this name occurs once, here, in the middle of a sūra of four verses, and never again.

This solitude gives the word a weight of its own, but it also creates a difficulty: there is no second usage inside the Qurʾān to explain the word. Its meaning has therefore had to be drawn out from the use of the root in Arabic and from the explanations transmitted from the first generations. This is why the number of views transmitted about this word in the classical commentaries is unusually large.

**The concrete senses of the root.** The root *ṣ-m-d* is used in Arabic in two main directions:

**1. To turn towards, to make for, to have recourse to someone.** *Ṣamada ilayhi* — he turned to him, he made for him, he went to him for his need. Hence **al-ṣamad**: the one who is turned to, the one resorted to for needs. Among the Arabs the chief of the tribe, the lord to whom one went and to whom troubles were told, was called *ṣamad* — because in their needs people *turned to him*.

**2. Without hollow, full within, solid.** A thing's having no cavity inside it, its being hard and full. A *ṣamad* rock is a rock that contains no crack and no hole. The explanation that comes from this: **al-ṣamad** is that which has no hollow inside it; that is, which harbours no lack, no defect, no need.

These two senses are not cut off from one another, and the place where they join is the heart of the sūra: **because there is no hollow in Him, all hollows turn towards Him.** Not needy, but needed. A relation in one direction is being described — every arrow towards Him, none outward from Him.

**The explanations transmitted in the classical commentaries.** Among the views gathered by al-Ṭabarī and the exegetes after him, these stand out:

| Explanation | Its emphasis |
|---|---|
| The lord who is resorted to in needs (*al-sayyid alladhī yuṣmadu ilayhi fi'l-ḥawāʾij*) | The "turning" sense of the root; the social and concrete usage |
| The one who has no hollow within (*alladhī lā jawfa lah*) | The "fullness" sense of the root; the denial of bodily attributes |
| Who does not eat and does not drink | The concrete unfolding of the above |
| Everlasting; not perishing, without end | Permanence |
| In need of nothing, while everything is in need of Him | A general summary |

These views transmitted from the first generations are less alternatives to one another than different faces of the same word. The majority of the classical exegetes took the sense "the one turned to in needs" as the basis in point of language, and recorded the explanation "the one with no hollow within" as the other wing of the root.

The expression "having no hollow within" may sound strange to a present-day ear; it did not to the first addressees. In the conception of the gods held by the Arab of the pre-Islamic period, the gods ate, drank, slept and had children. For a being to "have an inside" meant that it was a body, and therefore that it ate and drank, was depleted and filled. This explanation denies corporeality by way of the concrete sense of the word.

**The article of definiteness.** *Al-Ṣamad* comes with the article "al-". In Arabic two definite elements set over against one another (*Allāhu* — *al-Ṣamadu*) convey **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*): He alone is al-Ṣamad, and no other. The lord of a tribe could be called *ṣamad*; but *al-Ṣamad* — the true and absolute one — is God alone.

That *aḥad* comes without the article in the preceding verse and *al-Ṣamad* with it here is not an inconsistency but a division of labour: *aḥad* already carries uniqueness within itself and needs no article of definition; *ṣamad*, on the other hand, is a word that can be used of others too, and is therefore delimited by the article. This is not my own inference but a distinction treated in the classical grammatical analyses.

### The Qurʾān explained by the Qurʾān

Though the word has a single occurrence, its meaning is treated in other places in the Qurʾān with other words:

- *"O people! You are the ones in need of God; and God is al-Ghanī, al-Ḥamīd"* (Fāṭir 35/15). The needlessness in *ṣamad* is met here by the word *ghanī* (rich, in need of nothing).
- *"He feeds and is not fed"* (al-Anʿām 6/14). The most concrete expression of the one-directional relation.
- *"Praise be to God, who has taken no child, who has no partner in dominion, and who needs no friend out of incapacity"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/111). This verse is very close in structure to the three negative sentences of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ.

The expression *"a friend out of incapacity"* in the last example is important: taking a helper, taking a partner, continuing a line — all are signs of deficiency. In denying these of God, the sūra is in fact denying deficiency.

### Context — the link between aḥad and ṣamad

The two attributes complete one another, and their order is meaningful.

*Aḥad* says what God is **in Himself**: indivisible, unique, without parts. *Ṣamad* says what God is **in His relation to everything**: not needy, but needed.

This order is not accidental but a logical necessity. A being can make good another's lack only if it is itself without lack. What must itself turn somewhere cannot be the one turned to. The second verse is the consequence of the first — and the dropping of the connective shows this too.

And this pair prepares the two verses that follow in advance. A being that is not needy has no need of a child (verse 3), and it is impossible that an equal to Him should be found (verse 4).

## 112/3 — لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ

*Lam yalid wa lam yūlad*
"He has not begotten, and He has not been begotten."

### The words

Both verbs are from the root **w-l-d**: to beget, to be born. From the same root: *walad* (child), *wālid* (father), *wālida* (mother), *mawlid* (the place or time of birth), *awlād*.

The difference lies in the voice: **يَلِد** is active (he begets), **يُولَد** is passive (he is begotten, he is born). One root, two directions. English covers the two ends of this root with two unconnected everyday expressions — *give birth* and *be born*; the symmetry survives only with the older verb *beget*, which carries a passive of its own, and that is the rendering used above. In the original the two verbs are all but the same word; only their direction is reversed.

### لَمْ — the choice of the particle of negation

There are several ways of negating the past in Arabic. *Mā* + the perfect verb ("mā walada") could have been used. Instead the construction **lam + the imperfect** has been chosen.

The property of this construction is as follows: in form it is a present or general-tense verb, but its meaning has been turned towards the past; and the negation is **definite and comprehensive** — it has at no time taken place. *Mā walada*, in saying "he did not beget", is closer to denying a particular event; *lam yalid* declares that the act has never occurred at all, from the beginning.

Furthermore the negation built with *lam*, while it negates the past, leaves no possibility open with regard to the future either — because the act is being denied as a kind, not as a single instance.

### The meaning of the order

Why "He has not begotten" first and "He has not been begotten" second?

The logical order would be the reverse: a being is first born, then begets. The verse breaks this order, and the reason is **the priority of the one addressed.**

The first addressees were not claiming that God had been born. The mushrikūn of Mecca accepted God as creator and as the one who brought the heavens and the earth into being (the Qurʾān records this in several places: al-ʿAnkabūt 29/61-63, al-Zumar 39/3). Their claim was that God **had children**: the angels are His daughters. The Qurʾān brings this belief up many times and refuses it: *"The daughters are His and the sons yours? That is an unfair division"* (al-Najm 53/21-22); also al-Naḥl 16/57, al-Ṣāffāt 37/149-153, al-Zukhruf 43/16-19.

The verse, that is, first denies what the one addressed actually said, and then closes off the logical extension of that claim. The sequence is the order of the polemic, not the order of cosmology.

But the second clause is not merely a completion. The expression "He has not been begotten" closes off every road by which the defenders of the claim might escape. For if a being can have a child, that being is itself part of a chain of generation; whoever accepts one end of the chain cannot deny the other. The sūra cuts **both directions at once** of the idea of generation.

### Why begetting is denied

The act of begetting carries a series of deficiencies in its background:

- **Division.** The one who begets gives a part of himself. But verse 1 had said *aḥad*: that which is not divided. Begetting contradicts being *aḥad* outright.
- **Sex and a consort.** Begetting requires two parties. The Qurʾān says this plainly: *"How should He have a child when He has no consort?"* (al-Anʿām 6/101). And in the words of the jinn: *"Exalted is the majesty of our Lord; He has taken neither consort nor child"* (al-Jinn 72/3).
- **The search for continuance.** Why do living things reproduce? Because they die. Reproduction is the remedy the species has found against the perishing of the individual. What is deathless has no need of reproduction — this follows directly from the attribute *ṣamad* (needing nothing) in verse 2.
- **Producing a like.** What is born resembles the one who begot it; it is of the same kind. But verse 4 will say that He has no equal. To have a child is to produce a second being of one's own kind.

Not having been begotten closes another door: **beginninglessness.** A being that is born does not exist before it is born; it takes its existence from another; it is the effect of a cause. Not to have been begotten is to stand outside the chain of causation. In the tradition of *kalām* this has been held to be the expression of God's being *qadīm* (without beginning).

### The addressees — who is being refuted

There is not one single group facing this verse. In the region and the period in which it came down, the idea of "the child of a god" was widespread in different forms:

**The mushrikūn of Mecca.** They counted the angels as God's daughters; al-Lāt, Manāt and al-ʿUzzā were named within this frame as well (al-Najm 53/19-23). The function of this belief matters: the daughters are intermediaries, intercessors. God is distant, unreachable; one draws near to Him by way of His daughters. The Qurʾān records this logic too: *"We serve them only so that they may bring us nearer to God"* (al-Zumar 39/3). The claim of descent, that is, is the foundation of the institution of mediation. To cut the descent is to cut the mediation as well.

**The Christian Trinity.** In the period in which the Qurʾān came down there were Christian communities in the Arabian Peninsula — at Najrān, among the Ghassānids in the north, and by way of relations with Abyssinia. The concept of "son" stood at the centre of that theology. The Qurʾān takes up this belief separately and by name: *"Those who say that God is the third of three have disbelieved"* (al-Māʾida 5/73); *"They said, God has taken a child"* (Maryam 19/88 and what follows, where the gravity of that saying is set out in extraordinary language). And al-Nisāʾ 4/171 addresses the Christians directly and says "do not say three".

Note: Christian theology does not use "begetting" in a biological sense; in the classical formulations "eternal generation" is not a bodily reproduction. Whether what Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ denies coincides one-to-one with the internal self-definition of Christian belief is a matter for the debates of *kalām* and of the history of religions. What the verse denies with certainty is the establishing of **a bond of the order of descent** between God and any other being — by whatever fine definition it may be established.

**A saying ascribed to the Jews.** In al-Tawba 9/30 the Qurʾān records that a section of people said "ʿUzayr is the son of God". The historical scope of this expression — which community, how widely — is disputed; and the Qurʾān itself transmits the sentence without generalising it, saying "they said".

**The general Near Eastern and Mediterranean background.** In the world into which the sūra came down, that gods should have a line of descent was not the exception but the rule: families of gods in the Greek and Roman pantheons, triads of gods in Egypt, dynasties of gods in Mesopotamia. "The child of a god" was not a deviation but part of the standard grammar of the religious language of the period. Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ speaks against the whole of that grammar.

### The verse-ending

All four verses of the sūra end in the sound **d**: *aḥad — ṣamad — yūlad — aḥad*. This is one of the examples of the dense weave of sound in the short Meccan sūras. The unity of sound has a further meaningful side here: four different statements are sealed with the same sound.

## 112/4 — وَلَمْ يَكُن لَّهُۥ كُفُوًا أَحَدٌۢ

*Wa lam yakun lahū kufuwan aḥad*
"And there has never been anyone equal to Him."

### كُفُو — kufuw

Root **k-f-ʾ** (kāf-fāʾ-hamza): to be equal, to be a match, to be the counterpart of. This root too occurs **only in this verse** in the Qurʾān — the sūra holds two words of single occurrence in two of its verses.

*Kufuw* (or *kifʾ*, *kafīʾ*) means a thing's being on the same level as another, of the same weight, in a position to correspond to it. Like the two pans of a balance coming level. The word is not the same thing as "like" (*mithl*): likeness is a sharing of qualities, whereas *kufuw* is **equivalence and the capacity to correspond**.

**Its use in marriage.** The most widespread concrete use of the word in Arabic is in the context of marriage. From the same root, **kafāʾa** means the equality of the two parties to a marriage with one another — by such measures as descent, religion, occupation and property. It is treated as a separate topic in the books of jurisprudence; the schools of law have adopted differing views on which criteria *kafāʾa* covers and how far it is binding. I transmit no ruling here; I touch on it in order to show the place of the word in the language.

This concrete background sets the tone of the verse. *Kufuw* is a legal and social word: the equality required for two parties to be able to come together. By choosing this word the verse says: **there is nothing that could be set over against God, nothing that could be seated in the same pan of the balance with Him.** Nothing that could be His match, nothing that could enter into partnership with Him, nothing that could be compared with Him.

Differences among the authorities of the variant readings are transmitted in the pronunciation of the word (*kufuwan*, *kufwan*, a reading with hamza). These differences do not change the meaning; they are only a matter of articulation.

### Word order — what the sequence of the words does

The grammatical structure of the verse is this: *lam yakun* (there has not been) — *lahū* (to Him) — *kufuwan* (as an equal) — *aḥad* (anyone).

The English rendering gives no sense of how out of the ordinary this arrangement is. The expected word order in Arabic would be: *wa lam yakun aḥadun kufuwan lahū* — "no one has been an equal to Him." That is: first the subject (*aḥad*), then the complement of the predicate (*kufuwan*), then the prepositional phrase (*lahū*).

The verse breaks this order twice:

**1. *Lahū* ("to Him") has been brought right to the front.** In Arabic an element pulled forward from its normal place gains emphasis and restriction. The centre of gravity of the sentence becomes not "there is no one" but "there is no such thing as being an equal **to Him**". What is denied is not the impossibility of equality in general, but specifically the impossibility of establishing equality with Him.

**2. *Kufuwan* has moved in front of the subject.** The concept of "equality" is thereby denied before it is said who might hold it. The sentence runs to this rhythm: *there has not been — to Him — an equal — anyone.* The idea of equality is put out of action at the outset, and then every kind of subject that might fill it is erased.

This word order also establishes the **verse-ending**: because *aḥad* has moved to the end, the verse closes with the same word as the first verse of the sūra. Sound and meaning close at the same moment.

### The function of the verse — closing the doors

Verse 3 had denied the bond of descent. But descent is not the only way of establishing equality. A being may also be made a partner to God in these ways:

- As a partner in creating (the idea of two creators)
- As a partner in governing (a god of one domain, another god of another domain)
- As an opposing power (the duality of a god of good and a god of evil; in the period in which the Qurʾān came down this duality was alive in the religious conceptions current in Iran)
- As a being equal in attributes (at the philosophical level, a second necessary being)

Verse 4 closes all of these at once, because the concept of *kufuw* is wide enough to cover every kind of equality. Where verse 3 denies a particular claim, verse 4 draws **the frame of the denial**.

The Qurʾān expresses this principle elsewhere too: *"There is nothing like Him"* (al-Shūrā 42/11). And it gives the cosmological consequence of the principle thus: *"Had there been gods in the two other than God, the order of both would have broken down"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/22).

## The sūra as a whole — the logical sequence of the four verses

The four verses are not four separate pieces of information heaped on one another but a single course of reasoning advancing step by step:

| Verse | What it says | What it closes off |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Aḥad** | Indivisible, unique, without parts | Multiplicity; being broken into parts; the possibility of having a like |
| **2. Ṣamad** | Not needy, but needed | Deficiency; need; requiring an intermediary |
| **3. Lam yalid wa lam yūlad** | He neither begot nor was begotten | The bond of descent; a beginning; the need for continuance |
| **4. Lam yakun lahū kufuwan aḥad** | He has no equal | All the remaining forms of equality |

The order cannot be reversed. **Were He not aḥad** — that is, were He divisible — a part of Him could be separated off, and that part might not be in need of Him; being *ṣamad* would fall. **Were He not ṣamad** — that is, had He some deficiency — He would need a child, a helper, a partner in order to make that deficiency good; verse 3 would fall. **Had He begotten or been begotten** — He would have a bond with a being of His own kind; the absence of an equal in verse 4 would fall.

The sūra, that is, draws the other three verses out of the first. The first verse is the thesis; the remaining three are its necessary consequences.

One more thing: **the method itself is meaningful.** In describing God the sūra builds a negative sentence in three of its four verses. This is not an accidental stylistic preference. Positive description — "He is such and such" — always works by likening to something known; and likening has been forbidden here from the outset. The road that remains is **to draw the boundary by saying what He is not like**. This is what the sūra does: it does not draw a picture, it shows that a picture cannot be drawn.

## Its place in the history of kalām

Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ is, in Islamic thought, the densest text of the principle of **tanzīh** — of keeping God clear of resemblance to created things. Together with the expression *"There is nothing like Him"* in al-Shūrā 42/11, it makes up the two basic supports of this principle.

Throughout history this sūra has stood at the centre of several separate debates:

**The question of corporeality.** The sense of indivisibility in "aḥad" and the explanation of "ṣamad" as "having no hollow" were used against understandings that ascribe body, limb and place to God (*tashbīh*). What can be divided is a body; what is *aḥad* is not divided.

**The debate over the attributes.** One of the longest debates in the history of *kalām* is whether God's attributes are realities distinct from His Essence. The Muʿtazila, taking the absolute oneness of the Essence (its being *aḥad*) as their basis, held that the attributes cannot be conceived as distinct from the Essence — if distinct attributes are accepted, a multiplicity arises in the Essence, and that runs counter to being *aḥad*. The theologians of the Ahl al-Sunna, for their part, said that the attributes are neither identical with the Essence nor other than it, and held that accepting attributes does not amount to division. Both sides counted this sūra as the support of their own position. The detail of the debate goes beyond the limits of this text; what I want to note here is that the sūra is the **common ground** of that debate — the sides were divided not over what was true but over what exactly being "aḥad" requires.

**The philosophical interpretation.** The philosophers of Islam attached the concept "aḥad" to the thesis that the Necessary Being is in no way composite (*basāṭa* — simplicity): a being that has parts is in need of its parts; what is in need cannot be necessary. This is a restatement, in philosophical language, of the link the sūra establishes between "ṣamad" and "aḥad".

**Its position over against the Trinity.** For centuries the sūra was the text most often cited in Muslim–Christian theological debate. That this sūra and verses of similar content appear in the inscriptions of the Dome of the Rock, dated to the end of the seventh century, shows that the text was already used in the earliest period as a declaration of theological position.

## Historical background and the occasion of revelation

Several reports are transmitted as the reason for the sūra's descent. The most widespread is this: the mushrikūn came to the Prophet and said *"Tell us the descent of your Lord / make your Lord known to us"* (*insub lanā Rabbaka*), and the sūra came down upon this. In another report those who put the question are the Jews; this second version is one of the supports of the view that the sūra is Medinan.

**One must be honest about these reports.** These accounts transmitted as the occasion of revelation are found in the early sources, but their chains of transmission are disputed in point of hadith methodology; there are versions ascribed to different Companions and to different groups of questioners, and these versions do not fully agree with one another. This does not mean that the reports are fabricated; but neither are they sound enough to allow one to say "the sūra came down definitely upon this event".

By contrast, the **kind of question** the reports describe is entirely realistic. "What is the descent of your Lord" was the standard way of coming to know a god in that world: gods were known by their descent, by their families, by which god they were born from. The question is not a mockery but a legitimate question asked in the religious language of the period. The answer the sūra gives is striking in that it refuses the question itself: the question was put as "what is His descent", and the answer said "there is no such thing as descent".

Whatever the standing of the reports about the occasion of revelation, the content of the sūra already shows its own historical context plainly: it speaks to a world that ascribed children to God, that believed He could be reached by way of intermediaries, that knew its gods by their descent.

## The report of "a third of the Qurʾān"

The best-known hadith about this sūra is that it is counted as equal to a third of the Qurʾān. The report is found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim and comes by several different routes.

In one of them it is reported to the Prophet that a man kept reciting this sūra in his prayer at night; the Prophet says, "It is equal to a third of the Qurʾān." In another the Prophet asks his Companions, "Is one of you unable to recite a third of the Qurʾān in a single night?"; this weighs heavily on them, and then he says, "Qul huwa'llāhu aḥad is a third of the Qurʾān."

In another well-known report on the same matter, a Companion who recited this sūra in every prayer says, when asked the reason, "Because I love it"; the Prophet's answer is: "Your love for it has brought you into the Garden" (in another transmission, "God loves you too").

**What does "a third" mean?** There are two main interpretations of this expression:

**1. In point of subject matter.** The content of the Qurʾān is gathered roughly under three headings: what concerns God's Essence and attributes (*tawḥīd*, the articles of belief), what is to be done and what avoided (the rulings), and the accounts of past communities together with promises and warnings (the stories, the states of the hereafter). Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ is "a third" because it expresses the first of these three headings on its own and without remainder. This division has been widely voiced in the exegetical and Sufi traditions; a similar threefold classification is also found in works ascribed to al-Ghazālī.

**2. In point of reward.** That the one who recites the sūra is given the reward that would come to one who recites a third of the Qurʾān. This is understood as a divine favour.

**What is not meant matters too.** The classical scholars stated plainly that this report does not mean "whoever recites Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ is counted as having recited a third of the Qurʾān and has no need to recite that portion". It has been said within the same frame that one who recites it three times has not thereby completed the Qurʾān. The equivalence is in point of reward and density of content; it is not in the sense of standing in place of the reciting.

The reasonable side of the report is this: this sūra says the most basic claim of the Qurʾān — what sort of God God is — in four verses, leaving nothing out. Everything else is built upon that foundation. As, in a building, the foundation may be counted as "a third of the building".

**Its place in use.** The sūra is very widespread in the daily practice of Muslim society: it is recited regularly in the prayers; together with al-Falaq and al-Nās it is recited in the context of seeking refuge, as the *muʿawwidhāt*; it is one of the first sūras taught. This prevalence comes less from its shortness than from its function — there are other short sūras. This sūra is repeated because it is the summary expression of belief itself.

## What this says to the present

**First: the tendency to make God resemble oneself has not ended, it has changed form.** The sūra was speaking to a society that ascribed descent to God. Today the claim of descent has disappeared; but has the tendency to shape God according to one's own measures disappeared? The sentence "the God I believe in would be like this, and would not do that" is very common, and is most often, without one's noticing it, an attempt at description: making our own measures of justice, mercy and reasonableness absolute and dressing God in them. This is no different an operation from carving an idol out of stone — only the material is the mind. The ruling of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ that "there is no equal" makes precisely this operation impossible: one cannot arrive at Him by setting out from oneself, because the distance between is not a difference of degree but a difference of kind.

**Second: expecting from things that are not ṣamad the treatment due to al-Ṣamad.** *Ṣamad* is the one turned to in need. A human being cannot stay without turning; this is not a choice but a structural necessity. Everyone makes for something — a job, a person, a position, an approval, an institution. What the sūra says is not "do not turn"; it is **"know that the thing you turn to is itself full of hollows"**.

The practical equivalent of this is harshly concrete: something that is itself in need cannot make good your need for good. Waiting for approval from someone who is himself waiting for approval, hoping for security from someone who is insecure, gathering meaning from work that is looking for its own meaning — these collapse not because they are wrong but because **they exceed the capacity**. A considerable part of the disappointments we live through arises from treating what is not *ṣamad* as though it were.

**Third: service adds nothing to God.** The dimension of needlessness in *ṣamad* has a direct consequence: service does not make good any deficiency in God. This does not empty service of meaning; it corrects whose benefit it is for. The direction of service is upward, its benefit downward. This measure also prevents piety from being turned into a kind of creditorship: nothing that is done creates a debt on His side.

**Fourth: no authority can become absolute.** The ruling "He has no equal" has a political side, and it comes out at the same place as the expression "the Lord of all the worlds" in al-Fātiḥa. If there is a single authority that is absolute, then all the remaining authorities — the state, tradition, the leader, the institution, the majority, the expert, the algorithm — are by definition qualified, open to question, limited. While *tawḥīd* says something upward, it says something downward as well: no human authority stands above criticism, because none of them is an equal.

**Fifth: undividedness.** *Aḥad* is an attribute of God; it cannot be given to the servant as an attribute. But a life turned towards the one who is *aḥad* is expected to gain a wholeness within itself as well. What the Qurʾān calls *ikhlāṣ* is exactly this: an intention that has not been broken into pieces. For the same person to live by different measures in different settings within the same day — one measure at work, another at home, another in worship — is a divided life. The name of the sūra carries this link between what it describes and what it expects of its reader: that the one who comes to know the unmixed should himself become unmixed.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

At several points across the text a definite language was deliberately not used; the reader needs to know them:

- Whether the sūra is Meccan or Medinan is disputed; no preference is adopted.
- The chains of transmission of the reports transmitted as the occasion of revelation are disputed. The reports were transmitted, but it was not said that "the sūra came down upon this".
- The multiplicity of views about the meaning of the word *ṣamad* arises from the word's having a single occurrence in the Qurʾān. The views were given in a table; none of them was presented as certainly correct.
- The interpretations of the meaning of the expression "a third of the Qurʾān" are interpretations — the hadith itself contains no explanation.
- In summarising the debates in the history of *kalām*, no detailed view was ascribed to either side; general positions were transmitted.
