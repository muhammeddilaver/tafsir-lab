# 83. Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn

Thirty-six verses. It takes its name from the word in the first verse: **المطففين** — those who give a little short in measure and in weight.

This sūra opens with a crime, and what is striking about that crime is not its size but its **smallness**.

*Taṭfīf* is not theft. It is not seizure, it is not fraud, it is not taking anyone's property from his hand. The root of the word, **طفف**, names in Arabic the **small quantity** left at the rim of a vessel that has not been filled to the brim. *Taṭfīf* is the shaving of a sliver off the edge of the measure — an amount too small to be noticed, too small to be objected to, too trivial for it to be anything but embarrassing to raise.

And the sūra answers this act with one of the heaviest words in the Qurʾān: **ويل** — *wayl*.

This disproportion is the door into the sūra. But it is also open to a misunderstanding. The sūra is not saying "small offences are punished too"; that would be an ordinary warning. What it says is sharper, and it is repeated in three separate places across the whole sūra:

> **What is small goes unseen because it stays small; because it goes unseen it is repeated; because it is repeated it accumulates; and once it has accumulated it is no longer small.**

The sūra shows this mechanism in three separate fields, and in the three it does not use the same word, but it builds the same structure:

1. **In the measure** (1-3): the small quantity shaved off the edge.
2. **In the heart** (14): the thin layer that coats it over — *rān*, that is, rust. Rust too does not form in a single go.
3. **In seeing** (15): the veil that comes between. A veil is a thin thing; but it cuts off entirely what is behind it.

And there is a fourth, this time in the opposite direction. The fourth verse states the minimum asked of these people: *"Do they not **suppose** that they will be raised?"* What is asked is not faith, not certain knowledge — only a **supposition**. The sūra sets the smallest measure of belief opposite the smallest of frauds.

## The skeleton of the sūra

1. **The crime** (1-3): *wayl*, and the description of the crime — the same person using two different measures.
2. **The question** (4-6): do these people never consider that they will be raised?
3. **The book of the fujjār** (7-9): *sijjīn*, and "what will make you know".
4. **The deniers** (10-13): who denies, why he denies, what he says.
5. **The cause** (14): it has coated over their hearts.
6. **The punishment** (15-17): the veil, the fire, and the word.
7. **The book of the abrār** (18-21): *ʿilliyyūn*, and the witnesses.
8. **The state of the abrār** (22-28): couches, gazing, the freshness on the faces, the sealed drink — and a command.
9. **Those who mocked** (29-33): those who laughed in this world.
10. **The turning of the roles** (34-36): those who laugh in the hereafter.

The structure of the sūra **divides in two twice over**: first the fujjār and the abrār (7-28), then the criminals and the believers (29-36). The function of the second division is to put the first on a stage.

## The name of the sūra

*Al-Muṭaffifīn* is the word in the first verse. The sūra is also called *al-Taṭfīf* in the sources.

The word **occurs only here in the Qurʾān.** This is a decisive datum for the exegesis of the sūra: there is no possibility of comparing within the Qurʾān itself for the meaning of the word. The same held for the word that gives Sūrat al-Māʿūn its name, and it was recorded there.

## Meccan or Medinan?

Among the short sūras, this is one of the most disputed in the sources as to its place of revelation.

| View | Ground | Weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **Meccan** | The style of the sūra is the Meccan style: short verses, sharp rhyme, the depiction of the resurrection, an opening with *wayl*, the repetition of *kallā*, a scene of the hereafter. The last section (29-33) describes believers being mocked in Mecca, which is exactly the Meccan picture | The regulation of measure and weight calls to mind a settled environment of commercial law |
| **Medinan** | It is transmitted in the reports that when the Prophet came to Medina the people of the city were the worst of any community in the matter of measure, and that they mended their ways when this sūra came down | The soundness of the reports, and whether they cover the whole sūra or only its first verses, is disputed |
| **The first verses Medinan, the rest Meccan** | The style and the addressee of the two sections appear different | This is an inference made from the content of the text; the claim that a sūra came down piece by piece requires separate evidence |

**About the report.** Transmissions to the effect that the matter of measure came up on the arrival at Medina, and that the situation was mended after this sūra came down, are found in the sources. **I cannot give the wording and the source of this report with certainty**; I confine myself to recording that it is widely transmitted.

**My own judgement** — and it is not binding — is this: the last section of the sūra (29-36) describes an environment in which believers are mocked, in which believers are in the minority and without power. That is the Meccan picture. As for the first verses, nothing certain can be said about which city they came down in; cheating in the measure existed in Mecca as in Medina, because both were trading cities.

And this preference does not change the understanding of the sūra: **what is described is not a city but a behaviour.**

The analysis of the basmala was made in the section on al-Fātiḥa; it is not repeated here. The verse numbers are counted without the basmala.

## 83/1 — وَيْلٌ لِّلْمُطَفِّفِينَ

*Waylun li'l-muṭaffifīn*
"Woe to those who give a little short in measure and in weight!"

### وَيْل — *wayl*

The analysis of the word was made in the sections on al-Māʿūn and al-Humaza. In brief: the root is **و-ي-ل**; it is a word conveying destruction, torment, violent ruin; rendering it "alas" is too weak, because *wayl* conveys not pity but a **verdict**. Its opposite is *ṭūbā*. The widespread saying that it is a valley in Hell was met with caution, because the soundness of the report it rests on is disputed.

An observation was also recorded there, and it is exactly confirmed here:

> "If you see *wayl* at the head of a verse, what stands before you is most often either denial or **the diminishing of another's right**."

When that observation was made in the section on al-Māʿūn, this very verse was given as the example. Now it is in its own place: *wayl*, to those who shave off the measure.

**And the grammatical point was given there too:** *waylun* is indefinite, it is the subject at the head of the sentence, and the reason a sentence is permitted to begin with an indefinite is that the sentence carries the sense of a prayer or an imprecation. The indefiniteness is for **magnification** (*tafkhīm*): "a *wayl* beyond describing."

The only thing to be added here is that *wayl* occurs **twice** in this sūra: in verse 1 for the *muṭaffifīn*, in verse 10 for the *mukadhdhibīn* (the deniers). The sūra gives the same verdict to two separate groups, and in verse 12 it will say that the two are the same people.

### ٱلْمُطَفِّفِين — the lifeline of the sūra

**Root: ط-ف-ف.** And the concrete sense of this root carries the whole sūra.

**الطَّفّ (ṭaff)** — the **edge** of a thing, its brink, its boundary. The edge of a valley, the bank of a river, is called *ṭaff*.

**طُفَافَة / طَفَف** — the **empty space left at the rim** of a vessel not filled to the brim; or, conversely, the small quantity that spills over the rim. *Ināʾun ṭaffān* — a vessel not filled to the top.

**الطَّفِيف** — a small, trivial quantity, not worth registering.

**أَطَفَّ / طَفَّ** — it drew near, it came to the edge.

**The idea at the centre of the word is this: the immediate neighbourhood of a thing's exact boundary.** Not quite full, not quite empty either — that small difference left at the edge.

And **تَطْفِيف** (*taṭfīf*) is the turning of that difference to one's own advantage.

### The pattern: تفعيل

*Muṭaffif* is the active participle of the **تفعيل (tafʿīl)** form: *ṭaffafa → muṭaffif.*

This form is doing two jobs here:

**One — deriving a verb from a noun.** *Ṭaffafa* means "to do the work of *ṭafīf* (a small quantity)". The pattern produces a verb from a noun. This is common in Arabic: *khayyama* (he pitched a tent, from *khayma*), *qawwasa* (he made a bow, from *qaws*).

**Two — intensification.** As was recorded in the sections on al-Humaza and al-Infiṭār, the *tafʿīl* form conveys the **repetition and the intensity** of the verb.

The two together give the full sense of the word: **one who does the diminishing by a little, over and over.**

This is the force of the first sentence of the sūra. A *muṭaffif* is not a person who cheats once. He is a person who has **made a trade** of shaving off the edge of the measure. What was recorded in the section on al-Humaza for the *fuʿala* pattern holds here too: the pattern conveys that the behaviour has settled into the character.

### The matter of the smallness of the crime

Now we come to the sūra's real idea, and it has to be built step by step.

**First step: the word carries the size of the crime inside itself.**

There were words the Qurʾān could have used in naming these people, and all of them would have been heavier: *sāriq* (thief), *khāʾin* (traitor), *ẓālim*, *ākil amwāli'n-nās* (one who devours people's property). All of these are used in the Qurʾān.

The verse chooses none of them. **It chooses a word that puts the smallness of the amount inside the name.**

That is, the verse is not claiming that the crime is large. It **grants** that the crime is small, and says *wayl* all the same.

**Second step: the smallness does not lighten the crime; it makes the crime possible.**

To take half a kilo out of a kilo of flour is impossible — it is noticed. To take twenty grams is possible, because it is not noticed.

*Taṭfīf*, then, works **below the threshold of notice** of the measure. That is the technical condition of the crime. The smallness of the amount is not a mitigating circumstance; it is **the condition of the act's existing at all.**

This produces a logic different from ordinary theft:

| | **Theft** | **Taṭfīf** |
|---|---|---|
| Being noticed | Noticed | Not noticed |
| Repetition | Limited | Unlimited |
| The victim | Knows | Does not know |
| The perpetrator's self-defence | Hard | Easy: "such a trifle" |
| Total harm | Large at one stroke | Large over time |

**Third step: the victim of a crime that is not noticed cannot object.**

This is a silent side of the sūra. The person shaved off the measure does not know he has been shaved. If he knew he would object, would go to law, would break off the trade. Because he does not know, the relationship continues — and as long as it continues, the shaving continues too.

*Taṭfīf*, then, is a wrong sustained with the victim's **consent**; but that consent rests on ignorance.

In the section on al-Māʿūn the reason for the choosing of the orphan was taken up: the orphan was the person with no one behind him to call to account. Here the property of the chosen victim is different but the result is the same: **a person who does not know what is being done to him cannot call anyone to account.**

And the two sūras arrive at the same conclusion: in such a case the only thing that holds a person back is a belief in an unseen reckoning. The sūra will say this plainly in the fourth and fifth verses.

**Fourth step: this is why the *wayl* is not disproportionate.**

The punishment is not set as the price of a single transaction. It is set as the price of **a way of living**. The *tafʿīl* pattern of the word had already said as much: a behaviour that repeats, that has become a habit.

And there is something deeper: *taṭfīf* **requires a person to deceive himself.** The one who takes twenty grams must be able to form the sentence "I am not a thief." And to form it he must break the link between the amount and the act: the name of the act must change with the amount.

The sūra refuses exactly this breaking. It puts the amount inside the name — *muṭaffif*, one who diminishes by a little — and then issues the heaviest verdict. That is: **the amount does not change the name of the act.**

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān: measure and weight

The Qurʾān takes up the matter of measure in several places, and in all of them the same two words recur: *kayl* (measure of volume) and *wazn* (measure of weight).

**The story of Shuʿayb.** One community in the Qurʾān is named as a people destroyed directly on account of cheating in the measure: the people of Midian. What Shuʿayb said to them is related in three separate sūras:

- *"Give full measure, and be not of those who cause loss. And weigh with a true balance. And do not give people their things short, and do not go about the earth spreading corruption."* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/181-183)
- *"Do not give short in measure and weight. I see you in prosperity, and I fear for you the punishment of an encompassing day."* (Hūd 11/84)
- *"Give full measure and weight in justice; and do not give people their things short."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/85)

**The detail in Hūd 11/84 is worth dwelling on:** *"I see you in prosperity."* That is, those who shaved off the measure were not poor. They were not doing it out of need. This accords with the sūra's diagnosis — the matter is not livelihood but habit.

**The verb that occurs in al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/183 and Hūd 11/85 is a separate word:** **بخس** — *lā tabkhasū.* Root **ب-خ-س**: to take a thing by lowering its value, to give someone less than his due. Its best-known use in the Qurʾān is in the story of Joseph (Yūsuf): *"And they sold him for a paltry price, a few counted dirhams."* (Yūsuf 12/20). *Thamanin bakhsin* — a price driven down.

The difference between *bakhs* and *taṭfīf* is this: *bakhs* is a diminishing made in the price, *taṭfīf* one made in the amount. Both arrive at the same place, but they enter from different sides.

**The balance in Sūrat al-Raḥmān.** This is where the Qurʾān attaches the matter of measure to the highest point:

*"And the heaven He raised, and He set down the balance. So do not overreach in the balance. And establish the weighing in justice, and do not give the balance short."* (al-Raḥmān 55/7-9)

**These three verses are the background of Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn, and they need to be dwelt on.**

The verses name the balance **in the same sentence as the raising of the heaven**. That is, the *mīzān* is set down not as an instrument of trade but as **a part of the order of the cosmos**. And the cheating done in the balance is named *ṭughyān* (overreaching) — the word the Qurʾān uses of Pharaoh, of Thamūd, of insolence.

And the verb in the last verse: **لَا تُخْسِرُوا** — the same root, the same form, as *yukhsirūn* in al-Muṭaffifīn 83/3.

This root was taken up in the section on al-ʿAṣr, and there these two verses exactly — al-Muṭaffifīn 83/3 and al-Raḥmān 55/9 — were shown as evidence for how settled the root is in the commercial and the balance field.

**The commands in al-Isrāʾ and al-Anʿām:**

- *"And give full measure when you measure, and weigh with a straight balance. That is better and fairer in outcome."* (al-Isrāʾ 17/35)
- *"And give full measure and weight in justice. We do not burden any soul beyond its capacity."* (al-Anʿām 6/152)

**The clause at the end of al-Anʿām 6/152 is important:** *"We do not burden any soul beyond its capacity."* That is, absolute flawlessness in the balance is not demanded; what a person is capable of is demanded. This clause makes the sūra's target clear: the target is not error in measuring but **deliberate diminishing.**

A fraction left over on a scale, the limited sensitivity of an instrument, a rounding made in a calculation — these are not *taṭfīf*. *Taṭfīf* is the choosing of **which side** the difference falls on. This distinction will be set up plainly in verses 2 and 3.

### Historical background: what the measure stood in place of

To understand the weight of this verse, one has to know how buying and selling worked in that period.

In seventh-century Arabia there was no standard system of measures and no institution to supervise one. Measures were produced locally; each merchant had his own vessel, his own balance, his own weights. The measure of one city might not be the same as that of another.

The consequence is this: **the buyer had to trust the seller's measure.** There was no independent reference to check against. Trade rested not on the measure but on the trust felt towards **the person measuring**.

For this reason cheating in the measure is not a technical offence but **a violation of a relationship of trust**. And this is one of the reasons why Islamic law in the following centuries developed the institution of *ḥisba* — the supervision of the market; but the history of that institution is not the subject of this section.

One further point: Mecca was a trading city, and as was treated in the section on Quraysh its economy rested on the caravan trade. For trade to work, the parties had to trust one another, because a caravan journey lasted for months and the goods changed hands many times over. **To shave off the measure is to shave from inside that web of trust.**

That the first sentence of the sūra should have come down in a trading city is meaningful for this reason: the text takes up the instrument that city knows best and turns it into a measure of morals.

## 83/2 — ٱلَّذِينَ إِذَا ٱكْتَالُوا۟ عَلَى ٱلنَّاسِ يَسْتَوْفُونَ

*Alladhīna idha'ktālū ʿala'n-nāsi yastawfūn*
"Those who, when they take by measure from people, take in full."

### The beginning of the description

The first verse gave the verdict; the second verse begins the description. *Alladhīna* is a relative pronoun and qualifies *muṭaffifīn*.

As was recorded in the section on al-Humaza, this structure gives not an identity but a **description**: not "such-and-such people" but "those who do this".

### ٱكْتَالُوا۟ — to take by measure

**Root: ك-ي-ل.** *Kayl* — a measure of volume; to measure with a vessel. *Mikyāl* — the measure, the measuring vessel. *Kayla* — a vesselful.

The verb is in the **iftiʿāl** form: *iktāla.*

**This form is doing a key piece of work here.** One of the functions of the iftiʿāl form is **appropriation**: to do the act for one's own benefit, to one's own account.

- *kāla* — he measured (out to someone, gave to the other party).
- *iktāla* — **he measured for himself**, he took by measure.

So the same root acquires two opposite directions in two different forms: the giver and the taker. And the sūra uses these two forms side by side within two verses:

| Verse | Verb | Form | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | **ٱكْتَالُوا۟** | iftiʿāl | **When taking** |
| 3 | **كَالُوهُمْ** | base form | **When giving** |

**The whole argument of the sūra lies between these two words.** The same person, the same instrument, the same root — two different behaviours.

### عَلَى ٱلنَّاس — the matter of the particle

There is a grammatical point here, and the classical sources dwell on it.

The expected particle would have been **مِن**: *iktālū **mina'n**-nās* — "they took by measure from people". The verb "to take" is used with this particle.

The verse uses **عَلَى**.

The views:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **عَلَى here stands in place of مِن** | The use of particles in place of one another (*tanāwub al-ḥurūf*) is a recognised subject in Arabic. The sense: "when they take from people" |
| **عَلَى is in its own sense: against** | The act of taking works **to the harm** of the other party. The particle states not the direction of the transaction but against whom it goes |
| **عَلَى conveys "taking a position above"** | The taking party is positioned above while taking; in a position to use the measure to his own advantage |
| **The verb has taken the particle of *istīfāʾ*, whose sense it carries** | *Istawfā ʿalayhi* — he took his due from someone in full. The next word (*yastawfūn*) will have drawn its particle forward |

**The second and third views appear more consonant with the text**, since the sūra is already relating a one-sidedness. The particle plants that one-sidedness in the grammar of the sentence: they take not from people but **over people**.

**This is a preference, and it is not binding.** The first view is defended in the sources too and is possible in point of language.

### ٱلنَّاس — people

The distribution of the word within the sūra is worth noticing:

- **83/2:** *ʿala'n-**nās*** — they take from people.
- **83/6:** *yawma yaqūmu'n-**nās*** — the day people stand.

**There are four verses between the two, and the same word occurs twice.** In the first, people are in the passive position — it is taken over them. In the second, they stand up.

**I record this link as my own reading:** the repetition of the word is a datum of the text; the contrast drawn out of it is an interpretation. But the structure of the sūra supports it: verse 6 is the one sentence that reverses the picture of verse 2.

### يَسْتَوْفُونَ — to take in full

**Root: و-ف-ي.** This root was taken up at al-Baqara 2/40, and what was recorded there is the key here: **the sense of the root is "to complete, to pay out without shortfall"**; the Turkish *vefâ* — fidelity, keeping faith — comes from here; and as was stated there, *"partial fulfilment does not count as fulfilment."*

At al-Baqara 2/40 the verb was in the imperative: **أَوْفُوا۟** — "fulfil your covenant in full." Here it is in the **istifʿāl** form: *istawfā.*

**The function of the istifʿāl form is to convey a demand:** *istaghfara* (he asked forgiveness), *istaʾdhana* (he asked permission), *istahdā* (he asked for guidance).

So *yastawfūn* is not "they take in full" but, more exactly: **"they demand that it be full, they insist that it be without shortfall."**

This is the finest point the word carries. The verse is not saying that they "take too much" — they do not take extra. **They take exactly what is theirs.** And that, on its own, is no fault; indeed to take one's due in full is legitimate.

**The fault emerges in the comparison.** The next verse will say that the same person does not behave this way when giving.

So in the second verse the sūra is not yet relating a crime. It is only recording a behaviour. The crime is born with the third verse.

**This is the most elegant move of the sūra:** in the first verse it gave the verdict, in the second it described a behaviour that looks innocent, and it kept the crime back for the third. Reading the second verse, the reader does not yet see a problem.

### The verbs coming in the plural

Throughout the sūra the verbs are plural: *iktālū, yastawfūn, kālū, wazanū, yukhsirūn.*

Sūrat al-Humaza was describing one person and used the singular; at the end it moved into the plural. This sūra is plural from the start.

The reason for the difference may be this: *taṭfīf* is not the work of a single person but a **market behaviour**. If everyone in a bazaar is doing it, no one can give it up — whoever gives it up loses. This takes the behaviour out of being an individual choice and makes it a norm.

**I offer this as my own reading**; it is not the only explanation for the plural, and using the plural in stating a general ruling is in any case ordinary in Arabic.

## 83/3 — وَإِذَا كَالُوهُمْ أَو وَّزَنُوهُمْ يُخْسِرُونَ

*Wa idhā kālūhum aw wazanūhum yukhsirūn*
"But when they measure out to them or weigh out to them, they give short."

### كَالُوهُمْ — a grammatical matter

The expression has to be dwelt on, because at first sight it looks unintelligible.

*Kālū-hum* means, literally, "they measured them". But people are not measured.

The classical grammarians explain this by **ellipsis**: *kālū **lahum*** — "they measured for them". The lām has dropped, and the pronoun has attached directly to the verb. Ellipses of this kind are common in Arabic; the second object of a verb can come without a particle.

The same holds for *wazanūhum*: *wazanū lahum* — "they weighed for them".

**There is also a discussion concerning the orthography of the muṣḥaf:** the words are written *كالوهم* and *وزنوهم*, with no alif placed after the plural wāw. This spelling is interpreted as supporting the view that *hum* is not a separate pronoun but an object attached to the verb. The topic belongs to a heading on the orthography of the muṣḥaf, and I do not go into its details here.

A minority view holds that *hum* is a separate pronoun reinforcing the subject: "when they measured — when they themselves measured". This reading is mentioned in the sources but has not found wide acceptance.

### أَوْ وَزَنُوهُمْ — the adding of the second measure here

Now we come to one of the most striking details of the sūra, and it is generally passed over.

**In the second verse only measuring (*kayl*) was spoken of. In the third verse both measuring and weighing (*wazn*) are named.**

That is:

| | When taking (verse 2) | When giving (verse 3) |
|---|---|---|
| Measuring (*kayl*) | ✓ | ✓ |
| Weighing (*wazn*) | — | ✓ |

Why?

The explanations given in the classical sources:

**One.** When taking, a single method is enough — whichever it is, they take in full. When giving, they give short by **every method**. That is, the cheating does not depend on the kind of instrument; it depends on the person's intention. The instrument may change, the result does not.

**Two.** *Kayl* and *wazn* were used in trade for two different kinds of goods: bulk goods such as grain by the measure, valuable goods such as gold, silver and spices by the balance. Naming the two together is a widening of the scope.

**Three.** By the flow of the verse: the use of a single verb in the first clause and of two verbs in the second shows how detailed the attention on the giving side is. The person thinks harder when giving.

**I add this third explanation as my own reading** and find it consonant with the sūra's diagnosis: cheating requires not inattention but **extra attention**. Shaving takes more labour than measuring exactly.

### يُخْسِرُونَ — to give short

**Root: خ-س-ر.** This root was taken up in the section on al-ʿAṣr, and what was recorded there is decisive here: **its original sense is diminishing and loss, and this sense is settled in Arabic especially in the field of trade and the balance.** It was also recorded there that the usual English renderings have picked up an emotional colouring, whereas the Arabic *khusrān* is a cold balance sheet.

The verb here is in the **إفعال (ifʿāl)** form: *akhsara.*

**And this gives the root a different direction from its use in al-ʿAṣr.**

| | al-ʿAṣr 103/2 | al-Muṭaffifīn 83/3 |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | **خُسْر** (verbal noun) — within a nominal sentence | **يُخْسِرُونَ** (ifʿāl) — a transitive verb |
| Who suffers the loss | **The person himself** | **The other party** |
| Structure | *fī khusr* — inside the loss | *yukhsirūn* — they put into loss |

In al-ʿAṣr the human being is **inside** the loss; here the human being **puts** another into loss.

**And when the two sūras are read together a result emerges, which I write as my own reading:**

> Sūrat al-ʿAṣr said that everyone outside the exception is in loss. Al-Muṭaffifīn gives an example of how that loss is produced: **the one who puts another into loss writes his own balance sheet into the negative.**

That is, *ikhsār* (putting another into loss) and *khusr* (being in one's own loss) are not merely of the same root; in the logic of the two sūras the one becomes the cause of the other.

In drawing this link a reservation has to be entered: it was stated plainly in the section on al-ʿAṣr that *"a person does not have to harm anyone for his balance sheet to be in the negative."* That is, loss does not come by one road only. Al-Muṭaffifīn shows one of those roads.

### The same person, two measures

The diagnosis of the sūra is completed here, and it has to be dwelt on.

**The two verses are not describing two different people. They are describing the same person in two different situations.**

This is clear from the structure of the sentence: *alladhīna* (verse 2) is a single subject and two conditional clauses hang on it. *Idhā… wa idhā…* — "in this case thus, in that case so."

And what is described is not an **inconsistency**; it is an extremely consistent behaviour. In both situations the person acts by the same principle: **let the difference always fall on my side.**

Seen from outside, two different behaviours appear. Seen from inside, there is a single principle.

**This is the sharpest observation of the sūra:** the person who cheats in the measure does not break the measure. He **rounds the measure to his own side every time.** And taken one by one, this is defensible behaviour — because every rounding is right beside the boundary.

Another result follows from this: **the person does not see himself as in the wrong.** Taking in full when taking is his right; a small shortfall when giving is an error. Both sentences have a defence. The only thing that has no defence is that both are found **in the same person**.

This is why the sūra puts the two verses side by side. It is not possible to show the crime in a single sentence; **a comparison is required.**

### The relation of measure to justice

One link of vocabulary is worth recording.

The original sense of the Arabic **عَدْل** (justice) is, as was taken up in the section on al-Infiṭār, **equivalence**; *ʿidl* names the load balanced on one side of a camel against the other.

That is, the very concept of justice was born **from a picture of weighing**: the two pans being equal.

This explains why the Qurʾān gives the matter of measure so much weight. The balance is not a metaphor for justice; it is **the source of the word justice.** The person who breaks the balance is not breaking an instrument; he is breaking the model of justice itself.

That al-Raḥmān 55/7-9 names the balance in the same sentence as the raising of the heaven is understood in this light.

### What this says to the present

**The forms of shaving today are no different from those of that day — only more abstract.**

*Taṭfīf* is possible wherever two conditions are found together: (a) one of the parties holds the measure in his own hands, (b) the difference is below the threshold at which the other party would notice it.

These two conditions are extremely widespread today: the unread clauses of long contracts, the notes written in small type, the roundings made in a calculation, the silent reductions made in a product's contents, the advertised period differing from the actual one, a line left out of a file.

None of these on its own is grounds for a suit. Every one of them is defensible taken singly. And every one of them is the holder of the measure rounding the difference to his own side.

**The test is this question:** *when I do the same transaction and when it is done to me, do I give it the same name?*

The sūra's diagnosis lies exactly here. The person calls the rounding that favours him "an error", "a fraction", "a condition of the market"; the one that goes against him he calls "an injustice". Two names, for the same act.

And this is less a matter of a moral rule than a matter of **noticing**: to see that the two cases are the same is, most of the time, enough on its own.

## 83/4-5 — أَلَا يَظُنُّ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ أَنَّهُم مَّبْعُوثُونَ • لِيَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ

*Alā yaẓunnu ulāʾika annahum mabʿūthūn • Li-yawmin ʿaẓīm*
"Do these people not suppose that they will be raised, for a mighty day?"

### The turn of the sūra

Three verses set up a scene of trade. The fourth verse ties that scene, all at once, to the hereafter.

And the tie is made not with an explanation but with a **question**: *alā yaẓunnu* — "do they never consider?"

This is the very logic set up in Sūrat al-Māʿūn, and it was treated there in detail: **what a person actually believes emerges at the moment when what he does has no worldly consequence whatever.** In al-Māʿūn the example chosen was the orphan; here it is the customer shaved off the measure.

The common point is this: **no one sees, no one will call to account.** And what is done in precisely that situation is the one clean measure of a person's belief in the reckoning.

### أَلَا — the particle of alerting

**أَلَا** is made up in Arabic of two pieces: the interrogative hamza (أ) + the particle of negation (لا).

It has two functions:

1. **Alerting:** at the head of a sentence it gives the sense "attention!", "know well that".
2. **A question of rebuke (*istifhām tawbīkhī*):** "do they not… at all?"

Both are working here. The sentence both draws attention and rebukes.

And the combination of negation + question conveys **assertion** in Arabic: the answer to "do they not consider?" is not "they do consider"; the question states that they do not consider, and meets this with astonishment.

The same pattern was taken up in the section on al-ʿĀdiyāt: *"Does he not know?"* (al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/9) — there too it was a question of rebuke.

### يَظُنُّ — the sūra's second "little"

This word is one of the finest points of the sūra.

**Root: ظ-ن-ن.** It was taken up at al-Baqara 2/46, and what was recorded there was this: **the root can answer, according to context, both to a doubtful guess and to a firm conviction; in the study of language this is called contrariety of meaning.**

The technical term from al-Baqara can be given here: the lexicographers call words of this kind **الأضداد (aḍdād)** — a word carrying two senses opposed to one another. Arabic has other examples, and the lexicographers have written independent works on the subject.

**Which sense, then, in this verse?**

| View | Sense | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Ẓann = a doubtful guess** | "Do they not so much as **hold it possible** that they will be raised?" | The threshold demanded is at its lowest: not certain faith, only a possibility |
| **Ẓann = firm conviction** | "Do they not **know for certain** that they will be raised?" | Consonant with the use at al-Baqara 2/46: *"those who know that they will meet their Lord"* |

**The first view appears stronger in this context, and its ground is the sūra's own structure.**

At al-Baqara 2/46 it was said that the word carries the sense of certain knowledge, and the ground was given there plainly: *"the verse says this as praise, and one who doubts is not praised."* That is, what settled the sense was whether the sentence was praise or blame.

Here the sentence is a **rebuke**. And the logic of rebuke runs the other way: in praise the threshold is raised, in rebuke the threshold is lowered. To say "do you not know this?" is a lighter rebuke than to say "do you not even guess at this?"

So the verse is saying: **a full faith would not have been needed to prevent what these people are doing. A possibility, a misgiving, a "what if it is so" would have sufficed.**

**And here the architecture of the sūra becomes visible.**

The sūra opened by relating the smallest of frauds: *taṭfīf* — shaving off the edge. Now it asks after the smallest measure of belief that could have prevented that fraud: *ẓann* — a possibility.

**Two "littles" are set face to face:**

| The side of the act | The side of belief |
|---|---|
| **تطفيف** — the smallest fraud | **ظن** — the smallest belief |
| A diminishing too small to be noticed | A possibility too weak to be called faith |

And the sūra says: **had there been the second, there would not have been the first.**

This is the sūra's most elegant balance, and I offer it as my own reading. That the word is among the *aḍdād* and that the context here is rebuke are data of the text; the equivalence I set up between the two "littles" is an interpretation.

### أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ — a distant demonstrative

*Ulāʾika* is the plural demonstrative pronoun used for what is distant.

The two explanations recorded for *dhālika* in the section on al-Māʿūn hold here too: **belittling and distancing (*taḥqīr*)**, or **visibility** — "so plain that you can point to it from afar."

There is a third function as well: the pronoun puts a **distance** into the sentence. The first three verses described them from close up — enumerating one by one what they do. The fourth verse steps back and looks from a distance: *those ones.*

### مَّبْعُوثُون — they will be raised

**Root: ب-ع-ث.** To send, to raise up, to set in motion. *Baʿth* — raising the dead. *Bāʿith* — the one who sends. *Inbiʿāth* — to spring up (taken up at al-Shams 91/12: *inbaʿatha ashqāhā*).

The word is a **passive participle**: *mabʿūthūn* — "those who are raised, those who are sent". That is, passive: they do not rise of themselves, they are raised.

**And this root ties back to the preceding sūra.** At al-Infiṭār 82/4 the verb *buʿthirat* occurred, and as was recorded in the section on al-ʿĀdiyāt some lexicographers count this four-letter root a compound of *baʿatha* (to raise) + *athāra* (to turn up) — a disputed etymology, all the same.

Read side by side: in al-Infiṭār the graves are turned upside down (*buʿthirat*), in al-Muṭaffifīn people are raised (*mabʿūthūn*).

### لِيَوْمٍ عَظِيمٍ — for a mighty day

The particle **لِ** here conveys **purpose**: "for that day", "towards that day". That is, the raising has an aim; it is not a rising for nothing.

**يَوْمٍ comes indefinite.** As was recorded in the section on al-Infiṭār and earlier in al-Māʿūn, the indefiniteness here conveys **magnification** (*tafkhīm*): "such a day that…"

And the adjective: **عَظِيم.** Root **ع-ظ-م**. Its concrete sense is **bone**: *ʿaẓm* — bone. *ʿAẓīm* means, at bottom, "big-boned, of large build"; from there it has passed to "great, mighty".

That a bone should lie at the root of the word is worth noticing: greatness has been thought, in Arabic, by way of **frame and structure**. The size of a thing is the size of the frame that holds it up.

### The work the verse does

The fourth and fifth verses make the most important transition of the sūra: **they tie a commercial behaviour to a matter of belief.**

Attention has to be paid to how this is done. The verse could have said any of the following, and all would have been reasonable:

- *"This is injustice."*
- *"You are devouring people's rights."*
- *"The trust of trade is destroyed."*

The verse says none of these. **It passes straight to the raising of the dead.**

That is, the sūra does not take up cheating in the measure as a matter of law or as a matter of social order. It takes it up as a **matter of belief**.

In the section on al-Māʿūn this method was named "the backbone of the sūra": *"not supporting doctrine with morals; but making morals the evidence for doctrine."*

Al-Muṭaffifīn does the same work, but from the other direction: al-Māʿūn went from behaviour to belief ("have you seen the one who denies the religion? that is the one who pushes the orphan away"), al-Muṭaffifīn turns from belief to behaviour ("do these people not consider that they will be raised?").

**The two sūras together set up the two directions of the same link.** And in verse 12 the sūra will formulate that link plainly.

## 83/6 — يَوْمَ يَقُومُ ٱلنَّاسُ لِرَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ

*Yawma yaqūmu'n-nāsu li-Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn*
"The day people stand before the Lord of all the worlds."

### يَوْمَ — the explanation of the fifth verse

The word is in the accusative and explains the *yawmin ʿaẓīm* of the preceding verse (an appositive substitute). "A mighty day — that is, the day when…"

### يَقُومُ — to stand

**Root: ق-و-م.** This root was taken up in the section on al-Bayyina, and the line *mustaqīm* → *iqāma* → *qayyima* was set up there.

The root's original sense is **to stand, to be upright**. Its derivatives: *qiyām* (standing), *qiyāma* (the rising), *maqām* (a place where one stands), *qawm* (a people standing together), *qayyūm* (that which stands of itself and holds all things standing).

**That the word *qiyāma* comes from this root settles the image of the verse:** the name of that day is "the rising". And this verse displays plainly the picture inside the word.

The verb is in the **imperfect**: it conveys continuity. That is, not to stand up and then sit down; **to remain standing** — to wait.

### لِرَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِين — before whom

There are several views on the particle **لِ**:

| View | Sense |
|---|---|
| **Purpose** | They stand for His judgement, for the reckoning |
| **Reverence and obedience** | To stand in His presence is an expression of honouring — as one does not sit in the presence of a sovereign |
| **Submission to His command** | *Qāma li-fulān* — he held himself ready at someone's command |

All three work in the text and do not exclude one another.

The construct **رَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِين** is the second verse of al-Fātiḥa and was taken up there. Why it should be chosen here is worth dwelling on.

The verse does not say "for God" or "for the best of judges". It says **"for the Lord of all the worlds"**.

This construct says two things:

**One. A sovereignty from which nobody is left out.** *ʿĀlamīn* — all the worlds. The person who cheats in the measure is the owner of the measure in his own bazaar; on that day the owner of the measure is someone else, and His sovereignty is not partial.

**Two. The sense of nurture in the word *Rabb*.** As was recorded in the section on al-ʿAlaq, a *rabb* is "one who nurtures, who raises, who brings a thing to completion". So the station before which one stands is not merely a station that judges; it is a station that **feeds and brings up**.

This lies along the same line as the sixth verse of Sūrat al-Infiṭār: there too the reckoning was named together with the attribute *al-karīm*.

### ٱلنَّاس — the second transition

In the second verse there was *ʿala'n-nās* (over people); here there is *yaqūmu'n-nās* (people stand).

The same word, four verses apart, in two opposite positions:

| Verse | People |
|---|---|
| 83/2 | The party measured over — **passive** |
| 83/6 | The party that stands — **active** |

The people shaved off the measure are among those who stand. And so are those who shaved.

**I record this as an observation about the wording.** The repetition of the word is a datum of the text; the contrast is my reading.

### Standing itself

There is one more point, and it is important for the sūra as a whole.

As the description of that day the verse chooses **not sitting but standing**. And further on in the sūra, when the state of the abrār is described, exactly the opposite will be used:

*"On couches they look."* (83/23, 35) — **عَلَى ٱلْأَرَآئِكِ**

So the sūra sets up two postures: **those who stand waiting** and **those who sit and look.** And the difference between the two carries the whole message of the sūra.

To stand is the state in which the reckoning has not been given — waiting, uncertainty, weariness. To sit is the work's being finished.

This contrast will be taken up at verse 23.

## 83/7 — كَلَّآ إِنَّ كِتَٰبَ ٱلْفُجَّارِ لَفِى سِجِّينٍ

*Kallā inna kitāba'l-fujjāri la-fī sijjīn*
"No! The book of those who break every bound is in *sijjīn*."

### كَلَّا — the particle repeated four times in the sūra

The function of the particle *kallā* was taken up in the section on al-Takāthur: it conveys both **rejection** and **restraint**.

In this sūra the particle occurs **four times**: in verses 7, 14, 15 and 18. That is a high number for one sūra, and it settles the sūra's rhythm.

And the four do not do the same work:

| Verse | What *kallā* cuts off |
|---|---|
| **7** | Not the picture in the preceding verse, but the **failure to consider** that picture. "No, it is not so" — the matter is not unknown, the record has already been kept |
| **14** | The claim in the preceding verse: *"tales of the ancients"* |
| **15** | Not a continuation of the preceding verse but the beginning of a new verdict — reinforcement |
| **18** | It opens the opposite of the verdict in verse 7 |

Here, that is, in verse 7, there is disagreement over the target of the particle. Some exegetes attach *kallā* to the question in verse 4: they do not consider — *no*, they ought to have considered. Others count it a reinforcement of the following sentence and give it the sense **حَقًّا** (truly).

**That *inna* follows the particle** appears to support the second reading: the structure *kallā inna…* produces a cutting off and a fresh start of the form "no! truly…". **This is a preference, and it is not binding.**

### كِتَاب — book

The analysis of the root was made at al-Baqara 2/2, and what was recorded there is decisive here: **the original sense of the root is not "to write" but "to bring together, to join, to stitch".** *Katība* — a military unit gathered, not scattered. And the conclusion reached there: *"a book means, at bottom, what has been compiled, what has been brought together."*

In the section on al-Infiṭār, moreover, *kātibīn* was dwelt on and the difference between writing and memory was taken up: **writing makes what it carries independent of the one who carries it, and makes it producible afterwards.**

Now these two findings come side by side. In al-Infiṭār the scribes were writing; in al-Muṭaffifīn we are told **where what is written is kept.**

**There are two views on the sense of the word here:**

| View | Sense |
|---|---|
| **The record of deeds** | The record of what a person has done. Consonant with al-Infiṭār 82/11 and al-Isrāʾ 17/13-14 |
| **What has been written, that is, the decree** | *Kitāb* in the sense of "what is decreed"; the verdict given concerning them |

The first view appears better suited to the context, because verse 9 speaks of a concrete document, saying *kitābun marqūm* — "a marked writing". **This is a preference, and it is not binding.**

### ٱلْفُجَّار — the fujjār

The word occurred in the preceding sūra (al-Infiṭār 82/14) and was taken up there: the root is **ف-ج-ر**, a *fājir* is "a person who breaks through the bound that holds him", *fujūr* is not a lack but an **overflowing** (treated in the section on al-Shams).

**And the word's place in this sūra is meaningful in addition.**

The first three verses of the sūra had related a violation of a boundary: shaving off the edge of the measure. *Ṭaff*, in the root of the word, already means "edge". So the sūra opens by relating **those who violate the edge**.

The seventh verse names those people: **fujjār** — those who break through the bound.

That both words have to do with a boundary is a datum of the text:

| Word | Concrete sense of the root |
|---|---|
| **مطفف** (ط-ف-ف) | The **edge** of a thing; the small quantity left at the edge |
| **فاجر** (ف-ج-ر) | One who **splits** the bound and overflows it |

The difference between them is one of scale: *taṭfīf* is shaving off the edge, *fujūr* is tearing down the dyke. **The sūra gathers the one who shaves off the edge under the same name as the one who tears down the dyke.** This is the explanation of the disproportion in the first verse: between the small violation and the large one there is no difference of kind, only of degree.

**I offer this link as my own reading**; that the two roots have to do with a boundary is a datum of the language, the relation I set up between them is an interpretation.

### سِجِّين — *sijjīn*

The most disputed word of the sūra.

**The word occurs in the Qurʾān only here, in two verses (7 and 8).** And the sūra itself leaves it unexplained, saying "what will make you know?" This is the main cause of the disagreement in the exegesis.

**The views on the root and the pattern:**

| View | Derivation | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **An intensive from the root س-ج-ن** | *Sijjīn* is an intensive pattern on the measure **فِعِّيل** (like *sikkīr* — a heavy drinker, *shirrīb* — a great drinker). *Sijn* — prison | "That which imprisons much" or "that which is imprisoned". The place where the book is confined, from which it cannot be taken out |
| **A place name** | The word is the proper name of a particular place | A place beneath the earth, at the very bottom. On this view the word is not derived but is directly a name |
| **Related to س-ج-ل** | Some lexicographers set up a kinship with *sijjīl* (the stone that occurs in al-Fīl 105/4) | It has been counted weak; the two words are from different roots |
| **An attribute conveying lowness** | What is meant is not the place where the book is but its **condition** | "Their book is in a low, worthless position" |

**The first view is the soundest in point of language**, and the *fiʿʿīl* intensive measure is common in Arabic. And read together with its opposite — *ʿilliyyūn* (verse 18) — the sense becomes clear: the one is **below**, the other **above**.

**There is a description much transmitted in the sources:** *sijjīn* is said to be in the lowest layer of the earth, and *ʿilliyyīn* at the top of the heaven. This description is traced to early exegetical reports; **since I cannot give the soundness of those reports with certainty, I make no attribution of a source.**

**In my judgement what the text itself says with certainty is this:** the word conveys a **position**, and that position is below. Beyond this — whether it is a place, a register or a state — cannot be settled, because in the next verse the sūra plainly declares it unknowable.

### The idea the sense of imprisonment carries

If the first view is accepted, the word carries an idea worth dwelling on.

**To imprison is to put a thing where it cannot get out.** And the imprisoning of a document is not its destruction; it is **its being made unreachable**. That is, the record stands, but the person can do nothing to it.

Its connection with the first three verses of the sūra is set up here: the *muṭaffif* was the person who held the measure in his hands. To hold the measure is to be able to decide which side the difference falls on.

The seventh verse speaks of a record he cannot hold in his hands. A document that cannot be altered, cannot be shaved, cannot be taken from at the edge.

**I offer this reading as my own inference.**

## 83/8-9 — وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا سِجِّينٌ • كِتَٰبٌ مَّرْقُومٌ

*Wa mā adrāka mā sijjīn • Kitābun marqūm*
"And what will make you know what *sijjīn* is? A marked writing."

### The pattern working differently here

The *mā adrāka* pattern was taken up in the sections on al-Humaza, al-Qadr and al-Infiṭār, and the rule recorded there was this: **when the pattern comes in the perfect an explanation follows it, when it comes in the imperfect it does not.**

Here the pattern is in the perfect (*adrāka*) and a sentence follows it: *kitābun marqūm.*

**But there is a problem, and the classical exegetes have dwelt on it.**

The question was about *sijjīn*: "what is *sijjīn*?" The answer, however, describes not *sijjīn* but **the book**: "a marked writing."

That is, the question goes to one place and the answer to another.

**The explanations given:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| ***Sijjīn* is the book itself** | Then question and answer overlap: *sijjīn* is the name not of a place but of that book |
| **The answer returns to the *kitāb* of verse 7** | *Kitābun marqūm* means "their book is a marked writing"; what *sijjīn* is **is left unexplained** |
| **The answer describes what is inside *sijjīn*** | *Sijjīn* is a place; what is in it is being told |

**The second view is the one best suited to the wording of the text, and it yields an important result:** the sūra asks the question, then leaves the question unanswered and says something else.

This is consonant with the finding recorded in the section on al-Humaza: the *mā adrāka* pattern **declares, before giving the knowledge, that knowledge will not suffice.** It was said there that "the sūra describes the thing after saying that the thing it describes cannot be described."

Here a further step is taken: **it does not even describe it.** The name of the place is given, the question about it is asked, and in place of an answer the character of the record is given.

**And this will be repeated twice in the same sūra:** in verse 19 the same question will be asked of *ʿilliyyūn* and the same answer given — *kitābun marqūm.* In both places the word is left unexplained.

The conclusion to be drawn from this is the following, and I offer it as my own reading: **the sūra is saying that we do not need to know what the two places are. The knowledge that is needed is the character of the record.** Not where it is kept, but how it is kept.

### مَّرْقُوم — marked

**Root: ر-ق-م.** And one has to be careful about the sense of this word, because there is a difference between its sense in modern Arabic and its classical sense.

**In modern Arabic رقم means "number, figure."** But this sense is one the word acquired later.

**In classical Arabic رقم is:** to strike a **trace, a line, a mark** onto something. In particular:

- *raqama'th-thawba* — he embroidered the cloth, worked a pattern into it. *Raqm* — the lined pattern in a cloth.
- *al-arqam* — a kind of snake with speckles and stripes on it.
- *raqama'l-kitāba* — he pointed the writing, marked the letters, made it proof against confusion.

**Its only other use in the Qurʾān:** *"Or did you suppose that the People of the Cave and of **al-Raqīm** were among Our wonders?"* (al-Kahf 18/9). What *al-Raqīm* is is disputed; the most widespread view is that it is a tablet or inscription with writing incised on it.

**The idea at the centre of the word is this: the making of a writing unerasable.**

A pattern worked into a cloth is not a dye smeared on the cloth; it has gone into the thread. Writing incised on a tablet is not written on it but cut into it. *Marqūm* is a record of that kind.

And a second layer of sense: **pointed, marked, prevented from being confused.** In Arabic script the dots were added later, and their purpose is to keep the letters from being confused with one another. A writing that is *marqūm* is a writing that leaves no hesitation in its reading.

**This makes a link with the first three verses of the sūra.** What the *muṭaffif* did was to use the measure **in the field of hesitation**: right beside the exact boundary, a difference too indeterminate to be objected to.

A record that is *marqūm* is exactly the opposite of this: **a writing with no edge, that cannot be rounded, that admits of no interpretation.**

A document that cannot be shaved.

**I offer this reading as my own inference.** The sense of the word is the lexicographers' datum; the contrast I set up with the first verses of the sūra is an interpretation.

### The sentence coming indefinite

*Kitābun marqūm* — both words are indefinite.

The expected expression would have been *al-kitābu'l-marqūm*; in verse 7 *kitāb* had already occurred within a definite construct (*kitāba'l-fujjār*).

The indefiniteness can do two things here: **magnification** (*tafkhīm* — "such a writing that") or the **stating of a kind** ("something of the marked-writing sort"). Both are mentioned in the classical sources.

---

## 83/10-11 — وَيْلٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِّلْمُكَذِّبِينَ • ٱلَّذِينَ يُكَذِّبُونَ بِيَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ

*Waylun yawmaʾidhin li'l-mukadhdhibīn • Alladhīna yukadhdhibūna bi-yawmi'd-dīn*
"Woe on that day to the deniers! Those who deny the day of reckoning."

### The second *wayl*

The sūra says *wayl* a second time. And the difference between the two must be dwelt on:

| | 83/1 | 83/10 |
|---|---|---|
| Wording | *waylun **li'l-muṭaffifīn*** | *waylun **yawmaʾidhin** li'l-mukadhdhibīn* |
| Notation of time | **None** | **Present** — "on that day" |
| Addressed to | Those who diminish in the measure | The deniers |

The first *wayl* is without time: the verdict is laid down absolutely. The second is tied to a day: *yawmaʾidhin.*

**And *yawmaʾidhin* has been brought forward, before the predicate.** As was recorded in the section on al-Humaza, an element brought forward gains emphasis. So the emphasis lies not on "woe to them" but on **"on that day"**.

This ties verse 10 to verses 7-9: the record was kept, it stands in *sijjīn*, and on that day it will be opened.

### The sūra's joining of the two groups

Now we come to one of the most important moves of the sūra.

In the first verse the sūra said *wayl* to the *muṭaffifīn*. In the tenth verse it says *wayl* to the *mukadhdhibīn*.

**Are these two groups the same, or separate?**

The flow of the text says they are the same, and its ground is in verse 12: *"None denies it but every aggressor, every sinner."*

That is, the sūra sets up this chain:

> Those who diminish in the measure (1-3) → these do not consider the raising (4-6) → their records are in *sijjīn* (7-9) → woe to the deniers (10-11) → **and the deniers are precisely those who are aggressors and sinners** (12).

The twelfth verse ties the head of the chain to its end. So the *muṭaffif* of verse 1 and the *mukadhdhib* of verse 10 are the same person — the one named by his act, the other by his belief.

**This is the same work Sūrat al-Māʿūn does, and it was recorded there:** the two halves of al-Māʿūn took two different human types and brought them together at the same point. Al-Muṭaffifīn takes a single human being and names him twice over.

### The link with al-Mursalāt

The sentence **وَيْلٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِّلْمُكَذِّبِينَ** is repeated many times as a refrain in Sūrat al-Mursalāt. The structure of that sūra is built on the repetition.

That the same sentence should occur once in al-Muṭaffifīn sets up a shared wording between the two sūras. **I record this as an observation; I make no claim that there is a deliberate reference between the two sūras.**

### يُكَذِّبُونَ — to deny

The analysis of the verb was made in the section on al-Māʿūn: *kadhdhaba* (tafʿīl) is "to call another's true word a lie"; it differs from *kadhaba* (to lie). And *takdhīb* is not a silent unbelief but **an active attitude and a taking of a position**.

The function of the particle *bāʾ* was also recorded there: in the structure *kadhdhaba bi-* the particle carries a sense of **adhesion** — what is rejected is not an idea standing at a distance but something taken hold of and pushed away.

In this sūra the verb occurs **four times**: 10 (*mukadhdhibīn*), 11 (*yukadhdhibūna*), 12 (*yukadhdhibu*), 17 (*tukadhdhibūn*). The root is the backbone of the second half of the sūra.

And the last of these occurrences, in verse 17, is striking: there the verb will be put in the past tense and turned **towards the addressee**: *"This is what you used to deny."*

### بِيَوْمِ ٱلدِّين — the day of reckoning

The construct occurred three times in Sūrat al-Infiṭār (82/15, 17, 18) and was taken up there. The analysis of the root was made in the section on al-Māʿūn: **at the centre of the root د-ي-ن lies the idea of requital.**

And in the section on al-Māʿūn this verse — al-Muṭaffifīn 83/11 — was shown as the evidence that *dīn* carries the sense "the day of reckoning": *"at al-Muṭaffifīn 83/11 the same structure is written out plainly: those who deny the day of reckoning."*

**And there is something further here:** the word *dīn* meant "requital". The subject of the sūra is **measure** — that is, the very transaction of giving in return. In a sale, what is given and what is taken must be equal; and *dīn* is "the return of what was given".

So the sūra is saying that those who diminish the return in a transaction reject **the day of return**. The same idea rejected in two fields.

**I offer this link as my own reading.** The root sense of the word was established in the section on al-Māʿūn; the relation I set up with the subject of the sūra is an interpretation.

---

## 83/12 — وَمَا يُكَذِّبُ بِهِۦٓ إِلَّا كُلُّ مُعْتَدٍ أَثِيمٍ

*Wa mā yukadhdhibu bihī illā kullu muʿtadin athīm*
"And none denies it but every aggressor, every sinner."

The diagnostic verse of the sūra.

### The structure of restriction

The sentence is on the pattern **مَا … إِلَّا**. In Arabic this is among the strongest patterns of *ḥaṣr* (restriction): it assigns a ruling to a single group.

"None but these deny" — that is, the set of deniers lies **inside** the set of aggressors and sinners.

**And there is a point of logic here that is indispensable to understanding the verse rightly.**

The sentence says this: **every denier is an aggressor and a sinner.**

The sentence does not say: *every aggressor and sinner denies.*

These two propositions are different. The first states that denial rests on a moral ground. The second would count every faulty human being an unbeliever, and the verse does not say that.

**This distinction must be stated plainly**, because this is where the verse is most easily abused. The verse does not permit looking at a person's faults and passing a verdict on his belief. The direction of the link it sets up runs the other way: back from denial, towards morals.

### كُلُّ — universality

What was recorded in the section on al-Humaza for *li-kulli humaza* holds here too: **كل conveys universality, covering the whole of a class.**

And the conclusion reached there: *"the sūra does not take aim at a person, it takes aim at an attribute. Whoever bears that attribute is whom the verse is speaking of."*

So here too: the verse names no one, it describes an attribute.

### مُعْتَد — aggressor

**Root: ع-د-و.** Its concrete sense is **to pass, to cross, to go beyond**.

The derivatives:
- *ʿadā* — he ran, he passed swiftly. (The name of Sūrat al-ʿĀdiyāt is from this root and was taken up there: *al-ʿādiyāt* — those that run.)
- *ʿaduww* — enemy. The one who crosses the boundary and comes.
- *ʿudwān* — enmity, going beyond the bound.
- *iʿtidāʾ* (the iftiʿāl form) — to exceed the bound, to transgress. The *muʿtad* of the verse is from this form.
- *taʿaddā* — to pass something; in grammar *mutaʿaddī* (a transitive verb) comes from here.

**The idea at the centre of the root: to cross to the far side of a boundary.**

And this completes the sūra's weave of vocabulary. Three words, three separate roots, the same idea:

| Word | Root | Relation to the boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **مطفف** | ط-ف-ف | Shaves at the **edge** of the boundary |
| **فاجر** | ف-ج-ر | **Splits** the boundary |
| **معتد** | ع-د-و | **Crosses** the boundary |

Three words are three scales of a single behaviour. And the sūra uses all three of the same people.

**I offer this weave as my own reading**; the concrete senses of the three roots are the lexicographers' data, the sequence I set up among them is an interpretation.

The Qurʾān uses the verb *iʿtidāʾ* very plainly in one place: *"And fight in the way of God those who fight you; but do not exceed the bound. God does not love those who exceed the bound."* (al-Baqara 2/190). That is, *iʿtidāʾ* is the overflowing of a legitimate field — not a thing that should never be done at all, but a thing **whose measure is lost.**

### أَثِيم — sinner

**Root: أ-ث-م.** *Ithm* — sin.

**On the concrete sense of the root the lexicographers have recorded an explanation:** *ithm* is what **holds a person back, slows him** from what is good. It is formulated in the sources as *"al-ithmu mā abṭaʾa ʿani'l-khayr"* — sin is what delays the reaching of the good. From the same root *āthima* is used of a camel that lags behind.

**I transmit this explanation as the lexicographers have recorded it**; I do not offer it as a settled etymology.

The word *athīm* is on the measure **فَعِيل**, an intensive or quasi-participial adjective: one who has made sin a habit, who is settled in sin.

**The use of the two adjectives together:**

| | **معتد** | **أثيم** |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | **Outward** — he crosses another's boundary | **Inward** — a state settled within himself |
| The other party | Present | Absent; it concerns the person himself |

That is, the two adjectives describe the fault in two directions: what is done to another and what is in the person. This is a structure like the joining of two traits (mockery and hoarding) into a single portrait in Sūrat al-Humaza.

### Immorality as the cause of denial

Now we come to the most important idea of the sūra.

**This finding was made in the section on al-Māʿūn, and this very verse was shown as the evidence:**

> *"Note the direction of the sentence: immorality is shown not as the *result* of denial but as its **cause**. First the way of life, then the belief that fits it."*

Now we are at the verse's own place, and the finding has to be opened up.

**First step: the reversal of the usual assumption.**

The usual assumption is this: a person first believes or does not believe, and then acts according to his belief. Belief is the cause, behaviour the result.

This assumption is innocent but incomplete. The verse shows the opposite direction: **behaviour is the cause, belief the result.**

The structure of the sūra was already saying this. The sūra did not open with denial; it opened with **cheating in the measure**. For three verses a behaviour was related, and then it was said, "do these people not consider that they will be raised?" So the order is: first the act, then the belief the act requires.

**Second step: the mechanism itself.**

How does this work?

If a person is engaged in a behaviour that constantly brings him benefit, then the idea that the behaviour is wrong is for him an **unbearable** idea. Because to accept the idea requires giving up the behaviour; and to give up the behaviour requires giving up the benefit obtained.

In this situation there are two roads: to give up the behaviour, or to reject the idea.

The second is far cheaper. And it is generally the one chosen.

**Denial, then, is not the result of a thought; it is a need.** The person does not believe there is no reckoning; he **needs** there to be no reckoning.

**Third step: this finding is not new, and the Qurʾān sets it up in several places.**

*"No! Man wants to deny / to break through what lies ahead of him. He asks: when is the day of resurrection?"* (al-Qiyāma 75/5-6)

These two verses set up the same structure: first a desire (*li-yafjura amāmahu* — this expression was taken up in the section on al-Shams: "to split through the obstacle ahead of him"), then a mocking question. The source of denial is not a lack of knowledge but a want.

*"None reject Our signs but the wrongdoers."* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/49) — again a pattern of restriction, again a moral attribute.

**Fourth step: the practical consequence of this.**

This finding has a consequence, and it is important: **denial may not be dissolved by evidence.**

If a person rejects something out of a lack of knowledge, giving him knowledge works. But if he rejects it because it runs against his interest, giving him knowledge does not work — because the problem is not in the knowledge.

This is why the sūra does not line up proofs. It does not try to prove the resurrection. What it does is to show the person his **behaviour** and ask him to look at it: *you are shaving off the measure; what must you believe in order to be able to do that?*

**Fifth step: the sūra does not turn this finding into a weapon of defence.**

A limit has to be drawn. If this verse is read as "those who deny are bad people", it has been abused. The point of logic recorded above comes into play here: the verse says that the deniers have an attribute; it does not say that everyone who bears that attribute denies.

And a second limit: **the verse is an instrument of diagnosis, not an instrument of classification.** The principle recorded in the sections on al-Humaza and al-Māʿūn holds here too: the text is applied first to oneself.

This verse applied to oneself is the following question: *is there a relation between the things I find hard to believe and the things I do not want to give up?*

### The link with Sūrat al-Qalam

The closest relative of this verse is in Sūrat al-Qalam, and the overlap between the two texts is striking.

Sūrat al-Qalam draws a portrait of a person (68/10-16), and the portrait enumerates: one who swears much, contemptible, a **hammāz** (much given to faulting — this verse was cited in the section on al-Humaza), a carrier of tales, a hinderer of good, and:

**مَنَّاعٍ لِّلْخَيْرِ مُعْتَدٍ أَثِيمٍ** (al-Qalam 68/12) — "a hinderer of good, **an aggressor, a sinner.**"

That is, the construct *muʿtadin athīm* occurs in two places in the Qurʾān, and both are inside a portrait of a person.

And Sūrat al-Qalam continues:

**أَن كَانَ ذَا مَالٍ وَبَنِينَ • إِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ءَايَٰتُنَا قَالَ أَسَٰطِيرُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ** (al-Qalam 68/14-15)
"Because he is a man of wealth and sons; when Our signs are recited to him he says, 'tales of the ancients'."

**This is word for word the same sentence as al-Muṭaffifīn 83/13.**

So the two sūras use the same expressions across two verses:

| | al-Qalam 68 | al-Muṭaffifīn 83 |
|---|---|---|
| Attribute | *muʿtadin athīm* (12) | *kullu muʿtadin athīm* (12) |
| Cause | ***an kāna dhā mālin wa banīn*** (14) — because he is a man of wealth and sons | — |
| Speech | *idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā qāla asāṭīru'l-awwalīn* (15) | The same sentence (13) |

**And the one thing found in al-Qalam and not in al-Muṭaffifīn is that the cause is written out plainly:** *an kāna dhā mālin wa banīn* — "because he is a man of wealth and sons."

The classical grammarians explain the *an* at the head of this expression as **causal**: that is, he says this **because** he has wealth and sons.

This is the plainest example of what al-Muṭaffifīn 83/12 says: the cause of denial is not a piece of reasoning but a **position**.

**I record this overlap between the two sūras as a datum.** That the same construct and the same sentence are found in two places is a fact of the text; I make no claim that there is a deliberate reference between the two sūras.

## 83/13 — إِذَا تُتْلَىٰ عَلَيْهِ ءَايَٰتُنَا قَالَ أَسَٰطِيرُ ٱلْأَوَّلِينَ

*Idhā tutlā ʿalayhi āyātunā qāla asāṭīru'l-awwalīn*
"When Our signs are recited to him he says: tales of the ancients."

### From singular to plural, from plural to singular

In the preceding verse, *kullu muʿtadin athīm* — a singular description. Here the pronoun continues in the singular: *ʿalayhi* (to him), *qāla* (he said).

But in verse 14 it will turn back to the plural: *qulūbihim* (their hearts), *kānū* (they were).

This is the matter of *kull* recorded in the section on al-Humaza: *kull* is singular in wording and plural in sense, and in Arabic a pronoun may be used according to either.

### تُتْلَىٰ — to be recited

**Root: ت-ل-و.** This root was taken up in the section on al-Shams: *talā* — to follow, to go after, to come behind.

And as was recorded there, *tilāwa* (recitation) is from this root: **to recite is for letters to follow one another.** At the centre of the word lies not sound but **sequence**.

The verb here is in the **passive**: *tutlā* — when it is recited. Who is reciting is not said. This shifts the attention from the reciter to what is recited.

**And the verb is imperfect + *idhā***: a situation constantly repeated. Not an event that happened once; the same thing happens every time.

### ءَايَٰتُنَا — "Our signs"

**Root: أ-ي-ي.** *Āya* — a sign, a mark, an indication.

The word's use in the Qurʾān runs in two directions: both **the signs in the cosmos** and **the sentences of the revelation**. Both carry the sense "what shows, what points".

The construct is made to the **first person plural**: *āyātu-nā* — "Our signs". This is the only "we" pronoun used across the sūra.

**This choice is meaningful:** the verse states to whom the rejected thing belongs. The person is not rejecting "a text"; he is rejecting a word whose owner is known.

### أَسَٰطِير — tales

**Root: س-ط-ر.** And the root of this word explains the character of the objection.

*Saṭr* — **a line, a written line, a row.** *Saṭara* — he drew a line, he wrote line by line. *Misṭar* — a ruler. *Musaṭṭar* — set in lines, written.

The Qurʾān uses the root directly: *"Nūn. By the pen and by what they write line by line."* (al-Qalam 68/1) — this verse was cited in the section on al-ʿAlaq.

**أَسَاطِير** is a plural word. The lexicographers differ over its singular: is it *usṭūra*, or *isṭār/asṭār*? Some lexicographers count the word a **plural of a plural**. I confine myself to recording the discussion.

Its sense: **things written down and compiled, narratives passed on from the ancients.**

*(There are modern discussions to the effect that the word may have entered Arabic from outside; since I can say nothing certain, I do not enter into this subject. Its derivation from the Arabic root س-ط-ر is the explanation the classical sources give.)*

**ٱلْأَوَّلِين** — the earlier ones, the ancients. Root **أ-و-ل**: to be first, to return.

The construct *asāṭīru'l-awwalīn* is repeated in several places in the Qurʾān, and it always comes out of the same mouths: the mouths of those who reject the revelation.

### The form of the objection

Now we come to the most striking side of the verse.

**These people do not say "a lie". They say "a tale".**

The difference matters, and it is generally passed over.

To say "it is a lie" is a claim, and it opens a debate: one has to show why it is a lie. To say "tales of the ancients" is not a claim but a **classification**. It puts the text in a category and closes it.

And that category is chosen deliberately: **a tale is a thing that is neither true nor false.** One does not call a tale "wrong"; a tale makes no claim to truth in the first place. To put the text in the category of tale, then, is to place it **outside** the discussion of truth.

This is the most economical form of objection: **it requires no evidence whatever.**

And precisely for that reason it confirms the diagnosis of verse 12. An objection that requires no evidence is not a conviction resting on evidence. The person has not examined the text and rejected it; he has **declared that there is no need to examine it.**

There is one further layer. *Asāṭīr* are what "the ancients" wrote. So the objection rests on the **oldness** of the text: this is nothing new, it is a repetition of stories told before.

And beneath this lies an assumption: **what is old is not valid.** Novelty becomes the standard.

This assumption conflicts directly with the Qurʾān's own claim; for the Qurʾān presents itself not as something new but as **a confirmation of what the earlier messengers brought**. This matter was taken up in the section on al-Bayyina.

### A link of vocabulary: يكتبون / يسطرون / مرقوم

There are three separate words in the sūra having to do with writing, and this does not look like an accident:

| Verse | Word | Root | For what |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7, 9, 18, 20 | **كتاب** | ك-ت-ب | The record of deeds |
| 9, 20 | **مرقوم** | ر-ق-م | The character of the record: marked |
| 13 | **أساطير** | س-ط-ر | The name the denier gives the revelation: old written things |

So the sūra uses three roots having to do with writing, and two relate the record while one relates the rejection.

**And this picture emerges:** concerning the person who rejects the revelation as "what the ancients wrote", there is **a writing kept about him**. He cannot reject that writing, because he does not even know of it.

**I offer this as my own reading.** That the three roots are found in the sūra is a datum of the text; the contrast I set up among them is an interpretation.

## 83/14 — كَلَّا ۖ بَلْ ۜ رَانَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِم مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَكْسِبُونَ

*Kallā bal rāna ʿalā qulūbihim mā kānū yaksibūn*
"No! Rather, what they were earning has coated over their hearts."

The verse of cause in the sūra. The thirteenth verse said what they say; this verse says why they say it.

### كَلَّا بَلْ — two particles

The structure is the same as al-Infiṭār 82/9: *kallā bal.* As was taken up there, *kallā* rejects and *bal* changes direction (*iḍrāb*).

What is rejected is this time perfectly clear: the last word of the preceding verse — *asāṭīru'l-awwalīn.* The claim was set up, then demolished with a single particle.

And *bal* brings in the real cause: the matter is not what the text is, but **what has happened to them**.

### Variant reading and recitation: the pause between بَلْ رَانَ

In this verse there is a technical detail concerning recitation, and it serves to protect the sense.

In Arabic, when the letter **ل** is followed by **ر**, the usual rule is *idghām*: the lām is assimilated to the rāʾ and it is read as though *"barrāna"*.

Here that is not done. In the transmission of Ḥafṣ there is a short **pause** (*sakt*) between **بَلْ** and **رَانَ**: the sound is cut, no breath is taken, and then it continues.

**The reason is a concern for the sense:** if the assimilation is made, the two words are heard as one word and the presence of the particle *bal* is not heard. The pause makes the particle audible as a separate word.

This pause is one of the few in the transmission of Ḥafṣ and is recorded in the books of recitation. **Since I cannot give the practice in the other readings with certainty, I make no comparison.**

The reason the detail is worth recording is this: **a rule of recitation has been bent in order to protect the sense given by the grammar.** Sound has been made subordinate to the structure of the sentence.

### رَانَ — to coat over

The densest word of the sūra.

**Root: ر-ي-ن.** And the concrete sense of the root carries the whole weight of the verse.

**الرَّيْن (rayn):** the **layer that coats over** a thing. The most widespread description the lexicographers give: **the rust, the grime, the sediment that gathers on a bright and clean surface.** The dulling that forms on a sword, on a mirror, on a copper vessel.

The uses of the verb:

- *rāna'sh-shayʾu ʿalā qalbihi* — a thing coated over his heart.
- *rānat al-khamru ʿalā ʿaqlihi* — wine coated over his mind, covered it up.
- *rāna ʿalayhi'n-nawm* — sleep overcame him.
- *rīna bi-fulān* — someone was caught by something he could not get free of.

**The word has three distinguishing properties, and all three are decisive for the verse.**

**One. *Rayn* is a thin layer.**

Rust is not a thick crust coating a metal. At the beginning it is a dulling that is all but invisible. The first sediment on a mirror does not make the mirror unusable; it only diminishes it a little.

**And this accords with the whole logic of the sūra.** The sūra opened with *taṭfīf* — a diminishing too small to be noticed. Now it speaks of a layer on the heart too thin to be noticed.

**Two. *Rayn* forms by accumulation.**

Rust does not form in a single go. Damp, contact, neglect — and time. A little every day. And what is critical is this: **on no single day is that day's contribution noticeable.**

The subject of the verb in the verse confirms this: *mā kānū **yaksibūn*** — the structure *kāna* + imperfect conveys in Arabic **a continuing past**: "what they were earning", that is, over and over, as a habit.

The layer, then, is the product of repetition.

**Three. *Rayn* is born out of the reaction of the thing coated.**

This is the most important property of the word. Rust is not a substance smeared onto a metal from outside. **It is the metal itself transforming through reaction with its surroundings.** Rust is not added to metal; metal becomes rust.

This point was recorded at al-Baqara 2/7: *"Rust is not something smeared on from outside; it is born of the metal's own reaction."*

Now we have the word itself in hand, and it confirms that finding.

### Who is the agent?

The subject of the sentence has to be dwelt on, because the theological weight of the verse lies here.

The agent of the verb **رَانَ** is: **مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَكْسِبُونَ** — "what they were earning."

That is, what coats the heart is not a power coming from outside; it is **the person's own earnings.**

**This holds a distinct place among the Qurʾān's expressions for the closing of the heart.** The Qurʾān takes this subject up with several different words, and when the agents are compared a picture emerges:

| Expression | Place | Agent |
|---|---|---|
| **خَتَمَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ** | al-Baqara 2/7 | **God** |
| **طَبَعَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَيْهَا بِكُفْرِهِمْ** | al-Nisāʾ 4/155 | **God** (with the cause noted: their unbelief) |
| **جَعَلْنَا عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ أَكِنَّةً** | al-Anʿām 6/25 | **We** |
| **عَلَىٰ قُلُوبٍ أَقْفَالُهَآ** | Muḥammad 47/24 | Not stated (the locks are ascribed to the heart itself) |
| **رَانَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِم مَّا كَانُوا۟ يَكْسِبُونَ** | **al-Muṭaffifīn 83/14** | **Their own earnings** |

**What is seen in the table is this:** the same phenomenon is related with several different words, and the agent changes. It is mostly ascribed to God; in one place — here — it is ascribed directly to the person's own acts.

This matter was taken up at al-Baqara 2/7, and the conclusion reached there was this:

> *"The seal is not a barrier placed before a human being's choice but a locking that comes as the result of choice. A person covers over first; as the covering is repeated the cover thickens; in the end the possibility of return is closed in fact. The Qurʾān ascribes this last stage to God, because it is He who creates outcomes."*

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83/14 is that conclusion in a form **readable from the text**. In al-Baqara an inference was being made; here the Qurʾān says the same thing plainly.

In the section on al-Baqara this verse was already used as evidence, and it was cited there in translation. Now that the word itself has been analysed, the force of the evidence increases: **the subject of the verb is, at the level of grammar, their earnings.**

The theological note recorded in the section on al-Baqara holds here too: in the dispute between the Muʿtazila and the Sunnīs no side is taken in this commentary; and **none of the parties has argued that the human being is without responsibility.**

### كَسَبَ — to earn

**Root: ك-س-ب.** To obtain, to earn, to acquire.

The Qurʾān uses this root for what a human being obtains **by his own act**. The clearest example is in the last verse of Sūrat al-Baqara:

*"To it belongs what it has earned, and against it what it has acquired."* (al-Baqara 2/286) — *lahā mā kasabat wa ʿalayhā mā'ktasabat.*

Two forms are used in the verse: the base form (*kasabat*) and iftiʿāl (*iktasabat*). The classical exegetes explain the difference thus: the iftiʿāl form adds **effort and intent**; good is obtained of itself, while evil is obtained by expending labour. This is an interpretation widely mentioned in the sources.

**Attention must be paid to the commercial tone of the word.** *Kasb* is the word for earnings in Arabic — what is obtained by working. And this sūra is a **sūra of trade**: the measure, the balance, taking, giving, diminishing.

That is, the verse names the thing coating over the heart an **earning**.

**And here the tightest internal link of the sūra is set up:**

| Verse | Word | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **83/3** | **يُخْسِرُونَ** | They put another into loss |
| **83/14** | **يَكْسِبُونَ** | They earn — and what they earn coats their hearts |

What they shave off in the third verse becomes what they accumulate in the fourteenth. The twenty grams taken from the measure become the layer settling on the heart.

**That is, the sūra says: what they took, they really did take.** It does not say the earning is false; it says **what the earning is.**

The frame recorded in the section on al-ʿAṣr is completed here: *khusrān* is the outcome of a balance sheet. Al-Muṭaffifīn shows what the thing written into the credit column is.

**I offer this link as my own reading.** That the two words are found in the sūra is a datum of the text; the relation I set up between them is an interpretation.

### قُلُوب — hearts

**Root: ق-ل-ب.** As was recorded in the section on al-Humaza, the original sense of the root is **to turn, to be turned over, to pass from state to state**; the heart takes this name because it passes from state to state. *The word carries its own instability in its name.*

**Read together with the verse, this yields a result.**

The definition of the heart is "that which turns". Rust is what **stops the turning**: does a hinge that has rusted turn? Does a mechanism that has rusted work?

That is, *rayn* does not kill the heart; **it stops its motion.** The capacity to pass from state to state was the definition of the heart; the layer takes that capacity away.

**I offer this reading as my own inference.** The sense of the root was established in the section on al-Humaza; the application here is my interpretation.

And it links to the next verse of the sūra: a mirror that has taken rust reflects no image. The fifteenth verse will say exactly this — **deprivation of sight.**

### What the word says to the present

The process the word *rayn* describes is something recognisable in daily life, and it needs no religious frame.

**Moral sensitivity diminishes with repetition.** A behaviour done for the first time causes discomfort; the second time, less; after a certain number of times, none at all. This is not a weakening of the will; it is **a change in perception**. The person goes on doing the same thing but no longer feels the same thing.

And the most dangerous side of the process is that **no stage of it is noticeable.** Nobody thinks, "today I lost a little of my sensitivity." The loss is seen only in looking back — and to be able to look back one needs a measure.

The measure the verse gives is this: **not what you do, but what you feel.** If you do not feel today, faced with the same behaviour, what you used to feel, it is not the behaviour that has changed.

## 83/15 — كَلَّآ إِنَّهُمْ عَن رَّبِّهِمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ لَّمَحْجُوبُونَ

*Kallā innahum ʿan Rabbihim yawmaʾidhin la-maḥjūbūn*
"No! On that day they will be veiled from their Lord."

### The order of the punishment

This verse is the first link in the sūra's sequence of punishment. And the order is striking:

1. **Verse 15:** they are veiled from their Lord.
2. **Verse 16:** then they enter the fire.
3. **Verse 17:** then it is said to them.

**The fire comes second.**

Most of the classical exegetes find this order meaningful and explain it thus: the particle *thumma* (then) may convey here not time but **rank** — this use was taken up at al-Infiṭār 82/18. That is, the fire is not a stage coming after the veil; it is a degree **lower** than the veil.

On this reading the order of the verses says: **the heaviest punishment is not the fire but the veil.**

**This reading has support from the text:** the verse carries three reinforcements (*kallā*, *inna*, the lām), and the phrase *ʿan Rabbihim* has been brought forward before the predicate — the emphasis lies not on "they are veiled" but on **"from their Lord"**. The punishment is described not as being put somewhere but as being cut off from something.

### The root حجب

**Root: ح-ج-ب.** Its concrete sense: **to come between two things and prevent them from seeing one another.**

The derivatives:

- **حِجَاب (ḥijāb)** — a curtain, a covering, a barrier. What is stretched between two things.
- **حَاجِب (ḥājib)** — one who blocks; and in the language of the court, **the chamberlain** — the officer who stands between the sovereign and the people.
- **حاجب** — **the eyebrow.** What stands above the eye and protects it; what blocks the sweat and the light coming from the forehead.
- **مَحْجُوب** — veiled, blocked; one between whom and a thing a curtain has come.

**At the centre of the root there is a blocking, but a particular kind of blocking:** a *ḥijāb* does not close the road — it closes **the view**. This is the difference between a wall and a curtain.

The uses in the Qurʾān:

- *"It is not for a human being that God should speak to him except by revelation or from behind a veil."* (al-Shūrā 42/51). Here the *ḥijāb* does not block the speaking; it blocks **the seeing**.
- *"And between them is a veil."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/46). Between the people of the garden and the people of the fire.
- *"And they said: between us and you there is a veil."* (Fuṣṣilat 41/5). The words of the deniers — an interesting detail: **they declare the veil themselves.**

**Read together, Fuṣṣilat 41/5 and al-Muṭaffifīn 83/15 yield a result**, and I offer it as my own reading: in this world they placed the veil themselves; in the hereafter the veil is placed upon them. What was asked for is given.

### The setting of deprivation of sight as the punishment

Now we come to the most important matter of the verse.

**As the content of the punishment the verse puts not a pain but a deprivation.** And what they are deprived of is something they did not possess in this world in any case: nobody was seeing his Lord in this world.

That is, the punishment is not the taking away of something possessed; it is **the closing off of what could be**.

This can count as a punishment only if that thing is genuinely possible and genuinely valuable. And this is exactly the conclusion drawn from the verse in the classical sources.

### The disagreement: what does the verse say?

This verse is one of the most disputed in the history of theology. The views have to be recorded as they are.

| View | Reading | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **The veiling is a prevention of sight** | The verse states indirectly that the believers will see their Lord in the hereafter. For a punishment is a punishment only if it is particular to some; if everyone were veiled it would have no value as a threat | The verse's qualifications *yawmaʾidhin* and *innahum*; and further al-Qiyāma 75/22-23 |
| **The veiling is deprivation of mercy and honour** | It is not a matter of sight; what is meant is being kept far from God's favour, His honouring, His good pleasure. The "veil" is a metaphor | That God is not a body, and that seeing requires a direction |
| **Both together** | The veil covers both sight and favour | The two senses do not exclude one another |

**The first view is the one widely accepted in Sunnī theology, and its strongest support is the Qurʾān itself:**

*"On that day faces will be radiant; looking towards their Lord."* (al-Qiyāma 75/22-23)

**وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَّاضِرَةٌ • إِلَىٰ رَبِّهَا نَاظِرَةٌ**

These two verses are the exact opposite of al-Muṭaffifīn 83/15: there, being veiled; here, gazing. And both carry the qualification *yawmaʾidhin*.

**This verse has a particular place for this sūra as well, and it will be returned to at verse 24:** the two words occurring in al-Qiyāma 75/22-23 — *nāḍira* and *nāẓira* — occur in al-Muṭaffifīn too, in two consecutive verses (83/23 *yanẓurūn*, 83/24 *naḍrata*). The two sūras use the same two roots in the same order.

**The second view is that of the Muʿtazilī theologians**, and its ground is the principle that God is not a body: seeing requires direction, distance and orientation; these cannot be thought concerning God. For this reason they count the *ḥijāb* a metaphor.

**No side is taken in this commentary on this question.** The balance struck on the question of intercession in the section on al-Baqara holds here too: the views are recorded, no preference is counted binding. But this much can be said from the text: **the verse presents being veiled as a punishment and assigns it to a particular group.** The assigning itself is a datum of the text.

### A classical inference

Drawing from this verse an argument that the believers will see their Lord is very widespread in the literature of exegesis and theology. The logic of the inference is this: if being deprived of a thing is mentioned as a punishment, then that thing exists for those who are not deprived of it.

In the sources this inference is widely ascribed to **al-Shāfiʿī**. **Since I cannot give the soundness of the ascription with certainty, I do not transmit its wording**; I confine myself to recording that the inference itself is widespread in the literature and is ascribed to al-Shāfiʿī.

### The link between veil and rust

Between the fourteenth and fifteenth verses there is a link established by the words' own data.

| Verse | Word | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **14** | **رَانَ** | Coats over the heart — **from within** |
| **15** | **مَحْجُوبُون** | Comes between them and their Lord — **from without** |

Both are acts of **covering**. And both block sight: a mirror that has rusted does not reflect, what is behind a curtain is not seen.

**And there is a relation of direction between the two verses:** the first covering is their own earnings; the second covering is placed upon them.

That is, the sūra says: **the covering they placed themselves becomes the covering placed upon them.** What was voluntary in this world becomes a verdict in the hereafter.

**I offer this reading as my own inference.** That both words carry the sense of covering is a datum of the language; the relation I set up between them is an interpretation.

And this completes the idea at the head of the sūra: *taṭfīf* was a shaving too thin to be noticed. *Rayn* is a layer too thin to be noticed. *Ḥijāb* is a thin curtain. **All the objects of the sūra are thin** — and all three are complete in their effects.

## 83/16-17 — ثُمَّ إِنَّهُمْ لَصَالُوا۟ ٱلْجَحِيمِ • ثُمَّ يُقَالُ هَٰذَا ٱلَّذِى كُنتُم بِهِۦ تُكَذِّبُونَ

*Thumma innahum la-ṣālu'l-jaḥīm • Thumma yuqālu hādha'lladhī kuntum bihī tukadhdhibūn*
"Then they will surely enter the blaze. Then it will be said: 'This is what you used to deny'."

### لَصَالُوا۟ — the grammar

The expression **صَالُوا۟ ٱلْجَحِيمِ** is the plural of the active participle *ṣālūna* (entering, burning) entered into a construct. Because of the construct the plural nūn has dropped: *ṣālūna'l-jaḥīm → ṣālu'l-jaḥīm.*

The analysis of the root was made at al-Infiṭār 82/15: **ص-ل-ي**, to enter the fire, to be roasted in the fire. And as was recorded there, this root must not be confused with **ص-ل-و**, the root of the prayer; the two roots part at the final letter.

**There are again three reinforcements in the sentence:** *inna*, the lām, and the active participle's conveying of fixity.

The word **ٱلْجَحِيم** was also taken up at al-Infiṭār 82/14: the root is **ج-ح-م**, the burning coals of a fire, the flame at its most intense.

**The link between the two sūras appears here too:** al-Infiṭār said *"the fujjār are in the blaze"*; al-Muṭaffifīn says *"they will enter the blaze"*. The same word, for the same group.

### ثُمَّ ثُمَّ — three stages

The verses are joined twice with *thumma*, and a sequence of three stages is thereby set up:

| Stage | What happens | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **15** | Being veiled | **Sight** is cut off |
| **16** | Entering the fire | **The body** |
| **17** | A word being spoken | **Hearing** |

Three stages, three separate channels. And the last stage is not physical; it is **verbal**.

### يُقَالُ — passive

The verb is passive: "it is said". Who says it is not stated.

In the section on al-Humaza this choice was dwelt on: *"he will be flung" — who flings is not said; "it is closed over" — who closes is not said.* The same manner works here.

The effect of the passive pattern is this: **the one addressed falls into a wholly passive position.** There is no one before him to speak to; there is only a word that is said.

### هَٰذَا ٱلَّذِى كُنتُم بِهِۦ تُكَذِّبُونَ — the word itself

The content of the sentence has to be dwelt on, because what is said is not a torment but a **reminder**.

What is said is: *"This is the thing you denied."*

There is no accusation, no explanation, no justification. Only a **matching**: the link set up between what was rejected and what is met.

**And this is laid down as one layer of the punishment.** That is, entering the fire is not counted enough; this sentence has also to be said.

Why? Because pain by itself carries no verdict. For pain to acquire a meaning **its cause must be known**. The sentence establishes that link.

This accords with the whole sūra. From the start the sūra has been relating a matter of **knowing**: in verse 4 "do they never consider?", in verses 8 and 19 "what will make you know?", in verse 14 the coating of the heart — that is, the ruin of the capacity to know.

The seventeenth verse is the outcome of that matter of knowing: in the end it is known.

**And the tense of the verb is meaningful:** *kuntum bihī tukadhdhibūn* — *kāna* + imperfect, that is, **a continuing past.** Not "which you denied" but "which you used to deny". The same structure as *kānū yaksibūn* in verse 14.

That is, both the earning and the denying are named as acts repeated and continuing. **On neither side does the sūra describe a one-time act.**

## 83/18-21 — كَلَّآ إِنَّ كِتَٰبَ ٱلْأَبْرَارِ لَفِى عِلِّيِّينَ • وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا عِلِّيُّونَ • كِتَٰبٌ مَّرْقُومٌ • يَشْهَدُهُ ٱلْمُقَرَّبُونَ

*Kallā inna kitāba'l-abrāri la-fī ʿilliyyīn • Wa mā adrāka mā ʿilliyyūn • Kitābun marqūm • Yashhaduhu'l-muqarrabūn*
"No! The book of the good is in *ʿilliyyūn*. And what will make you know what *ʿilliyyūn* is? A marked writing; those brought near bear witness to it."

### The setting up of the symmetry

These four verses are the counterpart of verses 7-9, and the comparison can be made point for point:

| | **The fujjār (7-9)** | **The abrār (18-21)** |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | كَلَّآ إِنَّ | كَلَّآ إِنَّ |
| Book | كِتَٰبَ ٱلْفُجَّارِ | كِتَٰبَ ٱلْأَبْرَارِ |
| Place | لَفِى سِجِّينٍ | لَفِى عِلِّيِّينَ |
| Question | وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا سِجِّينٌ | وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا عِلِّيُّونَ |
| Answer | كِتَٰبٌ مَّرْقُومٌ | كِتَٰبٌ مَّرْقُومٌ |
| **Addition** | **—** | **يَشْهَدُهُ ٱلْمُقَرَّبُونَ** |

Three verses are on exactly the same pattern; the fourth exists only on the side of the good.

**This is another example of the matter of "the breaking of symmetry" taken up in the section on al-Qāriʿa.** There it was recorded that in relating two groups the symmetry is deliberately broken. Here the break comes in the form of **an extra sentence**, and it falls on the side of the good.

That is, the record is kept for both, both are *marqūm*, and for both the place is declared "unknowable". The difference is in one place only: **one of the two records has a witness.**

### ٱلْأَبْرَار — the abrār

The word was taken up at al-Infiṭār 82/13, and the analysis of the root was made at al-Baqara 2/44: **the original sense of the root ب-ر-ر is breadth**; *barr* — the land, the wide expanse facing the sea; *birr* — the goodness that is the opposite of narrowness.

And as was recorded in the section on al-Infiṭār, the *abrār* are, in the word's own logic, **"the broad ones"**.

**In this sūra the word occurs twice:** 18 (their book is in *ʿilliyyūn*) and 22 (they are in bliss). In al-Infiṭār it occurred once. Two of the three occurrences are exactly the same sentence: *inna'l-abrāra la-fī naʿīm* (al-Infiṭār 82/13 = al-Muṭaffifīn 83/22).

### عِلِّيُّون — *ʿilliyyūn*

**Root: ع-ل-و.** Height, elevation. *ʿAlā* — it rose. *ʿUluww* — loftiness. *Aʿlā* — highest. *ʿAliyy* — exalted; one of the names of God.

**The lexicographers dispute the form of the word, and the cause of the dispute is a grammatical oddity.**

*ʿIlliyyūn* is in form a **sound masculine plural** — that is, the regular plural used for rational beings (like *muslimūn*, *ṣāliḥūn*). But the singular of the word is not a rational being; it is the name of a place or of a rank.

The views:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The plural form is for magnification** | The word names a single place; the plural pattern is used to magnify the loftiness of that place |
| **It is a real plural: lofty ranks** | The plural of *ʿilliyy*; more than one high station is meant |
| **It is a proper name** | Not derived, but directly the name of a place |
| **By way of likening to rational beings** | Because the inhabitants of the place are rational beings, the word has taken their plural pattern |

**And the asymmetry here is worth recording:**

| | **سِجِّين** | **عِلِّيُّون** |
|---|---|---|
| Form | **Singular** | **Plural** |
| Direction | Down | Up |
| Sense of the root | To imprison | To rise |

The lower side is singular in form, the upper side plural.

The explanation given for this asymmetry in the classical sources is this: **height comes in degrees, lowness is a single place.** The Qurʾān speaks of "degrees" for the garden: *"They are of degrees with God."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/163).

**I transmit this as an explanation; I do not offer it as a settled ruling.** The grammatical oddity has other explanations too, and the lexicographers are not agreed.

The theme of height will continue in this sūra: in verse 27 **تَسْنِيم** will come, and that word too is from the root of "height". So the book of the good is above, and their drink is from above.

### كِتَٰبٌ مَّرْقُومٌ — a repetition

The sentence is exactly the same as verse 9. The analysis of the word was made there: **ر-ق-م**, to strike a trace and a mark onto something; a writing made unerasable and proof against confusion.

**And the repetition itself carries a verdict:** the character of the two books is the same. Kept with the same care, of the same exactness, marked in the same way.

That is, the record is impartial. The difference is not in the record but in **what is recorded**.

**This is directly connected with the measure theme of the sūra, and I offer it as my own reading:** the *muṭaffif* was the person who used the measure with a bias — the same instrument, two different results. The sūra shows that the record is not like this by framing the same sentence twice: **the same record, two different directions.**

### يَشْهَدُهُ ٱلْمُقَرَّبُون — witnesses

The place where the sūra breaks the symmetry.

**شَهِدَ** — root **ش-ه-د**. It carries two senses, and both are possible here:

1. **To be present.** *Shahida'l-majlisa* — he attended the gathering. *Mashhad* — a place where one is present. *Shāhid* — one who is there.
2. **To bear witness, to testify.** *Shahāda* — testimony.

The two senses are not separate from one another: to be able to testify one must have been present. The word holds both in a single root.

There are two readings of the verb in the verse:

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| **They are present** | The *muqarrabūn* are present where that book is; they have access to it |
| **They bear witness** | They testify to the truth of the record; the record is thereby confirmed |

Both are mentioned in the sources and they do not exclude one another.

**The second reading has the tighter link with the sūra**, and I state this as a preference. The reason is this: the subject of the sūra is **documentation.** The correctness of the measure, the exactness of the record, the markedness of the writing. The final stage of a document is **its confirmation by a witness.**

And the Qurʾān sets this up plainly in one place: al-Baqara 2/282, the longest verse, commands that **witnesses be called** after a debt is written down. That is, the writing is not counted sufficient on its own; witnesses are required.

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83/21 sets up the same two stages for the record of the hereafter: it was **written** (*marqūm*) and **witnessed** (*yashhaduhu*).

**I offer this link as my own reading.**

### ٱلْمُقَرَّبُون — those brought near

**Root: ق-ر-ب.** To be near. *Qurb* — nearness. *Qarīb* — near. *Qurbān* — that which brings near; the word for a sacrificial offering comes from here. *Aqribāʾ* — near relations.

**The word is a passive participle of the تفعيل form:** *muqarrab* — "one brought near". That is, **passive**: they do not draw near themselves, they are brought near.

This is the most important detail of the word. Nearness is named not as an achievement but as **a position given**.

Its use in the Qurʾān:

- *"And the foremost, the foremost; those are the ones brought near."* (al-Wāqiʿa 56/10-11)
- *"The Messiah will never disdain to be a servant of God, nor will the angels brought near."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/172)

**And this word occurs twice in the sūra:** 21 (they bear witness to the book) and 28 (they drink from the spring). Both are on the side of the good, and in both the *muqarrabūn* stand as though a group distinct from the *abrār*.

This distinction will be taken up at verse 28.

### What the witness is for

A question has to be asked: if the record is certain in any case, what need is there of a witness?

The explanation given in the classical sources: the testimony is not for the truth of the record but for **the honour of what is recorded**. Who has witnessed a document shows the document's value.

And this sets up the sūra's balance of punishment and reward. On the side of the fujjār there was no extra sentence; on the side of the abrār there is. That is:

> **The punishment is exactly what is deserved; the reward adds something on top of what is deserved.**

This is a balance the Qurʾān sets up plainly elsewhere: *"Whoever brings a good deed shall have ten like it; and whoever brings an evil deed shall be requited only with its like."* (al-Anʿām 6/160).

**The word occurring in al-Anʿām 6/160 is striking:** for the requital of the evil deed it says *mithluhā* — its like. That is, on the side of punishment the principle of **equivalence** works; on the side of reward it does not.

And *equivalence*, as was taken up in the section on al-Infiṭār, is the sense of the root **عدل**. That is, punishment is given with justice, reward with excess.

**I offer this link as my own reading**; I make no claim that al-Anʿām 6/160 explains the structure of al-Muṭaffifīn, only that the same principle appears in two places.

## 83/22-24 — إِنَّ ٱلْأَبْرَارَ لَفِى نَعِيمٍ • عَلَى ٱلْأَرَآئِكِ يَنظُرُونَ • تَعْرِفُ فِى وُجُوهِهِمْ نَضْرَةَ ٱلنَّعِيمِ

*Inna'l-abrāra la-fī naʿīm • ʿAla'l-arāʾiki yanẓurūn • Taʿrifu fī wujūhihim naḍrata'n-naʿīm*
"The good are in bliss. On couches they look. You will know in their faces the freshness of bliss."

### Verse 22: an exact repetition

The sentence **إِنَّ ٱلْأَبْرَارَ لَفِى نَعِيمٍ** is **word for word the same** as al-Infiṭār 82/13.

This sentence was taken up in the section on al-Infiṭār: the exact symmetry of the two verses (82/13-14), the sense of "being inside" carried by the particle *la-fī* (established in the section on al-ʿAṣr), the nominal sentence's conveying of fixity.

**This is the clearest evidence of the link between the two sūras.** Two sūras standing side by side in the arrangement of the muṣḥaf carry the same sentence.

But there is a difference, and it is important:

| | al-Infiṭār 82/13-14 | al-Muṭaffifīn 83/22 |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence | *Inna'l-abrāra la-fī naʿīm* | *Inna'l-abrāra la-fī naʿīm* |
| What follows | *Wa inna'l-fujjāra la-fī jaḥīm* — **the opposite comes at once** | **A description seven verses long** (23-28) |

In al-Infiṭār the sentence is laid down as a verdict and passed by. In al-Muṭaffifīn the same sentence becomes **the heading of a description**.

And this shows the difference between the two sūras: al-Infiṭār relates in nineteen verses the setting up of the reckoning; al-Muṭaffifīn sets up in thirty-six verses the **scene** of the reckoning.

### عَلَى ٱلْأَرَآئِك — on couches

**Its singular: أَرِيكَة.** Root: أ-ر-ك.

On the concrete sense of the word the lexicographers record this: an *arīka* is **a couch with a canopy or an awning** — with a curtain or covering drawn over it, set up inside a tent or a room. Not an ordinary bed or cushion, then, but a **sheltered and ornamented** seat.

*(It is also recorded that the word has been connected with the أَرَاك tree — the tree from which tooth-sticks are made. I transmit this derivation as a possibility; it is not certain.)*

**In the Qurʾān the word occurs only in descriptions of the garden:**

- *"Reclining there upon couches."* (al-Kahf 18/31)
- *"They and their spouses, in shade, reclining upon couches."* (Yā Sīn 36/56)
- *"Reclining there upon couches; they see there neither scorching sun nor bitter cold."* (al-Insān 76/13)

**And there is a difference here that has to be dwelt on.**

In all three verses above the verb used with the word is the same: **مُتَّكِئِين** — reclining, leaning.

In al-Muṭaffifīn 83/23 that verb is absent. In its place: **يَنظُرُون** — they look.

That is, the Qurʾān names the same object elsewhere together with **reclining**, and here together with **looking**.

**This choice can be explained by the sūra as a whole, and I offer it as my own reading:** in this sūra the matter is **seeing.** In the preceding group of verses the fujjār were veiled from their Lord (15). For the contrast here to be set up, a verb of **gazing** was needed in the description of the abrār. The sūra altered the usual description and put gazing in its place.

A contrast is also set up with the picture in verse 6: there people were **standing**; here the abrār are **seated.**

| Verse | Posture | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **83/6** | *yaqūmu'n-nās* — standing | Everyone, for the reckoning |
| **83/23, 35** | *ʿala'l-arāʾik* — seated | The abrār / the believers |

To stand is to wait; to sit is for the work to be finished.

### يَنظُرُون — they look

**Root: ن-ظ-ر.** To look, to watch, to turn towards. *Naẓar* — a look. *Manẓar* — what is looked at. *Intiẓār* — to wait (to wait watching for something). *Naẓīr* — a match, an equal (two things that look at one another).

**The object of the verb is not stated.** The question "what do they look at?" is left unanswered.

This is a technique the Qurʾān uses often, and it was taken up several times in earlier sections: the object of *iqraʾ* in Sūrat al-ʿAlaq, the referent of *anzalnāhu* in Sūrat al-Qadr, the object of *takāthur* in al-Takāthur were all left unstated. In the section on al-Takāthur it was given a name: **leaving indeterminate** — leaving the scope open.

**The views on the object:**

| View | Ground |
|---|---|
| **They look towards their Lord** | Al-Qiyāma 75/22-23: *"Faces will be radiant, looking towards their Lord."* And the contrast with verse 15: the fujjār veiled, the abrār looking |
| **They look at the blessings given to them** | The context: the preceding verse speaks of bliss |
| **They look at the state of their enemies** | Verse 35: the same sentence is repeated immediately after the believers' laughing at the deniers |
| **The object is deliberately left unstated** | The scope is left open; all of these are included |

**I prefer the fourth view, and its ground is the text itself:** the sentence occurs **twice** in the sūra (23 and 35) and its two contexts differ. In verse 23 the context is bliss, in verse 35 the reversal of the roles. That the same sentence should be used in two different contexts shows that the object is deliberately left open.

**This is a preference, and it is not binding.**

But this much can be said from the text with certainty: **the verb has to do with seeing**; and in verse 15 the same sūra said that for the other party seeing is blocked. The contrast between the two verses holds whatever the object may be.

### The sūra's theme of the eye

Here a weave that explains the whole sūra has to be recorded.

**Al-Muṭaffifīn is full of words having to do with the eye:**

| Verse | Word | Root | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| **15** | مَحْجُوبُون | ح-ج-ب | Seeing is **blocked** |
| **23** | يَنظُرُون | ن-ظ-ر | The abrār **look** |
| **24** | تَعْرِفُ / نَضْرَة | ع-ر-ف / ن-ض-ر | It is **seen** in their faces |
| **30** | يَتَغَامَزُون | غ-م-ز | They make **signs** with eye and brow |
| **32** | رَأَوْهُمْ | ر-أ-ي | When they **saw** them |
| **33** | حَافِظِين | ح-ف-ظ | They were not sent as **keepers** |
| **35** | يَنظُرُون | ن-ظ-ر | The believers **look** |

Seven separate places, seven separate roots, a single subject: **who is looking at whom.**

And the story the sūra tells can be read off this table:

> In this world the criminals looked at the believers and made signs with eye and brow (30, 32), and counted themselves keepers over them (33). In the hereafter the turn to look changes: they are veiled from their Lord (15), while the believers sit on couches and look (35).

**I offer this weave as my own reading**; that the words are found in the sūra is a datum of the text, the sequence I set up among them is an interpretation. But the tightness of the weave is striking: the sūra says that mockery is **a way of looking**, and that its punishment too has to do with looking.

### تَعْرِفُ — "you will know"

There is a sudden change of address in the verse: **تَعْرِفُ** — second person singular, active. "You will know, you will recognise."

An instance of the **shift of address** taken up in the section on al-Humaza: the narration is cut and the reader is drawn into the scene.

**Its function here is particular:** the verse places the reader in the position of a **witness**. It is not describing the scene; it is asking you to look at the scene. And what is described is a thing hard to describe — an expression on a face.

**A variant reading.** The word has been read in two ways:

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| **تَعْرِفُ** (active) — *naḍrata* in the accusative | "You will know in their faces the freshness of bliss" |
| **تُعْرَفُ** (passive) — *naḍratu* in the nominative | "In their faces the freshness of bliss is known" |

Both readings are transmitted. **Since I cannot give with certainty which reading belongs to which imam, I give no names.**

The difference in sense is small but real: in the first reading there is **someone looking**; in the second the state is known of itself. The first reading accords better with the sūra's theme of the eye.

The root **عَرَفَ** is also worth recording: **ع-ر-ف**, to know — but a particular kind of knowing. *Maʿrifa* is **to recognise again** something seen before; this is what distinguishes it from *ʿilm*. From the same root *ʿārif*, and *maʿrūf* — the good that everyone knows, recognises, counts as ordinary.

That is, the verse does not say "you will understand" but **"you will recognise"**. The expression on the face is not something that needs explaining; it is something known when it is seen.

### نَضْرَة — freshness

**Root: ن-ض-ر.** Its concrete sense: **freshness, brightness, vitality.**

The word's proper field is **the plant**: *naḍira'sh-shajaru* — the tree freshened, greened, came alive. *Naḍīr* — fresh, green, bright. *Nuḍār* — pure gold (on account of its brightness).

That is, the word carries **the look of a plant that has taken water** onto a human face. Whatever the difference is between a dry leaf and a fresh one, that is it.

**And this is the most concrete side of the verse:** the inner state striking through to the outward look. In relating the state in the garden the sūra gives no name of an emotion — it does not say happiness, peace, joy. It says **something seen in the face**.

**The uses in the Qurʾān:**

- *"On that day faces will be radiant; looking towards their Lord."* (al-Qiyāma 75/22-23). **وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَّاضِرَةٌ • إِلَىٰ رَبِّهَا نَاظِرَةٌ**
- *"And He gave them freshness and joy."* (al-Insān 76/11). *Wa laqqāhum naḍratan wa surūrā.*

**Al-Qiyāma 75/22-23 has to be dwelt on separately**, because its overlap with al-Muṭaffifīn is striking.

Two roots, in two verses:

| | **ن-ض-ر** (freshness) | **ن-ظ-ر** (to look) |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Qiyāma 75/22-23** | *nāḍira* | *nāẓira* |
| **al-Muṭaffifīn 83/23-24** | *naḍrata* (24) | *yanẓurūn* (23) |

Both sūras use the same two roots one after the other; only the order is reversed.

And the two roots are **very close in sound**: the only difference between them is the letters ض and ظ. In Arabic these two letters are distinct sounds, but they are close to one another.

**I record this as an observation and set up no claim.** That the two roots are separate is certain; the nearness of their sounds and their occurring together in two sūras are data of the text. The warning made for *falaq* and *falāḥ* in the section on al-Falaq holds here too: nearness of sound is not unity of root.

But this can be said: **the Qurʾān draws a link between "looking" and "shining."** The face that looks shines. And for the one whose looking is blocked in verse 15 there is no brightness in question.

### The repetition of the word نَعِيم

In verse 22, *naʿīm*; in verse 24, *al-naʿīm*. The same word two verses apart, once indefinite and once definite.

The indefinite states the kind and the greatness of the bliss: "such a bliss that". The definite returns to what was just named: "the freshness of that bliss."

This is an ordinary usage in Arabic: a thing is first named as indefinite, then repeated as definite.

## 83/25-26 — يُسْقَوْنَ مِن رَّحِيقٍ مَّخْتُومٍ • خِتَٰمُهُۥ مِسْكٌ

*Yusqawna min raḥīqin makhtūm • Khitāmuhu misk*
"They are given to drink of a pure wine, sealed; its seal is musk."

### يُسْقَوْنَ — they are given to drink

**Root: س-ق-ي.** To give water, to irrigate, to give to drink. *Siqāya* — the work of distributing water; it was the name of an office at the Kaʿba. *Suqyā* — a turn at drinking water (taken up at al-Shams 91/13: *nāqata'llāhi wa suqyāhā*).

**The verb is passive:** *yusqawna* — "they are given to drink". They do not take for themselves; it is given to them.

**This sets up a contrast with the opening verses of the sūra, and I offer it as my own reading:** the *muṭaffif* was the person who did the taking with his own hand and held the measure in his own hand (*iktālū* — he measured for himself). Here the abrār are on the side that is **given to**. The measure is not in their hands.

### رَحِيق — pure wine

**The word occurs in the Qurʾān only here.**

The sense the lexicographers give: **the purest, the most unmixed drink**; without sediment, admitting no admixture.

At the centre of the word lies **purity**. This bears directly on the subject of the sūra: *taṭfīf* is an intervention in the measure. For a drink to be *raḥīq* is for no intervention whatever to have been made in it.

The particle **مِن** here conveys the partitive (some of it) or the point of origin. The widespread reading is the partitive: some quantity of that drink.

### مَّخْتُوم — sealed

The most loaded word of the sūra, and a root the Qurʾān uses in two opposite contexts.

**Root: خ-ت-م.** It was taken up at al-Baqara 2/7, and the description recorded there is the key here:

> *"The root kh-t-m = to come to the end of a thing, and to set a seal. A seal does two things: it closes and it certifies. When an envelope is sealed it both becomes impossible to get into and is declared to have its contents settled."*

And it was said there that both these functions are at work: *"the situation has been closed and registered."*

**Now the same root stands before us in exactly the opposite context.**

| | **al-Baqara 2/7** | **al-Muṭaffifīn 83/25** |
|---|---|---|
| What is sealed | **The heart** | **A drink** |
| Result | Cannot be entered — **deprivation** | Untouched — **a gift** |
| In whose favour | Against him | In his favour |
| What it conveys | Being closed | **Being kept safe** |

**The same act, two opposite verdicts.** And the difference is which side of the seal you are on.

A similar observation was made in the section on al-Humaza: *"Being closed is of two kinds: the closedness that protects and the closedness that imprisons. The only difference between them is which side of the door you are on."*

Here the same distinction is set up with the same root.

### The link between seal and measure

Now we come to a point that explains the whole sūra.

**The seal is an invention of trade.**

The sealing of a good guarantees that **no one has touched** it. A sealed vessel has not been opened in transit; nothing has been taken out of it; it has not been adulterated. The seal is the instrument that closes the gap of trust between the carrier and the receiver.

And the opposite of the seal is exactly **taṭfīf**.

A *muṭaffif* is a person with access to the measure. The condition of his being able to shave at the edge is his being able to touch the vessel. **A sealed vessel cannot be shaved.**

That is, after saying *wayl* to those who steal from their measure, the sūra describes the drink of the good as **unshaveable**.

| | **al-Muṭaffifīn 83/1-3** | **al-Muṭaffifīn 83/25** |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Measure and balance | A sealed vessel |
| Access | Present — can be shaved | **Absent — cannot be shaved** |
| Who intervenes | The *muṭaffif* | **No one** |

**I offer this link as my own reading.** The function of the seal in trade is a historical datum; the relation I set up with the opening verses of the sūra is an interpretation, and I ascribe it to no classical exegete.

But the link has support from the text: the sūra is woven from beginning to end with words of **measure, record, document, mark**. *Marqūm* (marked) and *makhtūm* (sealed) both belong to the same field — both convey the **unalterability** of a document or of a good.

The vocabulary of the sūra is from beginning to end a merchant's vocabulary.

### خِتَٰمُهُۥ مِسْكٌ — "its seal is musk"

**A variant reading.** The word has been read in two ways:

- **خِتَامُهُ** (*khitāmuhu*)
- **خَاتَمُهُ** (*khātamuhu*)

Both readings are transmitted. **I give no names of imams.**

**The disagreement over the sense:**

| View | Sense | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Its seal is of musk** | The seal closing the mouth of the vessel is not clay or wax; it is musk | In this world a seal is made of a worthless substance. The gift here is that the seal itself is precious |
| **Its ending is musk** | *Khitām* is in the sense of "ending": the last sip of the drink, the taste it leaves in the mouth, is like musk | The sense "to come to the end of a thing" in the root *kh-t-m* — this sense was recorded at al-Baqara 2/7 |
| **Both together** | The word bears both senses | The root itself carries the two senses |

**Both readings work in the text and they do not exclude one another.**

The idea the first reading carries is this: **the seal is the packaging.** In this world the packaging is worth less than what is in it; it is thrown away. Here even the packaging is of musk.

The idea the second reading carries is deeper: **the taste of the ending.** What the drinks of this world have in common is that their ending is worse than their beginning — the taste left in the mouth, the state the next day. Here the opposite is said: **its ending is better than its beginning.**

And this accords with the whole sūra. From the start the sūra has been concerned with **outcomes**: the record, the reckoning, the requital. The sense "ending" in the root *kh-t-m* is the subject of the sūra.

**I record this not as a preference but as the contribution each of the two readings makes to the text.**

### مِسْك — musk

The most valuable scent known in that period. It is obtained from the musk deer and is worth its weight.

The word **occurs in the Qurʾān only here.**

*(It is widely said that the word entered Arabic from outside; since I can say nothing certain, I do not go into its etymology.)*

**The meaning of the choice is this:** scent is the sūra's one "unmeasurable" object.

Everything enumerated across the sūra is measurable: *kayl*, *wazn*, book, record, mark. Everything that can be measured is something whose measure can be played with.

**Scent is not measured.** It cannot be weighed, cannot be taken by the measure, cannot be shaved at the edge. And its presence is known not because it is measured but because **it is smelt**.

**I offer this as my own reading**; I make no claim that the sūra has made a deliberate choice, only that the word belongs to a different field from the rest of the sūra.

## 83/26 (continued) — وَفِى ذَٰلِكَ فَلْيَتَنَافَسِ ٱلْمُتَنَٰفِسُونَ

*Wa fī dhālika fa'l-yatanāfasi'l-mutanāfisūn*
"And in that let those who compete compete."

The sūra's one command.

### The place of the sentence in the sūra

It is worth pausing to look at where the sentence is placed.

The sūra is thirty-six verses. For twenty-five verses there are verdicts, descriptions, questions, threats and depictions. And in the very middle of a description of the garden — while the sealed drink is being related — the narration is cut and **a command** is given.

**There is no other command addressed to the hearer in the sūra.** There is no "give full measure", no "do not do wrong", no "believe". This is the one command the sūra gives: **compete.**

This is a choice that has to be dwelt on. The sūra relates cheating in the measure but does not command that the measure be put right. Instead it takes up **the motive at the source** of the cheating and turns it in another direction.

### Word order: the fronting of فِى ذَٰلِكَ

The ordinary order of the sentence would have been *fa'l-yatanāfasi'l-mutanāfisūna fī dhālika*. The verse pulls *fī dhālika* **in front of** the verb.

And an element pulled forward gains, as was recorded in the sections on al-Infiṭār and al-Humaza, **emphasis and restriction**.

**This is the place that carries the whole moral weight of the sentence.**

The command is not "compete"; it is **"in THIS let them compete"**. The emphasis falls not on the competing but on the **object** of the competition.

There is one further grammatical detail: the particle **فَ** that comes after the fronted element. In Arabic, when an element is fronted and a *fāʾ* follows it, the sentence acquires the flavour of the answer to a conditional clause. The classical grammarians liken this to the *fāʾ* of the apodosis.

On this reading the implicit sense of the sentence is: **"If there is to be competing, let it be in this."**

**I transmit this grammatical reading as an interpretation mentioned in the classical sources; I do not offer it as a settled ruling.**

### فَلْيَتَنَافَسْ — the lām of command

The **لِ** at the head of the verb is the **lām al-amr**, used in Arabic to give a command to the third person. In English it is answered by "let": *fa'l-yatanāfas* — "let them compete".

This pattern has a property: **it addresses not the hearer directly but a third person.** That is, the command is not "compete" but "let those who compete compete".

The effect of this is that the command stands not like a compulsion but like an **invitation**. A door is opened and shown to whoever wishes to enter.

### تَنَافُس — to compete

**Root: ن-ف-س.** This root was taken up at al-Shams 91/7; there the word *nafs* was analysed and it was recorded that the root carries both the sense of **breath** and the sense of **worth**. *Nafas*, *tanaffus*, *nafīs*, *munāfasa* were counted from the same root.

To gather the family of the root:

- **نَفْس (nafs)** — soul, person, the thing itself.
- **نَفَس (nafas)** — breath.
- **نَفِيس (nafīs)** — precious, valuable; what everyone wants.
- **مُنَافَسَة / تَنَافُس** — to compete with one another for something valuable.

**The link the lexicographers give is this:** *munāfasa* is to compete in order to obtain a thing because one sees it as *nafīs* (precious). That is, inside the word there is an acknowledgement that what is competed for **is valuable**.

**I transmit this explanation as the lexicographers have recorded it**; I say nothing certain about the order of derivation among the branches of the root's meaning.

### The pattern: تَفَاعُل — and a comparison with al-Takāthur

Now we come to the strongest link within the Qurʾān that the sūra makes.

**تَنَافَسَ is in the تَفَاعُل (tafāʿul) form.**

This form was taken up in detail in the section on al-Takāthur, and what was recorded there holds here exactly:

> *"The tafāʿul form conveys reciprocity in Arabic — it shows the verb going back and forth between two or more parties."*

And the examples given there: *taqātala* (they killed one another), *taʿārafa* (they came to know one another), *tafākhara* (they boasted against one another), and **تَكَاثَرَ** (they vied with one another in abundance).

**Now let us set the two words side by side:**

| | **تَكَاثُر** (al-Takāthur 102/1) | **تَنَافُس** (al-Muṭaffifīn 83/26) |
|---|---|---|
| Root | ك-ث-ر — **abundance** | ن-ف-س — **worth** |
| Pattern | **تفاعل** | **تفاعل** |
| Structure | Reciprocal competition | Reciprocal competition |
| Object | **Not stated** | **Stated and fronted** (*fī dhālika*) |
| The Qurʾān's verdict | **Censured** — *alhākum* (it distracted you) | **Commanded** — *fa'l-yatanāfas* |

**The same grammatical pattern, two opposite verdicts.**

This brings out what this sūra and al-Takāthur say together, and it has to be dwelt on.

**First result: competing itself is not censured.**

The Qurʾān censures a verb in the *tafāʿul* form in one place and commands it in another. So the fault is not in the pattern.

This was already stated in the section on al-Takāthur: *"what is criticised is not the accumulating itself but the accumulating's becoming relational."* Now al-Muṭaffifīn carries that finding a step further: **being relational is not, on its own, a fault either.** Competition, reciprocity, the effort to get past one another — the Qurʾān is able to command these.

**Second result: what makes the difference is the object of the competition.**

The object of *takāthur* is inside the word: **abundance.** The object of *tanāfus*, however, lies outside the word, and the verse is obliged to say it: *fī dhālika.*

**And this is a difference the grammar itself carries.**

*Takāthur* means "to compete in abundance"; its object is lodged in the meaning of the word and cannot be changed. One may ask how much is being competed over, but that the thing competed for is **abundance** is fixed.

*Tanāfus*, however, means "to compete for what is valuable", and **which thing is counted valuable** remains outside the word. This is why the verse must state the object — and in stating it, it brings it forward.

**Third result: the matter of countability.**

The sharpest observation recorded in the section on al-Takāthur was this:

> *"In the pattern of reciprocity there is no finish line. You can pass an absolute threshold… But a relative measure has no threshold, because the measure is in motion; as the other side advances, so does the target. This is why the natural end of takāthur is the place named in the second verse: the grave."*

Does the same problem not hold for *tanāfus*?

**The difference lies in the character of what is competed for, and I write this as my own reading.**

What *takāthur* competes for is **number**, and number is a divisible resource: more for one is less for the other. The competition is therefore necessarily against someone.

What *tanāfus* competes for is what is related in verse 25, and that thing **does not diminish.** One person's reaching it does not reduce another's share.

The difference between the two is therefore not only moral; it is **structural.** The one is a zero-sum competition, the other is not.

And this ties back to the opening verses of the sūra: the *muṭaffif* was playing exactly a zero-sum game. Every gram he took from the measure was subtracted from the other party. The competition commanded at the end of the sūra proposes a way out of that structure.

### The Qurʾān's other commands to compete

This command is not unique in the Qurʾān; its like is given with different verbs:

- *"So vie with one another in good things."* (al-Baqara 2/148). **فَٱسْتَبِقُوا۟ ٱلْخَيْرَٰتِ** — root **س-ب-ق**, to get ahead in a race.
- *"Race towards a forgiveness from your Lord and a garden whose breadth is as the breadth of heaven and earth."* (al-Ḥadīd 57/21). **سَابِقُوٓا۟** — the *mufāʿala* form, reciprocity.

**Al-Ḥadīd 57/21 has to be dwelt on separately**, because in the section on al-Takāthur the immediately preceding verse — al-Ḥadīd 57/20 — was named as that sūra's "dictionary": the verse in which *laʿb*, *lahw*, *zīna*, *tafākhur* and *takāthur* all occur.

That is, within two verses Sūrat al-Ḥadīd does exactly what al-Muṭaffifīn does:

| Verse | What it says |
|---|---|
| **al-Ḥadīd 57/20** | The life of this world is play, distraction, adornment, mutual boasting and **competing in abundance** (*takāthur*) |
| **al-Ḥadīd 57/21** | **Race** (*sābiqū*) — towards a forgiveness from your Lord |

**The same move, two verses apart.** First a competition is censured, then a competition is commanded. And the only thing that changes in between is the object.

**I record this overlap as an observation**; I make no claim that there is a deliberate link between al-Ḥadīd and al-Muṭaffifīn. But that the two texts set up the same structure shows that this is a consistent attitude of the Qurʾān.

### ٱلْمُتَنَٰفِسُون — those who compete

In the sentence the subject comes from the **same root** as the verb: *fa'l-yatanāfas… al-mutanāfisūn.*

In Arabic this is called *ishtiqāq* and is a device of rhetoric: the verbal and the nominal form of the same root coming side by side.

**Its function here is this:** the sentence takes competing not as a behaviour but as an **identity**. "Those who compete" — that is, people who already compete, who have competing in their character.

That is, the verse does not say, "let someone who does not compete start competing." It says, **"let someone who is competing anyway turn his competing here."**

This shows the sūra's understanding of the human being: the drive to compete is not seen as something that can be removed. It is seen as something that can be directed.

The principle recorded in the section on al-Shams holds here too: *"to suppress is not to destroy."* And in al-Takāthur it was related why the competition never ends. Al-Muṭaffifīn gives the competition not a finish line but a **direction**.

### What this says to the present

**Competing is not forbidden; what you compete over will be asked about.**

This is the side of the verse that looks most directly on the present, and it corrects a widespread misunderstanding.

A reading that counts competition itself an immorality is not supported by the Qurʾān. The verse asks for competition in the imperative.

The measure is this question: **does the thing I am competing over require someone else to lose in order for me to win?**

- If the competition is for a limited resource — position, share, rank, attention — then the competition is necessarily against someone. And in that competition *taṭfīf* carries an inescapable attraction: shaving at the edge is the cheapest way of getting ahead.
- If the competition is for something unlimited — knowledge, goodness, trustworthiness, the labour given — one person's advance does not reduce another's share.

**And a second measure:** who holds the measure of the competition? If you hold your own measure yourself — if you define your own success yourself — then you are in the situation of the opening verses of the sūra. Whoever holds the measure can shave at the edge.

## 83/27-28 — وَمِزَاجُهُۥ مِن تَسْنِيمٍ • عَيْنًا يَشْرَبُ بِهَا ٱلْمُقَرَّبُونَ

*Wa mizājuhu min tasnīm • ʿAynan yashrabu bihā'l-muqarrabūn*
"And its admixture is of Tasnīm; a spring from which those brought near drink."

### مِزَاج — admixture

**Root: م-ز-ج.** To mix, to add one thing to another. *Mizāj* — an addition, a mixture; and it later acquired the sense of "temperament, disposition" as well (from the idea of the mixture of the elements in the body).

The word occurs in three places in the Qurʾān, and in all three in the same context — the admixture of the drink of the garden:

- *"The admixture of their cup is camphor."* (al-Insān 76/5)
- *"There they are given to drink of a cup whose admixture is ginger."* (al-Insān 76/17)
- And here: *tasnīm.*

**A question arises:** in verse 25 the drink was described as **رَحِيق** — pure, unmixed. Now an admixture is spoken of. Is this not a contradiction?

The answer the classical exegetes give is this: *raḥīq* conveys being cleared of sediment and of any spoiling substance; *mizāj*, however, is the adding of something valuable. An addition that **increases**, then, not one that diminishes.

And this accords with the subject of the sūra: *taṭfīf* was an intervention that diminished; the intervention here adds.

### تَسْنِيم — *tasnīm*

**The word occurs in the Qurʾān only here.**

**Root: س-ن-م.** And the concrete sense of the root is decisive: **height.**

- **سَنَام (sanām)** — **the hump of a camel.** The highest point of the camel. In Arabic it is used for the highest point of anything.
- **تَسْنِيم (tasnīm)** — to raise. From the same root, the raising of a grave into a mound is also given this name.
- **سَنِمَ** — it rose, its hump grew.

**There are two views on the sense of the word:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **A proper name** | It is the name of a spring in the garden; the word is not derived |
| **A description** | It means "coming from above"; a water that flows from a height |

The two do not exclude one another: a name may be derived from a description.

**And the word's place in the sūra is meaningful.**

The sūra had set up two directions: **سِجِّين** below, **عِلِّيُّون** above. Now a third word of "above" comes: *tasnīm.*

| Word | Root | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **سجين** | س-ج-ن — prison, the lowest | **Down** |
| **عليون** | ع-ل-و — height | **Up** |
| **تسنيم** | س-ن-م — height | **Up** |

That is, the book of the good is above, and the water they drink comes from above too. The sūra uses the vertical axis consistently.

**I record this as an observation**; the root senses of the words are the lexicographers' data, the axis I set up among them is an interpretation.

### عَيْنًا — its place in the grammar

The word is in the **accusative**, and the grammarians differ over the reason:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Appositive substitute** | It is a substitute for *tasnīm*: "of Tasnīm, that is, of a spring" |
| **Accusative by way of praise** | In the accusative through an implied verb ("I praise, I mean"); it conveys commendation |
| **Specification** | An explanatory element removing the indeterminacy |
| **Circumstantial** | It states a state |

The difference in sense is small. The most widespread are the first two.

The word **عَيْن** is a word of many senses in Arabic: **eye**, **spring**, **the thing itself / its essence**, and **spy**. What all of these have in common is that they concern a source or a seeing — a spring is the "eye" of water; the *ʿayn* of a thing is its essence.

**And a nearness of wording arises with the sūra's theme of the eye:** the same word means both "eye" and "spring", and this sūra is from beginning to end concerned with seeing. **I record this as an observation about the wording; I set up no claim about the sense**, because in the verse the word plainly means "spring".

### يَشْرَبُ بِهَا — the matter of the particle

The expected expression would have been *yashrabuhā* or *yashrabu **minhā***. The verse uses **بِ**.

The explanations the classical grammarians give:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| ***Bāʾ* stands in place of *min*** | "They drink of it". The use of particles in place of one another (*tanāwub al-ḥurūf*) |
| ***Bāʾ* is in the sense of "with it"** | That is, they drink the water of Tasnīm not **as an admixture** but directly; or they quench their thirst with it |
| **The verb has taken the particle of *yarwā* (to be quenched), whose sense it carries** | *Rawiya bihi* — he was quenched with it. The structure called *taḍmīn* |

**The second view produces a difference of rank between the verses, and it is widely mentioned in the classical sources:**

> The abrār drink *raḥīq*, and *tasnīm* is **mixed into** it (*mizājuhu min tasnīm*).
> The *muqarrabūn*, however, drink of *tasnīm* **itself**.

That is, the same water reaches the two groups in two different ways: to the one as an admixture, to the other pure.

**This reading accords with the distinction the sūra set up earlier:** in verse 21 the *muqarrabūn* were bearing witness to the book of the abrār — that is, they appeared as distinct from them, in a position above them. Here the same distinction is repeated in the drink.

**This is a preference, and it is not binding.**

### Two groups: the abrār and the muqarrabūn

The sūra sets up two ranks, and it does so without saying it plainly:

| | **ٱلْأَبْرَار** | **ٱلْمُقَرَّبُون** |
|---|---|---|
| Their book | In *ʿilliyyūn* | — (they bear witness to it) |
| Their drink | *Raḥīq* + an admixture of *tasnīm* | *Tasnīm* itself |
| Position | In bliss | Brought near |

This distinction accords with the threefold classification in Sūrat al-Wāqiʿa: *"When you are three kinds: the companions of the right… the companions of the left… and the foremost, the foremost; those are the ones brought near."* (al-Wāqiʿa 56/7-11, in paraphrase).

**The note recorded in the section on al-Infiṭār finds its answer here:** there it was stated that that sūra's twofold division (abrār / fujjār) does not divide people into two boxes, that the Qurʾān says elsewhere that there are degrees, and this sūra was pointed to. Now that pointer falls into place: **al-Muṭaffifīn sets up a difference of degree on the side of the good.**

And it is striking that **no such distinction of degree is made on the side of the bad.** The fujjār are a single group.

This is a continuation of the asymmetry recorded at verse 21: on the side of good there is extra detail, on the side of evil there is none.

## 83/29-31 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ أَجْرَمُوا۟ كَانُوا۟ مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ يَضْحَكُونَ • وَإِذَا مَرُّوا۟ بِهِمْ يَتَغَامَزُونَ • وَإِذَا ٱنقَلَبُوٓا۟ إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِهِمُ ٱنقَلَبُوا۟ فَكِهِينَ

*Inna'lladhīna ajramū kānū mina'lladhīna āmanū yaḍḥakūn • Wa idhā marrū bihim yataghāmazūn • Wa idha'nqalabū ilā ahlihimu'nqalabū fakihīn*
"Those who committed crimes used to laugh at those who believed. When they passed by them they would wink to one another. And when they returned to their households they would return delighting."

### The beginning of the sūra's last section

Up to verse twenty-eight the sūra related two groups: the fujjār and the abrār. In the twenty-ninth verse **the scene changes**: it turns from the hereafter to this world.

And the function of this section is this: in the first half of the sūra a **verdict** was given; in this section the **behaviour** that verdict rests on is shown.

There is also a change of manner: verses 1-28 were sentences of verdict and description; verses 29-33 tell a **story**. A narration built with four *idhā*'s, advancing scene by scene.

### أَجْرَمُوا۟ — to commit a crime

**Root: ج-ر-م.** And the lexicographers have recorded an explanation of the concrete sense of the root:

*Jarm* means **to cut, to pluck** — in particular **to pluck fruit from its tree.** *Jarama'n-nakhla* — he gathered the dates, he cut them off. *Jarīm* — cut off, plucked.

From here *jurm* — crime. The explanation the lexicographers give: a crime is an act that **tears the person from where he stands** or that **cuts a bond.**

**I transmit this explanation as the lexicographers have recorded it**; I do not offer it as a settled etymology.

The word's form *ajrama* (the ifʿāl form) means "to commit a crime". *Mujrim* is a frequently used word in the Qurʾān.

**And one fixed expression is striking:** *lā jarama* — a formula of oath or assertion occurring in the Qurʾān, in the sense "assuredly, without any doubt". Its connection with the root's sense "to cut" runs through the idea of "decisively".

**The choice of the word is meaningful:** the verse does not say "the unbelievers" or "the deniers". It says **"those who committed crimes"**. That is, the mockery is named not as a matter of belief but as an **act**.

And this repeats the diagnosis of verse 12: the source of denial is a moral ground.

### كَانُوا۟ … يَضْحَكُونَ — they used to laugh

**The structure: *kāna* + imperfect.** In Arabic this conveys **a continuing past**: not an event that happened once but a behaviour that has **become a habit**.

The same structure occurred twice earlier in the sūra: *mā kānū yaksibūn* (14) and *kuntum bihī tukadhdhibūn* (17). In all three a repeated, settled act is being related.

**ضَحِكَ** — root **ض-ح-ك**: to laugh. And when the verb comes with the particle **مِن** it means "to laugh at someone, to mock" — the same structure as the English "to laugh at".

**And there is a detail in the word order:** *kānū **mina'lladhīna āmanū** yaḍḥakūn.* The phrase (*mina'lladhīna āmanū*) has been brought in front of the verb.

An element brought forward gains emphasis. That is, the verse stresses not their laughing but **whom they laughed at**: those who believed, in particular.

This shows that the mockery was not random. A target had been chosen.

### يَتَغَامَزُون — signs with eye and brow

**Root: غ-م-ز.** Its concrete sense: **to nudge, to press, to make a sign with the eye or the brow.**

- *ghamaza bi-ʿaynihi* — he made a sign with his eye.
- *al-ghamīza* — a fault, a weak point; a defect found in someone that can be pointed out.
- *ghamaza'd-dābba* — he felt out the lameness of the animal.

**The pattern: تَفَاعُل (tafāʿul)** — reciprocity. *Taghāmaza* — they winked **to one another**.

**This pattern explains the nature of mockery, and it has to be dwelt on.**

The verb is reciprocal, that is, **it requires at least two people.** Mockery is not done alone. If one person is mocking another, a third person must see it; otherwise the act is not completed.

That is, mockery is an act that appears two-sided but is in fact **three-sided**: the mocker, the mocked, and the one who sees the mockery.

And the real addressee is the third. The wink is made not to the one mocked but **to the one beside him**.

**This connects directly with لمز, taken up in the section on al-Humaza.** It was recorded there: *"لمز — to fault, to find defects, to belittle with a sign of brow and eye, to point in a way that lowers someone in others' esteem."*

And the conclusion reached there: *"the common ground of the two words is the act itself — to make someone small — and the difference lies in the instruments of that act."*

Al-Muṭaffifīn 83/30 shows exactly this form of *lamz* — and with the *tafāʿul* pattern adds that it is a **group act**.

**And the link set up in the section on al-Humaza appears here once more:** there the joining of mockery and the hoarding of wealth in one and the same person was taken up, and this conclusion was reached — *"belittling is the mechanism that turns accumulation into worth."*

Al-Muṭaffifīn too holds the same two things together: those who shave off the measure (1-3) and those who laugh at the believers (29-33). The sūra does not join them plainly, but they stand at the two ends of the same text.

**I record this link as my own reading.**

### وَإِذَا مَرُّوا۟ بِهِمْ — "when they passed by them"

A small detail, but it sets up the scene.

**The party passing is the criminals.** *Marrū bihim* — they pass. The believers are standing still.

That is, the scene is this: one group of people is standing somewhere; another group passes by them, and as they pass they look at one another and wink.

This picture accords with the other information the Qurʾān gives about the first years of Mecca: the believers were few in number, socially weak, and outside the main current of the society. As was taken up in the section on Quraysh, the leading men of Mecca held the trade and the standing of the city in their hands.

The verses recorded in the section on al-Humaza complete this picture:

- *"And when they see you they take you only in mockery."* (al-Furqān 25/41)
- *"Why was this Qurʾān not sent down to a great man of the two cities?"* (al-Zukhruf 43/31)

And the conclusion reached there: *"The objection is not theological but positional."* At al-Muṭaffifīn 83/32 the wording of that objection too will be given.

### ٱنقَلَبُوٓا۟ … ٱنقَلَبُوا۟ — a repeated verb

The same verb occurs twice in the verse: *idha'nqalabū… inqalabū.*

**Root: ق-ل-ب.** As was recorded in the section on al-Humaza, the original sense of the root is **to turn, to be turned over, to pass from state to state**; *qalb* (the heart) is from this root, because it passes from state to state.

*Inqalaba* (the infiʿāl form) — he turned, he turned back. And in Arabic it is used especially for **returning home**.

**The repetition of the verb is not a grammatical necessity.** The sentence could have been *wa idha'nqalabū ilā ahlihim kānū fakihīn*. The repetition is a choice.

In classical rhetoric the function of such repetitions is **emphasis** and **drawing attention**. Here there is a further function: the second occurrence of the verb states **how** the returning was. That is, "they returned" and "they returned delighting" are said separately.

**And the root's sense of "passing from state to state" is at work here.** The word itself conveys **a change of state**. But the verse says exactly the opposite: nothing changes in the returning. They come back in the same good spirits.

**I offer this reading as my own inference.**

### إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِهِمُ — "to their households"

This phrase is the sharpest detail of the section, and it is generally passed over.

The verse says **where the mockery ends**: it does not end. It goes home.

That is, the mockery made in the course of the day is recounted at home in the evening. The scene does not close; **it is passed on.**

This has two consequences:

**One. The mockery leaves no trace on the conscience.** If the person were troubled by what he had done, he would not be in good spirits on the way home. By saying *fakihīn* the verse records exactly the opposite: what was done is carried not as a burden but as a **gain**.

**Two. The mockery multiplies within the household.** A mockery recounted produces the same way of looking in those who hear it. The behaviour, that is, passes from one person to a household.

**I offer this finding as my own reading**; the verse does not speak plainly of a passing on, but the expression "returning to their households delighting" is open to this reading.

### فَكِهِين — delighting

**Root: ف-ك-ه.** An interesting root: it carries both the sense of **fruit** and the sense of **cheer**.

- **فَاكِهَة (fākiha)** — fruit. In particular fruit that is not a staple food, eaten for pleasure.
- **تَفَكُّه (tafakkuh)** — to eat fruit; and figuratively to pass the time pleasantly, to jest.
- **فَكِه (fakih)** — cheerful, jesting, in good spirits.

**The link the lexicographers set up is this:** fruit is what is eaten not for need but **for pleasure**. Cheer is in the same category — not necessary, but pleasant.

That is, the word names the mockery an **entertainment**. What is done is not a quarrel, an enmity, a struggle; it is **something sweet.**

**A variant reading.** The word has been read in two ways:

| Reading | Pattern | Difference in sense |
|---|---|---|
| **فَكِهِين** | Quasi-participial adjective | **A settled state**: cheerfulness is their condition |
| **فَٰكِهِين** | Active participle | **The state at that moment**: at that time they were delighting |

Both readings are transmitted. **I give no names of imams.**

The difference in sense is small but real: the first conveys permanence, the second momentariness. The context appears better suited to the first, because throughout the sūra continuity has been stressed with the *kāna* + imperfect structure.

Another use in the Qurʾān is worth recording: *"And a bounty in which they took delight."* (al-Dukhān 44/27) — in the enumeration of the things the people of Pharaoh left behind. Again the same root, again the same tone.

## 83/32-33 — وَإِذَا رَأَوْهُمْ قَالُوٓا۟ إِنَّ هَٰٓؤُلَآءِ لَضَآلُّونَ • وَمَآ أُرْسِلُوا۟ عَلَيْهِمْ حَٰفِظِينَ

*Wa idhā raʾawhum qālū inna hāʾulāʾi la-ḍāllūn • Wa mā ursilū ʿalayhim ḥāfiẓīn*
"And when they saw them they would say, 'these are surely astray'. Yet they had not been sent as keepers over them."

### The form of the claim

The words spoken carry two reinforcements: **إِنَّ** and **لَ**.

*Inna hāʾulāʾi la-ḍāllūn* — "these are surely, certainly astray."

In Arabic emphasis is calibrated to the position of the one addressed. Emphasis this heavy is used either against an addressee who denies, or to show **the speaker's own certainty**.

Here it appears to be the second: the speakers are saying it to one another, and there is no hesitation whatever in what they say.

**This sets up a contrast with the fourth verse of the sūra**, and it is striking:

| Verse | Who | State of knowledge |
|---|---|---|
| **83/4** | The criminals | *Alā **yaẓunnu*** — not even a **supposition** |
| **83/32** | The same criminals | *Inna… la-ḍāllūn* — **a firm verdict** |

That is, people who will not entertain so much as a possibility about their own end pass firm verdicts about others.

**I offer this contrast as my own reading**; the difference of certainty in the two verses is a datum of the text, the relation I set up between them is an interpretation.

But the structure of the sūra supports it: verse 4 and verse 32 speak about the same subjects at the two ends of the sūra, and both concern the matter of **knowing**.

### ضَآلُّون — astray

**Root: ض-ل-ل.** This root was taken up in the section on al-Fātiḥa (*wa lā'ḍ-ḍāllīn*) and in the section on al-Ḍuḥā (*wa wajadaka ḍāllan fa-hadā*).

The finding recorded in the section on al-Ḍuḥā is especially meaningful here: *"the same root in two different functions: a temporary initial state and a permanent attribute; what settles the verdict is not the root but the context."*

The concrete sense of the root is **to lose the way**: to lose one's bearings in the desert, to be unable to tell where something is.

**And the choice of the word is meaningful.** The criminals do not say "liars" or "unbelievers". They say **"astray"** — that is, "they have lost the way", "they are lost".

This is less an accusation than an expression of **pity and belittlement**. One is not angry with someone who is lost; one makes light of him.

The tone of the utterance, then, is not a tone of anger but a tone of **looking down**. And this accords with the *fakihīn* (delighting) of the preceding verse.

### وَمَآ أُرْسِلُوا۟ عَلَيْهِمْ حَٰفِظِينَ — the tightest external link of the sūra

One of the most striking sentences of the sūra.

**The structure of the sentence:**

- **مَا** — negation.
- **أُرْسِلُوا۟** — passive: "they were not sent". Who sends is not said.
- **عَلَيْهِمْ** — "over them".
- **حَٰفِظِينَ** — as "keepers / guardians". In the circumstantial position.

### The link with al-Infiṭār 82/10

This sentence meets the tenth verse of the preceding sūra almost word for word:

| | **al-Infiṭār 82/10** | **al-Muṭaffifīn 83/33** |
|---|---|---|
| Wording | **وَإِنَّ عَلَيْكُمْ لَحَٰفِظِينَ** | **وَمَآ أُرْسِلُوا۟ عَلَيْهِمْ حَٰفِظِينَ** |
| Sense | "Over you there **are** keepers" | "They were **not sent** as keepers over them" |
| Particle | عَلَىٰ + pronoun | عَلَىٰ + pronoun |
| Word | حَافِظِين | حَافِظِين |
| Verdict | **Affirmative and reinforced** | **Negative** |

**Two verses use the same word with the same particle and give opposite verdicts.**

In the section on al-Infiṭār the root **حفظ** was taken up and two branches of meaning were recorded: **to protect** and **to record / to memorise**; and that the two are joined in a single idea — *not to let a thing be lost.*

It was also noted that the particle *ʿalaykum* conveys both **oversight** and **being against**.

**Now what emerges when the two verses are read together is the following, and I offer it as my own reading:**

> **To be a keeper requires being sent.**

The *ḥāfiẓīn* of al-Infiṭār are appointed: their position has been given to them. The people in al-Muṭaffifīn, however, have **placed themselves** in that position.

And the verb of the verse stresses exactly this point: **أُرْسِلُوا۟** — "they were not sent". The verb is passive, because the matter is not who did not send them; it is **that they were not sent.**

That is, the verse's objection is not that the verdict they passed was wrong — it may be objecting to that too — but that **they do not hold the authority to pass a verdict.**

### The Qurʾān's other verses along the same line

This idea is repeated in several places in the Qurʾān, and what is striking is this: **it is mostly said to the Prophet.**

- *"We have not made you a guardian over them."* (al-Anʿām 6/107)
- *"You are not a tyrant over them."* (al-Ghāshiya 88/22)
- *"You are not a trustee over them."* (the continuation of al-Anʿām 6/107; the like of it at al-Zumar 39/41)

**This increases the weight of the verse.** When the Qurʾān says that people have no authority of oversight over one another, it says it first **to the Prophet.**

The bound, then, is the same for everyone. And al-Muṭaffifīn 83/33 recalls the same bound for those who mock.

**I record this link as an observation**; the shared words of the verses are a datum of the text, the conclusion I draw between them is an interpretation.

### The function of the sentence in the section

The thirty-third verse cuts the story and lays down a **verdict**. For four verses the scene had been related: they laugh, they wink, they return home delighting, they say "these are astray".

And the verse removes the assumption on which the whole scene rests: **these people are not in a position to pass this verdict.**

Then in verse 34 the scene will change and the roles will exchange places.

## 83/34-36 — فَٱلْيَوْمَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ مِنَ ٱلْكُفَّارِ يَضْحَكُونَ • عَلَى ٱلْأَرَآئِكِ يَنظُرُونَ • هَلْ ثُوِّبَ ٱلْكُفَّارُ مَا كَانُوا۟ يَفْعَلُونَ

*Fa'l-yawma'lladhīna āmanū mina'l-kuffāri yaḍḥakūn • ʿAla'l-arāʾiki yanẓurūn • Hal thuwwiba'l-kuffāru mā kānū yafʿalūn*
"So today those who believed laugh at the deniers. On couches they look. Have the deniers been requited for what they used to do?"

### The setting up of the symmetry

The thirty-fourth verse is the mirror of the twenty-ninth:

| | **83/29** | **83/34** |
|---|---|---|
| Time | *kānū* — past, continuing | *fa'l-yawma* — **today** |
| Who laughs | *alladhīna ajramū* | *alladhīna āmanū* |
| Who is laughed at | *mina'lladhīna āmanū* | *mina'l-kuffār* |
| Verb | *yaḍḥakūn* | *yaḍḥakūn* |

Of four elements two have changed places, one (the verb) has stayed the same, one (the time) has changed.

**This is a structure the Qurʾān uses often, and it was taken up several times in earlier sections:** in al-Humaza the reversal between *jamaʿa* (he gathered) and *nubidha* (he will be flung); in al-Māʿūn the pairing between *yaduʿʿu* (he pushes away) and *yudaʿʿūna* (they are pushed) at al-Ṭūr 52/13.

Here the reversal is plainer, because **the same verb** is used.

### An asymmetry: أَجْرَمُوا۟ and ٱلْكُفَّار

The symmetry is not complete, and the difference is worth dwelling on.

In the twenty-ninth verse those who laugh were named **by a verb**: *alladhīna **ajramū*** — "those who committed crimes". In the thirty-fourth verse those laughed at are named **by a noun**: *al-**kuffār*** — "the deniers".

In the same way, those who believe are named by a verb in both verses: *alladhīna āmanū.*

| | Verse 29 | Verse 34 |
|---|---|---|
| The believers | *alladhīna āmanū* — **verb** | *alladhīna āmanū* — **verb** |
| The others | *alladhīna ajramū* — **verb** | *al-kuffār* — **noun** |

**On one side, that is, the naming passes from verb to noun.**

In Arabic a verbal sentence conveys *ḥudūth* (an event's taking place), while a noun conveys *thubūt* (settledness, fixity). This distinction was taken up in the sections on al-Takāthur and al-Humaza.

On this basis the verses may be saying: in this world there was **something done** (they committed crimes); in the hereafter that thing has become **an identity** (the deniers).

**I offer this reading as my own inference and do not offer it as a settled ruling**; the choice of word may have other explanations — in particular the requirements of the verse-ending (the rhyme) affect the choice of words in Arabic texts.

And one further note is needed: that the believers are named **by a verb** in both verses accords with the principle recorded in `USLUP.md` — the Qurʾān describes an attribute, it does not hand out identities. Faith is named as something done.

### فَٱلْيَوْم — "today"

The word carries the **definite article** and is an adverb of time: "that day", "this very day".

And the **فَ** at the head conveys consequence: as the counterpart of the preceding verses.

**The choice of the word is meaningful.** The verse does not say "on that day" (*yawmaʾidhin*); it says **"today"** (*al-yawm*).

This changes the position of the narration: the sentence is framed as though spoken from within the hereafter. The reader hears a sentence as though he were inside that day.

The same manner was present in verse 17: *"This is what you used to deny"* — there too a "now" looking back on a past was set up.

### The matter of the believers' laughing

Here one has to stop and ask a question, because the verse is at first sight uncomfortable: **how can good people's laughing at people in a bad state be a reward?**

The answers the classical exegetes give:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The requital is of the same kind** | The punishment is given in the language of the act itself. This is a common method of the Qurʾān — it was shown in the section on al-Humaza in a five-line table |
| **The laughing is not mockery but joy** | *Ḍaḥk* is used without mockery too. The laughing here may be the joy felt at the truth's coming out |
| **A declaration that the mockery of this world is void** | The laughing is not an affliction directed at the other party; it is the showing that the verdict of this world has been reversed |

**One point can be read off the text and needs recording:** in verse 29 the verb of laughing came with the particle *min* and the phrase was brought forward — that is, the target was being stressed. The same structure is in verse 34.

But there is a difference: **verse 35 says, immediately after the laughing, "on couches they look."** That is, the believers are seated somewhere, looking. They do not do what the mocker does — pass by and wink.

**I record this as an observation**; that the two scenes are not exactly the same is visible in the text.

### عَلَى ٱلْأَرَآئِكِ يَنظُرُونَ — an exact repetition

The sentence is word for word the same as verse 23.

And as was recorded at verse 23, the object of the verb is left unstated there as here. But here the context gives a clue: the preceding verse speaks of the deniers.

**This supports one of the views given at verse 23** ("they look at the state of their enemies"), but does not cancel the others. That the same sentence should be used in two different contexts strengthens the preference that the object is deliberately left open.

**And the repetition itself carries a function:** the sentence used in verses 22-28 while relating the state of the abrār is repeated here for the believers. The two sections of the sūra — the description of the hereafter and the story of this world — are thus tied to one another by the same sentence.

### هَلْ ثُوِّبَ — the last verse of the sūra

The sūra ends with a **question**.

**هَلْ** here is not for seeking information; it is a pattern used in Arabic for *taqrīr* (eliciting assent) and *tahakkum* (saying something by way of mockery). Its answer is known: yes, they have been.

### ثُوِّبَ — the root ث-و-ب

One of the last words of the sūra, and its root carries the whole logic of the sūra on its own.

**Root: ث-و-ب.** Its concrete sense: **to return, to come back, to revert to one's origin.**

- **ثَابَ (thāba)** — he returned, he came back to his origin. *Thāba ilayhi ʿaqluhu* — his senses came back to him.
- **مَثَابَة (mathāba)** — a place returned to. In the Qurʾān it is used of the Kaʿba: *"And We made the House a place of return for people and a place of safety."* (al-Baqara 2/125).
- **ثَوَاب (thawāb)** — the requital of a deed that **comes back**. The Turkish *sevap* comes from here.
- **ثَوْب (thawb)** — a garment. The explanation the lexicographers give: cloth takes this name because it **returns** to the state destined for it.
- **تَثْوِيب (tathwīb)** — to requite, to reward.

**The idea at the centre of the root: what has gone comes back.**

And this is the whole logic of the sūra. The sūra is built on the idea that what is given will have its return: the shortfall given in the measure, the deed written in the record, the name of the day of reckoning (*yawm al-dīn* — whose root, as was recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn, is "requital").

**So the sūra is framed by two separate roots meaning "requital":**

| Word | Root | What it conveys |
|---|---|---|
| **دِين** (verse 11) | د-ي-ن | The seeing of the return for what was given |
| **ثُوِّبَ** (verse 36) | ث-و-ب | The coming back of what was done |

Both relate the same idea with different pictures: the one from a relation of debt, the other from the movement of **return**.

### The use of the word for reward for punishment

Now we come to the most striking side of the verse.

**The word ثَوَاب is used in Arabic and in the Qurʾān generally for the requital of a good deed.** The Turkish *sevap* carries this sense too.

In this verse, however, the verb is used for **the punishment of the deniers**: *hal thuwwiba'l-kuffār* — "have the deniers been requited?"

In classical rhetoric this is called **تَهَكُّم (tahakkum)**: the use of a word, by way of mockery, in a contrary context. The Qurʾān does this elsewhere too — for example the use of the root *bashīr / bushrā* in announcing a punishment.

**And the use here has a particular force.**

The sūra related those who laughed in this world (29-33). They were mocking. And the last sentence of the sūra speaks **in the same manner as they did**: a question by way of mockery.

That is, in saying its last word to the mockers the sūra uses their own language. This is the completion, at the level of language, of the reversal set up in verse 34.

**I offer this reading as my own inference**; the application of the term *tahakkum* to this verse is mentioned in the classical sources, while the link I set up with the general structure of the sūra is an interpretation.

### مَا كَانُوا۟ يَفْعَلُونَ — the last link with al-Infiṭār

The last three words of the sūra meet a verse of the preceding sūra:

| | **al-Infiṭār 82/12** | **al-Muṭaffifīn 83/36** |
|---|---|---|
| Wording | *yaʿlamūna **mā tafʿalūn*** | *thuwwiba'l-kuffāru **mā kānū yafʿalūn*** |
| Who | The angels **know** | The deniers **are requited** |
| Verb | ف-ع-ل | ف-ع-ل |

**Al-Infiṭār said that what is done is recorded; al-Muṭaffifīn says that what is done comes back.**

And as was recorded in the section on al-Infiṭār, the verse used not *taʿmalūn* but **tafʿalūn**; by the distinction the lexicographers record, *fiʿl* is wider than *ʿamal* and intent is not a condition of it.

The same word is used here too. That is, what is requited is not "their deeds" but **"what they did"**.

**I record this link as an observation;** that the two sūras use the same verb is a datum of the text, the conclusion I draw between them is an interpretation.

### The close of the sūra

The sūra ends with a question, and this is a pattern taken up in earlier sections: al-Takāthur ends with a question (*"you will be asked about the bounty"*), al-ʿAṣr with an answer, al-Humaza with a closed door.

The question of al-Muṭaffifīn is of a different kind: **it is a question whose answer has already been given.** Everything related across thirty-five verses is the answer to the question.

That is, the question is not seeking information; **it is addressing the reader.** Rather than proclaiming the verdict, the sūra ends by having the reader assent to it.

## The sūra as a whole

### The structure of sound

The verse-endings of the thirty-six verses are built almost entirely on a single sound: **a long vowel + a nasal consonant (نون or ميم).**

*Muṭaffifīn, yastawfūn, yukhsirūn, mabʿūthūn, ʿaẓīm, ʿālamīn, sijjīn, sijjīn, marqūm, mukadhdhibīn, dīn, athīm, awwalīn, yaksibūn, maḥjūbūn, jaḥīm, tukadhdhibūn, ʿilliyyīn, ʿilliyyūn, marqūm, muqarrabūn, naʿīm, yanẓurūn, naʿīm, makhtūm, mutanāfisūn, tasnīm, muqarrabūn, yaḍḥakūn, yataghāmazūn, fakihīn, ḍāllūn, ḥāfiẓīn, yaḍḥakūn, yanẓurūn, yafʿalūn.*

All thirty-six of the thirty-six verses are in the same family of sound. This is the same texture recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn, and it is a common property of the short Meccan sūras.

**But in this sūra there is a single break, and its place is meaningful:**

Verse 26 consists of two sentences: *khitāmuhu **misk*** and *wa fī dhālika fa'l-yatanāfasi'l-**mutanāfisūn**.*

That is, the word **مِسْك** stands in the middle of the verse, outside the rhyme. It is the one point in the flow of the sūra that breaks the unity of sound.

And immediately after it comes the sūra's one command.

**I record this not as a claim of a miracle but as an observation about form.** That the order of sound changes at that point stands in the text; the meaning I draw from it is an interpretation — but it is of the same kind as the observation made about the last verse in the section on al-Infiṭār: in saying its most important sentence the sūra steps outside its own order of sound.

There is also an accord between sound and content: the words **كَلَّا** (four times) and **وَيْل** (twice) repeated across the sūra give the text a broken, cut-off rhythm. That the sound of a sūra whose subject is **cutting and diminishing** should itself be interrupted is a consistent choice.

### Structure

The sūra consists of two large halves, and each is the explanation of the other:

| | **First half (1-28)** | **Second half (29-36)** |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Verdict, record, requital | The scene: who was doing what |
| Time | Now and that day | The past and that day |
| Manner | Sentences of verdict and description | Narrative — with four *idhā*'s |
| Function | It says what will happen | It shows why it will happen |

And the first half divides in two within itself:

| | **The fujjār (7-17)** | **The abrār (18-28)** |
|---|---|---|
| Book | In *sijjīn* | In *ʿilliyyūn* |
| Character of the book | *Marqūm* | *Marqūm* |
| Addition | — | There are witnesses |
| State | Veiled, in the fire | On couches, looking |
| Drink | — | Sealed, sealed with musk, mixed with *tasnīm* |
| Number of verses | **11** | **11** |

The two sections are of equal length. But the distribution of content is not equal: on the side of the fujjār the punishment is given in three verses (15-17), on the side of the abrār the bliss is related in seven verses (22-28).

**That is, the sūra allots more room to the reward than to the punishment.** This is a continuation of the asymmetry recorded at verse 21.

### The sūra's real idea: the ruling on the little

I return to the question I noted at the head of the sūra: why is a crime this small met with a word this heavy?

The answer stands distributed across the whole sūra and needs gathering.

**First step: all the objects of the sūra are thin.**

| Verse | Word | How much |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **تطفيف** | As much as the space at the rim of a vessel |
| **4** | **ظن** | As much as a possibility |
| **14** | **رَيْن** | As much as one coat of rust |
| **15** | **حِجَاب** | As much as a curtain |

All four are thin. And all four are complete in their effects.

This does not look like an accident. In four separate fields — in the measure, in belief, in the heart, in seeing — the sūra speaks of something on the same scale.

**Second step: thinness is what makes going unnoticed possible.**

What the four objects have in common is that **they remain below the threshold.**

- Twenty grams are not noticed.
- A possibility does not count as a conviction.
- One coat of rust does not blind a mirror.
- A curtain is not a wall.

Each is, in its own field, of a size that can be counted "insignificant".

**Third step: what goes unnoticed is repeated.**

This is a finding lodged in the sūra's grammar. Patterns conveying repetition stand together across the sūra:

- **مُطَفِّف** — active participle of the tafʿīl form: intensification, that is, repetition (1).
- **إِذَا … إِذَا** — a repeated situation (2-3).
- **مَا كَانُوا۟ يَكْسِبُونَ** — *kāna* + imperfect: a continuing past (14).
- **كُنتُم بِهِۦ تُكَذِّبُونَ** — the same structure (17).
- **كَانُوا۟ … يَضْحَكُونَ** — the same structure (29).

In five separate places, the same information: **these are not things that happened once.**

**Fourth step: repetition accumulates, and what has accumulated is no longer thin.**

The metaphor of rust shows this most plainly: one coat of rust is nothing, a hundred coats finish the mirror. And in between there is no critical moment — no single coat is the coat of which you can say, "this is the coat at which the mirror died."

The same holds for *taṭfīf*: twenty grams are nothing, ten thousand transactions later they are a fortune. And in between, no single transaction is a transaction of which it can be said, "this was the theft."

**Fifth step: this is why the punishment is given not to the act but to the structure.**

The first sentence of the sūra does not punish a transaction. It names a **person**: *muṭaffifīn*. And that naming, by its pattern, includes repetition.

That is, the *wayl* is given not to a single twenty grams but **to the reckoning that makes twenty grams possible.**

**Sixth step: and the same mechanism works in the opposite direction too.**

This is the most elegant side of the sūra, and it is generally missed.

The sūra set the smallest measure of belief opposite the smallest of frauds: *alā yaẓunnu* — even a supposition would have sufficed (4).

And at the end it gave a command: *fa'l-yatanāfas* — compete (26).

That is, the sūra speaks on the same scale in both directions. If a small diminishing can ruin a life, a small turning can also build one. **The scale is decisive not because it is small but because it is repeated.**

**I offer this reading as my own inference.** That the four "thin" objects are present in the sūra is a datum of the text; the structure I set up among them is an interpretation, and I ascribe it to no classical exegete.

### Words of boundary

There is another line in the sūra's weave of vocabulary, and it was recorded at verse 12. I gather it:

| Word | Root | Relation to the boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **مُطَفِّف** | ط-ف-ف | Shaves at the **edge** of the boundary |
| **فَاجِر** | ف-ج-ر | **Splits** the boundary |
| **مُعْتَد** | ع-د-و | **Crosses** the boundary |
| **مِيزَان** (at al-Raḥmān 55/8: *lā taṭghaw*) | ط-غ-ي | **Overflows** the boundary |

And on the other side of the sūra there is a word of boundary too: **مَخْتُوم** (25) — sealed, that is, its boundary protected, untouched.

The sūra is, then, a sūra of boundaries. On one side those who violate the boundary, on the other that whose boundary is protected.

### The link with al-Infiṭār

In the section on al-Infiṭār the overlap of vocabulary between the two sūras was listed. Here it is in completed form:

| Word | al-Infiṭār | al-Muṭaffifīn |
|---|---|---|
| **إن الأبرار لفي نعيم** | 82/13 | 83/22 — **exactly the same** |
| **الفجار** | 82/14 | 83/7 |
| **جحيم** | 82/14 | 83/16 |
| **يوم الدين** | 82/15, 17, 18 | 83/11 |
| **حافظين** | 82/10 — *ʿalaykum la-ḥāfiẓīn* | 83/33 — *ʿalayhim ḥāfiẓīn* |
| **ما أدراك** | 82/17, 18 | 83/8, 19 |
| **كلا** | 82/9 | 83/7, 14, 15, 18 |
| **كذّب** | 82/9 | 83/10, 11, 12, 17 |
| **ما تفعلون / ما كانوا يفعلون** | 82/12 | 83/36 |
| **كتاب / كاتبين** | 82/11 | 83/7, 9, 18, 20 |
| **بعثر / مبعوثون** | 82/4 | 83/4 |

Eleven separate overlaps. That is a higher rate than would be expected for two sūras standing side by side in the muṣḥaf.

**And the two sūras complete one another:**

| | **al-Infiṭār** | **al-Muṭaffifīn** |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | It says that the reckoning **is set up**: the record is being kept | It shows **how the reckoning works**: two books, two places |
| *Ḥāfiẓīn* | There are those who have been appointed | Those who have not been appointed take themselves for such |
| The crime | Not named — a general delusion | Named: shaving off the measure |
| The question | *Mā gharraka* — what deceived you? | *Alā yaẓunnu* — does he never consider? |
| The close | *Wa'l-amru yawmaʾidhin li'llāh* | *Hal thuwwiba'l-kuffāru mā kānū yafʿalūn* |

The last sentence of al-Infiṭār says with whom the authority lies; the last sentence of al-Muṭaffifīn asks whether that authority has worked.

**It must be stated that this is an observation about the arrangement of the muṣḥaf.** The relation between sūras is a recognised subject in exegesis, but it carries no claim about the order of revelation. And as was recorded in the section on al-Infiṭār: an overlap of vocabulary is a datum standing in the text; a historical conclusion drawn from it requires separate evidence.

### The link with al-Takāthur: two competitions

The contrast set up at verse 26 is the sūra's most important external link. I gather it:

| | **تَكَاثُر** — al-Takāthur 102/1 | **تَنَافُس** — al-Muṭaffifīn 83/26 |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | تفاعل | تفاعل |
| What is competed for | Abundance (inside the word) | *Fī dhālika* (outside the word, brought forward) |
| The resource | Divisible | Indivisible |
| Result | If one wins, another loses | No one loses |
| End | None — *"ḥattā zurtumu'l-maqābir"* | There is a definite goal |
| Verdict | **Censured** | **Commanded** |

And the finding recorded in the section on al-Takāthur finds its answer here: *"what is criticised is not the accumulating itself but the accumulating's becoming relational."*

Al-Muṭaffifīn carries this a step further: **being relational is not, on its own, a fault either.** The fault is in the relation's being zero-sum.

And what the two sūras say together is this: **the Qurʾān does not aim to remove the drive to compete in the human being.** The principle recorded in the section on al-Shams holds here too: *"to suppress is not to destroy."* The sūra directs the drive.

### What is not in the sūra

The **absence** of several things from the sūra is worth recording:

**One. There is no criminal law.** The sūra prescribes no worldly sanction for cheating in the measure. No prescribed penalty, no compensation, no procedure of inspection. This shows on what plane the sūra takes up the matter.

**Two. There is no command "give full measure".** In the story of Shuʿayb this command is given plainly (al-Aʿrāf 7/85, Hūd 11/85, al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/181). It is not in this sūra. The sūra's one command is *fa'l-yatanāfas*.

**Three. Who is addressed is not stated.** No name, no tribe, no city. The principle recorded in the sections on al-Humaza and al-Māʿūn holds here too: **the verse describes an attribute.**

**Four. What the believers are asked to do is not stated.** Those mocked are not counselled to patience, are not asked to answer back, are not advised to defend themselves. The sūra only says that the picture will be reversed.

## What this says to the present

**First: using two measures is not hypocrisy — it is something more widespread.**

The second and third verses of the sūra are not describing a defect of character. They are describing **a tendency present in everyone**: to prefer the interpretation that favours oneself.

This tendency does not require an intention to do evil. On the contrary, most of the time it works with good intentions. A person knows his own situation from the inside — its causes, its difficulties, its good faith. Another's situation he sees from the outside — only its result.

This asymmetry makes it easy to give two different names to the same act: my delay is "excusable", his is "irresponsibility"; my cutting short is "efficiency", his is "carelessness".

**The measure the verse lays down is single and can be applied:** *do I give both sides of the same act the same name?*

This is not a moral sermon but a method of checking. And the sūra does it not with a question but by placing two sentences side by side — because an inconsistency becomes visible only when it is set side by side.

**Second: the harm that stays below the threshold is the harm most often repeated.**

*Taṭfīf* is a harm that is measurable but cannot be objected to. And life today is particularly hospitable to harms of this kind.

The reason is structural: as transactions become automated and their scale grows, the small difference in a single transaction reaches a large sum in the aggregate — but no single transaction is of a size that can be objected to. And as long as the cost of objecting is higher than the difference itself, nobody objects.

This is a mechanism that requires no ill will; it forms of itself. But the sūra's verdict is built not on intent but on **which side the difference falls**. The measure is this: a difference that falls systematically in one direction is not an error.

**Third: the loss of sensitivity is not noticed.**

The word *rān* of the fourteenth verse describes moral habituation: the same behaviour ceasing over time to cause discomfort.

The most dangerous side of this process is that the instrument a person uses to judge himself is going through the same process. The conscience is both what measures and what is measured. And a balance that has rusted cannot show its own drift.

**For this reason the measure has to be sought not in the behaviour but in the reaction:** what is there that used to trouble you and does not today? If what has changed is not the behaviour, the measure has changed.

And this has a further consequence: a person cannot check his own measure alone. He needs a reference from outside — a text, a community, an interlocutor. What the people described in the sūra have in common is that they measure themselves only against one another (*yataghāmazūn* — they wink to one another).

**Fourth: mockery needs an audience.**

The *tafāʿul* pattern of the thirtieth verse said that mockery is a reciprocal act: the wink is made not to the one mocked but **to the one beside him**.

This explains why mockery is so widespread and so easy: mockery is a method of forming a group. Two people laughing at someone become, in that moment, a community. And the price of the community is paid by the person left outside.

The finding recorded in the section on al-Humaza is completed here: *"belittling is the mechanism that turns accumulation into worth."* Al-Muṭaffifīn adds: belittling is at the same time a mechanism of **coming together**.

And today this mechanism works in a particularly visible way: mocking a common target is the fastest way of forming a group. Because what is done carries no price — as the thirty-first verse says, the person returns home in good spirits — the mechanism feeds itself.

**Fifth: taking oneself for a keeper.**

The thirty-third verse is the sentence of this section that looks most directly on the present: *"they had not been sent as keepers over them."*

To pass a verdict on the state of another — who is on the right road, who has gone astray, who is sincere and who is not — has never been easier and never been more widespread. And no authority is needed to do it; a screen is enough.

The measure the verse lays down does not concern the correctness of the verdict: it concerns **authority**. And authority comes with "having been sent".

In the section on al-Infiṭār this link was set up: the true *ḥāfiẓīn* are appointed; they are moreover **kirām** — honoured. That is, what makes the record legitimate is the character of the one recording.

And the heaviest side of this is that the Qurʾān lays down this bound first **for the Prophet** (al-Anʿām 6/107, al-Ghāshiya 88/22). Nobody has the possibility of granting himself this authority over another.

**Sixth: competing is not forbidden.**

The command of the twenty-sixth verse is the most positive sentence of the sūra, and it corrects a widespread misunderstanding: the Qurʾān does not count competition an immorality. It asks for competition in the imperative.

The difference lies in what is competed for. And the practical measure is this: **does my winning require someone to lose?**

This question ties back to the opening verses of the sūra. The game the *muṭaffif* played was by definition zero-sum: every gram he took was subtracted from the other party. The sūra's last command proposes a way out of that structure.

**Seventh: on whom to read the sūra.**

This is the sūra's most serious trap, and it has to be said plainly.

The sūra describes those who mock and those who pass verdicts on others. To take this text in hand and fit others into the description — merchants, institutions, acquaintances — and to pass verdicts on them is **to commit the very act the sūra describes.**

The same warning was made in the section on al-Humaza: *"To use a text in order to place someone lower is exactly lamz."*

Here the warning is even more apt, because the sūra itself names this act: *"they had not been sent as keepers over them."* Whoever uses the sūra to measure another has taken up a duty to which he was precisely not sent.

What falls to the reader is to try the description on himself: **what is the measure I hold in my hand, and which side does the difference fall on?**

The judgement stated is not binding.
