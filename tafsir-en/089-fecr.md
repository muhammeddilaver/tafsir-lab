# 89. Sūrat al-Fajr

Thirty verses. Meccan. It takes its name from its first word: **الفجر** — the whitening of the dawn.

The sūra opens with an oath of five verses, then names three peoples that were destroyed, and then does something unexpected: it steps out of history and enters into a single human being, and there it finds a sentence. That sentence is the heart of the sūra, and it has two halves:

> *"My Lord has honoured me."*
> *"My Lord has abased me."*

The first is said when abundance comes, the second when want comes. Man takes these for two separate verdicts. The verse says that both are two faces of the same act — *ibtilāʾ*, testing — and rejects **both of them at once**: *kallā*.

The whole structure of the sūra hangs on that rejection. The peoples that precede it are the file of those who counted the power given to them as a certificate of approval. The scene that follows it is the place where that certificate will be read. And at the very end, the same human being is shown a counter-portrait: a soul whose verdict does not change as its condition changes — *al-muṭmaʾinna*.

That is, the sūra is not concerned with a question ("why has this happened to me?") but with an **inference** ("what does this tell me?"). What it diagnoses is not the events themselves but the reading made from the events.

### The skeleton of the sūra

| Section | Verses | Its function |
|---|---|---|
| The oath | 1-4 | The dawn, ten nights, the even and the odd, the travelling night |
| The condition of the oath | 5 | "Is there in this an oath for one who has understanding?" |
| History | 6-12 | ʿĀd, Thamūd, Pharaoh — three powers, three structures, the same transgression |
| The ruling | 13-14 | The pouring out of the punishment; "your Lord is in the place of watching" |
| The diagnosis | 15-16 | Man's two false inferences |
| Rejection and accusation | 17-20 | *Kallā*; the orphan, the needy, inheritance, the love of wealth |
| The resurrection | 21-26 | The pounding of the earth, the ranging of the ranks, remembering to no purpose |
| The call | 27-30 | To the soul at rest: return, enter, enter |

There is a striking proportion here: **exactly half** of the sūra (6-20) is given to the relation man builds with his power and his wealth. The description of the resurrection is six verses, the call four. The sūra is built not to describe the hereafter but **to correct an inference**; the hereafter comes as the support of that correction.

### Meccan

There is something close to consensus that the sūra is Meccan, and the content supports it: short and clipped verse-endings, an opening with an oath, the mention of destroyed peoples as a warning example, a scene of the hereafter, and — perhaps most decisive of all — that the matter of the orphan and the needy is spoken of not as a **legal regulation** but as a direct moral reproach. This matter, which in Medina will be given institutional form with the rulings on zakāt and guardianship, is here still a diagnosis with no sanction attached.

### The number of verses

In this commentary, following the widespread (Kūfan) count, the sūra is taken as **thirty verses**. It is transmitted that there is a difference among the schools of counting over the number of verses in al-Fajr, and that some of them give a higher number. The difference concerns where the verses are divided; it produces no change in the text itself or in the meaning. The division used in this commentary is the widespread codex arrangement we have.

Since the basmala was treated in earlier sections it is not taken up again here; the verse numbers are counted without the basmala.

## 89/1 — وَٱلْفَجْرِ

*Wa'l-fajr*
"By the dawn."

The sūra opens with an oath of a single word. In Arabic a sentence this short turns into a hammer-blow; and because the four oaths to follow are of the same shortness, the first five verses are heard as a series of strokes.

### فَجْر — *fajr*

**Root: ف-ج-ر.** The original sense of the root is **to split, to crack, to open, to make something gush out from within**.

This root was taken up in the last verse of Sūrat al-Qadr (*ḥattā maṭlaʿi'l-fajr*); I do not repeat what was recorded there — a brief reminder and the addition of what belongs here will be enough.

The main point established there was this: **the dawn is the splitting of the darkness. The light does not come; the darkness is split and the light comes out of it.** The word describes not an illumination but a **tearing**. And **fujūr** from the same root — to exceed the limit in sin, to tear the covering and do evil openly — is the counterpart of this image in the moral field: at dawn the covering of the night is torn, in *fujūr* the covering of shame. A conceptual kinship was also established with *ṭughyān* (water overflowing its bed), treated in Sūrat al-ʿAlaq.

**What is to be added here is this: the second face of the root is water.**

*Fajjara* and *tafjīr* are used in Arabic especially for **making a spring gush, splitting the ground and bringing out water**. Their occurrences in the Qurʾān show this clearly:

- *"There are stones from which rivers gush forth."* (al-Baqara 2/74). The verb is from the same root: *yatafajjaru*. The stone, hard and closed, is split, and water comes out of it.
- *"They said: We will not believe you until you make a spring gush for us from the earth."* (al-Isrāʾ 17/90). *Tafjura lanā mina'l-arḍi yanbūʿā.*
- *"And We made the earth gush with springs."* (al-Qamar 54/12). The part of the water in Noah's flood that came from the ground.
- *"A spring from which the servants of God drink, making it gush abundantly."* (al-Insān 76/6). *Yufajjirūnahā tafjīrā.*

That is, the root works **in two directions at once**: **light above, water below.** In the sky the darkness of night is split and brightness comes out; on the ground the hardness of the soil or the rock is split and water comes out. The common structure of the two events is the same: a mass that looks closed and impassable is opened at some point and something life-giving comes out of it.

There is a reason I record this, and it has to do with the end of the sūra. In the twenty-first verse the earth will be **pounded and levelled** (*dukkat*): this time not a splitting but a crushing. The sūra opens with a place that is split and closes with a place that is pounded. I record this as an observation about the structure, as my own reading; I do not ascribe it to a classical exegete.

### فلق and فجر: two roots of splitting

In Sūrat al-Falaq the root **ف-ل-ق** was taken up in detail. The description there was this: *falq* is not any sort of cutting; it is **the opening of something closed, from within or by a force, the whole parting and something coming out from between.** And the two verses at al-Anʿām 6/95-96 gave, one after the other, the two scales of the root: *fāliqu'l-ḥabbi wa'n-nawā* (the splitter of the grain and the date-stone) and *fāliqu'l-iṣbāḥ* (the splitter of the morning).

The two roots are very close to one another, and the lexicographers have noticed this. It would not be right to present the difference between them as a firm lexical boundary; but looking at their usages, a **difference of emphasis** appears:

| | **ف-ل-ق (*falq*)** | **ف-ج-ر (*fajr*)** |
|---|---|---|
| The image that stands out | A whole **parting in two**, cracking | A place splitting and something **gushing** out of it |
| Its typical objects | The date-stone, the grain, the shell, the sea (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/63 *fa'nfalaqa*) | Water, a spring, a river; and the dawn |
| For the morning | *Fāliqu'l-iṣbāḥ* — the splitter of the morning | *Al-fajr* — the splitting itself, the name of the event |
| Emphasis | **The parting** | **The coming out** |

That both words can be used for the morning shows how narrow the distance between them is. The difference is not an opposition but a difference of centre of gravity: one looks at **the fissure**, the other at **what comes out of the fissure**.

A conclusion for the sūra follows from this. The name of Sūrat al-Fajr is the name of the event — not the attribute of an agent. Sūrat al-Falaq was looking at the agent of the splitting by saying *"the Lord of the falaq"*; Sūrat al-Fajr swears by the splitting itself.

### Why is the first word of the oath the dawn?

Various answers are given to this question in the classical sources; I propose a reading that comes out of the text's own structure, **stating that it is my own reading**.

The sūra opens with the ending of a night: the dawn is the moment at which the night comes to an end. And the sūra closes with a call to **return**: *irjiʿī ilā rabbik* — "Return to your Lord." What passes between the two is the length of the night: peoples rise and fall, man swings between abundance and want, wealth is gathered and eaten.

The dawn is the proof that some point in this order can be split, and it repeats every day. The sūra begins from the moment at which the thing that looks most closed to man — the night — regularly opens.

There is a further aspect. The hour of dawn is the quietest and least crowded moment of the day; that the Qurʾān mentions this hour especially in other places (as a time of prayer, and with the expression *qurʾān al-fajr*) shows that this hour carries a distinct ruling. The oath is made at the most solitary moment within the day.

### Which dawn? — the definite article

The word comes **with the definite article**: *al-fajr*. In Arabic this article either covers the whole of the class (*istighrāq*) or points to a known particular instance (*ʿahd*). The exegetes have defended both:

| View | What it means | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The class** — every dawn | The daybreak of every day; the event itself | The most widespread function of the article; that the subsequent oaths are also natural phenomena (the night) | If the "ten nights" of the next verse points to a particular time, it may not fit with this |
| **A particular morning** | The morning of the Feast of Sacrifice / the morning at Muzdalifa | The reading of the subsequent verses as the time of pilgrimage; the tying of "the even and the odd" to the days of the pilgrimage | There is nothing in the text that determines this |
| **The first morning of Muḥarram** | The first dawn of the year | Another proposal within the same frame | It has no ground in the text |

**The preference of the majority is the first**, and its ground is sound: when a word comes unqualified, a proof from outside the text is needed to qualify it. But I do not write this as a binding ruling; the second and third views become consistent if the subsequent verses are tied to the calendar of the pilgrimage.

### The function of the oath

The subject of the oath (*qasam*) was established in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr and extended to long series of oaths in Sūrat al-Shams; I do not repeat it. What belongs here is this: the oath of al-Fajr **runs for five verses** and in the fifth verse is tied to a condition. This is an unusual structure among the series of oaths in the Qurʾān — most series of oaths are tied directly to the answer (*jawāb al-qasam*).

And the answer of al-Fajr **is not stated openly.** This is one of the most disputed grammatical questions of the sūra, and I shall dwell on it separately after the fifth verse.

## 89/2 — وَلَيَالٍ عَشْرٍ

*Wa layālin ʿashr*
"And by ten nights."

### That it comes indefinite — the source of the matter

The grammatical detail here is the source of the whole disagreement over the verse, and it is generally passed over.

In the preceding verse the word was **definite**: *al-fajr*. In this verse it is **indefinite**: *layālin* — "nights", "a ten of nights". The verse does not say *al-layāli'l-ʿashr*.

In Arabic indefiniteness does two things: it either conveys **indeterminacy** (which one is not said) or **magnification** (such a ten nights). In the classical sources the second is defended here as well.

But whichever it is, the result is the same: **the text does not say which ten nights.** And this is the reason the exegetes part company — they are trying to fill a gap.

This should not be read as a defect. **The existence of the disagreement is a feature of the text.** Had the Qurʾān wished to fix a time, it would have fixed it; as it names the Night of Power when it mentions it, as it names Ramaḍān when it mentions it. Here it has not. In that case the interpreter's task is not to close the gap but to ask what the gap does.

The effect the gap produces is this: the oath is made not upon **a particular calendar day** but upon **a counted stretch of time**. Ten nights — a countable span with a beginning and an end.

### The views

Still, the views transmitted must be recorded; because they have been seriously defended in the history of exegesis, and none of them is arbitrary.

| View | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **The first ten nights of Dhu'l-Ḥijja** | The most widespread view. That the rites of the pilgrimage fall in these days; the reading of the expression *"that they may mention the name of God on appointed days"* (*ayyāmin maʿlūmāt*) at al-Ḥajj 22/28 as referring to these days; that the next verse (the even and the odd) can be tied to the days of the pilgrimage | There is nothing about the pilgrimage in the text; the link is established from outside |
| **The last ten nights of Ramaḍān** | That the Night of Power is sought within this ten; that the nights are spent in worship | The expression "ten nights" is not used anywhere else in the Qurʾān for the last ten of Ramaḍān |
| **The first ten nights of Muḥarram** | The opening of the year; the value ascribed to these days | The view that finds the fewest supporters in the sources; the one with the weakest proof |
| **Indeterminate — general** | The text does not determine it; the indefiniteness is deliberate | It may be said that it reduces the concreteness of the oath |

**The first view is widespread**, but I do not impose it as a preference. The working of the sūra depends on no one view: whichever ten nights it is, the work the oath does within the text does not change. This is one of those places where the disagreement should be recorded and not magnified.

### A short note on numerical agreement

*Layālin* is a feminine plural, and the numeral has come in the form **عَشْر**, without the *tāʾ*. In Arabic, with the numbers from 3 to 10 there is inverse agreement between the numeral and what is counted: if what is counted is feminine the numeral is masculine (without *tāʾ*), and if what is counted is masculine the numeral is feminine (with *tāʾ*). Here, because what is counted, *layālī*, is feminine, the numeral is *ʿashr* — without *tāʾ*. The rule is working.

It looks like a small detail; but it also determines the verse-ending: *ʿashrin* ends with the same sound as the *fajr* of the preceding verse and the *watr* of the next. That the word comes in this form is required by both the grammar and the sound.

## 89/3 — وَٱلشَّفْعِ وَٱلْوَتْرِ

*Wa'sh-shafʿi wa'l-watr*
"And by the even and the odd."

One of the verses on which the most views are transmitted in the Qurʾān. Let us first open the two roots, because without knowing the roots the views cannot be understood.

### شَفْع — *shafʿ*

**Root: ش-ف-ع.** Its original sense is **to add one thing to another, to make a single thing a pair, to join a mate to it.**

That is, *shafʿ* does not mean "two"; it means **"the undoing of singleness"**. It is the joining of another to something that stands alone.

**And from here comes one of the most instructive word-links in Arabic: شَفَاعَة (*shafāʿa*, intercession) is from the same root.**

This link is generally passed over, and when it is passed over the word becomes unintelligible. Intercession is mostly heard as something like "mediation, pulling strings". What the root says is more basic: **intercession is someone joining himself to a person who stands alone.** The person is single; when the intercessor comes he becomes a pair.

The Qurʾān always sets the condition of permission when it uses this word: *"Who can intercede with Him without His permission?"* (al-Baqara 2/255). That is, the pairing does not happen of itself; permission to join must be given.

In this commentary I issue no ruling on the nature of intercession — that is a matter of theology and has no place here. What I record is only the logic of the root: the undoing of solitude.

The root's other derivatives carry the same idea: *shafʿ* (even), *shufʿa* (in jurisprudence, a partner's right of pre-emption — a joining to a property), *shafīʿ* (the one who joins himself alongside).

### وَتْر — *watr*

**Root: و-ت-ر.** Its meaning is **single, singleness, having no mate.**

Its derivatives show the flavour of the word:

- **وَتَر (*watar*)** — the string of a bow. The **single** cord stretched between the two ends of the bow. The Turkish *veter*, and *vetere* in music, are from this root.
- **مَوْتُور (*mawtūr*)** — one whose kin has been killed and who is left alone, who has not been able to take his revenge. That is, one who has been **diminished**, from whom a part has been torn away.
- The verb **وَتَرَ (*watara*)** carries the sense of "to diminish": *"He will not diminish your deeds"* (Muḥammad 47/35). The same root.
- **تَتْرَى (*tatrā*)** — one after another, at intervals (al-Muʾminūn 23/44). To come one by one.

These derivatives say something interesting: **singleness is not a neutral number in Arabic.** Within it there is either unmatchedness (superiority) or deficiency (solitude). The same root opens both to "unequalled" and to "having a piece torn away".

**A variant reading.** The word has been read as **وَتْر** (with *fatḥa*) and as **وِتْر** (with *kasra*). Both readings are transmitted and **they do not change the meaning**; both mean "odd". The difference is dialectal. Since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which authority, I write no name.

### The views

| View | What it means | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Every even and every odd** | All numbers; hence everything that can be counted, that is, the whole of creation | The general sense of the root; that the wording comes unqualified; *"Of every thing We created a pair"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/49) | Its generality reduces the concreteness of the oath; the two preceding oaths were concrete units of time |
| **Shafʿ = the created, witr = God** | Everything created is paired; the only unpaired one is He | It connects directly with *aḥad* at al-Ikhlāṣ 112/1; the Qurʾān's counting of pairedness as the mark of being created | That one element of the oath should be God amounts to saying "by God", which is unusual in this context |
| **The days of the pilgrimage** | The 8th, 9th and 10th of Dhu'l-Ḥijja; or the distinction at al-Baqara 2/203 between *"one who hastens in two days / one who stays behind"* (an even day / an odd day) | If the preceding verse has been read as Dhu'l-Ḥijja, this is the natural continuation; al-Baqara 2/203 really does speak of two and three days | Again a link from outside the text; it does not agree with the class-reading of the first verse |
| **The prayers** | Prayers with an even and an odd number of cycles; or specifically the *witr* prayer | That the numbers of cycles of the prayers are a known distinction | There is nothing about the prayer in the text |
| **The day of ʿArafa and the day of sacrifice** | Two days, one counted odd and one even | A sub-proposal within the frame of the pilgrimage | The narrowest and most indirect reading |

**The two views that stand nearest to the wording of the text are the first two**, because both take the words with their own general senses, without qualifying them. I state this as a preference and **I record that it is not binding**; we have no proof for counting the other views weak, and some of them are transmitted in the early sources.

### What the four oaths have in common — my own reading

When the three verses so far and the next verse are read together, a pattern emerges. I present this as my own reading:

- **The dawn** — the beginning moment of the day. A boundary.
- **Ten nights** — a counted span. It has a beginning and an end.
- **The even and the odd** — number itself. The name of countability.
- **The travelling night** — time in passage. What makes its way and will end.

All four say that time is **in pieces and has an end**. Time is mentioned here not as a flow but in **units**: a moment, a ten, an even, an odd, a journey.

And in the twenty-fourth verse of the sūra man will say: *"Would that I had sent something ahead for my life."* That is, someone who supposed his lifetime a whole realises at the end that it was a **span**.

The sūra opens, to a man who thinks his life boundless, with **countable units of time**. I record this as an observation about the structure; I do not ascribe it to a classical exegete.

## 89/4 — وَٱلَّيْلِ إِذَا يَسْرِ

*Wa'l-layli idhā yasr*
"And by the night when it travels on."

### سَرَى — to travel by night

**Root: س-ر-ي.** Its meaning is not merely "to travel": **to make one's way by night.** Arabic has other verbs for travelling by day; *sarā* is specifically a journey made at night.

From the same root comes **إسراء (*isrāʾ*)**, and it occurs in the Qurʾān: *"It is He who made His servant travel by night"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/1). The verb is *asrā*: He made him travel by night.

This feature of the word makes the choice in the verse meaningful: **the night is not "coming" or "falling", it is travelling.** And a thing that travels goes from somewhere to somewhere; it has a road, it has an end.

This is not a figure of personification — this is the word's own usage; in Arabic it is an ordinary expression for the night to *sarā*. But the effect is this: the night here is not a **condition** but a **traveller**. And a traveller will arrive.

Where it will arrive has also been stated: at the first verse. The place the night arrives at is the dawn. **The first and the fourth verses of the sūra hold the two ends of the same event** — one the moment at which the night ends, the other the journey that ends the night.

### The four verbs of the night in the Qurʾān

The Qurʾān does not use a single verb when it mentions the night. Four separate oaths in the short Meccan sūras give four separate images:

| Verse | Verb | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Shams 91/4** | *yaghshā-hā* — it covers | The night is a covering; it comes over and closes |
| **al-Layl 92/1** | *yaghshā* — it covers (without an object) | The same verb, its object not stated |
| **al-Ḍuḥā 93/2** | *sajā* — it grew still, it subsided | The night is a stillness; movement stops |
| **al-Takwīr 81/17** | *ʿasʿasa* — it came / it turned its back | The word conveys both coming and going (of the *aḍdād*) |
| **al-Fajr 89/4** | *yasri* — it travels | The night is a traveller; it makes its way and arrives |

As was treated in the sections on al-Shams and al-Layl, the root **gh-sh-y** conveyed covering, enveloping, coming over and mantling; there the night was something that **actively settles over** you. Here the same night is something that **goes its own way**.

The difference matters. A covering night looks permanent — when has something settled over you is going to lift is not known. A travelling night is **temporary**; a thing that travels stops somewhere.

Considering the content of the sūra, this choice takes on meaning: al-Fajr will speak of peoples that were destroyed and of a man who sways. What is said of both is the same — it is temporary. That the night is travelling is the first record of this idea. I present this as my own reading.

### The dropping of the final *yāʾ*

The original of the verb is **يَسْرِي** (*yasrī*) — imperfect, with a *yāʾ* at the end. In the verse it comes in the form **يَسْرِ**, without the *yāʾ*.

This is a known phenomenon in the Qurʾān and is done for **agreement of verse-ending**: the end of the verse is fitted to the sound of the preceding and following verses. The same elision is seen elsewhere too — *al-kabīru'l-mutaʿāl* (al-Raʿd 13/9), *yawma yadʿu'd-dāʿ* (al-Qamar 54/6).

It is transmitted that in the variant readings the word is read **with the *yāʾ*** as well (*yasrī*). There is no difference of meaning between the two readings. Since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which authority, I write no name.

I should like to add an observation from the point of view of rhetoric, **stating that it is my own reading**: the cutting-off of the end of the word agrees with what it describes. The night travels and **ends**; and the word too is cut off before it is complete. That the sound should move together with the content is something often seen in the short Meccan sūras; but I record this not as a claim of miracle, but as the consistency of the text's formal choices.

### Its being built with إِذَا

The oath is made not upon the night absolutely but upon **one of its states**: *idhā yasri* — "when it travels on."

This is an attitude seen in all the oaths of the sūra, and it is the same in al-Shams and al-Layl. The oath is made not upon the object but upon **a particular moment** of the object. Not the night itself but the night's travelling; not the morning itself but its splitting.

The result is this: what is sworn by is not an **object** but an **event**. And the common property of events is that they take place and pass.

## 89/5 — هَلْ فِى ذَٰلِكَ قَسَمٌ لِّذِى حِجْرٍ

*Hal fī dhālika qasamun li-dhī ḥijr*
"Is there in this an oath for one who has understanding?"

One of the densest verses of the sūra, and the place where the four oaths are tied to a condition.

### The structure of the question

The sentence is built as a question: *hal* — "is there?"

But no answer is expected. In Arabic this is called **istifhām taqrīrī**: the questioner asks in order to bring the one addressed to accept something; the answer is already known, and the one addressed is meant to say it with his own mouth.

English has the same structure: the sentence "That's enough, isn't it?" asks for no information; it asks for assent.

The assent here is this: **yes, there is an oath in this.**

But choosing the form of a question has a cost and a gain. Its cost: certainty is suspended to a degree. Its gain: the one addressed **is made a partner in the decision.** Had a ruling been stated ("in this there is a mighty oath"), the listener's task would have been to accept it. When a question is asked, his task becomes **to look**.

### قَسَم — two readings

The word *qasam* has been read here in two senses:

| Reading | Meaning | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Oath** | "Is there an oath in this?" — that is, are these words really an oath? | The one addressed is asked to confirm the validity of the oath |
| **A thing worth swearing by / the subject of the oath** | "Is there in this something to swear by?" — are these things worth being made the subject of an oath? | The one addressed is asked to weigh the weight of the objects |

The two readings do not exclude one another and come out at the same place in practice: the acceptance that the dawn, the ten nights, the even and the odd, and the travelling night are **weighty enough to be sworn by**.

To see this something is needed — and the verse names that thing.

### حِجْر — *ḥijr*

**Root: ح-ج-ر.** And this root is the most instructive word of the sūra.

The best-known derivative of the word is **حَجَر (*ḥajar*)** — stone. But the root's essential idea is not stone: **to prevent, to hold back, to turn away, to draw a boundary.** The stone is a member of this family because the stone is what sets a boundary and blocks passage.

Its derivatives show that the root turns around a single idea:

- **حِجْر (*ḥijr*)** — forbidden, prevented, made untouchable. In the Qurʾān: *"These are untouchable cattle and crops"* (al-Anʿām 6/138 — *ḥijrun maḥjūr*). And: *"They say: 'Forbidden, forbidden'"* (al-Furqān 25/22 — *ḥijran maḥjūrā*).
- **حَجْر (*ḥajr*)** — in jurisprudence, the restriction of a person's disposal over his property. The word's legal life carries the sense of the root exactly: the person is prevented.
- **حُجْرَة (*ḥujra*)** — a room. A place separated and enclosed by a wall. The Turkish *hücre* (cell) is from this root.
- **حِجْر (*ḥijr*)** — the lap. The area enclosed by the arms; the space that holds what is within it and does not let it fall.
- **الحِجْر (al-Ḥijr)** — the name of the homeland of Thamūd and the name of a sūra. A place enclosed by rocks.

**The common idea: to enclose something and prevent it from getting out.**

### Why is the intellect called *ḥijr*?

Now we come to what the verse does. Saying "one who has understanding", the verse does not say *dhī ʿaql*, it says *dhī ḥijr* — "possessor of a restraint", "possessor of a boundary".

The explanation the lexicographers give is this: **the intellect is given this name because it holds its possessor back from doing what he should not do.** The intellect is what restrains a person.

This description is interesting in itself. But what is really interesting is that the **other** word for intellect in Arabic does the same work.

### عقل and حجر: two words, one idea

In the section on al-Baqara 2/44 the root **ع-ق-ل** was taken up, and what was recorded there was this:

> The root *ʿ-q-l*: **to bind.** The *ʿiqāl* is the rope tied around a camel's knee so that it will not run off. Arabic has given the intellect this name — that is, the intellect, within the word's own logic, is **not a store of knowledge but a bond.** What holds a person, reins him in, prevents him from being flung about.

And the conclusion there was this: *"you have knowledge, you read, you even teach — but if the knowledge does not bind you, you have not used your intellect. This is the difference between knowing and using the intellect: one is acquiring, the other is being bound."*

Now let us set the two words side by side:

| | **عقل (*ʿaql*)** | **حجر (*ḥijr*)** |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete sense of the root | To bind — to put a rope on a camel's knee | To prevent — to turn away, to draw a boundary |
| The image | A rope, holding from within | A wall, enclosing from without |
| Where it is applied | To the animal's own limb | To the perimeter of an area |
| Common result | It cannot run off | It cannot get out |

**Both words describe the intellect not as a capacity but as a power of restraint.** One ties the rope, one draws the wall; but the work of both is the same: to prevent a thing from going somewhere other than where it should go.

And the matter is not confined to two words. Let us look at the other words the Qurʾān uses for the intellect:

| Word | Root | The sense of the root | What it says |
|---|---|---|---|
| **عقل** | ع-ق-ل | To bind | The intellect is a bond |
| **حجر** | ح-ج-ر | To prevent, to enclose | The intellect is a boundary |
| **نُهى** (*ulu'n-nuhā*, Ṭāhā 20/54, 128) | ن-ه-ي | **To forbid** | The intellect is what forbids |
| **لُبّ** (*ulu'l-albāb*) | ل-ب-ب | Kernel, inside, core | The intellect is what is essential inside the shell |
| **حِلْم** | ح-ل-م | Gravity, self-restraint | The intellect is knowing how not to react |

**Four of the five words come directly from a root of holding, binding, preventing or forbidding.** Only *lubb* looks from a different place — and it too says not "capacity" but "what is essential".

I record this finding as an **observation about the language** and draw from it no claim of cultural superiority; every language's conceptual map comes from its own history, and similar examples can be found in other languages too. What I record is only this: the words Arabic uses for the intellect picture it not as something **added** but as something that **holds back**.

The consequence of this, as my own reading, is: in modern language "intelligence" measures a capacity — how fast, how much, how complex. These words measure something else: **whether the brake is working.** That a person knows a great deal and that he can restrain himself are two separate things, and according to these words the name of the second is intellect.

The verse's choice of this word has a counterpart within the sūra too. The three peoples the sūra will describe were superior in capacity — they raised columns, they carved rock, they founded a kingdom. What was lacking was not capacity. In the eleventh verse what they were will be said: *ṭaghaw* — **they exceeded the limit.** That is, their brakes did not hold.

**The word in the fifth verse and the verb in the eleventh are exact opposites of one another: one holds the boundary, the other crosses it.** I record this as my own reading, but that both words turn around the concept of a boundary is a fact standing in the text.

### The addressee of the oath is sifted out

The last word of the verse sets a condition: *li-dhī ḥijr* — "for one who has *ḥijr*."

That is, the oath is not an oath for everyone. To look at the dawn, the ten nights, the even and the odd, the travelling night, and to see a weight in them, requires a condition.

This is a structure the Qurʾān often uses. Sūrat al-Baqara does the same work by saying *"that Book… is a guidance for those who are mindful"* (2/2): the text describes at the outset the person it will be of use to. This is not an exclusion but **a statement of condition** — and the condition is not an inborn quality but an acquired state.

The verse's being built this way has one further consequence: **someone who says "no" to the question has announced not the invalidity of the oath but his own condition.** The text deliberately leaves this door open.

### Where is the answer of the oath?

In Arabic an oath requires an **answer**: "By…, it is thus." In Sūrat al-Fajr this answer is not stated openly, and this is one of the oldest grammatical disputes about the sūra.

The solutions put forward:

| View | Where the answer is | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The answer is elided** | Not stated; left for the addressee to complete | The dropping of the answer of an oath is something seen in the Qurʾān; the content of the sūra already makes the answer plain ("you will surely be brought to account", and the like) |
| **Verse 14** | *"Surely your Lord is in the place of watching"* | The sentence is emphasised with *inna* + *lām*; this is the typical structure of the answers of oaths |
| **Verse 13** | *"Your Lord poured out upon them a scourge of punishment"* | The account of destruction is the fact the oath confirms |
| **Verses 21-23** | The scene of the resurrection | That is where the oaths mean to arrive |

**The second view is the strongest from the point of view of grammar**, because the fourteenth verse carries exactly the form the answers of oaths take. I state this as a preference; **it is not binding**; the first view is also widespread and agrees with the practice elsewhere in the Qurʾān.

Which the answer is does not change the reading of the sūra in any essential way. But it changes this: if the answer is the fourteenth verse, then **the whole five-verse series of oaths has been built for a single sentence** — and that sentence is where the rest of the sūra rests.

## 89/6-8 — أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِعَادٍ • إِرَمَ ذَاتِ ٱلْعِمَادِ • ٱلَّتِى لَمْ يُخْلَقْ مِثْلُهَا فِى ٱلْبِلَٰدِ

*Alam tara kayfa faʿala rabbuka bi-ʿĀd • Irama dhāti'l-ʿimād • Allatī lam yukhlaq mithluhā fi'l-bilād*
"Have you not seen what your Lord did to ʿĀd? — to Iram of the columns; the like of which had not been created among the lands."

### أَلَمْ تَرَ — what the pattern does

This pattern was taken up in the first verse of Sūrat al-Fīl; I do not repeat the finding there, I bring it briefly.

The main point recorded there was this: **the verb رأى (*raʾā*) conveys, besides seeing with the eye, knowing, grasping, coming to a conviction.** English has the same extension — no one is speaking of the eye when he says "you see that this won't work."

And the second finding there was more important: **the pattern leaves the one addressed obliged to accept the event.** It does not ask "did something happen?"; it asks "have you not seen how it happened?" The event is given; the only thing opened to discussion is its manner.

In the section on al-Fīl, when examples of this pattern were being counted, **this verse was already used**: *"Have you not seen what your Lord did to ʿĀd?"* (al-Fajr 89/6) — and this note was entered there: the people of ʿĀd are centuries before those addressed. That is, here too the pattern does not intend seeing with the eye.

What is to be added here is this: the object of the question is **not the event but the manner**. *Kayfa faʿala* — "how He did it." What is asked of the one addressed is not that he should learn that ʿĀd was destroyed; he knows that already. What is asked is that he should **look at the form of the destruction.**

This prepares the rest of the sūra. Because what will be said in the eleventh verse is what the peoples did; what will be said in the thirteenth is what the recompense was. That the question opens with "how" asks that the relation between them be looked at.

### رَبُّكَ — "your Lord"

The verse does not say "God", it says **"your Lord"**. And this is a choice that will run through the sūra.

When we count the word within the sūra, this table emerges:

| Verse | Form | In whose mouth | At what work |
|---|---|---|---|
| **6** | *rabbuka* | The narrator → to the Prophet | The one who destroys |
| **13** | *rabbuka* | The narrator → to the Prophet | The one who pours out the punishment |
| **14** | *rabbaka* | The narrator → to the Prophet | The one standing in the place of watching |
| **15** | *rabbuhu* | The narrator, about man | The one who tests |
| **15** | *rabbī* | **In man's own mouth** | "He has honoured me" |
| **16** | *rabbī* | **In man's own mouth** | "He has abased me" |
| **22** | *rabbuka* | The narrator → to the Prophet | The one who comes |
| **28** | *rabbiki* | Addressed to the soul | The one returned to |

**Eight occurrences.** And two things are striking in the distribution.

**One.** Six are in the narrator's mouth, **two in man's own mouth.** And the two sentences that come out of man's mouth are the two sentences the sūra rejects. That is, the sūra both uses the word *Rabb* itself and shows the place where it is used wrongly.

**Two.** Each time the word occurs at a different work: destroying, pouring out, watching, testing, coming, being returned to. These are not separate attributes; they are **successive phases of the same relationship.** The backbone of the sūra is this word: from beginning to end the text asks a single question — what is the relation between us and the *Rabb*?

And the word *Rabb* itself directs this question. As was treated in the section on al-Fātiḥa, the word carries ownership together with **nurture and care**; at al-Baqara 2/21-22 the command to serve was built not with "to God" but with "your Lord" and its ground was counted by way of care. In al-Fajr the same word is the word man uses when he looks at what is happening to him: *rabbī akraman*. **Man uses the right word and makes the wrong inference.**

### عَاد — ʿĀd

When the story of Thamūd was treated in Sūrat al-Shams a method was established: what the Qurʾān says, what the reports add, and the historical frame are given separately. I apply the same method for ʿĀd.

#### What the Qurʾān says

When all the sūras are brought together, the information the Qurʾān gives about ʿĀd is this:

- **Hūd was sent to them** (al-Aʿrāf 7/65; Hūd 11/50; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/123-124). Hūd is called the "brother" of the people — that is, one of them.
- **They came after the people of Noah and were given strength in their creation**: *"Remember that He made you successors after the people of Noah and gave you increase in stature"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/69).
- **They set up a sign on every height:** *"Do you build a sign on every height, sporting with vanities? And do you take for yourselves strongholds, hoping to abide forever?"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/128-129). **These two verses are particularly important for al-Fajr** and I shall come back to them below: the verb in the second is *takhludūn* — "that you may abide forever."
- **They used their strength tyrannically:** *"When you seize, you seize like tyrants"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/130).
- **They relied on their strength:** *"They said: Who is stronger than us?"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/15).
- **They were destroyed by a hurricane:** *"As for ʿĀd, they were destroyed by a roaring, raging wind; God loosed it upon them for seven nights and eight days without pause. You would have seen the people there laid low, like hollow trunks of palm trees"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/6-7). Al-Qamar 54/19-20 and Fuṣṣilat 41/16 also mention the same event.
- **Their land is al-Aḥqāf:** *"Mention the brother of ʿĀd: when he warned his people at al-Aḥqāf"* (al-Aḥqāf 46/21). *Aḥqāf* means sand dunes.
- **At al-Najm 53/50 "the former ʿĀd" is spoken of** (*ʿĀdan al-ūlā*). Whether this expression requires the existence of a second ʿĀd has been disputed; the verse gives no historical information and I build no chronology upon it.

#### What the Qurʾān does not say

The following come from the reports of exegesis, history and story; they are not in the text of the Qurʾān:

- **The bodily measurements of the individuals of the people.** Extraordinary figures are given in the reports. The Qurʾān says *"He gave you increase in stature"* and sets no measure. I give no figure.
- **The geographical location of Iram.**
- **The population of the people, the length of its rule, the names of its rulers.**
- **The place of ʿĀd in the genealogical tree** and the details of the distinction "the former ʿĀd / the latter ʿĀd".
- **The date of the destruction.**

I do not count these reports worthless; some of them are found in the early sources. But they are not of the same degree of certainty as the text of the Qurʾān, nor are they necessary for the working of the sūra.

#### The historical frame

What can be said with certainty is this: **for those addressed by the Qurʾān, ʿĀd was not a distant and unknown name.** The name was an example of destruction lodged in Arab memory and in Arab poetry; indeed the Qurʾān elsewhere mentions ʿĀd with no introduction at all, assuming that the one addressed knows. That the pattern *alam tara* can be used requires this too — one does not say "have you not seen?" of something unknown.

The description al-Aḥqāf (sand dunes) places the people in a geography looking to the south of the Arabian peninsula. To make a more precise determination of place than that exceeds the information we have.

### إِرَمَ ذَاتِ ٱلْعِمَادِ — a disagreement along two axes

This construct is one of the most discussed expressions in the Qurʾān, and the disagreement runs along two separate axes.

#### The first axis: what is Iram?

| View | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **A tribal / lineage name** — the ancestor of ʿĀd or a branch of it | In the Arab genealogical tradition it is ordinary for tribes to be named by an ancestor's name; it is read as an **explicative apposition** (*ʿaṭf al-bayān*) to *ʿĀd*: "to ʿĀd, that is, to Iram" | The *allatī* (feminine relative pronoun) in the next verse can be used both for a tribe and for a city; it is not distinguishing |
| **A city / town name** | The note *"the like of which had not been created among the lands"* suggests a settlement; *ذات العماد* calls up columned structures | In the Qurʾān ʿĀd is a people; there is no city at the centre of the story |
| **Another name for ʿĀd** | That the two names belong to the same community | It does not explain why the two names are given side by side |

**A grammatical note:** the word has also been read in a construct form, **عَادِ إِرَمَ** (*ʿĀdi Irama* — "the ʿĀd of Iram"). On this reading Iram becomes the larger community to which ʿĀd belongs, and the first view is strengthened. That variant readings exist is certain; since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which authority, I write no name.

#### The second axis: what is ذَاتِ ٱلْعِمَادِ?

**عِمَاد (*ʿimād*)**, root **ع-م-د**: the support that holds a thing upright. *ʿAmūd* — a pillar, a column. *Iʿtimād* — leaning, relying. *ʿUmda* — a support, a basis.

| View | What it means | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Possessors of columns** | A community that built monumental structures with stone columns | The most widespread sense of *ʿamūd*; it agrees with the description at al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/128-129 of "setting up a sign on every height" and "taking strongholds for yourselves" |
| **Possessors of tent poles** | A nomadic and numerous people; "possessors of poles" = of many tents, powerful | In Arabic the size of a community is expressed by the number of its tents; the description al-Aḥqāf (sand dunes) agrees with a nomadic life |
| **Possessors of stature / strength** | Figuratively: bodies like columns, strong | The figurative use of the word *ʿimād* for tall stature; the expression "given strength in their creation" at al-Aʿrāf 7/69 |

All three views are transmitted in the sources. **I impose no firm preference.** The link established with al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/128-129 appears to give the first view an advantage, because there ʿĀd is explicitly mentioned as **building structures**; but this does not refute the second — a community may be both tent-dwelling and building.

### What should not be said about Iram

I need to open this section separately, because the greater part of the narratives circulating around this verse are things not found in the text.

**One. The descriptions in the story literature.** In the books of exegesis and story there circulate extremely detailed accounts about Iram: a city made of gold and silver, gardens likened to paradise, a ruler who had it built and the name of that ruler. **These accounts have no ground whatever in the Qurʾān.** The ruler's name spoken of does not occur in the Qurʾān. Some of the classical scholars regarded these accounts as of the kind of Isrāʾīliyyāt and rejected them; others contented themselves with transmitting them and issued no ruling on their soundness. I do not convey the details; because to convey them gives them, unawares, a weight.

The information the Qurʾān gives is three words: *Irama dhāti'l-ʿimād*. And in the next verse it adds one more sentence: the like of it had not been created. What the text says is this much.

**Two. Archaeological claims.** At the end of the twentieth century an archaeological project carried out in the south of the Arabian peninsula — around Oman and the Rubʿ al-Khālī — was reported in the press under headlines of "Iram found". Care is needed here: **there is no proof that would identify an archaeological site with the Iram of the Qurʾān, and this equation is not a result accepted in the archaeological community.** That old settlements and trading centres have been found in the region is one thing; to say that what has been found is the same as the name in the text is another.

This is the sibling of the note entered for Thamūd in Sūrat al-Shams. There too it was stated that the structures standing today do not belong to Thamūd. **The same discipline is required here:** to tie the truth of a text to an excavation report is to tie the text to the fate of that report.

**Three. What is certain.** Those addressed by the Qurʾān knew ʿĀd; the name lived in Arab memory as an emblem of destruction; and the sūra mentions this name not to give a history lesson but for a **comparison**. The other side of the comparison will come in the fifteenth verse: a single human being and his wealth.

### ٱلَّتِى لَمْ يُخْلَقْ مِثْلُهَا فِى ٱلْبِلَٰدِ

**The pronoun is feminine:** *allatī… mithluhā.* What is its referent?

There are two possibilities: *Iram* (as a tribe or as a city, either can be taken as feminine) or directly *ʿĀd* (which as a tribal name can be treated as feminine). It produces no difference in meaning: what is without a like is the community or the place mentioned.

**What does "the like of which had not been created" mean?** There is disagreement here:

| Reading | Meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute** | Never, nowhere was its like created | The wording of the sentence appears unqualified |
| **Qualified** | In those lands, in that period, it had no like | The note **fi'l-bilād** at the end of the sentence; had it been a claim of absolute uniqueness, this note would not have been needed |

**The second reading appears stronger**, and its ground is in the text itself: the note *fi'l-bilād* narrows the scope of the claim. I state this as a preference; it is not binding.

### فِى ٱلْبِلَٰدِ — a word repeated within the sūra

And here there is a detail that must not be missed.

The same word will come again three verses later:

- **89/8:** *lam yukhlaq mithluhā **fi'l-bilād*** — "the like of which had not been created among the lands."
- **89/11:** *alladhīna ṭaghaw **fi'l-bilād*** — "those who exceeded the limit among the lands."

**The same ground, two different sentences.** The uniqueness takes place there, and so does the transgression. And between the two sentences there are only two verses.

This is a silent ruling of the sūra: **the claim and the offence are committed in the same place.** The stage of the uniqueness and the stage of the overstepping are not two separate places. The sentence "its like was not among the lands" is built as a praise, and three verses later what happened in those same lands is stated.

The repetition of the word may also be required by the verse-ending — both end with the sound *-ād*. But that the repetition produces meaning stands in the text. I record this as my own reading.

## 89/9 — وَثَمُودَ ٱلَّذِينَ جَابُوا۟ ٱلصَّخْرَ بِٱلْوَادِ

*Wa Thamūda'lladhīna jābu'ṣ-ṣakhra bi'l-wād*
"And to Thamūd, who carved out the rocks in the valley."

### جَابَ — to carve, to cut through

**Root: ج-و-ب.** Its meaning is **to cut, to pierce, to hollow out, to pass through the inside of something.** *Jāba'l-arḍa* — he traversed the land, he crossed it.

The concrete flavour of the word is this: to enter into a mass and **open** it. Not merely to cut; to bring a road or a hollow out of its inside.

**And from the same root comes جَوَاب (*jawāb*, answer).**

To establish this link is instructive. A *jawāb* is what **cuts through** and brings the counterpart of a question; *ijāba* and *istijāba* are from the same root as well — to respond to a call. The root's common idea is to come opposite a thing and meet it: to come opposite the rock and split it, to come opposite the question and meet it.

I record the observation that follows from this **as my own reading and in a measured way**: Thamūd was a people that could answer the rock; it could not answer what was said to it. Two works from the same root — one done, one not done. I do not present this as a claim of rhetoric; I record it as the echo the word carries.

### ٱلصَّخْر and ٱلْوَاد

**صَخْر (*ṣakhr*)** — hard, large rock. Not soil or soft stone; a resisting mass.

**ٱلْوَاد (*al-wād*)** — a valley. The original of the word is **الوادي** (*al-wādī*), with a *yāʾ* at the end. In the verse the *yāʾ* has dropped: *al-wād*.

This is the same phenomenon as *yasri* in the fourth verse: **the dropping of the final *yāʾ* for agreement of verse-ending.** The sūra performs the same operation twice, and in both the reason is the same: the settling of the verse-ends onto the sound *-ād* / *-r*.

I should like to add an observation, as my own reading: the nine verses in this part of the sūra (6-14) close **almost entirely with the same sound** — *ʿĀd, ʿimād, bilād, wād, awtād, bilād, fasād, ʿadhāb, mirṣād.* Eight of the nine end with the sound *-ād*. So tight a unity of sound makes the section a single block: three peoples, their transgressions and their recompenses, stand in the ear as a single chain.

### Thamūd — where it was treated, what is here

The story of Thamūd was treated fully in Sūrat al-Shams: what the Qurʾān says, what the reports add, and the historical frame were given separately. I do not repeat it.

Among the things recorded there **this verse already occurred**: *"They made houses by hewing the mountains (al-Aʿrāf 7/74; al-Ḥijr 15/82; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/149). In Sūrat al-Fajr they are mentioned as 'those who carved out the rocks in the valley' (al-Fajr 89/9)."* I close that link from here.

The only new thing belonging here is the choice of the word. Elsewhere the Qurʾān says *yanḥitūna mina'l-jibāli buyūtan* — "they hew houses out of the mountains." Here the verb is *jābū*: **they carved, they cut through.** There is no word for house, only the rock and the verb. The sūra is concerned not with what the structure was but **with the rock's having been split.**

This choice is meaningful, because in this part of the sūra all three peoples are mentioned by the **work** they did, not by the **life** they built.

### A warning about archaeology — it needs repeating here

I need to repeat here the note entered in the section on al-Shams, because **this verse of al-Fajr is the verse where that note is most needed.** The expression "those who carved out the rocks in the valley" is the sentence most open to being directly matched with the rock faces photographed today.

What was recorded there was this:

> The Qurʾān's description *"they made houses by hewing the mountains"* is associated with the rock-cut tomb façades in the region of **al-Ḥijr** (today Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ) in north-western Arabia. Care is needed here: **the monumental façades standing today belong to the Nabataean period (roughly the 1st century BC – 1st century AD).** Thamūd, however, is a community dated far earlier than that. That is, the tradition of rock-carving in the region shows a continuity, but the structures tourists see today are not the structures of Thamūd.

This distinction must be preserved. To say "here are the houses of Thamūd" is easy and impressive; but it is wrong, and when the error is discovered it damages the text as well.

The second thing recorded in the section on al-Shams holds here too: Thamūd was not a legendary name for those addressed by the Qurʾān. The caravan road from Mecca to Syria passed through the region. The sūra is not telling a tale; it is speaking of a ruin the one addressed saw on his way.

## 89/10 — وَفِرْعَوْنَ ذِى ٱلْأَوْتَادِ

*Wa Firʿawna dhi'l-awtād*
"And to Pharaoh of the stakes."

### وَتَد — stake

**Root: و-ت-د.** *Watid* / *watad*: a stake, a peg, a post driven into the ground. Its plural is **أوتاد (*awtād*)**.

The work of a stake is one thing: **to hold something in its place.** It fixes a tent, a rope, a boundary. A stake is not driven for its own sake; it is driven so that something else will not move.

**There is another occurrence of the word in the Qurʾān, and it should be read together with this one:**

*"Have We not made the mountains as stakes?"* (al-Nabaʾ 78/7 — *wa'l-jibāla awtādā*)

The same word, in two separate places, attached to two separate subjects:

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/7** | **al-Fajr 89/10** |
|---|---|---|
| Whose the stakes are | Creation's | Pharaoh's |
| What they do | They fix the earth | They fix the kingdom |
| Who placed them | God | Pharaoh (so he claims) |
| Their result | Order | In verse 21 it will be pounded and levelled |

This comparison must be made, because the logic of the sūra passes through here. **The idea of fixing is not evil in itself** — when the Qurʾān mentions the mountains as stakes it counts this as a favour. What is evil is the confusion about to whom the authority of fixing belongs.

And the sūra will resolve this in the twenty-first verse: *dukkati'l-arḍu dakkan dakkā* — the earth will be pounded and levelled. Stakes included.

### The disagreement: what do "the stakes" convey?

| View | What it means | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Soldiers, armies** | The force that holds up his dominion; those who hold up the kingdom as stakes hold up a tent | The use in Arabic of the metaphor of "stakes" for multitude and support; that the word comes in the plural |
| **A firm kingdom, settledness** | A power with its roots driven into the ground, unshakeable | The essential function of a stake is fixing; the metaphor is direct |
| **Structures, monuments** | Buildings made so as to be permanent | Egypt's monumental architecture; that ʿĀd and Thamūd too are mentioned by their structures |
| **An instrument of torture** | Stakes on which he stretched people | Some exegetes have understood it so |

**I impose no firm preference.** I do not go into detail on the reports concerning the fourth view; I content myself with saying "some exegetes have understood it so", because I cannot issue a ruling on the soundness of the details.

This much I can say: **the first three views come out at the same place** — a power thought to be fixed and unshakeable. And this is the only thing the flow of the sūra requires, because all three peoples are mentioned with the same claim.

**At Ṣād 38/12 the same epithet occurs again:** *"Before them the people of Noah, ʿĀd and Pharaoh of the stakes also denied."* There too the epithet is within the same list — and in the next verse Thamūd is mentioned. That is, the expression "of the stakes" is a settled designation the Qurʾān uses for Pharaoh; it is not an invention particular to al-Fajr. This shows that the expression was something understood by those addressed.

### Pharaoh's *ṭughyān*

When the subject of *ṭughyān* was treated in Sūrat al-ʿAlaq, what was recorded was this: **this root is used in the Qurʾān most often of Pharaoh** (*"Go to Pharaoh; he has exceeded all bounds"* — Ṭāhā 20/24; al-Nāziʿāt 79/17), and the content of the transgression was given in al-Nāziʿāt: *"He said: I am your lord, the highest"* (79/24).

The conclusion there was this: **the furthest point of *ṭughyān* is to put oneself in the position of Lord.**

In al-Fajr this link is closed. The next verse will state the common act of the three peoples as *ṭaghaw* — and the most recognised subject of that verb is Pharaoh, mentioned in the verse immediately before. The sūra names the emblem of the word first, then states the act.

### The descent from the crowd to a single person

There is an observation about the structure here, and I record it as my own reading.

The type of identity of the three links is not the same:

- **ʿĀd** — the name of a people. A community.
- **Thamūd** — the name of a people. A community.
- **Pharaoh** — a **person**; more precisely a title, but a singular subject.

The triad descends from the plural towards the singular. And in the eleventh verse all three are gathered by a plural relative pronoun (*alladhīna ṭaghaw*), and then in the fifteenth verse **a single noun** remains: *al-insān*.

That is, the sūra descends from the crowd to a single person, and from the single person to man himself. Pharaoh is the intermediate step of this descent: not a people, but not yet "man" either — a particular, historical, recognised subject.

And this descent explains the sūra's method. The historical narrative is not a history lesson; it is the operation of **enlarging the scale of the diagnosis and then bringing it down upon the one addressed.** The one addressed does not find himself in ʿĀd, and certainly not in Pharaoh; but he finds himself in *al-insān*. The sūra brings him there step by step.

## 89/11-12 — ٱلَّذِينَ طَغَوْا۟ فِى ٱلْبِلَٰدِ • فَأَكْثَرُوا۟ فِيهَا ٱلْفَسَادَ

*Alladhīna ṭaghaw fi'l-bilād • Fa-aktharū fīha'l-fasād*
"Those who exceeded the limit among the lands, and multiplied corruption in them."

Three separate peoples are gathered into a single sentence. And what gathers them is neither geography nor period nor belief — **the act.**

### طَغَىٰ — *ṭughyān*

The root **ط-غ-ي / ط-غ-و** was taken up in detail at al-ʿAlaq 96/6-7 and in the section on al-Layl; I do not repeat the findings there, I bring what is needed.

The main point recorded there was this: **the original, concrete sense of the root is water's exceeding its limit, overflowing.** There was a clean proof of this from within the Qurʾān: *"When the water overflowed, We carried you in the running ship"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/11) — here the word carries no moral sense at all, it is only the water's crossing its boundary.

And the definition there: **ṭughyān is a thing's exceeding its own boundary. When water passes its bed it becomes a flood; flood water is no longer water that nourishes but water that destroys. The same water, in the same quantity, is life when it stays within its bed.**

This image explained the whole weight of the word: transgression is not the possession of a bad thing; it is **the carrying of a good thing outside its own boundary.** Power is not evil; boundless power is transgression.

**What is to be added here is this:**

**One. The verb is plural and perfect.** *Ṭaghaw* — "they exceeded the limit." A completed and shared act. In al-ʿAlaq the verb was imperfect (*yaṭghā* — continuity, a tendency); here it is perfect, because what is spoken of is no longer a tendency but **a closed file.**

**Two. The note *fi'l-bilād*.** The transgression is mentioned here not as a personal moral fault but as something committed **in the land**. The word indicates the public sphere: cities, settlements, the place where people live together.

This distinction must be held. A person's exceeding the limit within himself and a community's exceeding the limit in the place where it lives are not the same thing. The sūra speaks of the second: transgression is described here as **a way of establishing an order.**

**Three.** And the repetition I recorded earlier is completed here: in the eighth verse the uniqueness was *fi'l-bilād*, in the eleventh the transgression is *fi'l-bilād*. **The same ground.** What is mentioned with praise and what is charged as an offence take place in the same place.

And the word in the fifth verse must be remembered: *dhī ḥijr* — possessor of a boundary. The sūra made its oath to "the person who has a boundary"; now it mentions "those who exceed the boundary". **The two words are two faces of the same concept.** I record this as my own reading.

### فَأَكْثَرُوا۟ فِيهَا ٱلْفَسَادَ — a point of word order

The structure of the sentence is striking. The expected phrasing would have been *fa-afsadū fīhā* — "they worked corruption there." The verse does not say this.

What it says is: *fa-aktharū fīha'l-fasād* — **"they multiplied the corruption there."**

There are two differences:

**One. The verb is not "to do" but "to multiply."** *Kathura* → *akthara*: to increase, to multiply.

**Two. The object is definite:** *al-fasād*, not "corruption" but "**the** corruption."

These two details together open a grammatical possibility: the sentence permits the reading that the corruption already existed and that they increased it. That is, the peoples did not invent a disorder from nothing; they enlarged what was there.

I present this as a **grammatical possibility**, not as a firm ruling. The definite article is also used in Arabic to convey the class (*al-fasād* = "the class of corruption"), and on that reading the subtlety above disappears. Both readings are possible.

But if the first reading is right, the meaning it produces agrees with the attitude of the sūra: **great destructions generally do not begin from nothing; they come about by the enlarging of the scale of an existing disorder.** What distinguishes the peoples is not that they invented evil but that they turned it into an order.

### فَسَاد — the carrying over of al-Baqara 2/11

The root **ف-س-د** was taken up in the section on al-Baqara 2/11-12. What was recorded there was this:

> The root **فسد**: to be spoilt, to rot, to lose balance — a thing's going out of its own order. **صلح**, on the other hand: to be fitting, to be in its proper place, to be repaired. From the same root come *ṣulḥ* (peace) and *ṣāliḥ*. The two concepts presuppose the existence of an order: corruption is to spoil the order, reform is to set it back in place.

And the essential finding there was this:

> **The corrupter does not define himself as a corrupter. He defines himself as a reformer. The great majority of evil is done not with the intention of doing evil but with the claim of putting right. Almost every one of the great destructions in history had a programme of reform.**

And: *"the matter is not lying but the inability to see one's own condition. This is a heavier diagnosis: the liar knows, this one does not."*

**When this finding is carried over to al-Fajr, one of the strongest links of the sūra emerges.**

Because the three peoples of al-Fajr did not count themselves corrupters. Let us look at what they did:

- **ʿĀd:** set up a sign on every height, took strongholds for themselves (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/128-129).
- **Thamūd:** carved the rock, founded a settlement in the valley.
- **Pharaoh:** drove stakes, fixed his kingdom.

**All three were building.** A community that raises columns, carves rock and drives stakes counts itself not a corrupter but a **founder**. The work it does is visible to the eye, tangible, measurable; it leaves a monument behind it.

Why then does the verse say "they multiplied corruption"?

The answer is in the continuation of the finding in al-Baqara: **the difference between building and corrupting lies not in the structure itself but in what it is built upon.** A structure can rise by crushing what is beneath it as well. To raise a column is an act of construction; to stand on someone's back in order to raise the column is corruption. The two works are done at the same moment, with the same hand, and the onlooker sees only the column.

And this is why the expression at al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/128-129 is important. What is asked of ʿĀd there is **why** the structures were built: *"hoping to abide forever."* The structure itself is not charged; the meaning loaded onto the structure is charged.

This is exactly the same structure as the sūra's later diagnosis. In the fifteenth verse man will not be charged with acquiring wealth; he will be charged **with the inference he makes from wealth.**

### "Corruption on the earth" in the Qurʾān

The word occurs in the Qurʾān within a settled construct: *al-fasād fi'l-arḍ.* A few examples:

- *"When he turns away, he strives to work corruption on the earth…"* (al-Baqara 2/205).
- *"Do not work corruption on the earth after it has been set right"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/56). **This verse is important:** corruption is forbidden **after** the setting right. That is, an order that can be spoilt is presupposed.
- *"Do not seek corruption on the earth; God does not love the corrupters"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/77).
- *"That abode of the hereafter We give to those who desire neither exaltation on the earth nor corruption"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/83).

The last two verses are within the story of Qārūn — that is, in the context of **wealth and self-exaltation**. Al-Fajr's transition from peoples to man is a transition the Qurʾān establishes elsewhere as well.

## 89/13-14 — فَصَبَّ عَلَيْهِمْ رَبُّكَ سَوْطَ عَذَابٍ • إِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَبِٱلْمِرْصَادِ

*Fa-ṣabba ʿalayhim rabbuka sawṭa ʿadhāb • Inna rabbaka la-bi'l-mirṣād*
"So your Lord poured out upon them a scourge of punishment. Surely your Lord is in the place of watching."

### فَ — the particle of cause

The sentence begins with *fāʾ*. In Arabic this letter conveys sequence and **cause**: "thereupon, as a result of this."

That is, between a three-verse file of offences and a one-verse ruling there is a connective, and the connective establishes causality. The sūra does not tell of the destruction as a misfortune or a natural event; it tells of it as **a recompense.**

### صَبَّ — to pour out

**Root: ص-ب-ب.** Its meaning is **to pour, to empty out** — and it is used especially of **liquid**. Water is poured, rain empties down.

The choice of the word says three things:

**One. From above.** What is poured comes from above. Not a direction that can be resisted.

**Two. All at once.** To pour is not to give piece by piece. It is to empty out in one go, as a whole.

**Three. Liquid.** A liquid is what has no shape of its own and seeps everywhere. It is not stopped by a wall; it finds an opening.

Another use in the Qurʾān shows the weight of the word: *"Then pour over his head the punishment of boiling water"* (al-Dukhān 44/48). The same verb, in the same direction.

And if it is remembered that the destruction of ʿĀd was by a **wind** (al-Ḥāqqa 69/6), the use of a verb for liquid is a metaphor: the punishment, even when it is wind, "pours" — because what the word conveys is not its substance but **its manner of arrival.**

### سَوْطَ عَذَابٍ — a scourge of punishment

The construct is odd and has occupied the exegetes.

**سَوْط (*sawṭ*)** — a scourge, a whip. It is a known and widespread word in Arabic.

| Reading | Meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Whip** | The punishment's being brought down like a whip | The most widespread sense of the word; combined with the verb "to pour" it builds a metaphor |
| **Mixture / assortment** | From the root's sense of "to mix" (*sāṭa* — he mixed; *miswāṭ* — a mixing instrument): "a mixture of punishment, punishment of every kind" | The root really does carry this sense; it appears more consistent with the verb "to pour" |
| **Share / portion** | Figuratively "a share of punishment" | That the construct comes indefinite (*ʿadhābin*) may convey a partiality |

All three readings are found in the sources. **I impose no firm preference.**

**But there is a subtlety and it needs recording.** A whip is not a lethal instrument. A sword kills, a spear kills; a whip is an instrument of **discipline and warning**. Some exegetes have drawn this conclusion from it: the destruction in this world is, beside the punishment of the hereafter, no more than a stroke of the whip — that is, what is shown is not the punishment itself but **a specimen** of it.

I convey this as a view, not as my own preference. But combined with the third reading (share/portion) a consistent reading emerges: what is poured out is not the whole of the punishment but a part of it.

And this fits the flow of the sūra. Because in the twenty-fifth verse the real scale will be given: *"On that day no one will punish as He punishes."*

### إِنَّ رَبَّكَ لَبِٱلْمِرْصَادِ

The most memorised sentence of the sūra and — grammatically — the verse that stands nearest to being the answer of the oath.

#### رَصَد — to watch

**Root: ر-ص-د.** Its meaning is **to watch, to wait, to keep a lookout for something's passing.**

The word lives on in Turkish today, and this keeps the root alive for the reader there: **rasat** (observation), **rasathane** (observatory), **rasat etmek** (to observe). The same root. English has no cognate; the sense has to be carried by "watching" and "observation post".

Its derivatives:

- **راصد (*rāṣid*)** — the one who watches.
- **رَصَد (*raṣad*)** — a watcher, a lookout; and the act of watching itself.
- **مِرْصَاد (*mirṣād*)** — by its pattern a noun of place or a noun of instrument: **the place of watching**, the point where the watchman stands.

Its occurrences in the Qurʾān give the flavour of the word:

- *"Whoever tries to listen finds a flame watching for him"* (al-Jinn 72/9 — *shihāban raṣadā*).
- *"He sends watchers before him and behind him"* (al-Jinn 72/27 — *raṣadā*).
- *"…as a lookout for one who wars against God and His Messenger"* (al-Tawba 9/107 — *irṣādan*).

**And most important of all:**

- ***"Surely Hell is a place of watching"*** (al-Nabaʾ 78/21 — *inna jahannama kānat mirṣādā*).

**The same word.** In al-Fajr the *Lord* is in the place of watching; in al-Nabaʾ *Hell* is a place of watching. The two verses should be read together, and when they are, an image emerges: two points held along a road.

The verse in al-Nabaʾ plainly shows the word's sense of "standing at the head of a place that will be passed" — Hell is mentioned not as a destination but as a **point of passage**. The use in al-Fajr follows the same logic.

#### Not "He is a watcher" but "He is in the place of watching"

This is the finest point of the sentence, and it is generally lost in translation.

The verse does not say *inna rabbaka la-raṣīdun* — it does not say "your Lord is a watcher". It says: *la-**bi'l**-mirṣād* — **"He is in the place of watching."**

The difference looks small; it is not:

- An **attribute** is a quality. It says what someone is.
- A **place** is a position. It says **where** someone is.

And a sentence conveying position presupposes a road. To be "in the place of watching" is to be upon the road that will be travelled. That is, what the sentence says is not an enumeration of attributes but **a statement of geography**: the place the fugitive will pass has been secured.

This is the sentence that closes the whole historical section of the sūra. ʿĀd passed, Thamūd passed, Pharaoh passed. All three had to pass the same point.

#### Threefold emphasis

Three elements of emphasis have come one upon another in the sentence:

1. **إنّ** — the strongest particle of emphasis in Arabic.
2. **لـ** — the *lām al-ibtidāʾ* at the head of the predicate, which further emphasises.
3. **بـ** — the *bāʾ* at the head of the predicate; some lexicographers see an emphatic function here too.

And the sentence is a **nominal sentence** — there is no verb. In Arabic a nominal sentence conveys fixity and continuity. Not "He begins to watch" or "He watches"; **He is, just so, continuously, in the place of watching.**

There is a reason for so much emphasis. By the measure of rhetoric, heavy emphasis presupposes that the one addressed is in a state of **denial or hesitation**. And to think oneself unobserved is one of man's most familiar assumptions; in Sūrat al-Balad this assumption was explicitly recorded: *"Does he suppose that no one has seen him?"* (al-Balad 90/7).

#### A note on the rendering "ambush"

The word *mirṣād* is often rendered into English as **"ambush"**. This rendering catches one sense of the word but produces a false association, and it needs to be cut off at the outset.

In English "ambush" suggests stealth, a trap and an unfair advantage: to ambush someone is to attack him without warning. The Arabic *mirṣād* carries no such value judgment; the word conveys only **the point from which watching is done**. A watchtower is a *mirṣād*, and so is an observatory.

To make this distinction is especially necessary where a divine attribute is in question. The verse does not speak of a trap; it says that **nothing escapes notice**. The difference between the two changes the kind of fear: a trap frightens by what is unexpected, watching by being called to account.

### The link with al-Ghāshiya 88/22: whose is the place of watching?

This is one of the tightest links between sūras, and it is established with the neighbouring sūra.

Sūrat al-Ghāshiya, addressing the Prophet, says this:

> *"You are not one who compels / oversees them."* (al-Ghāshiya 88/22 — *lasta ʿalayhim bi-musayṭir*)

**مُصَيْطِر (*musayṭir*)** is, by its root, one who stands over a thing and watches it, records it, holds the matter in hand; the word carries both "overseer" and "one who compels".

And al-Fajr, two sūras later, says this:

> *"Surely your Lord is in the place of watching."* (al-Fajr 89/14)

**Two neighbouring sūras attach the same work to two different subjects — and take it from one to give it to the other:**

| | **al-Ghāshiya 88/22** | **al-Fajr 89/14** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | The Prophet | The Lord |
| What | *musayṭir* — standing over them, overseeing | *bi'l-mirṣād* — standing in the place of watching |
| The ruling | **You are not** | **He is so** |

This pair says two things at once, and both are necessary:

**One. The limit of the messenger's authority.** The Prophet's work is to convey, not to produce the result. In the section on al-Baqara 2/6 this finding was made: *"the verse is said to the one who gives the warning. Its function is not to issue a ruling but to relieve the warner: your task is to convey, not to produce the result."* Al-Ghāshiya writes this openly.

**Two. But the watching really exists.** Had al-Ghāshiya been read alone, an impression could have arisen that there is no watching at all. Al-Fajr closes that gap: the place of watching is not empty — only the one standing there is not the messenger.

This sets a lasting measure regarding the distribution of religious responsibility. **Conveying and overseeing are two separate works, and the second has not been given to man.** I record this in the section on al-Ghāshiya as well; the two sūras complete one another at this point.

## 89/15-16 — فَأَمَّا ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ إِذَا مَا ٱبْتَلَىٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ فَأَكْرَمَهُۥ وَنَعَّمَهُۥ فَيَقُولُ رَبِّىٓ أَكْرَمَنِ • وَأَمَّآ إِذَا مَا ٱبْتَلَىٰهُ فَقَدَرَ عَلَيْهِ رِزْقَهُۥ فَيَقُولُ رَبِّىٓ أَهَٰنَنِ

*Fa-amma'l-insānu idhā ma'btalāhu rabbuhu fa-akramahu wa naʿʿamahu fa-yaqūlu rabbī akraman • Wa ammā idhā ma'btalāhu fa-qadara ʿalayhi rizqahu fa-yaqūlu rabbī ahānan*

"As for man: when his Lord tests him and honours him and gives him ease, he says, 'My Lord has honoured me.' But when He tests him and measures out his provision to him, he says, 'My Lord has abased me.'"

The heart of the sūra. The historical section ends here, and the text puts a single subject in place of the peoples: *al-insān*.

### ٱلْإِنسَٰن — the definite article

The word again comes with the definite article: *al-insān*. As was treated in the sections on al-ʿAṣr and al-ʿAlaq, this article conveys **istighrāq** here: it covers the whole of the class. Not "a man" or "that man" — the human **kind.**

And its coming in the singular is meaningful too. The measure recorded in the section on al-ʿAṣr holds here as well: when a name of a kind is used in the singular, the ruling does not come down upon this or that person; it is written into **the definition of the kind**. That is, this is not a majority statistic; it is the description of the default state, valid unless the contrary is proved.

Its counterpart within the sūra is this: after three peoples have been described, the one addressed might have relaxed — "they were transgressors, I am not." The word's coming as a class closes this door.

### ٱبْتَلَىٰ — to test

**Root: ب-ل-و / ب-ل-ي.** This root was taken up in the section on al-Baqara 2/49, and the finding there is of central importance here:

> The usual English renderings of *balāʾ* narrow it to calamity alone — "affliction", "ordeal"; in Arabic the root *b-l-w* means **to try, to test by wearing down**, and it **covers testing with good as well.** The Qurʾān says this openly: *"We test you with evil and with good as a trial"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/35).

**What is to be added here is the concrete sense of the root: to wear out, to make old.** *Baliya'th-thawb* — the garment wore out. *Ablā* — he wore it out. *Bilā* — wearing away.

This concrete sense sets the word in a quite different place. **Testing is wearing out by use.** The way to measure a thing's durability is to use it; a thing not used does not wear out, but neither has it been tried.

That is, *ibtilāʾ* is not a calamity coming from outside; it is **a thing's being put to work**. And what is put to work here is man himself.

The English word "examination" does not carry this concreteness; it gives the association of a school test — an operation that comes from outside and is over when it is passed. The root's own image is closer: a cloth's being worn out by use, and what comes to light when it is worn.

The verb is here **in Form VIII (*iftiʿāl*)** (*ibtalā*): this form generally adds an intensity and a deliberateness to the verb. And the verb is **perfect**, that is, it is mentioned as having taken place.

### The symmetry: changing content, unchanging verb

The single most important finding of the sūra is here, and it must be set out in a table.

| | **The first state (15)** | **The second state (16)** |
|---|---|---|
| **The verb of testing** | *ibtalāhu* — He tested him | *ibtalāhu* — He tested him (**the same verb**) |
| **The content** | *fa-akramahu wa naʿʿamahu* — He honoured him, He gave him ease | *fa-qadara ʿalayhi rizqah* — He measured out his provision |
| **Man's inference** | *rabbī akraman* — "My Lord has honoured me" | *rabbī ahānan* — "My Lord has abased me" |
| **The tense of the inference** | *fa-yaqūlu* — imperfect, continuous | *fa-yaqūlu* — imperfect, continuous |

**The verb is the same. The only thing that changes is the content of the testing.**

Man, however, reads these two states as **two separate verdicts**: he counts the first a certificate of acceptance, the second a certificate of rejection. Whereas by the grammar of the text both are two applications of the same act.

This is where the whole force of the sūra is gathered. And its force lies in what is not said: the verse does not say "abundance is really bad", nor does it say "want is really good". **It sets up no value judgment at all.** It only shows that the two states are attached to the same verb, and records that man draws two different conclusions from this.

### Points of grammar

**One. The فَأَمَّا… وَأَمَّا pattern.** This is Arabic's pattern of comparison, and it was used in Sūrat al-Layl as well:

> *"As for one who gives and guards himself… but as for one who is miserly and counts himself in no need…"* (al-Layl 92/5-8)

**But the difference between the two sūras is very important and is generally not seen:**

| | **al-Layl 92/5-10** | **al-Fajr 89/15-16** |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | *fa-ammā man… wa ammā man…* | *fa-amma'l-insān… wa ammā…* |
| Subject | **Two different people** (*man* — "whoever") | **A single man** (*al-insān*; the subject is not repeated in the second verse) |
| What divides them | The person's **choice** | The person's **condition** |
| Result | Two separate outcomes | Two separate inferences in one person |

In al-Layl the pattern was dividing **people**: the one who gives and the one who is miserly. In al-Fajr the pattern divides **conditions**, and the man is one. That is, al-Layl was setting up a classification; al-Fajr is drawing a **portrait**.

This difference sharpens the sūra's diagnosis. The verse does not speak of two types of man — one grateful, the other rebellious. It speaks of two sentences said by the same man **at two different times**. That is, the matter is not character but **measure**. The person is consistent: in both cases he uses the same measure. The measure itself is faulty.

**Two. In the second verse the subject is not repeated.** The first verse says *ibtalāhu rabbuhu* — "his Lord tested him"; the second says only *ibtalāhu* — "He tested him", the agent not stated, understood from the preceding verse.

This is an ellipsis and is ordinary in Arabic. But the effect it produces is worth recording: in the first state who does the testing is written **openly**, in the second it stays **hidden**. I record this as a grammatical observation, not as a claim of rhetoric; because the shortening may also be required by verse-ending and fluency.

**Three. أَكْرَمَنِ and أَهَٰنَنِ.** The first-person *yāʾ* at the end of the two verbs (*akramanī* → *akraman*) has dropped. This is the same phenomenon as *yasri* in the fourth verse and *al-wād* in the ninth: **the elision of the final *yāʾ* for agreement of verse-ending.** The sūra performs this operation four times.

It is transmitted that in the variant readings these words are read **with the *yāʾ*** as well. There is no difference of meaning. Since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which authority, I write no name.

**Four. The verb is imperfect: فَيَقُولُ.** Not "he said" but **"he says"**. In Arabic the imperfect conveys continuity and renewal. That is, this is not a word said once; it is a sentence built anew each time. The sūra records not a single utterance from man's mouth but **a working reflex.**

### قَدَرَ عَلَيْهِ رِزْقَهُ — to narrow, or to measure?

The wording of this expression refutes man's inference **on its own**. For that reason it must be dwelt on.

**Root: ق-د-ر.** This root carries three separate families of sense in Arabic, and all three are given in the same entry:

1. **Power, capability.** *Qadīr*, *qudra*, *muqtadir*.
2. **Measure, determination, apportioning.** *Qadr*, *miqdār*, *taqdīr*. This sense was treated in the section on Sūrat al-Qadr: the relation of the night's name to the sense of "apportioning".
3. **To narrow, to straiten.** The use in the verse.

The third sense follows from the second: to measure a thing is to set a limit to it; to set a limit is to take it out of being limitless.

**And here is the fine point of the verse.**

The verse does not say *fa-manaʿa ʿanhu rizqahu* — it does not say "He **withheld** his provision". It does not say *fa-salaba* — it does not say "He **took it away**". It says: *fa-qadara ʿalayhi rizqah* — **"He measured out his provision to him"**, that is, He gave it by measure.

The Qurʾān uses this verb with the same pair elsewhere too:

- *"God **extends** provision to whom He wills and **measures it out**"* (al-Raʿd 13/26 — *yabsuṭu… wa yaqdir*).
- *"Surely your Lord extends provision to whom He wills and measures it out"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/30).
- *"Let him whose provision is measured out spend from what God has given him"* (al-Ṭalāq 65/7 — *wa man qudira ʿalayhi rizquh*). **This verse is particularly important:** even the person in a state of straitening is asked to give. That is, straitening does not take a person outside obligation.

In these verses what stands opposite the verb is *basṭ* — to spread out, to widen. **Both verbs are verbs of giving**; one gives widely, one narrowly. Neither is "not giving".

The conclusion is this: **straitening is not being denied; it is being given by measure.** And a thing given by measure shows the existence of someone measuring.

Man draws the conclusion "I have been abased" from this word. The word itself says "I have been measured". **The verse has hidden man's error inside his own sentence.** I present this as my own reading; the meaning of the word, however, is what the lexicons record.

### أَكْرَمَ and أَهَانَ — the measure man uses

Both verbs are words of **value**. And this shows where the sūra's diagnosis is looking.

**كرم (*karam*)** — root **ك-ر-م**: generosity, worth, nobility, hospitality. *Karīm* is both the generous and the valuable. *Ikrām* is behaviour that shows worth is being given to someone — receiving him, taking him seriously, holding him high.

**هون (*hawn*)** — root **ه-و-ن**: lightness, insignificance, worthlessness. *Hāna* — he became light and worthless. *Ihāna* — to make someone worthless. *Muhīn* — abasing.

That is, what does man say? "I have been **given worth**" and "I have been **made worthless**."

**Man reads wealth as a declaration of worth.** He reads what is in his hand not as a resource, a means, a trust; but as a **verdict** issued about himself.

And in doing this he even uses the right word: *rabbī* — "my Lord". As I recorded in the table of *Rabb* in the sūra, two of the word's eight occurrences in the sūra are in man's own mouth, and both are sentences that are rejected.

**That is, man's error is not unbelief.** In his sentences there is God, there is lordship, there is the acknowledgment that it is He who gives. The error concerns **what the giving means.**

### The depth of the diagnosis

Why these two verses are so important must be opened out in several points.

**First: this is not a doctrine but a reflex.**

No one sits down and founds a teaching that "wealth is divine approval". Asked, everyone would reject it. But behaviour works that way.

This is the sibling of the finding made for the verb *yaḥsabu* at al-Humaza 104/3. What was recorded there was this: *"No one who piles up wealth says 'I shall become immortal with this'; asked, he would deny it. But the verb is 'he supposes', and this conveys not a declared belief but **a working assumption**. The behaviour itself carries a belief, even if the person does not defend that belief in words."*

In al-Fajr the assumption has been **put into words**: man builds the sentence. The difference is this: al-Humaza was moving from behaviour towards belief, al-Fajr records the sentence directly. The two are two records of the same mechanism.

**Second: the two inferences require one another.**

This is the point most often passed over in the sūra.

Whoever counts abundance an "honouring" **must** count want an "abasing". Because the two are the two ends of the same measure: if the quantity of wealth is the indicator of divine assessment, then much wealth is a high mark and little wealth a low one. Whoever accepts the measure has accepted both results.

This is why the Qurʾān rejects the two **together**. The *kallā* in the next verse cuts not a single sentence but the measure itself.

And from this a practical consequence follows: **a person who counts his own abundance divine approval is doomed to collapse when he falls into want.** Because his own measure condemns him. The form faith takes in a time of abundance turns against the person in a time of want.

**Third: the measure is more harmful when applied to others.**

And this is where the logic of the sūra turns.

A person who counts his own straitening an abasement counts another's straitening so as well. The measure does not stay personal; it becomes a view of the world. In that view the poor man is a person who has received a low mark in the divine assessment.

And the natural consequence of such a view is **indifference towards the poor and the orphan.** Because within that logic their situation is a deserved situation.

**This is the reason the seventeenth verse is attached here with *bal*.** The verse is not changing the subject; it is stating the result of the diagnosis. The fifteenth and sixteenth verses set up the wrong measure; the seventeenth and eighteenth show what that measure produces in practice.

This is the bridge between the two halves of the sūra, and it stands in the grammar itself: *kallā* cuts the measure, *bal* brings the result.

**Fourth: the Qurʾān rejects this supposition openly elsewhere.**

- *"Do they suppose that, in the wealth and sons We give them, We are hastening to them with good things? No — they are not aware"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/55-56). **This verse is the direct rejection of al-Fajr 89/15:** the same supposition, the same family of verbs (*yaḥsabūna*), and the same cutting-off (*bal*).
- *"Do not let their wealth and their children impress you; God wishes only to punish them by these in the life of this world"* (al-Tawba 9/55). The same object, exactly the opposite function.
- *"Know that your wealth and your children are a trial"* (al-Anfāl 8/28). The word is directly *fitna* — testing.
- **The story of the owner of the two gardens** (al-Kahf 18/32-44). The man who enters his garden and says *"I do not think this will ever perish"* is the long telling of the sentence at al-Fajr 89/15.

And conversely: that most of the prophets were in want is plainly seen in the life stories the Qurʾān tells. If abundance were the indicator of divine approval, most of these stories would have to be read backwards.

**Fifth — and this note must be entered: it is not being said that abundance is not an honouring.**

I need to write this distinction plainly, because otherwise the text is read as a hostility to wealth, and that would be wrong.

The Qurʾān mentions a favour as a favour; it says that what is given is good; it commands thanksgiving. The verse itself says *"He honoured him and gave him ease"* — and it says this **in the narrator's mouth**, that is, it records it as true.

What is rejected is not what is given but **the inference.** That is:

- "It was given to me" → true.
- "What was given is good" → true.
- "Its being given is a verdict about me" → **this is where the rejection falls.**

The difference is fine but its consequences are large. The first and second sentences come out at thanksgiving; the third comes out either at arrogance or at collapse.

### What this says to the present

The present-day counterpart of these two verses is the most concrete place in the sūra.

**The moralising of success.** In modern language the verb "to deserve" is often used when speaking about a person's material condition: deserved wealth, deserved success. The verb itself is innocent — receiving the return of one's labour is a real thing. But when the verb turns into a scheme of explanation, the reverse begins to be counted true as well: that whoever has nothing deserves to have nothing.

For this scheme to work no one needs to defend it. When enough people explain their own situation this way, other people's situations are automatically explained too.

**The counting of poverty as a personal fault.** Whether the cause of poverty in a society is seen as personal choices or as structural conditions determines how that society will treat the poor. The sūra does not enter this debate as a party; the place it enters is further back: **no material condition is a divine verdict about a person.** Neither the higher nor the lower.

I do not turn this into a claim of social science and I do not attach it to a current policy. The limit the verse sets is theological: **material condition is not a proof.** Neither in your own favour nor against another's.

## 89/17-18 — كَلَّا ۖ بَل لَّا تُكْرِمُونَ ٱلْيَتِيمَ • وَلَا تَحَٰٓضُّونَ عَلَىٰ طَعَامِ ٱلْمِسْكِينِ

*Kallā bal lā tukrimūna'l-yatīm • Wa lā taḥāḍḍūna ʿalā ṭaʿāmi'l-miskīn*
"No! Rather, you do not honour the orphan; you do not urge one another to feed the needy."

### كَلَّا — what does it cut?

The function of *kallā* was taken up in the sections on al-Takāthur 102/3 and al-Humaza 104/4: the particle conveys both **rejection** (the erasing of a judgment) and **restraint** (deterrence). I do not repeat it.

The real question here is this: **which sentence does it cut?**

| Possibility | What is cut | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Only 16** | The words "My Lord has abased me" | The particle attaches to the nearest sentence; and "He has abased me" is openly a wrong thing to say |
| **15 and 16 together** | Both inferences; that is, the measure itself | The *bal* that follows brings a new diagnosis, it does not correct a single utterance; and the two sentences are the two ends of the same measure |

**The second reading is the stronger**, and its ground is in the structure of the text.

Had *kallā* cut only the words "He has abased me", the expected continuation would have been a correction: "no, He has not abased you; He has tested you." The verse does not say this. The verse changes the subject and passes to **the behaviour of the one addressed.**

This transition takes on meaning only if what is rejected is the measure itself. Because when the measure is rejected a question arises: if wealth is not an indicator of worth, then where does worth show itself? And the verse gives the answer at once: **in how a person behaves towards one who has no wealth.**

I state this as a preference; **it is not binding.** Both readings have been defended.

**And one finding must be made plainly, because it is generally passed over: the Qurʾān is rejecting both inferences here.** The text is often read as though only the second — the words "He has abased me" — were being rejected; as though the first sentence ("My Lord has honoured me") were a proper expression of thanks. It is not. The structure of the sūra gives the two within the same grammatical pattern, attached to the same verb, and cuts them both with a single *kallā*.

### بَل — *iḍrāb*

**بل** is in Arabic the particle of *iḍrāb*: to leave off the preceding judgment and pass to something else. Its counterparts in English are "the real matter is", "rather", "in fact".

What the particle does here is not to change the subject but **to show the subject's real place.** The one addressed was discussing his own condition; the particle takes the conversation away from him and carries it to someone else's condition.

### The shift in the direction of address — *iltifāt*

The fifteenth and sixteenth verses were speaking **in the third person**: *al-insān… fa-yaqūlu* — "man… says."

In the seventeenth verse there is a sudden shift to the **second person plural**: *lā tukrimūna* — "you do not honour."

This is the style called **iltifāt** in Arabic (a shift in the direction of address); it was treated in the section on al-Humaza 104/5 and its function was recorded there thus: *"the narration halts for a moment, the reader/listener is pulled onto the stage… Up to here the sūra had been describing someone else; the reader was watching from a safe distance. The fifth verse removes that distance."*

In al-Fajr the same operation is performed for the same reason — but harder. Because in al-Humaza the place turned to held a question (*"and what will make you know?"*); here the place turned to holds an **accusation**.

And there is a further reason for this choice. A fault recounted in the third person can be discussed; a fault said in the second person cannot be discussed, only accepted or rejected. The sūra closes the discussion.

### The honouring turned back

Now we come to the sharpest rhetorical move of the sūra, and this move is made **with the root itself**.

What was man saying?

- *rabbī **akrama**n* — "My Lord has **honoured** me."
- *rabbī ahānan* — "My Lord has abased me." (That is: He did not honour me.)

At the centre of both sentences stands the root **ك-ر-م**: one positive, one negative. Man is discussing whether or not he has received honouring.

And what does the answer say?

- *lā **tukrimū**na'l-yatīm* — "you do not **honour** the orphan."

**The same root, the same form (Form IV), turned back.**

The person who expects honouring is told that he does not honour. The person weighing whether worth has been given to him is reminded of whom he himself gives no worth to.

This is the tightest move of the sūra and it is built entirely at the level of the word. Translation generally loses it, because English covers the word with terms that each catch only a part — "to honour", "to treat generously", "to show hospitality"; Arabic *ikrām* is **the whole behaviour of according worth.**

And note: the verse does not say "you do not give to the orphan". It says **"you do not honour him"**. Giving is material; *ikrām* is the whole of the behaviour — how he is looked at, how he is spoken to, where he is seated. The threshold has been shifted from wealth to behaviour.

### The link with al-Māʿūn 107/2-3

The two sūras count exactly the same two offences, in the same order and in almost the same words. This is one of the clearest overlaps among the short Meccan sūras.

> **al-Māʿūn 107/2-3:** *"That is the one who repels the orphan and does not urge the feeding of the needy."*
> **al-Fajr 89/17-18:** *"You do not honour the orphan and you do not urge one another to feed the needy."*

| | **al-Māʿūn 107/2-3** | **al-Fajr 89/17-18** |
|---|---|---|
| **The orphan** | *yaduʿʿu* — he repels (an act done) | *lā tukrimūna* — you do not honour (an act not done) |
| **The needy** | *lā yaḥuḍḍu* — he does not urge (Form I) | *lā taḥāḍḍūna* — you do not urge one another (**the form of reciprocity**) |
| **The address** | Third person singular; a portrait is drawn | Second person plural; said directly to the one addressed |
| **The threshold** | Not repelling was counted enough | Not repelling is not enough; **honouring** is required |

**In al-Fajr the threshold rises in two directions at once:**

First, in the matter of the orphan: al-Māʿūn was forbidding an act (repelling), al-Fajr requires an act (honouring). Not doing harm is not counted enough.

Second, in the matter of the needy: al-Māʿūn asked that the person himself urge, al-Fajr asks for **mutual** urging.

And this second difference was already recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn. This verse was explicitly mentioned there: *"al-Fajr 89/18: 'You do not urge one another to feed the needy.' Here the verb is in the* taḥāḍḍūna *pattern — the Form III/VI pattern conveying reciprocity. That is, not merely 'you urge' but 'urge one another'. The social dimension is expressed openly."* I close that link from here.

### حَضّ — to urge

The root of the verb, **ح-ض-ض**, was treated in the section on al-Māʿūn 107/3. What was recorded there:

> The sense of the verb *ḥaḍḍ*: **to urge insistently, to incite, to try to move to action.** There is a compulsion in it, a prodding. Not a gentle recommendation.

And the essential finding there:

> The verse does not say "you do not give"; it says **"you do not even want it to be given."** The threshold has been drawn as low as it can go. Because **urging costs nothing.** To give, one must have property; to urge, one need have nothing… The sūra catches not the man's hand but his **intention.** A person who does not give can say "I had no means." But for the person who does not even urge there is no such excuse.

This finding holds for al-Fajr as well, and goes one step further. Because what al-Fajr asks for is **mutual** urging: not that one person urge, but that the urging circulate within the community.

The reason for this was also recorded in the section on al-Māʿūn: *"urging takes the matter out of being individual and makes it social. The alms one person gives feeds one person; a widespread conviction that it ought to be given changes the system."*

**A variant reading.** The word has been read in the forms **تَحَاضُّونَ** (*taḥāḍḍūna*, in the reciprocal form) and **تَحُضُّونَ** (*taḥuḍḍūna*, in Form I); and a reading **تَحَٰضُّونَ** is also transmitted, in which the original was *tataḥāḍḍūna* and one *tāʾ* has been dropped. That the difference exists is certain. **On the second reading the sense of reciprocity falls away** and the verse comes closer to the expression in al-Māʿūn; on the first reading the reciprocity is preserved. Since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which authority, I write no name.

### Why these two groups?

The orphan and the needy do not stand side by side in the sūra's list by accident. Their common property is this: **neither can give anything back.**

This measure was established in Sūrat al-Balad, and it was recorded there thus: *"The definition of the steep pass is spending directed where no return will come: the freed slave has nothing to give, the orphan has no one behind him, the thanks of a poor man clinging to the dust carries no price."*

The same measure works in al-Fajr. Behind the orphan there is no one to protect him — in the society of the Jāhiliyya this was, in the full sense of the word, a legal defencelessness. And the needy has nothing to give.

**And the link of the two offences with the sūra's diagnosis is here.**

Whoever counts wealth a divine declaration of worth counts the person without wealth a declaration of worth as well: worthless. And he has no reason to honour someone he counts worthless — within his own logic, that person is already where he deserves to be.

That is, the offence in the seventeenth verse is the **natural consequence** of the measure in the fifteenth. The sūra is not counting two separate faults; it is counting one fault and the behaviour it produces.

The Qurʾān sets this out even more plainly in another place:

> *"He did not believe in God the Most Great, and did not urge the feeding of the needy."* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/33-34)

The note recorded for this verse in the section on al-Māʿūn holds here too: *"Two sentences, joined with 'and'. Faith and urging, side by side in the same list."*

## 89/19-20 — وَتَأْكُلُونَ ٱلتُّرَاثَ أَكْلًا لَّمًّا • وَتُحِبُّونَ ٱلْمَالَ حُبًّا جَمًّا

*Wa taʾkulūna't-turātha aklan lammā • Wa tuḥibbūna'l-māla ḥubban jammā*
"You devour the inheritance without distinction; you love wealth with a heaped-up love."

### تُرَاث — inheritance

**Root: و-ر-ث.** *Wirātha*, *wārith*, *mīrāth* are from the same root. In the form **تُرَاث** the initial letter **و** has turned into **ت**; this is a sound change seen in some Arabic words beginning with *wāw* (as in *wuqāt* → *tuqāt*). The word lives on in Turkish too as *turâs*, and indirectly in the cultural sense of the concept of "heritage"; English has no cognate here.

**The choosing of inheritance is meaningful and is generally passed over.**

The verse does not say "you devour wealth", nor "your earnings", nor "your trade". It says **the inheritance** — that is, **property in which no labour has been invested.**

This connects directly with the diagnosis of the sūra. The man in the fifteenth verse counted what was in his hand an indicator of desert. Inheritance is the place where this claim is weakest: property that comes into a person's hand with no contribution of his own. And precisely for that reason, counting an inheritance "deserved" is the plainest contradiction.

The sūra strikes at the point easiest to refute.

### Historical background

Regarding the practice of inheritance in the Arab society of the Jāhiliyya we have this general information: strength and the ability to fight were taken as the basis in the division, and women and small children were most often left out of the shares. The Qurʾān changed this order and determined the shares in detail (the inheritance verses of Sūrat al-Nisāʾ).

The Qurʾān also explicitly forbids one practice:

> *"It is not lawful for you to inherit women against their will."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/19)

The existence of the verse shows that there was a practice by which a woman could in some cases become a **subject** of inheritance. I do not give the details, because I cannot say anything certain about the scope and the currency of the reports; but the prohibition itself is in the text.

**And when this is joined with the orphan, the picture is completed.**

Two verses earlier the orphan was mentioned. Now inheritance is mentioned. In the conditions of the Jāhiliyya these two join in a single event: **the property of a child whose father has died being devoured by the very people who ought to protect him.**

The Qurʾān meets this with one of its severest sentences:

> *"Those who unjustly devour the property of orphans devour only fire into their bellies."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/10)

And further: *"Give orphans their property; do not exchange the good for the bad, and do not devour their property by adding it to your own"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/2).

**Al-Nisāʾ 4/10 is the best commentary on al-Fajr 89/19.** In both verses there is the same verb — *akl*, to eat — and al-Nisāʾ explains what that verb is.

### أَكْلًا لَّمًّا

**The absolute-object structure.** After the verb *taʾkulūna* comes a verbal noun from the same root: *aklan*. In Arabic this structure **emphasises** the verb; and the adjective that follows (*lammā*) conveys the quality of the verb. In English the counterpart is an expression such as "you devour it utterly", "you eat it right up".

**لَمّ — root ل-م-م:** to gather, to collect together. *Lamma'sh-shayʾa* — he gathered up what was scattered, he gleaned it.

| Reading | Meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Without distinction** | Eating it all gathered up, without separating the lawful from the unlawful | The root's sense of "gathering up"; bringing together without discriminating |
| **Mixing what is one's due with what is not** | Not separating one's own share from another's | The reading most fitting the context of inheritance; it overlaps with the expression at al-Nisāʾ 4/2, *"by adding their property to your own"* |
| **Insatiably, violently** | Greedily, ravenously | A figurative sense derived from *lamm*'s sense of "gathering" |

All three readings are found in the sources and they do not exclude one another. **The second reading overlaps most tightly with the sūra's context of the orphan**; I state this as a preference, it is not binding.

The common core of the word is this: **not separating.** To separate is to distinguish what is one's due from what is not; *lamm* is the failure to make that distinction. And this has to do not with the quantity of the wealth but **with the undifferentiatedness of the relation built with it.**

### حُبًّا جَمًّا

Again an **absolute object**: *tuḥibbūna… ḥubban.* The sūra uses the same grammatical pattern in two consecutive verses — and this makes the two sentences into a single pair.

**جَمّ — root ج-م-م:** multitude, a heap, being full to overflowing. *Jamma'l-māʾ* — the water collected, it increased. *Jamām* — a vessel's being filled to the brim, the point of overflowing.

That is, *ḥubban jammā* is not "to love much"; it is **"to love more than the vessel can hold"**. There is an image of overflowing in the word.

**And this connects silently with the eleventh verse of the sūra:** there the peoples *ṭaghaw* — they overflowed. Here the love overflows. I record it as my own reading, but that both words carry the image of overflowing stands in the text.

**Note the measure.** The verse does not say "you love wealth". Loving wealth is not recorded as an offence in the Qurʾān; on the contrary the Qurʾān uses the word *khayr* (good) for wealth as well (al-Baqara 2/180: *"if he leaves behind some good"*). What the verse criticises is not the **existence** of the love but its **proportion**.

This forms a pair with Sūrat al-ʿĀdiyāt:

> *"And he is indeed fierce in his love of good things."* (al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/8 — *wa innahu li-ḥubbi'l-khayri la-shadīd*)

| | **al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/8** | **al-Fajr 89/20** |
|---|---|---|
| The name of the love | *ḥubb al-khayr* — "the love of good things" | *ḥubb al-māl* — "the love of wealth" |
| What qualifies it | *shadīd* — hard, tight, gripping firmly | *jamm* — heaped, brimming over |
| What stands out | The **tightness** of the love | The **volume** of the love |

Two words on two separate scales: one states the strength of the grip, the other its quantity. Together they give the two dimensions of the relation built with wealth.

### The link with al-Humaza 104

Before establishing this link a warning is needed, because it is open to an easy error:

**جَمّ (ج-م-م) and جَمَع (ج-م-ع) are not the same root.** Their final letters differ, and in Arabic the root is determined by the identity of the three consonants. There is **no** root-kinship between *jamaʿa mālan* in al-Humaza and *ḥubban jammā* in al-Fajr.

But the **family of sense** is the same: both carry the idea of piling up, increasing, coming together. I record this as an association, not as a claim of derivation.

The real link is elsewhere and deeper:

| | **al-Humaza 104** | **al-Fajr 89** |
|---|---|---|
| The behaviour | He gathered wealth and kept counting it (104/2) | He devours the inheritance, he loves wealth heaped up (89/19-20) |
| The supposition beneath it | *yaḥsabu anna mālahu **akhlada**hu* — "wealth makes me **lasting**" | *rabbī **akrama**n* — "my wealth shows my **worth**" |
| The layer of the diagnosis | Helplessness in the face of death | The search for worth |
| The rejection | *kallā* (104/4) | *kallā* (89/17) |

**The two sūras state two different false beliefs about the same act.** One says "wealth makes me lasting", the other "wealth shows my worth". Both take wealth out of being a means and turn it into a **certificate**: one a certificate of immortality, the other a certificate of worth.

The finding recorded in the section on al-Humaza holds here as well: *"The value of a means is in its use; the value of an end is in its existence."* The man in al-Fajr uses his wealth — he eats it. But what he eats is not what he laboured for, and while eating he does not distinguish. That is, for him wealth is neither a means nor a trust; it is **a state.**

And an observation as to subject matter: in the triad al-Takāthur–al-ʿAṣr–al-Humaza, number, wealth and death were treated together. Al-Fajr adds a fourth term to the same triad: **worth.** I must state that this is not a claim about the arrangement of the codex — al-Fajr is far from that triad; the link I establish concerns the subject, not the arrangement.

### The sequence of the four verbs (17-20)

The sūra counts four verbs in four verses, and their sequence is not accidental. I record this as **my own reading**:

| Verse | Verb | Positive/negative | Its direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17 | *lā tukrimūna* — you do not honour | Negative: **not done** | Outward (to the orphan) |
| 18 | *lā taḥāḍḍūna* — you do not urge | Negative: **not done** | Outward (to the needy) |
| 19 | *taʾkulūna* — you devour | Positive: **done** | Inward (to oneself) |
| 20 | *tuḥibbūna* — you love | Positive: **done** | Innermost (to the heart) |

**The first two are omissions, the last two excesses.** And the ordering descends from outside inwards: first what is not done for another, then what is done for oneself, and at the very end **the act of the heart.**

The last of them — *ḥubb*, love — is not a behaviour but the source of behaviour. And the sūra puts it **last.**

This is the same as what al-Humaza does: there too the behaviour was counted first (he gathered, he counted), and then the supposition beneath it was stated (*yaḥsabu*). The Qurʾān generally builds its diagnosis this way: it begins from the visible act and descends to the source.

And a conclusion follows from this: the sūra is not counting four separate faults. **It is counting four appearances of a single fault** and naming it in the fourth.

## 89/21-22 — كَلَّآ إِذَا دُكَّتِ ٱلْأَرْضُ دَكًّا دَكًّا • وَجَآءَ رَبُّكَ وَٱلْمَلَكُ صَفًّا صَفًّا

*Kallā idhā dukkati'l-arḍu dakkan dakkā • Wa jāʾa rabbuka wa'l-malaku ṣaffan ṣaffā*
"No! When the earth is pounded to pieces; when your Lord comes and the angels rank on rank…"

### The second كَلَّا

*Kallā* occurs twice in the sūra: at 17 and at 21. The function of the two occurrences is not the same:

| | **Verse 17** | **Verse 21** |
|---|---|---|
| What it cuts | An **inference** — the counting of wealth as an indicator of worth | A **way of living** — undifferentiated eating and overflowing love |
| What follows it | *bal* — a new diagnosis | *idhā* — a new **scene** |
| Its effect | The talking continues, its direction changes | The talking ends, the curtain opens |

The first *kallā* was inside the argument. The second ends the argument. And the manner of ending is meaningful: instead of giving an answer it **changes the time.** Instead of persuasion, a moment is shown that will require no persuasion.

### دَكّ — to pound and level

**Root: د-ك-ك.** Its meaning is **to pound a thing, to crush it flat, to break down a rise and level it with the ground.** From the same root *dakka* — a flat hill, a low bank.

At the core of the word is **levelling**: what is high is brought low, what is uneven is smoothed out.

Its occurrences in the Qurʾān show this:

- *"When his Lord manifested Himself to the mountain, He made it crumble to dust."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/143 — *jaʿalahu dakkā*). The mountain loses its height.
- *"When the promise of my Lord comes, He will level it."* (al-Kahf 18/98 — *jaʿalahu dakkā*). The barrier — that is, the sturdiest structure made by human hands — is levelled.
- *"When the earth and the mountains are lifted and crushed together with a single blow…"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/14 — *fa-dukkatā dakkatan wāḥidah*).

### دَكًّا دَكًّا — the sense of the repetition

The expression is repeated twice. This is the repeated form of the absolute object, and it has been read in two ways:

| Reading | Meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Repetition = multiplicity** | Not one blow but blows **one after another**; a pounding many times over | In Arabic the repetition of a word conveys multiplicity and continuity |
| **Conveying a state** | "Piece by piece", "layer upon layer" — the earth being levelled section by section | The reading of the repeated structure as a circumstantial expression |

And here there is **an interesting tension with al-Ḥāqqa 69/14:** there it says *dakkatan wāḥidah* — "with **a single** blow". In al-Fajr there is repetition.

How should this difference be read? The solutions put forward are these: (a) the two verses may be describing different phases of the event; (b) al-Ḥāqqa may be stressing the **decisiveness** of the blow and its completion at one stroke, al-Fajr its **intensity**; (c) if the repetition is read as a circumstantial expression the tension disappears anyway.

**I issue no firm ruling.** The recording of the difference between the two verses is not a claim of contradiction; it is the observation that the two descriptions look from different places.

### The carrying over of a grammatical pattern

And here there is an observation about the structure, which I record **as my own reading**.

The nineteenth and twentieth verses of the sūra were built with the absolute-object pattern: *aklan lammā*, *ḥubban jammā*. The twenty-first and twenty-second verses use **the same pattern**: *dakkan dakkā*, *ṣaffan ṣaffā*.

All four are in the same grammatical structure, all four have the same weight of sound, and all four come one after another. But the first two are **on the stage of this world** (eating, loving), the last two **on the stage of the resurrection** (being pounded, being ranged).

**The sūra carries the same pattern between two scenes.** This is a seam binding the two scenes together: because the ear hears the same rhythm, it hears the two sections as a single sentence.

### صَفًّا صَفًّا — rank on rank

**Root: ص-ف-ف.** Row, rank, line. *Ṣaffa* — he lined up, he ranged.

Its relatives in the Qurʾān: *al-Ṣāffāt* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/1 — those ranged in ranks), *"in a rank, as though a compacted structure"* (al-Ṣaff 61/4). The word "rank" in the prayer is this too.

**And a link is established with the neighbouring sūra:**

Al-Ghāshiya 88/15, in its description of the garden, says: *namāriqu maṣfūfa* — **"cushions ranged in rows."** The same root.

| | **al-Ghāshiya 88/15** | **al-Fajr 89/22** |
|---|---|---|
| What is ranged | Cushions | Angels |
| The scene | The garden, rest | The court, the reckoning |
| Common | *ṣ-f-f* — orderly ranging | |

Two neighbouring sūras use the same root in two scenes whose ends do not resemble one another. The root itself is neither positive nor negative: it conveys **order**. The measure recorded for the word *mabthūth* in the section on al-Qāriʿa holds here too: the word carries no value in itself; the value comes from what it qualifies.

**A grammatical note: ٱلْمَلَك comes in the singular** but *ṣaffan ṣaffā* conveys plurality. In Arabic it is ordinary for a generic noun to be used in the singular and give a plural sense — "angel" here is the name of the kind, it does not mean a single angel.

### وَجَآءَ رَبُّكَ — a *mutashābih* expression

This expression falls within the group of verses called **mutashābih** in the Qurʾān: its wording ascribes to God a verb used of human beings, and it is not easy to issue a firm ruling on its meaning.

In this commentary I take no theological side; by the measure recorded in `USLUP.md` I convey the two main attitudes as they are.

| Attitude | What it says | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Tafwīḍ (the attitude of the early generations)** | The wording is accepted as it is, and its meaning is committed to God; the modality is not entered into, no likeness is drawn. "He came" is whatever it is; how it is has not been made known to us | Faithfulness to the Qurʾān's own wording; that speaking about the modality exceeds the limit of knowledge |
| **Taʾwīl** | A governed term has been elided; it is understood as "the **command / decree / punishment** of your Lord came" | The Qurʾān's use of the word *amr* in the same context elsewhere: *"Or until the command of your Lord comes"* (al-Naḥl 16/33) |

**There is a principle both attitudes share** and it must be stated: *"There is nothing like Him"* (al-Shūrā 42/11). Neither does the attitude of *tafwīḍ* draw a likeness, nor does the attitude of *taʾwīl* depart from this principle. The difference between them concerns how the principle is to be preserved: one prefers silence, the other explanation.

The detail of this question is the subject of the science of theology and has no place to be decided here. The function of the verse within the sūra, however, is the same on both attitudes: **to announce that the moment of judgment has come.**

### The character of the scene — and the link with verses 7-10

This is where one of the tightest internal links of the sūra is established.

In the historical section of the sūra the three peoples were mentioned with three **structures**:

- **ʿĀd** — *dhāti'l-ʿimād*: possessor of **columns / poles.**
- **Thamūd** — *jābu'ṣ-ṣakhra*: **the carved rock.**
- **Pharaoh** — *dhi'l-awtād*: possessor of **stakes.**

All three are things that rise from the ground or are driven into it. All three are the matter of a claim to fixity and permanence.

And in the twenty-first verse the earth is **pounded and levelled.**

**On levelled ground no column remains, no hollow remains, no stake remains.** This is exactly the meaning of the verb *dakk*: the bringing down of height, the smoothing out of what is uneven.

That is, the sūra lays flat in the twenty-first verse what it set upright in the seventh. This is one of the plainest symmetries within the text, and it is left to the reader — no connective joins these two places.

And what comes onto the levelled ground? **Ranks.**

The image is completed: the earth is levelled and turned into a **place of hearing**. There is no longer any structure on it; only those who are ranged. What determines who is where is no longer the column he raised but the rank he stands in.

### The three structures of the three peoples and the individual's wealth

This link must be gathered here, because it is one of the main ideas of the sūra.

In the first half of the sūra three communities wrote their claim to permanence **on stone**. In the second half a single human being writes the same claim **on wealth**.

And the Qurʾān establishes this link explicitly for ʿĀd:

> *"Do you take for yourselves strongholds, hoping to abide forever?"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/129 — *laʿallakum **takhludūn***)

The verb is from the root **خ-ل-د**: permanence. And this is the very verb at al-Humaza 104/3: *yaḥsabu anna mālahu **akhlada**hu* — "he supposes that his wealth has made him everlasting."

**The same root, two different objects:** ʿĀd expects permanence from a structure, the man in al-Humaza from wealth.

Al-Fajr sets these two lines side by side in a single sūra. And what it says is this: **what is written on stone and what is written on wealth are the same claim.** The material changes, the sentence does not.

I present this as my own reading; but that the verb at al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/129 and the verb at al-Humaza 104/3 are from the same root is a fact standing in the text.

## 89/23-24 — وَجِا۟ىٓءَ يَوْمَئِذٍۭ بِجَهَنَّمَ ۚ يَوْمَئِذٍ يَتَذَكَّرُ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ وَأَنَّىٰ لَهُ ٱلذِّكْرَىٰ • يَقُولُ يَٰلَيْتَنِى قَدَّمْتُ لِحَيَاتِى

*Wa jīʾa yawmaʾidhin bi-jahannam, yawmaʾidhin yatadhakkaru'l-insānu wa annā lahu'dh-dhikrā • Yaqūlu yā laytanī qaddamtu li-ḥayātī*
"And on that day Hell is brought; on that day man remembers — but what use is the remembering to him? He says: 'Would that I had sent something ahead for my life.'"

### وَجِا۟ىٓءَ — it is brought

The verb is **passive**: "it was brought / it is brought." **Who brings it is not said.**

The note recorded for the same choice in the section on al-Humaza holds here as well: the passive form is not the concealing of the subject but **its removal from the stage**; the attention is on the act, not on the one who performs it.

But what is really striking is the word itself.

**Hell is "brought."** That is, not a place to be gone to but **a thing that comes.**

This turns the accustomed conception upside down. Hell is generally thought of as a place: something standing at a distance, to which a person is dispatched. The verse does not do this; it mentions Hell like an **object**, like something carried onto the stage.

The verbs the Qurʾān uses elsewhere point the same way (such as Hell's being "brought near"). The common point is the disappearance of distance: on that day man does not go to Hell, nor does Hell stand far off; the two are on the same stage.

And this agrees with the preceding verse of the sūra: the earth was levelled, distances were removed, everything came onto the same plane.

### The repetition of يَوْمَئِذٍ

The expression occurs **twice** in the same verse: *"on that day Hell is brought; on that day man remembers."*

The repetition conveys emphasis and also establishes a simultaneity: **the coming of Hell and the beginning of the remembering are the same moment.**

That is, the remembering does not happen of itself. It happens with the appearing of something. This is necessary in order to understand the second half of the verse: what is remembered was already known; what was lacking was not the knowledge but **the sight.**

### يَتَذَكَّرُ — to remember

**Root: ذ-ك-ر.** The verb is **in Form V** (*tafaʿʿul*): *tadhakkara* — to remember of oneself, to bring to mind, to think out. This form generally conveys the verb's turning back upon the subject and its containing an effort.

**And a link is established with the neighbouring sūra — one of the most striking pairs between sūras:**

Sūrat al-Ghāshiya says to the Prophet:

> *"So remind; you are only a reminder."* (al-Ghāshiya 88/21 — *fa-dhakkir, innamā anta mudhakkir*)

And al-Fajr says:

> *"On that day man remembers — but what use is the remembering to him?"* (al-Fajr 89/23)

| | **al-Ghāshiya 88/21** | **al-Fajr 89/23** |
|---|---|---|
| Root | *dh-k-r* | *dh-k-r* |
| Who | The Prophet — *mudhakkir* (the one who reminds) | Man — *yatadhakkaru* (the one who remembers) |
| Time | **Now** | **On that day** |
| Result | A command: do it | A question: what use is it? |

**Two neighbouring sūras hold the two ends of the same root.** One shows the time at which reminding is commanded, the other the time at which remembering is of no use. The difference between them is not a word but a **time.**

And when the two sūras are read together a measure emerges: why is the work of reminding done now? Because remembering has a time, and when that time has passed the operation is void. I record this in the section on al-Ghāshiya as well.

### وَأَنَّىٰ لَهُ ٱلذِّكْرَىٰ

**أَنَّى (*annā*)** — an interrogative particle: "from where, how, by what way." The question here conveys **impossibility**: "from where should he get any use?" — that is, he cannot.

**And what the verse says is fine: the remembering itself is not denied.**

The verse does not say "he does not remember"; it says *yatadhakkaru* — **he remembers.** The verb is positive. What is denied is the **function** of the remembering at that time.

That is, man at last thinks the right thing. The knowledge comes, the grasp forms, the judgment is rightly built — but there is nothing left to do. **The value of knowledge depends on the time at which it arrives.**

This is another form of the distinction established in Sūrat al-Takāthur. There the difference between *ʿilm al-yaqīn* and *ʿayn al-yaqīn* was treated: knowledge given by report has a limit, knowledge that comes by seeing is another thing. In al-Fajr that transition takes place — and at the moment it takes place, it is too late.

And it is a relative of the finding recorded in the section on al-Humaza 104/5: *"the description is not a definition but an indication. The sūra describes what it describes after saying that it cannot be described."* Both sūras point to the same matter: **the difference between what a report can carry and what seeing gives**, and why the moment at which that difference closes is a late moment.

### يَٰلَيْتَنِى — an impossible wish

**لَيْتَ (*layta*)** is in Arabic the particle of wishing, and it has an important difference from **لَعَلَّ (*laʿalla*)**:

- *Laʿalla* — hoping. It is used for something whose realisation is **possible**.
- *Layta* — wishing. It is used for something whose realisation is **impossible** or remote.

That is, the particle announces the outcome at the very moment the sentence is built: **this wish will not be granted.** That this particle should come immediately after the verse's question "what use is the remembering?" is no accident; the question and the particle say the same thing.

### قَدَّمْتُ — to send ahead

**Root: ق-د-م.** *Qadima* — to be in front, to go ahead. **In Form II** *qaddama* — **to send ahead, to put forward, to dispatch in advance.**

The Qurʾān uses this verb often for deeds, and each time it builds the same image:

- *"Whatever good you send ahead for yourselves, you will find it with God."* (al-Baqara 2/110; the same expression recurs at al-Muzzammil 73/20)
- *"Let every soul look to what it has sent ahead for tomorrow."* (al-Ḥashr 59/18)
- *"On that day man is told of what he sent ahead and what he left behind."* (al-Qiyāma 75/13)

**The image is this: man is on a journey and can send his provision on ahead.**

This is a concrete image taken from Arab life: a caravan setting out on a long road would send some of its loads ahead. What is sent waits for its owner at the place of arrival.

And the choice of the verb says one thing more: **a deed is not something left here but something sent there.** The work done does not stay here; it is carried. This is why the Qurʾān often says "you sent" instead of "you did".

And the man's regret is exactly here: **he had a great deal in his hand, and he sent none of it.** In the nineteenth and twentieth verses what he did was stated — he ate and he loved. Both are verbs of consuming.

### لِحَيَاتِى — "for my life"

This is the finest point of the sūra and it is generally flattened in translation.

The person calls the life he is in at that moment **"my life"**. That is, he no longer calls the lifetime he left behind a life.

There are two readings:

| Reading | Meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **"For my real life"** | *Ḥayāt* here is the life of the hereafter; the person has now seen that this is the real life | *"The abode of the hereafter — that is the real life"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/64 — *la-hiya'l-ḥayawān*). The Qurʾān calls the hereafter "the very living" |
| **"For this life of mine"** | "I should have sent something ahead for this state I am now in" | The direct reading of the sentence; there is no demonstrative, it is indeterminate |

**The first reading is widespread and strong**, and its ground is al-ʿAnkabūt 29/64. There the Qurʾān uses the word *ḥayawān* for the hereafter — "living itself", the most intense state of life. The life of this world, in the same verse, is called *lahw* and *laʿib*.

**And from here the most striking finding of the sūra emerges. I present it as my own reading:**

When the man voices his regret he does not make a correction of **action**. He does not say "would that I had done this, would that I had not given that". What he does is a correction of **naming**: what he called "life" all his life he no longer calls life.

**And this is the same as the diagnosis at the beginning of the sūra.**

In the fifteenth and sixteenth verses too what was wrong was not an action. The man was not charged with acquiring wealth; he was charged with **naming it wrongly** — with calling it "honouring" and "abasing". That is, the sūra's diagnosis at the outset was an error of naming.

**The same matter stands at the two ends of the sūra:**

| | **At the beginning (15-16)** | **At the end (24)** |
|---|---|---|
| What is wrongly named | Wealth — "honouring" / "abasing" | A lifetime — "life" |
| Who names it | Man | The same man |
| When it is corrected | It is not corrected; it is cut off with *kallā* | It corrects itself — but the time has passed |

The sūra opens with a man using words wrongly and closes with his beginning to use them rightly. The only difference between the two is at what time it happens.

## 89/25-26 — فَيَوْمَئِذٍ لَّا يُعَذِّبُ عَذَابَهُۥٓ أَحَدٌ • وَلَا يُوثِقُ وَثَاقَهُۥٓ أَحَدٌ

*Fa-yawmaʾidhin lā yuʿadhdhibu ʿadhābahu aḥad • Wa lā yūthiqu wathāqahu aḥad*
"On that day no one punishes as He punishes; no one binds as He binds."

*(On the other reading: "On that day no one is punished with a punishment like his; no one is bound with a binding like his.")*

### The variant reading — here it does change the meaning

These two verses are among the few places where a variant reading really does change the meaning, and recording it is obligatory.

| Reading | The verbs | Whose the pronoun is | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Active** | *lā yuʿadhdhibu… lā yūthiqu* | God's | "No one can punish **as He punishes**, no one can bind **as He binds**" — **the unmatchedness of the one who punishes** |
| **Passive** | *lā yuʿadhdhabu… lā yūthaqu* | The one punished | "No one is punished **with a punishment like his**, no one is bound **with a binding like his**" — **the unmatchedness of the punishment** |

**Both readings are transmitted and both are meaningful.** They do not exclude one another: one conveys the unmatchedness of the agent, the other of the object; and the unmatchedness of the agent already entails the unmatchedness of the punishment.

Since I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to which authority, I write no name.

I should like to add an observation: **the first reading connects more tightly with verses 6-14 of the sūra.** There the one who destroys, who pours out the punishment and who stands in the place of watching was always *rabbuka*; here, to state the unmatchedness of the work of that same subject would be the natural close of that section. I state this as an observation; **it is not binding** and we have no proof for counting the second reading weak.

### وَثَاق — a bond

**Root: و-ث-ق.** Its meaning is **to bind, to make firm, to fasten tightly.** *Wathāq* / *withāq* — a bond, a rope, a fetter.

Its use in the Qurʾān: *"Bind the bonds tightly"* (Muḥammad 47/4), concerning captives.

**And from the same root comes مِيثَاق (*mīthāq*) — a covenant, a treaty, an agreement.**

The root's other derivatives are in the same family: *wathīqa* (a document — the Turkish *vesika*), *thiqa* (a trustworthy person), *tawthīq* (documentation), *al-wuthqā* (the firm handhold).

**Establishing this link is instructive.** A covenant is a bond, and so is a fetter. In a *mīthāq* the parties are bound to one another — willingly, and the bond holds the parties. In a *withāq* the person is bound — unwillingly, and the bond holds him.

The same root, the same work: holding. The only thing that changes is whether the bond is accepted or not.

From this I draw an observation and **state that it is my own reading**: the bond is either accepted at the outset or applied at the end. The two ends of the root say this. I record it not as a claim of rhetoric but as the echo the word carries.

And a line is established with the fifth verse: there we had *ḥijr* — a restraint, a boundary, what holds. The sūra opened with "the one who holds himself" and closes with "the one who is bound". **The work of holding is done either from within or from without.**

### أَحَدٌ

The word occurs in a negative sentence, and in Arabic in this case it conveys **generality**: "no one."

It is the same word as *aḥad* at al-Ikhlāṣ 112/1 — but at an entirely different work. There, in an affirmative sentence, as a noun and an attribute, it conveyed **unmatchedness**. Here, in a negative sentence, it conveys inclusiveness.

I record this as a short note; I establish no link between the two uses, because the word's two functions are already separate in Arabic.

### The verse-ending of the two verses

Both verses end with the same word: *aḥad*, *aḥad*. There is one other place in the sūra where two verses close with the same word — 8 and 11, *fi'l-bilād* — but there the verses were not consecutive; here they are.

This repetition makes the two sentences into a single ruling: the punishment and the binding are two faces of one operation. And the repetition itself serves as a closing — the scene of the resurrection ends here, and in the next verse an entirely different voice begins.

## 89/27-30 — يَٰٓأَيَّتُهَا ٱلنَّفْسُ ٱلْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ • ٱرْجِعِىٓ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكِ رَاضِيَةً مَّرْضِيَّةً • فَٱدْخُلِى فِى عِبَٰدِى • وَٱدْخُلِى جَنَّتِى

*Yā ayyatuha'n-nafsu'l-muṭmaʾinna • Irjiʿī ilā rabbiki rāḍiyatan marḍiyya • Fa'dkhulī fī ʿibādī • Wa'dkhulī jannatī*
"O soul at rest! Return to your Lord, well pleased and well pleasing. Enter among My servants. And enter My garden."

The close of the sūra. And the opposite pole of everything built through it.

### The change of address

The sūra used three separate modes of address:

- **Third person:** *al-insān… fa-yaqūlu* (15-16). A portrait.
- **Second person plural:** *lā tukrimūna… tuḥibbūna* (17-20). A direct accusation.
- **Second person feminine singular:** *irjiʿī… fa'dkhulī* (28-30). A direct call.

The last transition is the sharpest. From a reproach spoken to a crowd there is a move to a call spoken to **a single addressee**. And the addressee is not a human being but a **soul** — a feminine word in Arabic, which is why the verbs are in the feminine.

**Who is speaking is not said.** The verb is not passive; it is a direct address. But the pronouns give the speaker away: *rabbiki* — "your Lord"; *ʿibādī* — "My servants"; *jannatī* — "My garden". In the third verse there is a move to the first person singular, and there the matter becomes clear.

This gradual disclosure is striking: the call begins without saying whom it comes from, and says it as it closes.

### ٱطْمَأَنَّ — to come to rest

**Root: ط-م-أ-ن.** A four-letter root; its meaning is **to settle, to subside, to come to rest, to find stillness.**

Its concrete sense must be dwelt on: the word is used of something **whose agitation has stopped and which has settled into place**. Water clearing, a tremor dying down, movement ceasing. In Arabic it is used of ground as well: a *muṭmaʾinn* place is a low and level one, still, where the wind does not strike.

That is, at the centre of the word there is not **motionlessness** but **the stopping of motion**. First there is an agitation; the word describes the ending of that agitation.

The uses in the Qurʾān show this:

- *"So that my heart may be at rest"* (al-Baqara 2/260). **The words of Abraham (Ibrāhīm).** When he asks how He revives the dead he is told *"do you not believe?"*; his answer: *"Yes, but so that my heart may be at rest."* This verse is the clearest example showing that *iṭmiʾnān* comes **not with knowledge but with sight.** Abraham already believed; what was lacking was not belief but **stillness.**
- *"Hearts find rest only in the remembrance of God."* (al-Raʿd 13/28). The central verse of the word.
- *"While his heart is at rest in faith…"* (al-Naḥl 16/106).
- Within the frame of *"when the fear has gone, perform the prayer in full"*: *"when you are at rest"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/103).
- Also Āl ʿImrān 3/126, al-Anfāl 8/10 (help setting hearts at rest), Yūnus 10/7.

**The opposite of the word is not another word but a state: agitation.**

### The contrast with the man at the beginning of the sūra

And this is where the close binds the sūra back to its beginning.

What was the man in the fifteenth and sixteenth verses doing?

- When abundance came: "My Lord has honoured me."
- When want came: "My Lord has abased me."

**This man was swaying.** As the situation outside changed, the judgment inside changed. His measure was not in himself but in what he held; and because what he held changed, the measure changed too.

*Al-nafs al-muṭmaʾinna* is the exact opposite of this: **a soul whose judgment does not change as its condition changes.**

| | **The man at 89/15-16** | **The soul at 89/27** |
|---|---|---|
| Where its measure is | Outside — in what it holds | Inside — settled |
| When abundance comes | "I have been honoured" | It does not change |
| When want comes | "I have been abased" | It does not change |
| Its state | Agitation | *Iṭmiʾnān* — the subsiding of the agitation |
| Its outcome | It is cut off with *kallā* | It is called |

**This is what binds the close of the sūra to its beginning.** The text made a diagnosis, then showed the opposite of that diagnosis. And it gave the opposite not as a behaviour but as a **state**: *al-muṭmaʾinna* is an adjective, not a verb.

This has a consequence: the sūra does not say "do thus", it says "be thus". What is asked is not an operation but a settling.

### The qualifications of the soul in the Qurʾān

The Qurʾān mentions the soul with an adjective in three places:

- *"The soul is a persistent commander of evil"* — **أَمَّارَة بِٱلسُّوٓءِ** (Yūsuf 12/53).
- *"I swear by the self-reproaching soul"* — **ٱللَّوَّامَة** (al-Qiyāma 75/2).
- *"O soul at rest"* — **ٱلْمُطْمَئِنَّة** (al-Fajr 89/27).

**A note must be entered here.** In the later Sufi tradition these three adjectives were made into an ordered classification under the name "the stages of the soul", and further steps were added. **No such ordering of degrees is explicitly established in the Qurʾān.** The three adjectives occur in three separate sūras, in three separate contexts; that there is a line of ascent among them is an interpretation drawn out of the text.

I convey this not as a classification but as three separate findings. I do not count the interpretation itself worthless; but to present it as the Qurʾān's own classification would not be right.

The subject of the soul was also treated in Sūrat al-Shams: the soul's being *shaped* (*taswiya*), the two directions being inspired into it, and the distinction between *tazkiya* and *tadsiya*. The adjective in al-Fajr is the outcome of one of the two directions described there.

### ٱرْجِعِىٓ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكِ — "Return to your Lord"

**Root: ر-ج-ع.** *Rujūʿ* — to return, to come back.

**And one more link is established with the neighbouring sūra.**

Sūrat al-Ghāshiya ends thus:

> *"Surely to Us is their return. Then upon Us is their reckoning."* (al-Ghāshiya 88/25-26 — *inna ilaynā iyābahum*)

And al-Fajr ends thus:

> *"Return to your Lord."* (al-Fajr 89/28)

| | **al-Ghāshiya 88/25** | **al-Fajr 89/28** |
|---|---|---|
| Mood | Statement — "their return is to Us" | **Imperative** — "return" |
| Person | Third person plural — *hum* | Second person singular — *irjiʿī* |
| What it announces | A **fact** | An **invitation** |
| To whom | No one is called; a state is recorded | Directly to the one addressed |

**The same movement, in two different moods.** And the difference is not empty: the return is a fact for everyone — al-Ghāshiya records this. But to say it in the imperative is another thing; a command is said to one who is called.

That is, the two sūras together say this: **everyone returns, but not everyone is called.** The return is inescapable, the invitation is attached to an attribute — *al-muṭmaʾinna*. I record this in the section on al-Ghāshiya as well.

The Qurʾān records the same root in man's own mouth too: *"We belong to God, and to Him we are returning"* (al-Baqara 2/156).

**When is the return?** There is disagreement on this:

| View | When | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **At the moment of death** | Words said to the spirit as it parts from the body | Agreement with other scenes in which the Qurʾān describes the moment of death |
| **At the resurrection / the reckoning** | As the continuation of the scene of the resurrection | The preceding verses are the scene of the resurrection; it continues without a connective |
| **Both** | Both at death and at the resurrection | No firm separation between the two scenes is needed |

**I impose no firm preference.** In respect of context the second view has an advantage — the verse comes immediately after the scene of the resurrection. But the first view is also widespread, and the text excludes neither.

**A fine point:** that "return" is said implies that there is a **going back**. One does not return to a place one has not been. This can be related to the scene at al-Aʿrāf 7/172 (*"Am I not your Lord?"*): the return has a beginning.

I record this as a **possibility of interpretation**, not as the verse's definite intent. The verb *rujūʿ* in Arabic does not always require a prior physical presence.

### رَاضِيَةً مَّرْضِيَّةً — mutual good pleasure

The two densest words of the sūra. Two forms of the same root side by side:

- **رَاضِيَة** — the active participle: **the one who is well pleased.**
- **مَرْضِيَّة** — the passive participle: **the one with whom there is good pleasure.**

This matter was treated fully in the section on al-Bayyina 98/8. I bring the findings from there:

> **رِضًى (*riḍā*)** — root **ر-ض-و / ر-ض-ي:** to be content, to approve, to accept. Its opposite is **سَخَط (*sakhaṭ*)** — displeasure (Āl ʿImrān 3/162).
>
> *Riḍā* is not merely "approving". Within it there is **finding sufficient**: one who is well pleased seeks nothing else. For this reason *riḍā* is less a feeling than a **stance** — **the stopping of the search.**

And the three conclusions recorded there:

1. **The servant's attitude goes on record.** It is said that a created being can have an *attitude* towards the Creator, and that this matters. Obedience can happen unwillingly; good pleasure cannot.
2. **The opposite is possible.** A thing is praised only because it is possible. The Qurʾān records the one who is not well pleased too: *"Among people is one who serves God on an edge: if good touches him he is content with it, but if a trial comes he turns on his face"* (the sense of al-Ḥajj 22/11). **This verse is particularly meaningful for al-Fajr** — because the person described there is exactly the man at al-Fajr 89/15-16: one whose attitude changes as his condition changes. The measure recorded for this in the section on al-Bayyina was: *"The person here is not well pleased; he is **satisfied** — and satisfaction is conditional."*
3. **The order is meaningful.** In al-Bayyina **God's good pleasure** comes first, then the servant's; because the servant's good pleasure is an **answer**.

And in the section on al-Bayyina **this verse was already quoted**: *"The Qurʾān establishes this two-sided good pleasure in one more place, this time as two adjectives… What is built in al-Bayyina with two verbs is built in al-Fajr with two adjectives."* I close that link from here.

#### What belongs here: the order reversed

In al-Bayyina the order was: *raḍiya'llāhu ʿanhum wa raḍū ʿanh* — **first God was well pleased, then they.**

In al-Fajr the order is reversed: *rāḍiyatan marḍiyya* — **first the one who is well pleased (the servant), then the one with whom there is good pleasure.**

There are two possible grounds for this difference:

**One. The verse-ending.** *Marḍiyya* fits the sound pattern of this part of the sūra (*al-muṭmaʾinna / marḍiyya*); the reverse order would have broken the rhyme.

**Two. The addressee.** Here the addressee is the soul itself. **Its own state** is told to it first: you are well pleased. In al-Bayyina, by contrast, the narration is in the third person and an enumeration of reward is being made; there it is natural for the priority to lie with the giver.

**I issue no firm ruling;** both grounds are possible and they do not exclude one another.

#### The link between *iṭmiʾnān* and good pleasure

There is an order between the two words, and the verse sets it: **first *muṭmaʾinna* (an adjective, a lasting state), then *rāḍiya* (the state of the return).**

The one at rest becomes well pleased; the one well pleased comes to rest. The two feed one another. But the arrangement of the verse puts one first: the soul is already at rest before it is called. Good pleasure is what appears at the moment of the return.

#### The three objects of good pleasure

The word *rāḍiya* occurs in several places in the Qurʾān and each time its object is different. Setting the three side by side is instructive:

| Verse | Expression | The object of the good pleasure |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Ghāshiya 88/9** | *li-saʿyihā rāḍiya* | **Its own striving** — content with what it did |
| **al-Fajr 89/28** | *rāḍiyatan marḍiyya* | **Not stated** — left absolute |
| **al-Bayyina 98/8** | *wa raḍū ʿanh* | **God** |

Three verses hold three separate aspects of good pleasure: to be well pleased with what one did, with one's state, and with one's Lord. That al-Fajr does not state the object leaves the word in its most general form — this is not a deficiency but an openness.

And at al-Ḍuḥā 93/5 the same root occurs as a promise: *"Your Lord will give to you, and you will be well pleased."* As was recorded in the section on al-Bayyina, **the Qurʾān marks good pleasure again and again as the final stop**; not as an intermediate stage.

### فَٱدْخُلِى فِى عِبَٰدِى — "enter among My servants"

There is a letter here, and when it is missed half the verse is lost.

The verse does not say *kūnī min ʿibādī* (be one of My servants); nor does it say *ilḥaqī bi-ʿibādī* (join My servants). It says: **فِى عِبَادِى** — "**enter into My servants.**"

The letter *fī* conveys **being inside** something. The note recorded for this letter in the section on al-ʿAṣr holds here too: *fī* indicates being encompassed; what is entered into becomes an **environment**.

That is, the place to be entered is a community. And a community surrounds the one who enters it.

**And note the order: verse 29 comes before verse 30.**

- First: *fa'dkhulī **fī ʿibādī*** — enter among My servants.
- Then: *wa'dkhulī **jannatī*** — enter My garden.

**The congregation is entered before the garden.** The place of arrival is first not a place but a **community**.

#### The link with al-Fātiḥa

This is one of the most distant and — I say it as my own reading — most meaningful links that can be established between sūras.

What was recorded in the section on al-Fātiḥa was this:

> **Not "I do" but "we do."** Nowhere in the sūra is the singular used. Even one who prays alone says "we do"… it says that worship is not a personal matter but belongs to a community — a person speaks with the mouth of a congregation even when he is alone.

**In the first sūra of the Qurʾān, service begins in the plural mouth. In al-Fajr its counterpart is given by entering into the plural.**

Service began with the congregation; the reward ends with joining the congregation. When the person said *iyyāka naʿbudu* he was already speaking with the mouth of a community; when *fa'dkhulī fī ʿibādī* is said, he enters into that community.

I present this as my own reading; not as a link established in the classical sources between the two sūras. But that both texts build service within a plural is a fact standing in both.

#### عِبَاد — the word itself

It is the plural of **عَبْد**. In Arabic *ʿabd* has two plurals: **عِبَاد (*ʿibād*)** and **عَبِيد (*ʿabīd*)**. Some lexicographers record that the Qurʾān uses *ʿibād* especially in relation to God and in contexts conveying honour. I do not make this distinction definite; I convey it as a finding voiced in the sources.

The concrete sense of the root was given in the section on al-Fātiḥa and is worth recalling here:

> **"*Ṭarīq muʿabbad*"** — a road levelled by much walking, its roughness worn away, now easily passable. That is, *ʿibāda*, within the word's own logic: **to be levelled by being trodden over and over, to have one's roughness worn away.** Servitude here does not mean abasement but **being worked.**

And there is a silent affinity with the twenty-first verse of the sūra: there the earth was *dukkat* — pounded and levelled. Here the servant is named with the name of a road levelled by being walked on. **Two levellings: one by force, one by use.** I record this as my own reading, as a measured observation.

#### A variant reading

In some sources it is transmitted that the word is read as **فِى عَبْدِى** (*fī ʿabdī* — "enter into My servant"); on this reading the expression is understood as the spirit's returning to the body. **The majority have read *ʿibādī***, and this reading is both widespread and suited to the context. I content myself with recording the other; I give no name of an authority.

### وَٱدْخُلِى جَنَّتِى — "enter My garden"

#### The construct

The Qurʾān mostly speaks of the garden as *al-janna* (definite), *jannātin tajrī min taḥtiha'l-anhār* (qualified) or *jannāti ʿadn* (by name). Here, however, it is directly **attached to God**: *jannatī* — "My garden".

The analysis made for *nāru'llāh* (the fire of God) at al-Humaza 104/6 holds here too. What was recorded there:

> A thing's being attached to God conveys its greatness in Arabic… And when "fire" is said the one addressed will think of what he knows; the construct cuts off that thought.

The same operation works here in reverse: when "garden" is said the one addressed will think of the gardens he knows; the construct cuts off that thought.

**Two sūras, two constructs:**

| | **al-Humaza 104/6** | **al-Fajr 89/30** |
|---|---|---|
| What | *nāru'llāhi'l-mūqada* — the kindled fire of God | *jannatī* — My garden |
| Common | Being named with its owner's name | |
| Its effect | Ownership becomes indisputable | |

The note entered for this construct in the section on al-Humaza works here as well: a question of ownership was being treated throughout the sūra — who owns what. In al-Fajr it is the same: the man devours the inheritance, loves wealth, counts what is in his hand the indicator of his own worth. **The last word of the sūra points to the one place where ownership is indisputable.**

#### "Enter" twice

The verse repeats the verb **twice**: *fa'dkhulī… wa'dkhulī.*

It could have been said with one verb: *fa'dkhulī fī ʿibādī wa jannatī.* It would have been shorter and grammatically faultless.

The repetition announces that the two enterings are **two separate works**. To enter the community and to enter the garden are not the same operation; one leads to the other, but the two are not said together.

This is the same logic as that recorded in the section on al-Fātiḥa for the twofold repetition of *iyyāka*: there too a single word would have sufficed, but it was repeated because the two verbs were meant to be assigned separately.

#### The last word of the sūra

The sūra closes with **جَنَّتِى** — with a first-person singular pronoun at the end: "My".

And what was the first word of the sūra? **ٱلْفَجْر** — a natural event in the third person. A phenomenon of the sky; no one's name is mentioned.

**The sūra opens with a natural event and closes with an expression of ownership.**

The distance between them closes: the text begins from a distant event and arrives at a word said in the speaker's own mouth. And in the last two verses the first-person singular pronoun occurs twice: *ʿibādī*, *jannatī*.

I present this as my own reading. But that the verse-ending is built with the first-person singular pronoun in the last two verses is a fact standing in the text and is repeated nowhere else in the sūra.

## The sūra as a whole

### The structure of the verse-endings

In respect of sound, al-Fajr differs from most of the short Meccan sūras. The nine verses of al-Humaza closed with a single sound, the twenty-one verses of al-Layl with a single long *ā*. Al-Fajr is not like this: **the verse-ending changes four times through the sūra.**

| Verses | Verse-endings | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| **1-5** | *al-fajr, ʿashr, al-watr, yasr, ḥijr* | Short, ending in a consonant, a hard **-r** |
| **6-14** | *ʿĀd, ʿimād, bilād, wād, awtād, bilād, fasād, ʿadhāb, mirṣād* | Long **-ād** (verse 13, *ʿadhāb*, the single exception) |
| **15-20** | *akraman, ahānan, al-yatīm, al-miskīn, lammā, jammā* | Variable; three separate sounds, two verses each |
| **21-26** | *dakkā, ṣaffā, al-dhikrā, li-ḥayātī, aḥad, aḥad* | Variable again; the last two verses **the same word** |
| **27-30** | *al-muṭmaʾinna, marḍiyya, ʿibādī, jannatī* | Soft, long; the last two **with the first-person singular pronoun** |

**And the places where the verse-ending changes coincide with the places where the sections of the sūra change.** The block of oaths closes with a single sound; the block of history with another; the block of diagnosis loses its unity of sound; the block of the resurrection advances with repetitions; the block of the call softens entirely.

The scatteredness between 15 and 20 is particularly worth recording: the place where the sūra's unity of sound breaks down is the place where the faultiness of man's measure is described. I record this as an observation of **agreement between form and content**, not as a claim of miracle. In the short Meccan sūras the sound often moves together with the content, and this suggests that the choice is deliberate.

### The skeleton and the internal symmetry

| Section | Verses | What it does | Its counterpart |
|---|---|---|---|
| The oath | 1-5 | Countable units of time; the condition *dhī ḥijr* | 11: *ṭaghaw* — those who exceed the boundary |
| History | 6-14 | Three peoples, three structures, transgression, recompense | 21: the levelling of the structures |
| Diagnosis | 15-16 | *akraman / ahānan* — a swaying judgment | 28: *rāḍiya / marḍiyya* — a settled state |
| Accusation | 17-20 | The orphan, the needy, inheritance, the love of wealth | 24: "would that I had sent something ahead" |
| The resurrection | 21-26 | Levelling, ranks, remembering to no purpose | — |
| The call | 27-30 | An invitation to the soul at rest | The man at 15-16 |

**The clearest symmetry is in the two pairs of words at the two ends of the sūra:**

| | **89/15-16** | **89/28** |
|---|---|---|
| The words | *akraman* / *ahānan* | *rāḍiya* / *marḍiyya* |
| Who says them | Man, about himself | The narrator, about the soul |
| The structure | Two opposite judgments, at two different times | Two complementary attributes, at the same time |
| What they announce | Swaying | Settling |
| Their answer | *Kallā* — rejected | *Fa'dkhulī* — called |

The first pair **excludes itself**: one cannot say at the same time both "I have been honoured" and "I have been abased"; if one comes the other goes. The second pair **requires itself**: being well pleased and being one with whom there is good pleasure exist at the same time.

This is the tightest structural feature of the sūra: **an unstable duality and a stable duality.**

### The distribution of the word *Rabb*

The word's **eight** occurrences in the sūra (the full table was given in the section on 89/6) establish this chain:

**The one who destroys (6) → the one who pours out the punishment (13) → the one who stands in the place of watching (14) → the one who tests (15) → [in man's mouth: the one who honours (15) / the one who abases (16)] → the one who comes (22) → the one returned to (28).**

The two links in the middle of the chain are in man's own mouth, and both are rejected. That is, the sūra has placed within the text itself the place where its own key word is used wrongly.

### The essential idea of the sūra

What has been counted so far can be gathered into a single sentence:

**Man reads what happens to him as a verdict; the sūra calls him to read it as a testing.**

This distinction looks simple but its consequences are wide:

- **A verdict looks to the past** — it is a decision already given, not open to dispute, only to be accepted. And it is final about the person.
- **A testing looks to the future** — it is an open operation, it has as yet no result, and it leaves the person the possibility of doing something.

The person who counts abundance a verdict relaxes and stops. The person who counts want a verdict collapses and stops. **Both arrive at the same place: immobility.**

The person who reads it as a testing asks the same question in both states: what shall I do with this?

And the sūra gives the answer to this question too — in the seventeenth and eighteenth verses: the orphan and the needy. That is, the answer to the question "what shall I do with this?" has to do not with what is in one's hand but with the one who has nothing in his.

Finally: the one who will give the verdict is in the place of watching (14). Man's attempting to pass a verdict about himself is his supposing an occupied post to be empty.

## What this says to the present

**First: the counting of success as divine approval and of poverty as a personal fault.**

This is the place in the sūra that faces the present most directly, and one must speak carefully.

A way of thinking that establishes a link between a person's material condition and his moral condition is supposed to be new; it is not. The sentence al-Fajr describes is at least fourteen centuries old and probably much older. What has changed is the manner of expression.

Today's forms are recognisable: languages that count material success the outward showing of an inner quality; explanations that assume abundance to be "deserved" and want to be "deserved"; and, when it is presented in a religious dress, the reading of what is given as a certificate of approval.

**The limit the sūra sets is single and works in both directions: no material condition is a divine verdict about a person.** Neither in your own favour nor against another's. This is not an economic opinion; it is an assessment of evidence. The text forbids counting what one holds as proof.

**Second: the symmetry of the testing.**

That the verb *ibtilāʾ* is the same in both verses is the point most often passed over in practice.

Man generally counts hard times a test and good times a reward. By the grammar of the sūra both are tests. And on reflection the test of good times is the harder — because one does not feel that one is being tested. Hardship makes itself heard; abundance does not.

The concrete counterpart of this is: what state a person is in changes which questions are put to him but does not stop their being put. To say "I am comfortable now" in a period when things are going well shows, by the measure of the sūra, not that the test has ended but that the question has changed.

**Third: the ethics of property in which no labour has been invested.**

That the nineteenth verse chooses inheritance is particularly operative today. Property in which no labour has been invested is the property about which a person cannot say "I deserved it" — and precisely for that reason it is the most easily counted as deserved.

The right of the orphan does not appear today in its Jāhiliyya form; it appears in other forms. Arrangements about bequests, the management of the property of a person under guardianship, the distribution of shares in family firms, transactions carried out in the name of a person in need of care, a shared inheritance left undivided for a long time and remaining in practice in the use of one person. All of these are matters of law; but the question the sūra asks comes before law: **are you eating without distinguishing?**

The core of the word *lamm* was "not separating". This is the measure: to know where one's own share ends, and not to prefer not knowing.

**Fourth: the reciprocity of urging.**

That the pattern *taḥāḍḍūna* conveys reciprocity says that help cannot be left to personal good intention.

The finding recorded in the section on al-Balad works here exactly: *"What is counted normal in a community largely determines what the people in that community will do. An environment in which help is ordinary, asking is not shameful, and the one in difficulty is seen; and an environment in which help is exceptional, asking is weakness, and the one in difficulty is invisible — the same people behave differently in these two environments."*

Al-Fajr establishes this measure more plainly than al-Balad, because it establishes it in the pattern of the verb. What is asked is not "you help"; it is that **help be something spoken of, expected, and recalled to one another.**

The present-day counterpart of this requires, beyond individual donations, a question: in the environment I am in, is it easy for someone to say that he is in difficulty? And is someone's helping an ordinary thing, or a thing that draws attention?

**Fifth: where the measure stands.**

*Iṭmiʾnān* is not changing one's judgment as one's condition changes. And its practical counterpart is a matter of exhaustion.

A person whose measure is outside is obliged to reassess himself every time the outside changes. This assessment never ends, because the outside never stops. However good a person's condition may be, the measure depends on the next piece of news.

What the sūra offers is not a consolation but a proposal to **change place**: to take the measure inside. And al-Raʿd 13/28 says how this happens — with *dhikr*. That is, taking the measure inside is not a display of will but a work of establishing a bond.

A measurable sign of this is: how much does a person's opinion of himself change with the news that comes in during the day?

**Sixth: postponement, and to whom the text is to be read.**

The structure of the sentence *"Would that I had sent something ahead for my life"* shows how postponement works. The man did not forget to do something; **he understood too late that it mattered.** His measure changed afterwards.

Postponement is generally supposed to be a weakness of will. What the sūra shows is otherwise: postponement is a matter of an **ordering of priorities**, and when the ordering changes the postponement ends of itself. The man was not lazy all his life — he ate, he loved, he accumulated. He had only built the order wrongly.

**And finally, a warning particularly necessary for this sūra.**

The fifteenth and sixteenth verses of al-Fajr are very open to being used to pass judgment about others. To see a rich man and say "there is the man who supposes his wealth an honouring"; to see a poor man and say "so he is being tested". **Both are the very thing the text forbids.**

Because what the verse takes as its target is not a person's condition but **the inference made from the condition.** To look at another's material state and draw a spiritual conclusion about him is to use the very measure the sūra cut away — only the direction has changed.

The measure recorded in the section on al-Humaza holds here too: *"The text is an instrument of diagnosis; and the diagnosis is applied first to oneself."*

The question that falls to the reader is this: **what do I think today about what is in my hand, and how does that thought change the way I look at one who has nothing in his?**

The sūra has bound these two questions to one another, and between them stands a single word: *kallā*.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

At many points throughout the text a definite language was deliberately not used; the reader should know these:

- **That there is a difference among the schools of counting over the number of verses of the sūra** was recorded, but no definite figure was given as to which school counts how many verses, because I am not sure. The widespread (Kūfan) count was taken as the basis.
- **Whether *al-fajr* in the first verse is the class or a particular morning** is disputed; three views were given in a table, the preference of the majority was stated, and it was recorded that it is not binding.
- **Which ten nights "the ten nights" are** is not certain; four views were given with their proofs and their weak sides, and none was preferred. The reports about the merits of these nights were not entered into, because I cannot issue a ruling on their soundness.
- **Five views on "the even and the odd"** were given in a table; it was stated that the first two stand nearer to the wording, and it was recorded that this is not binding.
- **Where the answer of the oath is** is not certain; four views were given, a preference was stated on grammatical grounds, and it was written that it is not binding.
- **What Iram is** (a tribe / a city / another name for ʿĀd) and **what "possessor of columns" conveys** (columns / tent poles / stature) are disputed; a table was given on two separate axes, and no firm preference was adopted.
- **The story-accounts about Iram — the golden city, the ruler's name — were not conveyed;** it was stated that they have no ground in the Qurʾān and that some of the classical scholars rejected them. The ruler's name in question does not occur in the Qurʾān and was not written in this commentary.
- **Archaeological claims of the "Iram found" kind were not entered into.** It was recorded openly that there is no proof that would identify an archaeological site with the Iram of the Qurʾān and that this equation is not an accepted result.
- **No figure whatever was given about the bodily measurements of ʿĀd, the populations of the peoples, or the dates of the events.** What the Qurʾān says and what the reports add were separated under distinct headings.
- **The warning that the monumental façades at Madāʾin Ṣāliḥ / al-Ḥijr belong to the Nabataean period and are not the structures of Thamūd** was repeated, preserved exactly from the section on al-Shams.
- **The four views on the expression "possessor of the stakes"** were given; no firm preference was adopted. The report concerning torture was not detailed; I contented myself with saying "some exegetes have understood it so".
- **The subtlety drawn from the definite article in the sentence "they multiplied corruption"** was presented as a grammatical possibility; the possibility that the definiteness conveys the class was also recorded.
- **The three readings in the construct "a scourge of punishment"** were given; no preference was adopted. The interpretation that the whip is an instrument of discipline was conveyed as the view of "some exegetes".
- **Variant readings** — *watr/witr* (3), *yasr/yasrī* (4), the construct *ʿĀdi Irama* (7), *akraman/akramanī* and *ahānan/ahānanī* (15-16), *taḥāḍḍūna/taḥuḍḍūna* (18), *yuʿadhdhibu/yuʿadhdhabu* and *yūthiqu/yūthaqu* (25-26), *ʿibādī/ʿabdī* (29) — were recorded; **in none of them was the name of an authority given**, because I cannot say with certainty which reading belongs to whom. That the difference in verses 25-26 changes the meaning was stated openly and both readings were counted meaningful.
- **No theological side was taken on *wa jāʾa rabbuka*;** the attitudes of *tafwīḍ* and *taʾwīl* were conveyed in a table, both with respect, and the shared principle (al-Shūrā 42/11) was recorded.
- **When the command "return" is said** (at death / at the resurrection / both) is disputed; a table was given, an advantage in respect of context was stated, but no preference was imposed. The link established with al-Aʿrāf 7/172 was presented as a **possibility of interpretation**.
- **The classification of "the three stages of the soul" was not presented as the Qurʾān's own classification;** it was written openly that the three adjectives occur in three separate sūras, in three separate contexts, and that the ordering among them is an interpretation developed in the later tradition.
- **A general frame was given regarding the inheritance practice of the Jāhiliyya**, without entering into details and into the scope of the reports; only the presence in the text of the prohibition at al-Nisāʾ 4/19 was recorded.
- **The tension between *dakkan dakkā* and *dakkatan wāḥidah* at al-Ḥāqqa 69/14** was recorded; three proposed solutions were given and no firm ruling was issued.
- **Marked openly as my own reading:** that the four elements of the oath are "countable units of time"; that the elision in *yasri* produces meaning through sound; the opposition between *ḥijr* and *ṭaghaw*; the echo between *jābū* and *jawāb*; the triad's descent from the crowd to a single person; that the repetition of *fi'l-bilād* in verses 8 and 11 produces meaning; the carrying of the absolute-object pattern from the stage of this world to the stage of the resurrection; the symmetry between the structures of the three peoples and *dakk*; the symmetry of the "error of naming" between *akraman/ahānan* and *li-ḥayātī*; the link between *mīthāq* and *withāq*; the link between the plural mouth of al-Fātiḥa and *fī ʿibādī*; the affinity of levelling between *ṭarīq muʿabbad* and *dukkat*; and the closing of the distance between the first word of the sūra and its last.
- **Observations about verse-endings and sound** were presented as an agreement between form and content; **not as a claim of miracle.**
- **The reports about the recitation of Sūrat al-Fajr in the prayer were not entered into**, because I am not sure of their sources. No ḥadīth was conveyed with an ascription to a source.
- The finding that the word *Rabb* occurs **eight** times in the sūra was made by counting the text itself; the verses were shown one by one in the table.
