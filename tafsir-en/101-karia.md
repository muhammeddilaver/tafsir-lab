# 101. Sūrat al-Qāriʿa

Eleven verses. It takes its name from its first word: **ٱلْقَارِعَة.**

The sūra opens with a question and gives the question no definition. *"Al-Qāriʿa. What is al-Qāriʿa? And what made you know what al-Qāriʿa is?"* — the same word three times in three verses. Then an answer comes, but the answer is not a definition: **two similes** and **two outcomes.**

The backbone of the sūra is a single concept, and that concept is nowhere in the sūra called by its name: **weight.**

See how it is built. First people are likened to scattered moths — light. Then the mountains are likened to carded wool — light. That is, the sūra first **empties the world of all its weight**: the lightest living thing and the heaviest body become one and the same. Then, in the very middle of this scene in which everything has lost its weight, the balance is set up and a single question is asked: **whose scales weighed heavy?**

Once you have passed the two similes and arrived at the balance, an unavoidable question arises: *on a day when even the mountains have no weight left, what can weigh heavy?* The sūra does not ask this question, but it makes it be asked. The purpose of this section is to show that link step by step.

## Meccan or Medinan

On its being Meccan there is something close to unanimity. The style supports this as well: short verse-endings, a scene of the resurrection, the *mā adrāka* pattern, the absence of legal content.

The basmala was treated in earlier sections and is not taken up again here; the verse numbers are counted without it.

## 101/1-3 — ٱلْقَارِعَةُ · مَا ٱلْقَارِعَةُ · وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا ٱلْقَارِعَةُ

*Al-qāriʿa · Ma'l-qāriʿa · Wa mā adrāka ma'l-qāriʿa*
"Al-Qāriʿa! What is al-Qāriʿa? And what made you know what al-Qāriʿa is?"

### ٱلْقَارِعَة — the one that strikes

**Root: ق-ر-ع.** The concrete sense of the root is **to strike with something hard, to hit**. Its derivatives show how concrete the root is:

- **قَرَعَ ٱلْبَابَ** — he knocked at the door. In Arabic, to knock at a door is "to strike the door".
- **مِقْرَعَة** — a stick, a whip. An instrument for striking.
- **قَارِعَةُ ٱلطَّرِيقِ** — the middle of the road, the part the feet tread. The "struck" part of the road.
- **قِرَاع** — clashing, colliding.
- **ٱلْقَرْع** — the blow itself, its sound.

So the word is not the name of a disaster; it is **the name of a movement.** *Qāriʿa* is an active participle: **the one that strikes, the one that hits.**

And the movement chosen matters. Not to demolish, not to crush, not to burn — **to strike.** A blow is the most sudden form of contact: unprepared for, in a single stroke, together with a sound.

That the sense of knocking at a door is present in the word is worth noticing besides. To knock at a door is for someone arriving to announce that he is there. So too al-Qāriʿa: it makes its coming heard, and it must be answered.

### The sound of the word

Read the word aloud: *al-qāriʿa.*

It is built on three hard letters: **ق** (a stop that comes from the very bottom of the throat), **ر** (a trill), **ع** (a pharyngeal). Three of the hardest sounds in Arabic, in a word of four letters.

As has been recorded several times in this commentary, the fit between sound and meaning is a phenomenon found in every language, and it is especially productive in Arabic. Here too it is real and striking. I do not present this as a proof of miracle; I record that at the opening of the sūra the sound of the word carries its meaning — especially when it is repeated three times in a row.

### The three repetitions

The first three verses of the sūra are built like this:

1. **ٱلْقَارِعَةُ** — the noun alone. A verse of one word.
2. **مَا ٱلْقَارِعَةُ** — a question.
3. **وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا ٱلْقَارِعَةُ** — a question declared unanswerable.

The same word **three times** in three verses. And note: in none of them is a pronoun used. It could have said *"What is it?"* (*mā hiya*); it does not. It could have said *"And what made you know what it is?"*; it does not. Each time the whole word is repeated.

In Arabic, repeating the noun where a pronoun would have served conveys **magnification** (*tafkhīm*) and **the instilling of dread** (*tahwīl*). We saw the same technique in this commentary at the second verse of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ: *"Allāhu'l-Ṣamad"* is said where *"huwa'l-Ṣamad"* could have been.

But there is something more here. The meaning of the word is **a blow**; and the word is struck three times. The text performs by its form the very movement it names.

I record this as a reading. But this too should be noted: that the word **never occurs again** in the rest of the sūra makes the density of the first three verses still more marked. The sūra says its name three times and then leaves it.

### The grammar of the second verse

*Ma'l-qāriʿa* — the analysis of the grammarians runs as follows:

- **ٱلْقَارِعَةُ** (verse 1) — the subject.
- **مَا ٱلْقَارِعَةُ** (verse 2) — the predicate. Within it *mā* is the subject and *al-qāriʿa* the predicate; this interrogative sentence as a whole becomes the predicate of the first verse.

That is, the three verses are a single sentence: *"Al-Qāriʿa — what is that Qāriʿa?"*

The question here **is not a question after information.** In Arabic this use is called *istifhām al-taʿẓīm* or *tafkhīm*: the question is asked not in order to obtain an answer but in order to show the magnitude of the thing asked about. The closest thing to it in English is an exclamation of the kind "What a thing that is!"

**Exactly the same structure occurs once more in the Qurʾān:**

*"Al-Ḥāqqa. What is al-Ḥāqqa? And what made you know what al-Ḥāqqa is?"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/1-3)

Three verses, the same pattern word for word. The two sūras name the resurrection by two different names but enter it by the same three steps.

### وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ — the pattern itself

**Root: د-ر-ي.** *Dirāya* — to know, to become aware of. **أَدْرَىٰ** (Form IV) — to make known, to inform.

This pattern was treated in detail in the section on Sūrat al-Humaza; rather than repeat it here I shall recall what was established there and apply it to this sūra.

The rule recorded there was this:

| Pattern | What happens |
|---|---|
| **وما أدراك** (perfect) | An explanation follows; the knowledge is given |
| **وما يدريك** (imperfect) | No explanation follows; the knowledge is not given |

**Sūrat al-Qāriʿa uses this pattern twice, and in both the perfect comes:**

- **Verse 3:** *wa mā adrāka ma'l-qāriʿa* → an explanation of two verses follows (4-5).
- **Verse 10:** *wa mā adrāka mā hiyah* → an explanation of one verse follows (11).

The rule holds both times. This is worth recording as an instance that shows how firm the rule is.

**The answer to the question is "you do not know".** As was recorded in the section on al-Humaza, this pattern, before it gives any knowledge, declares **that knowledge will not be enough.** The description that follows is not a definition but a pointing.

This is another form of the distinction drawn in Sūrat al-Takāthur between *ʿilm al-yaqīn* and *ʿayn al-yaqīn*: knowledge given by report has a limit.

**A shift of address.** *Adrā-ka* — "you". The sūra is speaking in the third person and turns all at once to the second person singular. This device — *iltifāt* (a shift in the direction of address) — was treated in the section on al-Humaza; its function here is the same: the telling stops, the listener is drawn into the scene, then the telling goes on.

### The names of the resurrection

The Qurʾān uses many names for the day of resurrection, and each name brings one aspect of the event forward:

| Name | Root sense | What it brings forward |
|---|---|---|
| **ٱلْقَارِعَة** | To strike, to hit | Sudden contact, a blow |
| **ٱلْحَاقَّة** | To be true, to come about | Verification; the claim proving true |
| **ٱلْوَاقِعَة** | To fall, to occur | The inescapability of its occurring |
| **ٱلصَّاخَّة** | A sound that deafens the ears | Hearing (ʿAbasa 80/33) |
| **ٱلطَّامَّة** | To overwhelm everything, to overflow | Comprehensiveness (al-Nāziʿāt 79/34) |
| **ٱلْغَاشِيَة** | To cover, to overspread | Enclosure (al-Ghāshiya 88/1) |
| **ٱلسَّاعَة** | Time, the hour | Time itself |

All these names share one grammatical feature: nearly all of them are on the pattern of the **active participle**, and they are **feminine**. That is, they all stand in the position of the one that acts. The resurrection is named not as something that happens but as **something that acts**.

**قارعة: its use in this world.** The word is not used in the Qurʾān for the resurrection alone:

*"A qāriʿa does not cease to befall those who disbelieve, because of what they have done…"* (al-Raʿd 13/31)

The *qāriʿa* here is a disaster that comes in this world. And:

*"Thamūd and ʿĀd denied al-Qāriʿa."* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/4)

Here it is the resurrection. So the word names both the small blow and the great one — the small one standing as the herald of the great.

## 101/4 — يَوْمَ يَكُونُ ٱلنَّاسُ كَٱلْفَرَاشِ ٱلْمَبْثُوثِ

*Yawma yakūnu'n-nāsu ka'l-farāshi'l-mabthūth*
"On that day people will be like scattered moths."

The answer to the question begins. And the answer is not a **definition** but **a date and a picture**.

This is a choice that deserves attention: the sūra does not say "al-Qāriʿa is such and such". It says "al-Qāriʿa is the day on which people are like such and such". That is, it describes the event not by itself but by **its effect**. The way to describe a blow is to show what the blow does.

### يَوْمَ — what the adverb attaches to

*Yawma* is an adverb of time in the accusative. The grammarians differ over what it attaches to: to an elided verb (*"al-Qāriʿa comes to pass on the day when…"*), or to the verbal sense carried by the word *qāriʿa* before it, or to the sentence of judgement in the verses that follow.

It makes no practical difference to the meaning; I content myself with recording it.

### ٱلنَّاس — all people

The verse does not say "the disbelievers" or "the wrongdoers". **ٱلنَّاس** — people, all of them.

This detail matters, because the sūra **makes no distinction whatever until the sixth verse.** The simile takes in everyone. The distinction will come later, when the balance is set up.

So the order the sūra builds is this: **first everyone becomes the same, then they are divided.** What lies beneath the simile of the moth is not a belittling but a **levelling**: in that scene no one has any weight.

### ٱلْفَرَاش — butterfly or moth?

**Root: ف-ر-ش.** The concrete sense of the root is **to spread, to lay out**. And this root has already been treated in this commentary — but in a quite different sense.

**At al-Baqara 2/22** the same root occurred thus:

*"He who made the earth a **bed** (فِرَاش) for you."*

What was recorded there was this: *firāsh* is the thing that is spread out; *farsh* (a spread, a carpet) is from the same root; and what the word says is not the shape of the earth but its **function** — that it has been made something that can be stood on, that can be lived on.

Here the same root now comes in the sense of an **insect**.

**How do the two senses meet?** The common core is **spreading**:

- **فِرَاش** (*firāsh*) — the thing that is spread. Spread out, flat, stood upon.
- **فَرَاش** (*farāsh*) — a swarm of insects that **spreads** and scatters as it flies. It is a collective noun; its singular is *farāsha*.

The same root, with two different vowellings, gives two different words. In the one the spreading is **deliberate and ordered**: a covering is laid out. In the other the spreading is **uncontrolled and scattered**: the insects fly off in every direction.

And when you set the two words side by side within the Qurʾān, the picture that comes out is this:

**In al-Baqara the earth was a bed spread out for man. In al-Qāriʿa it is people themselves who turn into a scattered swarm.** A spreading that could be stood upon turns into a spreading that cannot.

I record this as a reading I have drawn from comparing two verses and a single root. The sharing of the root, on the other hand, is an uncontested datum of the language.

**A problem of translation.** English renderings give *farāsh* as "butterflies", as "moths", or as "like scattered butterflies". The Arabic word is a collective noun that covers both: small winged creatures that fly about a light, throw themselves into a fire, and are swept along in swarms. Because the English word "butterfly" calls up a coloured creature that flies by day, the rendering "moth" may stay closer to what the verse suggests. This is a matter of translation preference; the range of the word in Arabic includes them both.

### Why this simile?

The aspects the exegetes bring forward in this simile:

| Aspect | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Scatteredness** | Moths fly in a swarm, but the swarm has no shared direction; each one goes a different way |
| **Lightness** | A creature with no weight, one the wind carries |
| **Multitude** | Too many to be counted; the individual cannot be made out |
| **Bewilderment** | The moth's flight is irregular; where it is going is not clear |
| **Falling into the fire** | The best-known behaviour of the *farāsh* is to circle a fire and drop into it |

The last item calls for a pause. In this verse the sūra **does not mention fire at all** — the fire will come in the eleventh verse. But the word chosen is the name of the insect that throws itself into fire. That is, the last verse of the sūra is already hidden inside the word used in the fourth.

I record this as a reading. The word's association with fire is a known one in the Arabic language; but I do not claim that the sūra intended it.

### ٱلْمَبْثُوث — scattered

**Root: ب-ث-ث.** To spread, to strew, to disperse. The passive participle: *mabthūth* — strewn, dispersed.

The occurrences of the root in the Qurʾān:

- *"…and from the two of them He **spread abroad** many men and women."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/1). The dispersal of the human race across the earth.
- *"I **lay** my anguish and my grief before God alone."* (Yūsuf 12/86). The **بَثّ** (*bathth*) here means a grief that cannot be held within and spills out. An interesting branch of the root: something that has gathered inside spreading outwards.
- *"…and **scattered** cushions."* (al-Ghāshiya 88/16). In a description of the Garden, cushions laid out here and there. The same passive participle pattern, in an entirely positive scene.

The last example is striking: the same word qualifies the cushions of the Garden in one sūra and people at the resurrection in another. The word is not negative in itself; it conveys **being scattered**. Whether being scattered is good or bad depends on what it is that has been scattered.

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

The same scene is told in the Qurʾān with another insect as well:

*"With their eyes downcast they come out of the graves as though they were **locusts spread abroad**."* (al-Qamar 54/7)

The difference between the two similes is worth recording:

- **A swarm of locusts** goes in one shared direction. It has a direction, it has an order; what is frightening about it is its numbers and its destructiveness.
- **A swarm of moths** has no direction. Each one goes somewhere different.

Al-Qāriʿa has chosen the second. The emphasis of the sūra is not on the crowd but on **the loss of direction.**

And this connects with al-Zalzala, the neighbour of the preceding sūra: there people came out *"ashtātan"* — in separate groups, scattered. Both sūras tell of the same dispersal with two different words.

## 101/5 — وَتَكُونُ ٱلْجِبَالُ كَٱلْعِهْنِ ٱلْمَنفُوشِ

*Wa takūnu'l-jibālu ka'l-ʿihni'l-manfūsh*
"And the mountains will be like carded, coloured wool."

### The mountains — why the mountains?

In the Qurʾān the mountain is the emblem of **not moving from its place**. It is named as what holds the earth steady:

- *"He set firm mountains upon the earth, lest it sway with you."* (al-Naḥl 16/15)
- *"And the mountains as pegs [did We not make]?"* (al-Nabaʾ 78/7)

So in the Qurʾān's own language the mountain is **the nail of the earth**. And in the scenes of the resurrection it is the thing most often transformed — because when what does not change changes, it shows that the change has no limit.

The resurrection images the Qurʾān uses for the mountains:

| Verse | Image |
|---|---|
| al-Muzzammil 73/14 | **كَثِيبًا مَّهِيلًا** — a heap of sand that pours away and scatters |
| al-Wāqiʿa 56/5-6 | **هَبَآءً مُّنۢبَثًّا** — motes of dust blown abroad |
| Ṭāhā 20/105 | **نَسْفًا** — to blow away and scatter |
| al-Ṭūr 52/10 | **تَسِيرُ ٱلْجِبَالُ سَيْرًا** — the mountains will move |
| al-Maʿārij 70/9 | **كَٱلْعِهْنِ** — like wool |
| al-Qāriʿa 101/5 | **كَٱلْعِهْنِ ٱلْمَنفُوشِ** — like carded wool |

Two things in this list are worth noticing.

**One.** The **مُنۢبَثًّا** (blown abroad) of al-Wāqiʿa 56/6 is **from the same root** as the **ٱلْمَبْثُوث** of al-Qāriʿa's fourth verse. Al-Wāqiʿa uses the word of the mountains, al-Qāriʿa of people. In two sūras the same root is applied to two different subjects — and this is the sign that the two have become the same.

**Two.** Al-Maʿārij 70/9 and al-Qāriʿa 101/5 are almost identical: *"and the mountains will be like wool."* Al-Qāriʿa adds a single word: **ٱلْمَنفُوش.** What that word adds is what we shall take up below.

### ٱلْعِهْن — coloured wool

**عِهْن** — the definition the lexicographers give: **wool**, and according to the majority **dyed wool** in particular. Some lexicographers say the word means only "wool" and that no note of colour attaches to it; but the note "dyed" is widely transmitted.

There is a reason for taking the note about colour seriously, and it comes from within the Qurʾān itself:

*"In the mountains too [We made] tracks, white and red, of differing colours, and others raven-black."* (Fāṭir 35/27)

The Qurʾān records separately that the mountains are **coloured**: white veins, red veins, black rocks. A mountain is not a mass of a single colour.

Now set the two verses side by side. Fāṭir enumerates the colours of the mountains; al-Qāriʿa likens the mountains to **coloured wool**.

What the simile does is this: **the colour stays, the weight goes.** The mountain does not vanish, nor does it become unrecognisable — it stands there with its colours. The only thing that has changed is that it now carries nothing.

I record this as a reading I have drawn from comparing two verses; I do not present it as transmitted. But the description of *ʿihn* as "dyed wool" and the content of Fāṭir 35/27 are both present in the text.

### ٱلْمَنفُوش — carded, teased apart

**Root: ن-ف-ش.** *Nafasha'l-ṣūfa* — he teased the wool, separated its fibres from one another, made it fluff up.

This is the process carried out before wool is spun. Raw wool is a compressed, matted mass. When it is carded (teased apart) the fibres come away from one another and the wool puffs up: its volume grows, its weight stays the same, its density falls. Carded wool is almost entirely air.

The other occurrence of the root in the Qurʾān is interesting:

*"When the sheep of a people **strayed and spread** by night into a field…"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/78)

The verb here is from the same root and means "to be left without a shepherd and scatter". The common core is again **coming apart from one another, dispersing**: the fibres of the wool come apart, the animals of the flock come apart.

### The two layers of the simile

The verse does not content itself with likening the mountain to wool; it likens it to **carded** wool. This is a fall in two stages:

1. **Mountain → wool.** From solid to soft, from heavy to light, from rock to fibre.
2. **Wool → carded wool.** From compressed to loose, from mass to fluff.

Al-Maʿārij 70/9 contented itself with the first stage. Al-Qāriʿa adds the second.

And the function of the second stage is this: **wool too is a mass.** A ball of wool has weight in your hand, it drops to the ground, it has to be carried. But carded wool is in the air; one breath scatters it.

That is, the sūra brings the mountain's weight to nothing in two steps.

### What the two similes have in common

Now let us read the fourth and fifth verses together:

| | **People (4)** | **Mountains (5)** |
|---|---|---|
| What they are likened to | Butterfly/moth | Wool |
| The qualifier | **مَبْثُوث** — blown about | **مَنفُوش** — teased apart |
| Shared root sense | Dispersal, strewing | Dispersal, separation |
| Their former state | Gathered, settled, established | Fixed, heavy, pegged down |
| Their later state | Weightless, directionless, blown about | Weightless, without density, blown about |

**The two similes are chosen from the two ends of the scale.**

The moth stands where weight comes to its least among living things — when it settles on your hand you do not feel its weight. The mountain stands where weight is most gathered on the earth.

The sūra takes these two ends and makes them **the same**. The lightest is blown about; the heaviest is blown about too. The difference between the two is removed.

And the verb the two similes share lies in the passive participles by which they are both qualified: **مبثوث** and **منفوش.** Both mean "dispersed"; both are passive — not what disperses, but **what is dispersed.**

This sharing is no accident. The two verses are in identical grammar: *ka + definite noun + definite passive participle.* And their verse-endings rhyme as well: *al-mabthūth / al-manfūsh.* **The two similes are matched in sound too.**

## 101/6-7 — فَأَمَّا مَن ثَقُلَتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · فَهُوَ فِى عِيشَةٍ رَّاضِيَةٍ

*Fa-ammā man thaqulat mawāzīnuh · Fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiya*
"As for him whose scales weigh heavy, he is in a contented life."

### The turning point of the sūra

Note this: two verses earlier the mountains had turned into carded wool. And now, in the very middle of that scene, the verse speaks of **weight**.

To see how abrupt this transition is, read the sequence:

> *People will be like scattered moths — the mountains will be like carded wool — as for him whose scales weigh **heavy**…*

For two verses the reader has watched weight being done away with. Now he is told that weight is the measure.

**This is the backbone of the sūra.** Without asking a question, the text makes a question be asked: *on a day when even the mountains have no weight left, what thing can weigh heavy?*

The answer is not written in the sūra. But the framing of the question is the work of the sūra's whole structure: the two similes are not idle, they are there to prepare the balance.

I record this as my own reading. The structure itself, however, is plain in the text: two pictures of lightness, then two judgements of heaviness and lightness. The ordering belongs to the text.

### فَأَمَّا … وَأَمَّا — the pattern that divides in two

**أَمَّا** is in Arabic the particle of *tafṣīl* (setting out in detail): it is used when a whole is being divided into its parts. Its apodosis must carry a **فَ**.

The property of the pattern is this: **the division is absolute and leaves no gap between.** When *ammā… wa-ammā…* is said, there are two categories and there is no third.

By using this pattern the sūra divides mankind in two. In the fourth verse there was a single mass, *"people"*; from the sixth verse on there are two groups.

So the movement of the sūra is this: **one → two.** First everyone becomes the same (the moths), then they are divided in two (the balance).

And this must be stated: **the sūra does not say who is on which side.** It gives the criterion, not the list. This accords with the principle recorded in the method of this commentary (`STYLE.md`): what the verses describe are attributes, and whoever bears that attribute is included in it.

### ثَقُلَتْ — to weigh heavy

**Root: ث-ق-ل** — and this is the very root that occurred twice, two sūras earlier, in al-Zalzala.

The course of the root in three places:

| Place | Word | For what |
|---|---|---|
| al-Zalzala 99/2 | **أَثْقَالَهَا** | The heaviest burdens the earth carries |
| al-Zalzala 99/7-8 | **مِثْقَالَ ذَرَّةٍ** | The smallest weight that can be weighed |
| al-Qāriʿa 101/6 | **ثَقُلَتْ** | The pan of the balance that comes down heavy |

Al-Zalzala used the root at the two extremes: the greatest burden and the smallest mote. Al-Qāriʿa uses the root as **the result of a measurement**. That is, the unit was introduced first, and then it was put on the scale.

I record this as an observation about the arrangement of the muṣḥaf; it is not a claim about the order of revelation.

### مَوَٰزِين — its coming in the plural

**Root: و-ز-ن.** To weigh. **مِيزَان** — a balance; it is a noun of instrument on the *mifʿāl* pattern (originally *miwzān*, the *wāw* having turned into a *yāʾ*). Its plural is **مَوَازِين**.

**Why the plural?** There are three basic views:

| View | Explanation | Its result |
|---|---|---|
| **The plural of مِيزَان** | There is more than one balance | A separate balance for each person, or a separate measure for each kind of deed |
| **The plural of مَوْزُون** | The plural of the things weighed | "His scales weighed heavy" = the deeds weighed for him came out heavy |
| **A plural for magnification / multiplicity** | The plural used to show the greatness of a single thing | The balance is one, but its greatness is conveyed by a plural |

All three have been defended in the classical sources, and they make no great difference to the meaning.

But the second view has a practical side: when it is read as the plural of *mawzūn*, what is heavy is not the balance but **the things weighed**. This sits more easily with the grammar of the verse, because the expression "the balance weighs heavy" requires a figure of speech in Arabic just as it does in English.

**The distribution in the Qurʾān.** There is a striking distribution between the singular and the plural uses of the word:

- **The singular (ٱلْمِيزَان)** occurs in the context of the measure of justice in this world: *"He raised the sky and set the balance; do not transgress in the weighing"* (al-Raḥmān 55/7-8); *"We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and We sent down with them the Book and the balance, that people might uphold justice"* (al-Ḥadīd 57/25); *"Give full measure and full weight"* (Hūd 11/85).
- **The plural (ٱلْمَوَازِين)** occurs in the scenes of the hereafter: al-Aʿrāf 7/8-9, al-Muʾminūn 23/102-103, al-Anbiyāʾ 21/47, and this sūra.

I want to draw attention to this distribution; I cannot say for certain whether it has exceptions, and for that reason I do not present it as a rule. But if the distribution holds, it gives one this to think about: **in this world justice is a single measure; in the hereafter the measures have been multiplied.** In this world there is one balance that weighs everyone; on that day each life is weighed on its own balance.

### What is weighed?

The exegetes have differed on this:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Deeds** | The things done are weighed |
| **The records of deeds** | The records themselves are weighed |
| **The person himself** | Man is weighed |

There is a further debate in the classical theological literature: **is the weighing a real operation, or is it an expression of absolute justice?** Some scholars, keeping to the plain sense of the verses, have said that a real weighing is spoken of; others have held that the weighing is a description conveying that no right will be lost by so much as a mote.

I take no side in this debate. The point both accept in common is this: **the measurement is exact and no one is wronged.** The Qurʾān says this separately: *"We shall set up the scales of justice for the day of resurrection; no soul will be wronged by so much as a mote"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/47).

This verse, as was recorded in the section on al-Zalzala too, **joins the two sūras**: al-Zalzala's *dharra* and al-Qāriʿa's *mawāzīn* occur in the same sentence.

### The light and the heavy

In a well-known hadith found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim it is transmitted that the Prophet said: *"There are two words light on the tongue, heavy in the balance."*

The idea the report carries is the idea of this sūra: **there is no relation between how easy a thing is to say and what it comes to on the scale.** The balance weighs not the effort spent but the thing that is placed on it.

And this connects directly with the sūra's two similes. The mountain looked heavy and came out light. A word looks light and can come out heavy. **Apparent weight and weighed weight are not the same thing.**

### عِيشَةٍ رَّاضِيَةٍ — a contented life

**عِيشَة** — **Root: ع-ي-ش.** To live. *ʿAysh* — life, livelihood. *Maʿīsha* — livelihood, the manner of getting a living. **عِيشَة**, however, is on the *fiʿla* pattern, and in Arabic this pattern conveys **the manner of doing**: *jilsa* (the manner of sitting), *mīta* (the manner of dying), *ʿīsha* (the manner of living).

So the verse does not say "life", it says **"a manner of living"**. What is given is not a span of life but a way of living.

**رَاضِيَة** — **Root: ر-ض-و.** To be content, to be well pleased.

The word is an active participle: **"the one that is content"**, "the one that is well pleased". But who is it that is content? Is it the life that is content?

The grammarians give three explanations here:

| Explanation | Account |
|---|---|
| **An active participle in a passive sense** | *Rāḍiya* = *marḍiyya* — "a life that is approved, a life one is content with" |
| **A relational adjective** | *Dhātu riḍan* — "a life possessed of contentment", that is, a life with contentment in it |
| **Figurative predication** | The attribute has been taken from its true bearer (the person living) and given to the living |

All three have been defended in the classical sources. The third explanation is a recognised device of language: we say "a sleepless night", though what is sleepless is not the night but ourselves.

**An indication from within the Qurʾān.** The active form of the word is used elsewhere expressly of a person:

*"O soul at peace! Return to your Lord, well pleased and well pleasing."* (al-Fajr 89/27-28)

There the two words stand side by side: **رَاضِيَة** (the one that is well pleased) and **مَرْضِيَّة** (the one that is well pleased with). That is, the Qurʾān can use these two patterns separately and knows the difference between them. That the active is the one chosen in al-Qāriʿa strengthens the sense **"the one that is content"** rather than "the one that is approved".

**And there is an exact parallel:**

*"He is in a contented life."* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/21)

The same expression word for word — *fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiya.* In al-Ḥāqqa this sentence is said of the person given his record in his right hand. The two sūras tell of the same outcome in the same words.

**فِى — to be inside something.** The verse does not say "he has a contented life", it says **"he is in a contented life"**. The person does not possess the life; he is inside it. Not ownership but enclosure.

## 101/8-9 — وَأَمَّا مَنْ خَفَّتْ مَوَٰزِينُهُۥ · فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌ

*Wa ammā man khaffat mawāzīnuh · Fa-ummuhū hāwiya*
"And as for him whose scales weigh light, his mother is Hāwiya."

### خَفَّتْ — to weigh light

**Root: خ-ف-ف.** To be light. Its derivatives:

- **خَفِيف** — light.
- **تَخْفِيف** — a making lighter. *"This is an alleviation from your Lord, and a mercy"* (al-Baqara 2/178).
- **اِسْتَخَفَّ** — to count light, to belittle, to make light of. *"He made light of his people, and they obeyed him"* (al-Zukhruf 43/54).

This last derivative is worth pausing on. In Arabic the same root carries both **lightness on the scale** and **not taking a thing seriously**. English has the same relation: we say "to make light of" something.

This is an observation about the language, and I make no claim about how tight the bond between the two senses is. But it adds a layer to the reading of the verse: **the one who weighs light on the scale is the one who has made light of things.**

### The breaking of the symmetry

Now set the sixth and seventh verses beside the eighth and ninth:

| | **The heavy pan (6-7)** | **The light pan (8-9)** |
|---|---|---|
| The condition | *man thaqulat mawāzīnuh* | *man khaffat mawāzīnuh* |
| The apodosis | *fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiya* | *fa-**ummuhū** hāwiya* |

The conditional clauses are exactly symmetrical: the same structure, the same words, only the verb reversed.

**But the apodoses are not symmetrical.**

In the first apodosis the subject is the person: *"**he** is in a life."* In the second the subject is not the person but **his mother**: *"**his mother** is Hāwiya."*

The symmetry one expects would have been: *fa-huwa fī nārin ḥāmiya* — "he is in a blazing fire." That sentence could have been built; indeed the eleventh verse very nearly says it. But the sūra inserts **a sentence of another kind**.

This break is deliberate, and the surprise of it is the verse's own work. By breaking the expected pattern the text brings the reader to a halt. And in the tenth verse it will itself acknowledge the strangeness of what it has said: *"And what made you know what it is?"*

### فَأُمُّهُۥ هَاوِيَةٌ — "his mother is Hāwiya"

One of the most debated expressions in the Qurʾān.

**هَاوِيَة — Root: ه-و-ي.** *Hawā* — to fall from a height. **هَاوِيَة** is an active participle: **the one that falls**, or (in a passive sense) **the thing fallen into**, that is, a deep abyss.

The occurrences of the root in the Qurʾān:

- *"By the star when it sets."* (al-Najm 53/1). The verb is *hawā* — to go down.
- *"Whoever associates anything with God is as though he had fallen from the sky and the birds had snatched him up, or the wind had **swept** him away to some far place."* (al-Ḥajj 22/31). This verse is striking: both **being swept away by the wind** and **falling** are in the same sentence. The same cluster of images as the sūra's two similes and its ninth verse.
- **ٱلْهَوَىٰ** — *hawā*, the craving of the self. *"He followed his craving"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/176); *"Have you seen the one who has taken his own craving as his god?"* (al-Jāthiya 45/23).

**The last item is important.** In Arabic "craving" and "falling" come from the same root. What the lexicographers say about this connection is this: *hawā* is what pulls a person downwards; the word itself contains the fall.

That is: **the one who goes after his craving arrives at the Hāwiya.** The two words are from the same root. I record this connection as a relation established by the language itself; I do not ascribe it to any particular exegete.

**هَاوِيَة is one of the names of hell.** As was recorded in the sections on al-Takāthur and al-Humaza, the Qurʾān uses many names for hell: *jahannam*, *nār*, *jaḥīm*, *saqar*, *laẓā*, *saʿīr*, *ḥuṭama* and **hāwiya**. Each name brings one aspect of the fire forward; *hāwiya* is unique in the aspect it brings forward: it names **not the heat of the fire but the manner of arriving at it**. The place reached by falling.

### The disagreement: what does "his mother" mean?

The strangeness of the expression lies in the word *umm* (mother). The classical explanations:

| View | Explanation | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A refuge / the place one comes to** | *Umm* means "origin, source, the place one takes refuge in". As a child takes refuge with its mother, so this person will take refuge there — he has no other shelter | In Arabic uses in the sense of "origin/centre" are common: *umm al-qurā* (Mecca, al-Anʿām 6/92), *umm al-kitāb* (Āl ʿImrān 3/7); and a mother's being a refuge is a universal association | It requires the assumption of a figure of speech |
| **A continuation of a pattern of imprecation** | In Arabic *hawat ummuhū* ("may his mother lose him", "may his mother sit in mourning") is a curse of destruction. The expression carries the trace of this pattern | The pattern is settled in the Arabic language and occurs in poetry | The verse has a nominal sentence, not a verb; it is not the exact counterpart of the pattern |
| **The crown of the head** | *Umm al-raʾs* means the crown of the head, the brain. Since the person is to be flung headfirst into the fire, "his head falls" | *Umm al-raʾs* is used in Arabic; being flung headfirst occurs in the Qurʾān: *"Their faces will be dragged in the fire"* (al-Naml 27/90) | That *hāwiya* is a name of hell weakens this reading; and in verses 10-11 the thing asked about as "it" is answered as a place |

**The first view is the most widespread** and the one most consistent with the rest of the sūra: that the tenth verse asks *"what is it?"* and the eleventh answers *"a blazing fire"* shows that *hāwiya* is a place.

I too prefer the first view; it is not binding.

### What the expression does

If we take the first view as our basis, what the verse builds is extremely harsh.

**A mother** is **the first and safest refuge** in a person's life. All the associations of the word run on protection, on shelter, on a place one can return to.

The verse takes this word and gives it to **the last and worst place**.

And in doing so it says one thing more: this person **has no other refuge.** To say "his mother is Hāwiya" is to say "that is the place he can go back to". The person is not merely thrown somewhere; he arrives at **the place he belongs to**.

On the matter of taking refuge this builds a closing that runs in the opposite direction to what was treated earlier in this commentary. Sūrat al-Falaq and Sūrat al-Nās opened with taking refuge: *"I take refuge with the Lord of…"* Al-Qāriʿa closes with the story of the one who has no refuge.

### The balance turned the other way round

There is an oddity here that anyone reading the sūra attentively will notice, and it is worth pausing on.

In a real balance **the heavy pan goes down and the light pan rises.** The direction of weight is downwards.

In the sūra the case is the reverse:

- **The one whose scales weigh heavy** does not go up; he is found within a life.
- **The one whose scales weigh light** does not rise; he **falls** — *hāwiya* is the place one falls into.

That is, lightness brings not a rising but a falling. The person whose pan rises goes downward himself.

I record this as my own reading. But the sūra's words are there in plain sight: *khaffat* (weighed light) and *hāwiya* (the place fallen into) are in the same sentence.

And this reversal links up with the first half of the sūra. The moth and the carded wool **are blown about** because they have become light — they cannot carry themselves. Lightness here is not a rising but a **loss of control**. It is being left to the wind. The picture at al-Ḥajj 22/31 is exactly this: the wind sweeps him away to some far place.

## 101/10 — وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا هِيَهْ

*Wa mā adrāka mā hiyah*
"And what made you know what it is?"

### The same question a second time

In the third verse the sūra had said *mā adrāka*. Now in the tenth verse the same pattern is repeated.

**The difference between the two questions lies in what is asked about:**

- **Verse 3:** *ma'l-qāriʿa* — the question is asked **by repeating the noun**.
- **Verse 10:** *mā hiyah* — the question is asked **by a pronoun**.

In the first three verses the word was never turned into a pronoun; each time the whole of it was repeated. Here, for *hāwiya*, a pronoun is used.

I record this as an observation. Besides, *hāwiya* had occurred as an indefinite; to refer back to an indefinite word by a pronoun is natural.

### The function of the question

The tenth verse is **the text's own acknowledgement of the strangeness** of the ninth.

*"His mother is Hāwiya"* had been said — an expression that will leave the hearer confused. And immediately after it: *"And what made you know what it is?"*

That is, the text knows that what it has said calls for an explanation, and before giving the explanation it declares that the explanation will fall short. As was recorded in the section on al-Humaza: **the description is not a definition but a pointing.**

### هِيَهْ — the *hāʾ* of pause

The **هـ** at the end of the word bears in grammar the name **هاء السكت** (the *hāʾ* of pause).

Its function is this: in Arabic certain short words lose the sound at their end when they are stopped on (*waqf*). When one stops on the word *hiya* the final vowel drops and the word becomes unrecognisable. The vowelless *hāʾ* added at the end preserves that sound.

So this letter carries no meaning; it is there **to preserve the sound**.

**Its densest use in the Qurʾān is in Sūrat al-Ḥāqqa:**

*"Here, read my record!"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/19 — *kitābi**yah***); *"Would that I had never known my reckoning!"* (69/26 — *ḥisābi**yah***); *"My wealth has not availed me"* (69/28 — *māli**yah***); *"My authority has perished from me"* (69/29 — *sulṭāni**yah***).

Al-Qāriʿa's *mā hiyah* and this series in al-Ḥāqqa are the same grammatical phenomenon. And if we recall that the first three verses of the two sūras are on the same pattern as well, there is a marked closeness between the two sūras in point of sound.

**A note on the variant readings.** There is agreement that this *hāʾ* is preserved when the word is read in pause. As to whether it is preserved in continuous recitation (*waṣl*, when one goes on without stopping), a difference is transmitted among the imams of the readings. Since I cannot give with certainty which imam held which preference, I give no names; it does not change the meaning.

## 101/11 — نَارٌ حَامِيَةٌۢ

*Nārun ḥāmiya*
"A blazing fire."

The answer of the sūra, and the close of the sūra. **Two words.**

### The brevity of the answer

Note how the sūra's budget of information narrows:

- **Verse 3:** *"And what made you know what al-Qāriʿa is?"* → the answer, **two verses** (4-5).
- **Verse 10:** *"And what made you know what it is?"* → the answer, **two words** (11).

The second question is answered more briefly. And the answer given is not a description but **a naming**.

This is the last step of what the sūra has been doing from the beginning: the text refuses to define. For *al-Qāriʿa* no definition was given, a simile was given. For *hāwiya* no definition was given, a name was given.

### نَار — its coming indefinite

**نَارٌ** — indefinite, that is, with no **al-**. Not "the fire" but **"a fire."**

In Arabic indefiniteness normally conveys indeterminacy; but in some places it serves **the instilling of dread** (*tahwīl*) and **magnification** — the word is left indefinite in order to show that its extent is too great to be told.

We recorded the same phenomenon in this commentary at the first verse of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ: *aḥad* coming with nunation conveyed magnification. Here the same grammar works in the opposite direction.

That is, the verse says this: *"not the fire you know — a fire."* The indefiniteness makes void the concept the hearer has in hand.

### حَامِيَة — blazing

**Root: ح-م-ي.** *Ḥamiya'l-shayʾu* — a thing grew hot, became very hot. **حَامِيَة** — active participle: blazing, heated.

From the same root, **حُمَّى** (*ḥummā*) — fever. The rising of the body's heat.

**A note on the language, and a record.** In Arabic the same root letters appear also in the words **حِمَايَة** (protection) and **حِمَى** (a protected pasture, an inviolable area). The lexicographers differ over whether these two clusters of meaning (heat / protection) are gathered under a single root or are two separate homophonous roots. **I do not enter into this disagreement and I draw no meaning from this sharing of sound**; I merely record it.

**An exact parallel within the Qurʾān:**

*"It will enter a blazing fire."* (al-Ghāshiya 88/4)

The same construction word for word — *nāran ḥāmiya*, indefinite again. In al-Ghāshiya this is said of the faces that on that day are "humbled, toiling, labouring".

### Verse-ending and pattern

And now let us come to the hidden order of the sūra.

Look at the key words of the sūra:

- **قَارِعَة** — the one that strikes
- **رَاضِيَة** — the one that is content
- **هَاوِيَة** — the one that falls / the one fallen into
- **حَامِيَة** — the one that blazes

**All four are on the same pattern: فَاعِلَة** — the feminine active participle.

All the words that decide the sūra's destinies are cast from a single mould. The name, the two outcomes, and the quality of the outcome are all in the same grammatical pattern.

And this determines the verse-endings too. The verse-endings of the eleven verses:

| Verse | Verse-ending | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| 1-3 | *al-qāriʿa* (three times) | **-iʿa** |
| 4-5 | *al-mabthūth / al-manfūsh* | **-ūsh** |
| 6, 8 | *mawāzīnuh / mawāzīnuh* | **-nuh** |
| 7, 9, 11 | *rāḍiya / hāwiya / ḥāmiya* | **-iya** |
| 10 | *hiyah* | **-yah** |

The order that appears is this:

**One.** The verses that are paired are paired in sound as well. The two similes (4-5) rhyme with one another; the two conditions of the balance (6, 8) rhyme with one another.

**Two.** The dominant sound of the sūra is **-iya/-iʿa**, and this sound binds together the name of the sūra (*qāriʿa*) and the three words of outcome (*rāḍiya*, *hāwiya*, *ḥāmiya*).

**Three.** The two pairs that come between (4-5 and 6-8) stay outside this dominant sound — that is, the text departs twice from its main theme and twice returns to it.

The observation recorded in the section on al-Takāthur holds here too: in the short Meccan sūras sound and content often move together. I record this not as a claim of miracle but as an instance of the dense sound-weave of these sūras.

## The sūra as a whole — the theme of lightness

Now let us read the sūra from beginning to end with a single concept.

### The course of weight

| Verse | What happens | In point of weight |
|---|---|---|
| 1-3 | Al-Qāriʿa is named | — |
| 4 | People like moths | The lightest living thing; weight brought to nothing |
| 5 | Mountains like carded wool | The heaviest body; weight brought to nothing |
| 6 | *He whose scales weigh heavy* | **Weight comes back — but of another kind** |
| 7 | A contented life | The result of weight |
| 8 | *He whose scales weigh light* | Lightness turns into a judgement |
| 9 | Hāwiya | The result of lightness: **falling** |
| 10-11 | A blazing fire | — |

**The work the sūra does has three steps:**

**First step — the world is emptied of its weight (4-5).** The sūra takes the two ends of the scale of weight and makes them the same. The moth was light already; the mountain becomes light too. On that day there is no longer any such thing as heavy.

**Second step — weight is defined anew (6, 8).** At the very moment when everything has become light, the balance is set up. And what the balance weighs is none of the things that lost their weight in the fourth and fifth verses.

**Third step — the price of lightness is stated (9).** What is light does not rise; it falls.

### The question the sūra does not ask but makes one ask

*On a day when even the mountains have no weight left, what can weigh heavy?*

The sūra neither asks this nor answers it. But it brings the question into being — because it sets up the balance **after** it has shown that weight is gone. Had the order been the other way round (first the balance, then the similes) the question would not have arisen.

The Qurʾān gives the answer elsewhere, and there too there is a similar reversal:

*"Those are the ones who disbelieved in the signs of their Lord and in the meeting with Him; so their works have come to nothing, and on the day of resurrection **We shall set up no weighing for them**."* (al-Kahf 18/105)

This verse is the exact counterpart of the sūra before us: for a life **not to be weighed at all** is heavier than being weighed and coming out light. That a balance is set up at all is itself a statement of worth.

And al-Anbiyāʾ 21/47, which we recorded in the section on al-Zalzala, comes into play again here: the balance and the mote are in the same sentence. **The measure has no lower limit; but what kind of thing enters the measure is a separate matter.**

### The logic by which the two similes were chosen

One last observation. In likening people and mountains the sūra **does not turn either of them into its own opposite**; it turns them both into the same third thing: **matter left to the wind.**

The moth flies, but its flight is by its own will. A moth blown about is not flying; it is being carried.
The mountain stands, but its standing is by its own mass. Carded wool is not standing; it is being carried.

So what the two similes have in common is not lightness — it is **having lost one's own movement.** Neither can what flies fly, nor can what stands stand. Both are now objects carried by something else.

Joined with the metaphor of the balance, this gives the following picture: **what cannot carry itself is weighed.** To be weighed is for one who cannot carry his own weight to be left to another's measure.

I record this as my own reading.

## The link with al-Takāthur — counting and weighing

In the order of the muṣḥaf al-Qāriʿa comes immediately before al-Takāthur, and there is a direct link of subject between the two sūras. Drawing this link clarifies both sūras.

In the section on al-Takāthur the core of that sūra was established thus: *takāthur* is on the *tafāʿul* pattern and conveys **reciprocity**; what is criticised is not accumulation itself but **accumulation becoming relational**. And in the reciprocal pattern there is no finishing line, because the measure is moving.

Now let us compare the ways of measuring in the two sūras:

| | **Al-Takāthur (102)** | **Al-Qāriʿa (101)** |
|---|---|---|
| **The operation** | Counting (*takāthur*, *akthar*) | Weighing (*mawāzīn*) |
| **The kind of measure** | Number — the units are counted as equal | Weight — the units are not equal |
| **The reference** | Relative: by what another has | Absolute: by its own weight |
| **An end** | None — the measure is moving | Yes — the weighing is done once |
| **Who performs it** | The person counts for himself | It is weighed for the person (passive) |
| **What it leaves out of account** | The worth of what is counted | — |
| **The close of the sūra** | *"You will be asked"* | *"A blazing fire"* |

**The most critical row is the third.**

Counting is by its nature **comparative**. The meaning of a number appears beside another number. This is exactly the sickness Sūrat al-Takāthur diagnosed: worth being born of comparison.

Weighing, on the other hand, is **without comparison**. The weight of a thing is independent of the weight of anything else. The balance asks what is in your pan; not what is in your neighbour's.

And this has a consequence: **counting goes on for ever, weighing is done once.** In Sūrat al-Takāthur what ended the race was not satisfaction but death (*ḥattā zurtumu'l-maqābir*). In al-Qāriʿa the operation happens once, and its result is announced in two words.

The second difference is worth recording too: in al-Takāthur the one who counts is **the person himself**; in al-Qāriʿa the person weighed is not asked what he will do. The "fall from subject to object" recorded in the section on al-Takāthur stands here in completed form.

**I record this as an observation about the arrangement of the muṣḥaf**; it is not a claim about the order of revelation.

## The sequence of three sūras — al-Zalzala, al-ʿĀdiyāt, al-Qāriʿa

In the order of the muṣḥaf sūras 99, 100 and 101 come one after another, and all three are connected with the scene of the resurrection. I take up here the sequence these three build.

**First a record:** this is **an observation about the arrangement of the muṣḥaf, not about the order of revelation.** The mutual relation of the sūras (*tanāsub al-suwar*) is a recognised subject in exegesis, but some of the scholars have preferred to be cautious against the forcing of such links. The table below must be read with that record in mind. The same record was entered for the trio al-Takāthur–al-ʿAṣr–al-Humaza.

### The table of the three sūras

| | **Al-Zalzala (99)** | **Al-ʿĀdiyāt (100)** | **Al-Qāriʿa (101)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opening** | A condition (*idhā*) — a scene | An oath — a scene | A name — a single word |
| **The central verb** | **to see** (*yarah*) | **to sift out** (*ḥuṣṣila*) | **to weigh** (*mawāzīn*) |
| **What happens to the graves** | The earth **brings out** its burdens | What is in the graves **is turned upside down** | (does not occur) |
| **The object of the reckoning** | Deeds — outward | What is in the breasts — inward | What is weighed — the total |
| **The unit of measure** | **The weight of a mote** | (none) | **The balance** |
| **Man's condition** | *Ashtāt* — scattered | *Kanūd* — yielding nothing | *Farāsh* — blown about |

### The logic of the sequence

When the three sūras are laid one on top of another, an order of operations emerges:

**First step — bringing to light (al-Zalzala).** The earth is shaken, brings out what is within it, tells what has happened. People come out scattered and **their deeds are shown to them.** At this stage there is as yet no judgement; there is only becoming visible. And a unit of measure is laid down: the weight of a mote.

**Second step — sifting (al-ʿĀdiyāt).** What is in the grave is brought out (*buʿthira*) and what is in the breast is sifted out (*ḥuṣṣila*). Al-Zalzala contented itself with the deeds being seen; al-ʿĀdiyāt **goes inside.** The threshing floor is winnowed: what is light flies off, what is heavy stays. At this stage too there is as yet no judgement; there is a separating.

**Third step — weighing (al-Qāriʿa).** What has been sifted is weighed and judgement is given. *Ammā… wa-ammā…* — the sūra's pattern is the pattern that divides in two. For the first time in the three sūras a **distinction** is made.

That is: **becoming visible → being sifted → being weighed.** All three stand like successive stages of one and the same operation.

And note: **the unit of measure and the instrument of measure are in different sūras.** Al-Zalzala gives the unit (*mithqāla dharra*); al-Qāriʿa gives the instrument (*mawāzīn*). Al-Anbiyāʾ 21/47 joins the two in a single sentence: *"We shall set up the scales of justice; no one will be wronged by so much as a mote."*

### One word, two senses

One last observation, and I record this entirely as my own reading.

The word **خَيْر** occurs in these two neighbouring sūras in two different senses:

- **Al-Zalzala 99/7:** *"Whoever does a mote's weight of **khayr**…"* — here *khayr* is **good**.
- **Al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/8:** *"He is violent in his love of **khayr**"* — here *khayr* is **wealth**.

The same word, in two consecutive sūras: the one the thing put into the pan of the hereafter, the other the thing held on to in this world.

These are the word's two settled senses in Arabic and both uses are apt; I claim no play on words. I record only this: the language itself has shown, in two neighbouring sūras, that what a person calls "khayr" can go to two different places.

## What this says to the present

**First: apparent weight and weighed weight are not the same thing.**

This is the most practical measure the sūra gives. The mountain is the visual counterpart of weight: you look at it and you see that it is heavy. And the sūra says that this mountain weighs nothing.

In a life too there are "mountains" of this kind: bulky things, visible to the eye, giving an impression of weight. The size of the work done, the scale of the structure built, the number of years spent, the breadth of the effect left on those around. These **look** heavy.

What the sūra says is that this appearance does not guarantee the result of the measurement. And the reverse as well: that what looks light may come out heavy. The measure recorded in the hadith was exactly this — light on the tongue, heavy in the balance.

The practical consequence of this is not a rule but a precaution: **not to estimate the weight of a thing by looking at how much room it takes up.**

**Second: the difference between counting and weighing.**

The link drawn with al-Takāthur is directly serviceable today.

Almost all the measuring instruments of modern life are instruments of **counting**: income, followers, publications, hours, steps, sales, points. What these instruments have in common is that they count the units as equal — an hour is an hour, a follower is a follower.

Weighing, by contrast, does not count the units as equal. Two things of the same number may be of different weight; one may even be heavy and the other zero.

The everyday counterpart of this distinction is as follows: when a life is assessed by numbers, every unit occupies the same place, and so the total can be increased. When it is assessed by weight the total cannot be increased; only **what is placed on the scale** changes.

And counting has one further trap: what can be counted throws everything that cannot be counted into shadow. Sūrat al-Takāthur gave the subject of the questioning as **the favour received** — health, time, safety, means; that is, items that cannot be counted. What comes out when the two sūras are read together is this: **at the end of a life spent on things that are counted, the things that were not counted are weighed.**

**Third: "making light of".**

The second sense in the root of *khaffat* — to count light, not to take seriously — is present in English in the same form: we say we "make light of" a thing.

What a person makes light of can be read from his conduct: which task he leaves to the last minute, which word he says without weighing it, which relationship he assumes will keep going by itself, which right he counts as "small".

Sūrat al-Zalzala had already closed this door: what is of a mote's weight enters the reckoning too. Al-Qāriʿa says it in the language of the balance: **what is made light of becomes the thing that weighs light** — because what is put on the scale is the weight given to it.

**Fourth: two categories, but no list.**

The sūra divides mankind in two and leaves no third place between. At first sight this is a hard division.

But one must notice that the sūra **does not say who is where.** The criterion is given, no name is given; not even the name of a group is given. This accords with the principle this commentary has recorded from the beginning, and it has a practical consequence: **making the division is not someone else's business.**

A judgement as to which pan a person, a community or a group is in is not an authority the sūra confers. The sūra describes the balance; it does not do the weighing, and it does not have it done.

**Fifth: being without refuge.**

The expression *"his mother is Hāwiya"* builds the concept of taking refuge from the other side.

Man has a structural need, and it was treated from different sides in the sections on al-Falaq, al-Nās and al-Ikhlāṣ in this commentary: a person cannot stand without taking refuge somewhere, without turning towards something. What was recorded in the discussion of *al-Ṣamad* was this: everyone turns towards something; the question is what the thing is that you turn to.

Al-Qāriʿa shows the consequence of this need. A person's refuge is the very thing he turns towards. If you have turned towards something for a lifetime, that thing is your "mother" — the place you will return to.

And the harshness of the verse lies here: the person is not **thrown** somewhere; he **arrives** at the place he belongs to.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

- Whether the word *farāsh* is to be rendered "butterfly" or "moth" in English is a matter of translation preference; the Arabic word covers both, and no preference was imposed.
- The lexicographers differ over whether the word *ʿihn* means "dyed wool" or only "wool"; the widespread note "dyed" was taken as the basis, but the disagreement was stated. The reading built on colour rests on that note and was presented as **my own reading**.
- The three views about *mawāzīn* coming in the plural were given in a table; none of them was presented as certainly correct.
- The observation about the singular–plural distribution of *mīzān* (the measure of this world singular, the measure of the hereafter plural) **was not presented as a rule**; it was stated openly that I could not verify whether it has exceptions.
- What it is that is weighed (deeds / records / the person himself) is disputed; no preference was stated.
- Whether the weighing is a real operation or a description of justice is a debate of theology; **no side was taken.**
- Three explanations were given of the grammatical analysis of the word *rāḍiya*; none of them was presented as certainly correct.
- The three explanations of the expression *"his mother is Hāwiya"* were given in a table. The first view was preferred, its ground was written out, and it was recorded that it is not binding.
- On the reading of the *hāʾ* of pause in verse 10 in continuous recitation a difference is transmitted among the imams of the readings; since I cannot give with certainty which imam held which preference, **no names were written.**
- Whether the root letters ح-م-ي gather the senses "heat" and "protection" under a single root is disputed among the lexicographers; **no meaning was drawn from this sharing of sound.**
- For the hadith "There are two words light on the tongue, heavy in the balance", al-Bukhārī and Muslim were recorded.
- The readings that the backbone of the sūra is the theme of "lightness", that the two similes prepare the balance, that the physical direction of the balance is reversed, and that what the two similes have in common is "having lost one's own movement" **are mine**; they were not presented as transmitted. The textual data they rest on (the roots of the words, the ordering of the verses) were shown one by one.
- The fit between the sound and the meaning of the word *qāriʿa* was recorded, but **it was not presented as a proof of miracle**; it was stated that the fit between sound and meaning is a phenomenon found in every language.
- That the word *farāsh* carries an association with fire was recorded, but it **was not claimed** that the sūra intended this.
- The links drawn with the three sūras (99-100-101) and with al-Takāthur **are observations about the arrangement of the muṣḥaf**; they are not claims about the order of revelation. The shared roots in the table of verbal links are verifiable data; the claim that they form an order has been presented as an observation.
- The observation that the word *khayr* occurs in two different senses in al-Zalzala and al-ʿĀdiyāt is mine; it was not presented as a claim of a play on words.
