# 61. Sūrat al-Ṣaff

Fourteen verses. Medinan. A short sūra, but inside it stands one of the sharpest sentences the Qurʾān speaks about morality — and that sentence comes in the form of a question.

The whole matter of the sūra is a single gap: **the distance between what is said and what is done.** The sūra first opens this distance with a question (2), then puts a name to it (3), then shows what it looks like once closed (4), then gives two examples from history (5-6), then describes the state of the other side within the same gap (7-9), and finally presents the price of closing the gap as an offer of trade (10-14).

## What the sūra does

**The opening (1):** Everything that exists declares God free of all imperfection. This is the ground for the rest of the sūra: nothing in the universe says anything other than what it is.

**The heart (2-4):** And then man comes. *"Why do you say what you do not do?"* Then one of the heaviest words of feeling in the Qurʾān is given to this behaviour: **maqt**. And immediately after, what the reverse looks like: those who hold the rank like a structure sealed with lead.

**Two examples (5-6):** Moses (Mūsā) is hurt by his own people — *though they know*. Jesus (ʿĪsā) gives good news of the one coming after him, and it is called *manifest sorcery*. Two prophets, two communities, the same gap: the distance between what is known and what is done.

**The other side (7-9):** Those who manufacture fabrications, and those who try to put out a light with their mouths.

**The offer (10-14):** An offer of trade, a price, two returns, and a command: *"Be the helpers of God."*

The skeleton of the sūra is pinned in place by two interrogative words. In the second verse God asks the believers: **لِمَ تَقُولُونَ** — "why do you say?" In the fifth verse Moses asks his people: **لِمَ تُؤْذُونَنِى** — "why do you hurt me?" The same pattern, the same interrogative particle, two different addressees. The sūra casts the example from history that it gives its addressees in the very same form as the question it puts to them.

## The name of the sūra

The name comes from the word **صَفًّا** (*ṣaffan* — in rank) in the fourth verse. It is transmitted that the sūra is also called **al-Ḥawāriyyūn** and **al-Ṣāff**; the first from the disciples in the last verse, the second from the same root.

The choice of name is meaningful: the most discussed place in the sūra is the second and third verses, but the name is taken from the fourth. That is, the tradition named the sūra not by its **diagnosis** but by the **opposite** of the diagnosis.

## Medinan

There is no disagreement that the sūra is Medinan. Fighting, standing in rank, help and the expectation of victory are matters belonging to the Medinan period.

**The occasion of revelation.** Several different accounts are transmitted as the occasion of revelation of this sūra, and of verses 2-3 in particular. Two of them stand out:

- It is said to have come down when certain people gave exaggerated accounts of what they had done in battle.
- It is said to have come down when some of the Companions wished to know which deed God loves most and said "had we known, we would do it", and then, when that deed was made known, some of them held back.

**I adopt no preference between these accounts, and I present none of them as the definite occasion of revelation.** Both are widely transmitted; I issue no ruling on their degrees of soundness. The verse itself already shows its context: a community said something and did not do what that something required.

This caveat must also be entered: the address is **يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟**. That is, the verse calls out not to the hypocrites but to the believers. This directly affects how the sūra is read — we shall dwell on it below.

## 61/1 — سَبَّحَ لِلَّهِ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ

*Sabbaḥa lillāhi mā fi'l-samāwāti wa mā fi'l-arḍ, wa huwa'l-ʿAzīzu'l-Ḥakīm*

"Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth has declared God free of all imperfection. He is al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm."

### سَبَّحَ — tasbīḥ

Root **س-ب-ح**. And the concrete sense of the root is the key to every use of the word: **to move swiftly and without obstruction in water or in air — to swim.**

- **سَبَحَ فِى ٱلْمَآءِ** — he swam in the water.
- **ٱلسَّابِحَات** — those that swim swiftly (al-Nāziʿāt 79/3).
- **سَبْح** — as was treated in the section on al-Muzzammil (73/7: *sabḥan ṭawīlā*), the long current of the daytime that takes a person into itself and carries him along.
- The Qurʾān uses the same root of the heavenly bodies: *"each one swims in an orbit (yasbaḥūn)"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/33, Yā Sīn 36/40).

So what lies between "to swim" and "to declare God free of all deficiency"?

The classical explanation is this: *tasbīḥ*, in the word's own logic, is a verb of **removal**. What swims departs swiftly from where it was. The one who performs *tasbīḥ* likewise **carries God far away** from any deficiency that might be conceived of Him. *Tasbīḥ*, then, is not a praise; it is a **purification**. Accordingly the expression *subḥānallāh* means "I declare God free of all deficiency" — not "I commend God".

This distinction also shows the difference between *ḥamd* and *tasbīḥ*. As was seen in the section on al-Fātiḥa, *ḥamd* is a positive word turned towards beauty. *Tasbīḥ* works by way of negation: it says what is not the case. The two often come side by side in the Qurʾān (*subḥānallāhi wa bi-ḥamdih*) and together they set up a complete frame: far from deficiencies (*tasbīḥ*) and the owner of all beauties (*ḥamd*).

### The verb form: the perfect

The verb is **سَبَّحَ** — the perfect (*māḍī*, past tense). "It declared / it has declared God free of all imperfection."

This is a striking choice, because *tasbīḥ* is an ongoing business and the imperfect (*muḍāriʿ*) would have been expected. Indeed the Qurʾān does use the imperfect with this root in other openings.

The sūras that open with this root form a group in the Qurʾān, and in the tradition they are called *al-musabbiḥāt*. The distribution of the verb forms is as follows:

| Sūra | Opening | Form |
|---|---|---|
| al-Isrāʾ 17/1 | **سُبْحَٰنَ** | Verbal noun |
| al-Ḥadīd 57/1 | **سَبَّحَ** | Perfect |
| al-Ḥashr 59/1 | **سَبَّحَ** | Perfect |
| **al-Ṣaff 61/1** | **سَبَّحَ** | **Perfect** |
| al-Jumuʿa 62/1 | **يُسَبِّحُ** | Imperfect |
| al-Taghābun 64/1 | **يُسَبِّحُ** | Imperfect |
| al-Aʿlā 87/1 | **سَبِّحِ** | Imperative |

The same verb, in four different forms. This distribution is no coincidence; each form states a direction. The perfect: done, completed, entered on the record. The imperfect: it is going on. The imperative: it is asked of you.

The function of the perfect here opens up when it is considered together with the rest of the sūra — and this is a reading, not a transmitted report: the sūra is about to address a community that **does not do what it says**. In the opening, by contrast, it is recorded that the whole of existence has long since done its own work. Nothing in the universe says anything other than what it is; nothing defers its promise. And then, in the second verse, man comes.

This reading is not binding; but that the two verses stand side by side is worth noting.

### مَا — not *man*

The verse says **مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ**. In Arabic *mā* is used of beings without reason and of things in general, *man* of those who possess reason.

Here *mā* has been chosen. The scope is therefore not limited to those who possess reason; **everything** is meant. The Qurʾān elsewhere generalises this *tasbīḥ* explicitly: *"There is nothing that does not declare Him free of all imperfection together with His praise; but you do not understand their tasbīḥ"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/44).

### ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْحَكِيمُ — and an adjacency

The two names were analysed in the section on al-Mumtaḥana, at 60/5: *al-ʿAzīz* — its root ع-ز-ز, ground that is hard and difficult of access; *al-Ḥakīm* — its root ح-ك-م, to bridle, to hold back.

And a detail worth noting: **the fifth verse of al-Mumtaḥana, the sūra immediately preceding it in the order of the muṣḥaf, also ended with exactly these two names** (*innaka anta'l-ʿAzīzu'l-Ḥakīm*). Two neighbouring sūras share the same pair of names.

That the two names stand at the opening of this sūra agrees with its content. *al-ʿAzīz*: a rank is to be formed, a fight is to be mentioned, a superiority is to be promised — the source of the power is stated at the outset. *al-Ḥakīm*: but that power is not used without measure; the promise that will come at the end of the sūra is not arbitrary either.

## 61/2-3 — لِمَ تَقُولُونَ مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ ۝ كَبُرَ مَقْتًا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ أَن تَقُولُوا۟ مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū lima taqūlūna mā lā tafʿalūn ۝ Kabura maqtan ʿindallāhi an taqūlū mā lā tafʿalūn*

"O you who believe! Why do you say what you will not do? That you say what you will not do is a cause of great anger/revulsion in the sight of God."

**This is the heart of the sūra.** And it is one of the shortest and most comprehensive sentences the Qurʾān frames on the subject of morality. It needs to be dwelt on in detail.

### First, the addressee: to whom is this verse spoken?

The address is plain: **يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟** — "O you who believe".

This is the first thing that determines how the verse is read. The verse is not about the hypocrites. When the Qurʾān describes the hypocrites it uses a different language and mentions them in the third person (*"and among people there are those who…"*, al-Baqara 2/8). Here, by contrast, the people of faith are addressed directly, in the second person.

The consequence is this: **not doing what one says is not a state outside faith.** It is a state into which the believer too may fall; and it is precisely for this reason that the verse uses so harsh a word. The harshness is a harshness directed not at the one outside but at the one inside.

### Word order: why a question?

The verse could have begun with a sentence of ruling: "Do not say what you do not do." The prohibitive form could have been used. It is not used.

**لِمَ** — its origin is *li-mā*; the alif at the end of the interrogative *mā* drops when a preposition comes before it. Its meaning: "for what, why?"

In Arabic this is called a question of *tawbīkh* (reproach): it does not ask for information, it seats the addressee opposite his own deed. And its force lies exactly there. A prohibition comes from outside; a question is answered from inside. When "do not do it" is said, the addressee faces a command; when "why do you do it" is said, he is forced to look for his own justification — and most of the time he finds none that can be defended.

The verse forces the addressee not into defence but into **looking at himself**.

### Why two verses? The function of the repetition

The expression *mā lā tafʿalūn* occurs in two consecutive verses, in exactly the same wording. In the Qurʾān a repetition this close always does a piece of work.

The function is this:

| | **61/2** | **61/3** |
|---|---|---|
| Form | **Question** (*lima taqūlūna*) | **Statement / ruling** (*kabura maqtan*) |
| Addressee | Second person (you) | The deed itself (*an taqūlū*) |
| What it does | Places the addressee opposite his deed | Assigns a **value** to the deed |
| Point of reference | The person's own conscience | **عِندَ ٱللَّهِ** — the sight of God |

That is: first the question is asked, then the ruling is laid down. And as the ruling is laid down the addressee changes: the subject is no longer "you" but **the deed itself**. The sentence separates from the person and passes over to the behaviour.

This is a meaningful structure in terms of the method (`STYLE.md`) as well: the ruling is given not to the person but to the deed.

### مَقْت — maqt

The most heavily loaded word in the sūra.

Root **م-ق-ت**. The word is rendered "hatred", but its place in Arabic is narrower and heavier.

*Bughḍ* (ب-غ-ض) is a general dislike; it may have no cause, it may be arbitrary, it may be personal. *Maqt*, by contrast, is **the revulsion felt on account of an ugly deed**: it has a cause, and its cause is that deed itself. The English word "revulsion" comes closer to it than "hatred" — because revulsion arises from the quality of the thing found revolting, whereas one may hate for no reason at all.

And now comes the word's use in the Arabic of the Jāhiliyya, and it is this above all that shows the weight of the verse.

**نِكَاحُ ٱلْمَقْت — "the marriage of *maqt*".** It is transmitted that in the period of the Jāhiliyya this name was given to a man's marrying his father's wife after his father's death; and it is said that a separate name was used for the child born of such a marriage. That is, *maqt* was the name of a deed that that society found **revolting** even by its own moral measure.

And when the Qurʾān forbids this practice it uses exactly this word:

> *"Do not marry the women your fathers married — save what is already past. Truly that is an indecency, a **maqt**, and an evil way."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/22)

**The word occurs rarely in the Qurʾān, and the places where it occurs are striking.** In one place it is used of this marriage, to which the Jāhiliyya itself had given the name. In another, in a word spoken to those who deny in the hereafter: *"God's maqt is greater than your maqt towards yourselves"* (Ghāfir 40/10). And here.

**And in one further place — in exactly the same wording as the sentence of this verse.** In Sūrat Ghāfir it occurs thus:

> *"Those who dispute about the verses of God without any authority having come to them… **كَبُرَ مَقْتًا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ وَعِندَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟**"* (Ghāfir 40/35)

The same sentence, with a single difference: there **وَعِندَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟** ("and in the sight of those who believe") has been added.

Comparing the two occurrences shows how narrowly the caveat in al-Ṣaff is kept:

| | **Ghāfir 40/35** | **al-Ṣaff 61/3** |
|---|---|---|
| The deed | Disputing without authority | Saying and not doing |
| In whose sight | **In the sight of God and in the sight of those who believe** | **In the sight of God alone** |

The difference is meaningful. One who disputes without authority is seen by those who believe as well and is censured in their sight too — the deed is out in the open. Not doing what one says, by contrast, most often goes unnoticed by people, is excused, is even looked on kindly. For this reason the caveat in al-Ṣaff is placed at **a single address**: *ʿindallāh*.

That is, the sentence implies this: you cannot learn the weight of this deed from those around you. The measure is not there.

So this is what the verse does: **it names the failure to do what one says with the word the language reserves for the deed it finds most revolting.**

It is worth thinking about what this means. Not doing what one says is, socially, a light fault. No one is taken to court for it; most of the time it is not even noticed; it is passed over with "he meant well, but it did not come off". The verse rejects this lightness and places the deed beside the things people count heaviest.

### كَبُرَ مَقْتًا — word order

The construction of the sentence is this: **كَبُرَ** (it grew great / how great it is) + **مَقْتًا** (the specifier, *tamyīz*) + **عِندَ ٱللَّهِ** (adverbial) + **أَن تَقُولُوا۟ مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ** (the real subject).

Three things stand out:

**First: the verb *kabura* here expresses *taʿajjub* (astonishment and horror).** In Arabic this form expresses the greatness of a thing with amazement. The Qurʾān uses the same verb this way elsewhere: *"How great a word it is that comes out of their mouths!"* (al-Kahf 18/5).

**Second: *maqtan* comes as a specifier.** The specifier is the element that removes an ambiguity in a sentence: when "how great it is" is said, the question "in what respect?" arises, and the specifier answers it — "in respect of revulsion". So the sentence works like this: this thing is great — great in what? — **in its power to revolt.**

**Third, and most important: the real subject has been thrown to the end of the sentence.** The normal order could have been "*an taqūlū mā lā tafʿalūna kabura maqtan*". The verse states the ruling first, and only then what it is a ruling on. In Arabic this is a technique that builds tension: while the addressee waits, asking "what is so great?", the answer comes.

The caveat **عِندَ ٱللَّهِ** must not be passed over either. The place of assessment is specified: **in the sight of God.** That is, among people this behaviour may be looked on kindly, may be counted excusable, may even go unnoticed; the measure is not there.

### Comparison with al-Baqara 2/44

This verse is constantly mentioned together with another verse in Sūrat al-Baqara. At al-Baqara 2/44 it was said:

> *"Do you enjoin righteousness upon people and forget yourselves, while you recite the Book? Will you not use your reason?"*

Two things were analysed in that section: that the verb is **"to forget"** (not "you do not do"), and that the verse closes with the root of *ʿaql* (to bind).

The two do not say the same thing. Seeing their differences explains both better:

| | **al-Baqara 2/44** | **al-Ṣaff 61/2-3** |
|---|---|---|
| The verb | **تَنسَوْنَ** — you forget | **تَقُولُونَ مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ** — you say and do not do |
| Where the fault lies | **In awareness**: not calling to mind that the addressee is oneself too | **In the will**: the mismatch between what is said and what is done |
| Awareness | The person is not aware | The person may be aware |
| Addressee | The Children of Israel (the People of the Book) | **O you who believe** |
| Closing | **أَفَلَا تَعْقِلُونَ** — reason (a bond) | **كَبُرَ مَقْتًا عِندَ ٱللَّهِ** — *maqt* (revulsion) |
| Direction of the address | To understanding | To the ruling |

**The most important difference is in the diagnosis.** What is described in al-Baqara is a **loss of attention**: while the person expounds the truth, he counts the expounding itself as having done it; it does not occur to him that he is an addressee too. As was written in that section: "expounding takes the place of doing."

What is described in al-Ṣaff is not a loss of attention but a **divergence**: declaration and deed have gone in two different directions. The person has not forgotten what he said; he said it and did not do it.

**The second important difference is in the closings.** al-Baqara calls out to reason: *a-fa-lā taʿqilūn* — with a word whose root is "to bind", it says that the knowledge does not bind the person. al-Ṣaff, by contrast, goes not to reason but to **the ruling**: this thing is revolting in the sight of God.

The two addresses touch two different places. One tries to set understanding in motion; the other lays down a value judgement where understanding does not suffice.

### The link with al-Baqara 2/177: ṣidq

And now let us look at the positive side. al-Baqara 2/177, after giving the long definition of *birr* (righteousness), closed thus:

> **أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ صَدَقُوا۟ وَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْمُتَّقُونَ**
> *"These are the ones who have been true; and these are the ones who are mindful of God."*

As was recorded in that section, **صِدْق** here carries a particular sense: **one whose saying and whose doing agree.**

al-Ṣaff 61/2-3 is exactly the negative of this definition. Where al-Baqara enumerates *birr* and at the end says "these are the ones who *ṣadaqū*", al-Ṣaff names the reverse of the same measure.

The two together form a pair:

| | **al-Baqara 2/177** | **al-Ṣaff 61/2-3** |
|---|---|---|
| The measure | **صَدَقُوا۟** — one whose saying and doing agree | **مَا لَا تَفْعَلُونَ** — one whose saying and doing diverge |
| Direction | Definition (positive) | Diagnosis (negative) |
| Result | *the muttaqūn* | *maqt* |

And what is striking is this: at al-Baqara 2/177 *ṣidq* comes as the **result** of a long list of righteousness — after faith, giving of wealth, prayer, alms, keeping the covenant and patience have been enumerated, it is said "these are the ones who *ṣadaqū*". *Ṣidq*, then, is not one of the items on the list; it is **the name for the whole of the list having been carried out.**

al-Ṣaff says this from the reverse side: however well the list is enumerated, if it is not done, what comes out is *maqt*.

### The limit of the verse: what it does not say

Used carelessly, this verse breaks down in two directions. Both must be recorded.

**Wrong use 1: "Never say what you cannot do; therefore do not enjoin righteousness."**

This cannot be drawn from the verse — because the same Qurʾān lays down the enjoining of righteousness as an obligation. And at al-Baqara 2/44 too, what was criticised was not the enjoining itself but the forgetting of oneself. The verse's solution is not "stop saying" but **"start doing"**.

This distinction matters in practice. A person's looking at his own fault and falling silent serves no purpose but to multiply that fault; for then neither has anything been said nor has anything been done.

**Wrong use 2: "This verse is used to silence other people."**

This is the most widespread abuse of the verse: invalidating what someone says by asking him "do you do it yourself?" This is an invalid move logically as well — the condition of the speaker does not change the truth of what is spoken.

And the word order of the verse does not permit this use. The address is **in the second person**: "you". That is, the verse does not give the reader a measure about someone else; it puts a question to the reader himself. The same caveat was entered in the section on al-Baqara 2/44: the structure of the verse is turned towards **the one reading it**.

**What the verse actually establishes** is this: a word is an undertaking. To say a thing generates the obligation to do it. The verse does not devalue speech; on the contrary, it makes speech **binding**.

## 61/4 — إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلَّذِينَ يُقَٰتِلُونَ فِى سَبِيلِهِۦ صَفًّا كَأَنَّهُم بُنْيَٰنٌ مَّرْصُوصٌ

*Inna'llāha yuḥibbu'lladhīna yuqātilūna fī sabīlihi ṣaffan ka-annahum bunyānun marṣūṣ*

"Truly God loves those who fight in His way in rank, as though they were a structure sealed with lead."

### Word order: a sentence of love coming after two verses

Note the sequence:

- **Verse 3:** *كَبُرَ مَقْتًا* — a great **revulsion** in the sight of God.
- **Verse 4:** *إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ* — God **loves**.

Two words of feeling, one after the other, in exact opposition. Immediately after saying what it is revolted by, the Qurʾān says what it loves.

This is the most important structural move in the sūra. When a fault has been diagnosed there are two roads: to describe the fault in greater detail, or to show what the faultless state looks like. The sūra chooses the second — and it does so not as a piece of advice but as an **image**.

### صَفّ — the rank

Root **ص-ف-ف**. Its concrete sense: **to set several things side by side on a straight line, in one alignment.**

Derivatives:

- **مَصْفُوف** — set in rows. The Qurʾān uses it in describing the garden: *"cushions set in rows"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/15).
- **ٱلصَّٰٓفَّٰت** — those drawn up rank upon rank (al-Ṣāffāt 37/1).
- And of birds: *"Have they not seen the birds above them, spreading their wings out (ṣāffāt) and folding them?"* (al-Mulk 67/19). A bird's spreading its wing and holding it **steady** is named with this root too.

At the centre of the root are two things: **alignment** and **being side by side**. A rank is not a random crowd; everyone's place is fixed and everyone stands on the same line.

The word **صَفًّا** stands in the sentence as a circumstantial accusative: "those who fight in rank". What is praised, then, is not fighting but fighting **in rank**. The caveat is placed not on the deed but on the form of the deed.

The caveat **فِى سَبِيلِهِۦ** must not be passed over either: "in His way". Not every fight.

### بُنْيَٰنٌ مَّرْصُوصٌ — a structure sealed with lead

**بُنْيَان** — root ب-ن-ي: to build, to construct. *Bunyān* is a structure that has been built. And from the same root comes **ٱبْن** (son); lineage too has been thought of as a structure.

**مَّرْصُوص** — root ر-ص-ص. And the sense of the root: **to fasten things tightly to one another, to join them so that no gap is left between them.**

In the classical commentaries, when this word is explained, reference is made to **رَصَاص** (lead) from the same root, and *marṣūṣ* is explained as "sealed with lead". The technique behind this is real: in stone buildings, to bind the blocks to one another, sockets were cut into the stones, metal clamps were set between them, and **molten lead** was poured over them. As the lead cooled it filled the gaps; the two stones behaved as a single piece. This technique was widespread in the buildings of the ancient and of the Islamic period.

The image the word carries is for this reason very concrete: **a structure with no gap left between its parts.**

### What exactly the comparison compares

Care is needed here, because the comparison is not point for point, and if it is taken point for point it is misunderstood.

A building is **motionless**. A battle rank, by contrast, moves. The aspect being compared, therefore, cannot be motionlessness.

The aspect being compared is **the absence of gaps**. And this has three consequences:

**First: a gap is the point at which a structure collapses.** In a wall, the gap between the stones is the place where the load cannot be transferred; the structure breaks there. It is the same in a battle rank: the weakest place in the line is the place where someone does not hold his position.

**Second: no single part can stand on its own.** In a building each stone is in its own place, but none of them is a building by itself. The load is shared. The comparison describes not individual heroism but **mutual dependence**.

**Third: what seals them together is not the stones themselves.** The lead comes from outside and fills the gap. This detail of the comparison is worth dwelling on: what makes a community a community is not the qualities of its members but the bond between them.

**كَأَنَّ** — "as though". The particle says that the comparison is not full identity. People are not stones; it is their standing that is like stone.

### Its relation to verses 2-3: one who does not keep his word cannot hold the rank

And now we come to the link that lights up the whole of the sūra.

Why does this comparison come immediately after the diagnosis of "not doing what one says"? The answer lies in a technical observation:

**In a battle rank each man stands *trusting* that the man beside him will hold his position.**

The rank is the physical form of mutual trust. A soldier flees because, if the man beside him flees, his flank will be left open; he holds if he knows the man beside him will hold. The strength of the rank, then, comes not from individual courage but from **the expectation that a word given will be kept**.

So then: **every point of a line built out of people whose declaration and deed do not agree is a candidate for a gap.** The fault in the second and third verses is not merely a moral deficiency; it is **the reason the structure of the fourth verse becomes impossible**.

And the words themselves establish this link. The meaning of *marṣūṣ* was "to leave no gap". The distance between word and deed is likewise a **gap**. In two verses the sūra speaks of a gap; in the third it praises the absence of gaps. Two levels of the same concept: the gap inside the person, and the gap in the rank.

I present this reading as my own inference; the verse does not establish this link explicitly. But that the two verses come one after the other, and that the words overlap this closely, is worth noting.

### A detail: the object of the love

Look once more at the construction of the sentence: **إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلَّذِينَ يُقَٰتِلُونَ** — the object of the love is not a deed but **those who do the deed**.

This is the same structure as the closing of al-Mumtaḥana 60/8: *"God loves those who deal justly"* — there too the object was a type of person. When the Qurʾān ties love not to the act but to the actor, it records the act as a **character**: not a piece of work done but a state one is in.

## 61/5 — وَإِذْ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِقَوْمِهِۦ يَٰقَوْمِ لِمَ تُؤْذُونَنِى وَقَد تَّعْلَمُونَ أَنِّى رَسُولُ ٱللَّهِ إِلَيْكُمْ فَلَمَّا زَاغُوٓا۟ أَزَاغَ ٱللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ وَٱللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلْفَٰسِقِينَ

*Wa idh qāla Mūsā li-qawmihi yā qawmi lima tuʾdhūnanī wa qad taʿlamūna annī rasūlullāhi ilaykum, fa-lammā zāghū azāgha'llāhu qulūbahum, wa'llāhu lā yahdi'l-qawma'l-fāsiqīn*

"And when Moses said to his people, 'O my people! Why do you hurt me, when you know that I am God's messenger to you?' Then, when they swerved, God made their hearts swerve. God does not guide the transgressing people."

### The same question, the same pattern

The most striking link of word order in the sūra is here.

- **61/2:** *"يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ **لِمَ** تَقُولُونَ…"*
- **61/5:** *"يَٰقَوْمِ **لِمَ** تُؤْذُونَنِى…"*

The same interrogative particle, the same pattern of reproach, the same structure: **address + لِمَ + imperfect verb.** In the one the asker is God and the addressees are the believers; in the other the asker is a prophet and the addressees are his own people.

This cannot be coincidence. The sūra gives the example from history to its addressees **cast in the same pattern** as the question it puts to them. The example, then, is presented not as a distant story but as an older form of the same question.

And this determines the function of the example as well: a people's hurting its prophet and a community's not doing what it says are, within the frame the sūra sets up, deeds of **the same family**. Both are things done in spite of a piece of knowledge.

### أَذًى — adhā

Root **أ-ذ-ي**. And the measure of the word is striking: **a discomfort that is slight but continuous.**

The Qurʾān does not use *adhā* for great calamities. The places where it does use it show this:

- The discomfort given by a thorn, an obstacle, on the road.
- A state of illness: *"whoever among you is ill or has an **adhā** on his head…"* (al-Baqara 2/196).
- And, strikingly: *"They ask you about menstruation. Say: it is an **adhā**"* (al-Baqara 2/222) — here too the word states not an illness or an impurity but a state of discomfort.

*Adhā*, then, is not deadly but **wearing**. It has continuity; it is not a great blow but something that comes drop by drop.

This makes Moses' complaint more intelligible. The complaint is not about a torture, an assassination, a rebellion. The scenes the Qurʾān transmits between Moses and his people fit this description: continual objection, continual excuse, "you and your Lord go and fight", the calf, "give us other food". None of them is deadly by itself; their sum is enough to wear out a prophet.

### وَقَد تَّعْلَمُونَ — "when you know"

This is the centre of gravity of the sentence.

This construction, beginning with **وَقَد**, is a **circumstantial clause**: it states the condition under which the deed takes place. And the condition it states is this: **knowingly.**

This attaches to a definition set up in two places in Sūrat al-Baqara:

**First, al-Baqara 2/6.** In that section the root ك-ف-ر was analysed: the sense of the root is **to cover** — the farmer is called *kāfir* because he covers the seed with soil; the night is called *kāfir* because it covers everything over. And the definition that came out of it was this: **kufr is not a failure to know; it is covering over what one knows.**

**Second, al-Baqara 2/89.** There the verse *"when there came to them what they recognised, they denied it"* (*fa-lammā jāʾahum mā ʿarafū kafarū bih*) was treated, and it was recorded thus: "There is knowing, there is recognising, there is no acceptance."

al-Ṣaff 61/5 is **the plainest expression** of this definition — because here the "knowing" is not an inference but the verse's own wording: *wa qad taʿlamūn*.

And there is a difference worth dwelling on: **the addressee at al-Baqara 2/89 was the one outside; the addressee here is Moses' own people.**

That is, "covering knowingly" is not an attitude belonging only to the other side. Those inside do it too. This looks to the same place as the second verse of the sūra: there too the address was *"O you who believe"*.

### زَاغُوا۟ / أَزَاغَ — the analysis of the root ز-ي-غ

And now the second half of the verse. This sentence was used as evidence in the discussion of the **seal** at al-Baqara 2/7; the analysis there was this: the seal is not an obstacle placed in front of a person's choice, but a locking that comes as the result of the choice. I do not repeat that discussion here.

The work to be done here is different: **to analyse the root fully.** For the root itself adds something to the analysis given there.

Root **ز-ي-غ**. Its meaning: **to deviate from the straight line, to incline, to go crooked.**

Concrete uses show the measure of the root:

- **زَاغَتِ ٱلشَّمْس** — the sun **inclined** from its zenith. That is, it left its highest point and leaned over. This is an everyday observation and is used as the description of the noon hour.
- **زَاغَ ٱلْبَصَر** — the eye slipped, it strayed from the target. The Qurʾān uses this in two places: *"The eye neither slipped (mā zāgha) nor overstepped"* (al-Najm 53/17) — here in the negative, to say that the gaze held exactly in its place. And: *"When the eyes slipped…"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/10) — the gaze losing its focus in a moment of fear.

**At the centre of the root is this: a departure from a line, a difference of angle.**

And this is the most important property of the word: ***zaygh* is not a sudden break but an *inclination*.** The sun does not fall from its zenith all at once; it departs by a small angle, and that angle grows with time. The eye does not break from the target all at once; it slips.

**This is what is added to the analysis at al-Baqara 2/7.** There it was shown that the locking is the result of the choice. Here the root gives one further detail: **the deviation that gives rise to the locking is small at the start.** It is not a break but a bending. And the bending grows as the distance lengthens — a small difference of angle produces, at the end of the road, a great remoteness.

The Qurʾān uses the same root very plainly in these two verses:

> *"Those in whose hearts is **a crookedness (zaygh)** pursue what is ambiguous in it, seeking discord and seeking to interpret it."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/7)

> *"Our Lord! Do not **make our hearts swerve (lā tuzigh)** after You have guided us."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/8)

The second verse is especially important: the same verb, in the same *ifʿāl* form, occurs as a **prayer**. That is, the Qurʾān records protection from this locking as something that may be asked for. If a door has closed, it is understood that it was open before it closed, and that it could be asked to stay open.

### The difference of form: زَاغُوا۟ → أَزَاغَ

Note the grammar of the verse. The same root, in two different forms:

| Verb | Form | Subject | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **زَاغُوا۟** | Triliteral (simple) | **They** | They went crooked, they deviated |
| **أَزَاغَ** | *Ifʿāl* (transitive) | **God** | He made crooked |

And the connector between them: **فَلَمَّا … أَزَاغَ** — *lammā* is an adverb of time meaning "when, at the time that", and in Arabic it establishes a relation of cause and effect.

So the structure of the sentence **fixes the order with certainty**: first their deed, then God's deed. The analysis carried out at al-Baqara 2/7 by way of this ordering is here made necessary by the grammar itself.

This is a striking detail: using the same root, the Qurʾān sets two verbs side by side and shows the relation between them by the difference of form. What man does is **to go crooked** (intransitive); what God does is **to make crooked** (transitive). The second verb takes the object of the first — that is, the effect is mounted upon the cause.

### ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلْفَٰسِقِينَ

**فَاسِق** — root ف-س-ق. And its concrete sense gives a very apt image: **to come out of one's shell.**

- **فَسَقَتِ ٱلرُّطَبَةُ** — the date came out of its skin, it split its skin.

From this comes *fisq*: to split the boundary of the order one is inside and come out of it. The *fāsiq* is the person who, while inside a structure, overflows out of it.

This root sense of the word overlaps with the context of the verse. What is at issue is not an enemy coming from outside but a community that has **come out from inside**. Moses' addressees are his own people.

And the choice of word here differs from *kāfir*. The *kāfir* is the one who covers; the *fāsiq* is the one who comes out. The two describe different deeds, and the verse chooses the second — because what is at issue is not covering over knowledge but overflowing outside a structure that is known.

**And this closing will be repeated two verses later:** 61/7 ends with *"wa'llāhu lā yahdi'l-qawma'ẓ-ẓālimīn"*. The same skeleton, a different attribute:

| Verse | Closing | Attribute | Root sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| 61/5 | *lā yahdi'l-qawma'l-**fāsiqīn*** | The *fāsiqūn* | Those who come out of their shell |
| 61/7 | *lā yahdi'l-qawma'ẓ-**ẓālimīn*** | The wrongdoers | Those who put a thing out of its place |

The two verses name two different groups (the people inside / those who fabricate) with two different words; but the sentence of ruling is the same.

## 61/6 — وَإِذْ قَالَ عِيسَى ٱبْنُ مَرْيَمَ يَٰبَنِىٓ إِسْرَٰٓءِيلَ إِنِّى رَسُولُ ٱللَّهِ إِلَيْكُم مُّصَدِّقًا لِّمَا بَيْنَ يَدَىَّ مِنَ ٱلتَّوْرَىٰةِ وَمُبَشِّرًۢا بِرَسُولٍ يَأْتِى مِنۢ بَعْدِى ٱسْمُهُۥٓ أَحْمَدُ فَلَمَّا جَآءَهُم بِٱلْبَيِّنَٰتِ قَالُوا۟ هَٰذَا سِحْرٌ مُّبِينٌ

*Wa idh qāla ʿĪsa'bnu Maryama yā banī Isrāʾīla innī rasūlullāhi ilaykum muṣaddiqan limā bayna yadayya mina't-Tawrāti wa mubashshiran bi-rasūlin yaʾtī min baʿdī ismuhu Aḥmad, fa-lammā jāʾahum bi'l-bayyināti qālū hādhā siḥrun mubīn*

"And when Jesus son of Mary (Maryam) said, 'O Children of Israel! I am God's messenger to you, confirming the Torah that came before me and giving good news of a messenger who will come after me, whose name is Aḥmad.' But when he came to them with clear proofs, they said, 'This is manifest sorcery.'"

### Two testimonies, two directions

Jesus defines himself by two attributes, and the two attributes look in two different directions:

- **مُصَدِّقًا لِّمَا بَيْنَ يَدَىَّ** — **backwards**: confirming what is before me.
- **وَمُبَشِّرًۢا بِرَسُولٍ يَأْتِى مِنۢ بَعْدِى** — **forwards**: giving good news of the one who comes after.

This is a definition of messengership as **a link in a chain**. A messenger stands between the one before him and the one after him; he is neither the beginning nor the end.

As was treated at al-Baqara 2/4, this is the claim the Qurʾān sets up for itself as well: *"they believe in what has been sent down to you and in what was sent down before you"*. The idea of the chain is central to the Qurʾān's understanding of prophethood.

**بَيْنَ يَدَىَّ** — "between my two hands", that is, **what is in front of me**. It is an established idiom in Arabic and is used of time as well: what came first, what has stayed in the past. This is a logic the reverse of the English expression "the week before us": here "before" is the past, because the past is the side that can be seen.

**مُصَدِّق** — root ص-د-ق: to be true. *Taṣdīq* is to confirm a thing. And it is the same root as the *ṣadaqū* seen at al-Baqara 2/177. The fault in the second verse of the sūra (the divergence of word and deed) was the opposite of this root; here the same root occurs on the positive side.

**مُبَشِّر** — root ب-ش-ر. In the section on al-Muddaththir it was analysed that the centre of this root is **the skin** (*bashara*): both good news and scorching show on the skin. The *bashīr* is the one who brings news that changes the expression of the face. Good news, then, in the word's own logic, is news that **changes the appearance** of the one who receives it.

### ٱسْمُهُۥٓ أَحْمَدُ — Aḥmad

Root **ح-م-د**. This root was analysed in full in the section on al-Fātiḥa, and there three words were separated from one another:

- **Commendation (*madḥ*)** — can be given for anything, even for what was not gained by will.
- **Thanks (*shukr*)** — is given only in return for a good that has reached you.
- **Ḥamd** — is directed at beauties done by will (narrower than commendation), but does not require that the favour reach you (wider than thanks).

And there the derivatives of the root were enumerated: **Muḥammad** (much praised), **Aḥmad**, **Maḥmūd** (praised), **Ḥamīd**. I do not repeat that analysis here.

The only thing to add here is **the matter of the pattern.** The word *Aḥmad* comes on the **أَفْعَل** pattern, and in Arabic this pattern can be read in two directions:

| Reading | Meaning | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **In the direction of the agent** | "The one who gives most *ḥamd*" | The *afʿal* pattern, as an elative, is derived from the agent of the verb: *aʿlam* (the one who knows most). |
| **In the direction of the patient** | "The one most praised" | The same pattern carries a passive sense in some words. |

These two directions were already recorded in the section on al-Fātiḥa. Both have been defended in the classical commentaries, and I adopt no firm preference between them. This much I can say: both readings agree with the work the name carries — the one who performs the *ḥamd*, and the one who is the subject of the *ḥamd*.

### The source of the good news: what is said here, and what is not said

Throughout history this has been one of the most debated verses of the Qurʾān, and the subject of the debate is this: **in which text is this word of Jesus to be found?**

**In this commentary I do not answer this question.** I write my ground for that plainly:

**First:** To make such a claim requires expertise in the linguistic strata of the texts we have to hand, in the history of their translation, and in the manuscript tradition. That is not the area of competence of this commentary.

**Second:** The strictest rule of the method (`STYLE.md`) is not to present as a transmitted report something that cannot be verified. A significant part of the comparisons made on this subject rests on arguments about language, and to pass over to one side without doing justice to those arguments would be to present to the reader as certain something of which I am not certain.

**Third:** The verse's own claim is not a claim to be found in an existing text. The verse transmits what Jesus **said**.

**What is the classical approach?** The general tendency of the exegetes is that this good news is present in the earlier books, and this view is supported by other verses of the Qurʾān — particularly by the prophet's being described at al-Aʿrāf 7/157 as one *"whom they find written down with them in the Torah and the Gospel"*. I transmit this approach **as a report**; not as my own verification.

**What can be drawn from the verse itself** is this: the sūra speaks of a chain of messengership and says that the links of the chain are aware of one another. Its function within the sūra agrees with this: in the fifth verse a prophet was hurt by his own people; in the sixth a prophet gives good news and his good news is called "sorcery". Both scenes describe the same thing — the knowledge arrives, it is not accepted.

### هَٰذَا سِحْرٌ مُّبِينٌ — the problem of the pronoun, and a subtlety

**First a matter of word order:** *fa-lammā **jāʾahum** bi'l-bayyināt* — "when he came to them with the clear proofs". Who came?

| Reading | Who came | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Jesus** | The subject of the sentence has been Jesus from the start; the pronoun returns to the nearest noun. |
| **2** | **The messenger announced** | The sentence comes immediately after the good news; and in the Qurʾān the charge of "sorcery" is frequently used against the Prophet. |

Both readings have been defended in the classical commentaries. I adopt no firm preference between them. Let me record this: as far as the flow of the sūra is concerned the difference is not great, because in either case the same thing is being described — the proof came, and it was called sorcery.

**سِحْر** — root س-ح-ر. In the concrete semantic field of the word lie **showing a thing as other than it is, deceiving**. And from the same root comes **سَحَر** (*saḥar* — the last part of the night, before dawn); according to the classical explanation, because it is the hour at which night and day mingle with one another and cannot be told apart.

**مُّبِين** — root ب-ي-ن. And there is a subtlety here. In Arabic this pattern can mean both "that which is manifest" and "that which makes manifest" — the same root was analysed in the section on al-Bayyina, and it was shown that at the centre of the root is **distinguishing**.

Now look at the sentence: *"This is manifest sorcery."* The charge does not reject the **manifestness** of the proof; it accepts the manifestness and changes its source. That is, the other side does not deny the extraordinariness of what it sees; it gives it another name.

This is an established reaction that the Qurʾān records. As was treated in the section on al-Muddaththir, the same charge was set up there too: *"This is nothing but sorcery handed down"* (al-Muddaththir 74/24). And it was recorded there that the verdict is given **before** the grounds; the ground is produced afterwards.

And also the choice of the word *bayyināt*: as was analysed in the section on Sūrat al-Bayyina, the *bayyina* is the proof that **distinguishes**. The verse thus records a paradox: the thing that distinguishes came, and it was not distinguished. The same paradox was set up at al-Bayyina 98/4 — the division came **after** the proof arrived.

## 61/7 — وَمَنْ أَظْلَمُ مِمَّنِ ٱفْتَرَىٰ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ ٱلْكَذِبَ وَهُوَ يُدْعَىٰٓ إِلَى ٱلْإِسْلَٰمِ وَٱللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ

*Wa man aẓlamu mimmani'ftarā ʿala'llāhi'l-kadhiba wa huwa yudʿā ila'l-Islām, wa'llāhu lā yahdi'l-qawma'ẓ-ẓālimīn*

"Who does greater wrong than one who fabricates a lie about God while he is being called to Islam? God does not guide the wrongdoing people."

### The مَنْ أَظْلَمُ pattern

This pattern was treated at al-Baqara 2/114. What was recorded there was this: the heaviest attribute (*aẓlam* — "more wrongdoing") is attached to a single deed, and the expression is kept **general**; it is not reduced to a particular event.

What is to be added here is the **interrogative** form of the pattern. *"Who could do greater wrong?"* — in grammar this is the kind of question called *istifhām inkārī*: it expects no answer, it states that the answer is "no one". The sentence, then, is really a judgement of the superlative, in the guise of a question.

And the distribution of the pattern in the Qurʾān is worth noting: each time it is attached to a different deed — hindering access to the places of worship (2/114), fabricating a lie about God, turning away from the verses, concealing testimony. The ruling "the greatest wrongdoer", then, is not assigned to a single offence.

This shows how the pattern is to be understood: each of them marks the deed at the extreme within its own field. It is not a competition but **the establishing of a category**.

### ٱفْتَرَىٰ — fabrication

Root **ف-ر-ي**. And its concrete sense is interesting: **to split, to cut, to cut out leather.**

- **فَرَى ٱلْأَدِيمَ** — he cut and shaped the leather (prepared it for sewing, for working).

From this **ٱفْتِرَاء** is born: **to fabricate a thing by cutting and shaping it.** Fabrication, then, in the language's own logic, is a **manufacture**: raw material is taken, it is worked, and a product comes out.

This is the most important idea the word carries. Fabrication is not a mistake but a **production**. To be mistaken is passive; to fabricate is active. And the thing produced has a form — not a random utterance but something cut out, sewn, ready to be presented.

**عَلَى ٱللَّهِ** — "about God / against God". The particle *ʿalā* here states being against: what is fabricated is ascribed to God.

**ٱلْكَذِب** — definite. "The lie" — that is, a known, particular kind of lie.

### وَهُوَ يُدْعَىٰٓ إِلَى ٱلْإِسْلَٰمِ — the circumstance that aggravates the offence

And this is the finest point of the verse. The sentence ends with a circumstantial clause: **"…while he is being called."**

This caveat is what aggravates the offence. And it works like this: to manufacture a fabrication is an offence in any case; but to manufacture a fabrication **while the door stands open** is another thing.

The verse, then, establishes a point about timing. Two roads lie before the person and both are open: to follow the call, or to manufacture an alternative. He chooses the second. The gravity of the offence comes from the first having been **within reach**.

This is a relative of the point established in Sūrat al-Bayyina. There it was shown that the division came not from ignorance but **after the proof had arrived**. Here too the fabrication is made not in the absence of the call but in its presence.

**يُدْعَىٰ** — passive. Who is calling is not said. This widens the scope of the verse: what matters is not the **agent** of the call but its **existence**. If the door is open, it makes no difference who opened it.

**ٱلْإِسْلَٰم** — root س-ل-م. As was analysed in the section on al-Baqara 2/130-132, this root carries both the sense **to submit** and the sense **to be sound, to be in well-being**; and as was recorded there, in the story of Abraham (Ibrāhīm) the word occurs not as the name of a religion but as a **deed**.

### وَٱللَّهُ لَا يَهْدِى ٱلْقَوْمَ ٱلظَّٰلِمِينَ

A closing symmetrical with the fifth verse. There the *fāsiqūn*, here the wrongdoers.

**ظَالِم** — root ظ-ل-م. As was analysed in the section on al-Mumtaḥana, the root has two senses: **darkness** and **putting a thing somewhere other than its own place.**

The second sense is exactly apt in this verse. What is fabrication? Putting a word where it does not belong — ascribing to God something a human being produced. The offence, then, in the word's own logic, is an offence of **displacement**.

And this speaks quietly with the second verse of the sūra. There word and deed diverged; here the word is separated from its owner and ascribed to another. Both deeds produce a **disconnection**: in the one between the speaker and the doer, in the other between the word and its source.

## 61/8 — يُرِيدُونَ لِيُطْفِـُٔوا۟ نُورَ ٱللَّهِ بِأَفْوَٰهِهِمْ وَٱللَّهُ مُتِمُّ نُورِهِۦ وَلَوْ كَرِهَ ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ

*Yurīdūna li-yuṭfiʾū nūra'llāhi bi-afwāhihim, wa'llāhu mutimmu nūrihi wa-law kariha'l-kāfirūn*

"They want to put out the light of God with their mouths. But God will complete His light, however much those who deny may dislike it."

### A grammatical subtlety: يُرِيدُونَ لِيُطْفِـُٔوا۟

The verb *arāda* (to want, to will) normally comes with **أَن**: *yurīdūna **an** yuṭfiʾū*. Here **لِ** has come.

The classical explanations divide in two:

| View | The function of the *lām* | The meaning of the sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | The *lām* has taken the place of *an*; there is interchangeability between the two particles | "They want to put it out." |
| **2** | The *lām* is in its original function (cause/purpose) and there is an ellipsis in the sentence | "They will (something) / they exert themselves **in order to** put it out." |

There is a subtlety in the second reading: the **aim** of the will and its **act** are separated from one another. That is, the sentence implies that they do something but that what they do is not sufficient for the aim: they exert themselves, but there is a distance between what they exert themselves at and what they want to reach.

This agrees very well with the image that comes next in the verse — trying to put out a light with the mouth is exactly such a disproportion. I find the second reading the more interesting; but this is a preference, it is not binding, and the first has been widely defended as well.

### بِأَفْوَٰهِهِمْ — the disproportion at the centre of the verse

**أَفْوَاه** — the plural of the word *fam/fū* (mouth).

And the whole force of the verse lies in this single word. Think of the image: trying to put out a light **with the mouth**.

This works for a candle. It works for an oil lamp. As the scale grows the act becomes ridiculous; and the verse states that scale: **نُورَ ٱللَّهِ.**

The verse, then, sets up in a single sentence the disproportion between the scale of the effort and the scale of the target — using no adjective at all, simply by placing two words side by side.

**And the choice of "mouth" is meaningful in itself: not with a weapon, but with speech.** This binds the verse to the one before it. The seventh verse described **the manufacture of fabrication** (*iftarā* — to cut and shape). The eighth verse says what that fabrication is for: to put out a light. Two consecutive verses take the same organ as their target.

The sūra's portrayal of the other side, then, is built from beginning to end on **speech**. And note: the criticism the sūra directs at its own community was also about speech (*lima taqūlūna mā lā tafʿalūn*). Two different faults, the same organ:

| | **Inside (61/2-3)** | **Outside (61/7-8)** |
|---|---|---|
| The fault | What is said is not done | What is said is fabricated |
| Between word and reality | The word is true, the deed is missing | The word is false, the source is counterfeit |
| The ruling | *maqt* (revulsion) | *aẓlam* (the greatest wrongdoer) |

### نُور — and its difference from نار

This distinction was analysed at al-Baqara 2/17. There, in the comparison set up for the hypocrites, the finest detail was this: the man kindled a **fire** (*nār*), but God took away its **light** (*nūr*). And two properties of fire were distinguished: to give light and to burn.

Also recorded there was the pattern that **nūr is singular and ẓulumāt plural**: the true is one, the false is countless.

What is to be added here is an observation about the language:

**نَار** (fire) is a **source** — the burning thing itself. **نُور** (light), by contrast, is what **radiates** from it. And the verb of extinguishing (*iṭfāʾ*, root ط-ف-أ) is a verb belonging to the source: what is put out is fire, not light. You put out a fire; light you can only darken by cutting off its source.

The verse thus records an inconsistency at the level of the words: the verb and its object do not agree. The verb *yuṭfiʾū* (to put out) takes *nūr* as its object — yet *nūr* is not a thing that can be put out. The effort of the other side, then, aims at a task that is impossible even in the logic of the language itself.

This is an observation about the language; it is not a claim about physics and must not be read as one.

### وَٱللَّهُ مُتِمُّ نُورِهِۦ

**مُتِمّ** — root ت-م-م: to be complete, to become free of deficiency. *Mutimm* is an active participle and has come in a genitive construct: "the completer of His light".

**The choice of word is striking: not "the protector" but "the completer."**

The difference matters. Protection is an answer given to an attack; it is defence. Completion, by contrast, is a work independent of the attack, running its own course. The verse, then, positions the effort of the other side not as a threat that has to be answered but as **a noise that does not change the course**.

And the active participle form states continuity: the work is not finished, it is going on.

### وَلَوْ كَرِهَ ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ — and its difference from the ninth verse

**كَرِهَ** — root ك-ر-ه: to dislike, for one's inner self not to accept. And from the same root comes **إِكْرَاه** (compulsion): to compel a person to something he dislikes. The *"there is no compulsion in religion"* of al-Baqara 2/256 is from the same root.

This pattern — *wa-law kariha…* — occurs twice in the sūra, but with two different subjects:

| Verse | Subject | Root sense | Context |
|---|---|---|---|
| **61/8** | **ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ** | ك-ف-ر: to cover | The effort to **put out / cover** the light |
| **61/9** | **ٱلْمُشْرِكُونَ** | ش-ر-ك: to associate partners | The religion's **prevailing** |

This duality is no coincidence, and it agrees with the deeds:

**In the eighth verse** what is wanted is a **covering** — to put out the light, to make it invisible. And the root of *kufr* is exactly to cover (al-Baqara 2/6). The verse, then, names those who try to cover as "the coverers": deed and noun from the same root.

**In the ninth verse**, by contrast, the matter is the position of one religion relative to the other religions — that is, a matter of **plurality**. And there the other side is named with the word that expresses plurality: the mushrikūn, those who associate partners.

The choices of word have been made according to the subject of each verse.

## 61/9 — هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَرْسَلَ رَسُولَهُۥ بِٱلْهُدَىٰ وَدِينِ ٱلْحَقِّ لِيُظْهِرَهُۥ عَلَى ٱلدِّينِ كُلِّهِۦ وَلَوْ كَرِهَ ٱلْمُشْرِكُونَ

*Huwa'lladhī arsala rasūlahu bi'l-hudā wa dīni'l-ḥaqqi li-yuẓhirahu ʿala'd-dīni kullihi wa-law kariha'l-mushrikūn*

"He is the one who sent His messenger with guidance and the religion of truth — to make it prevail over all religion, however much the mushrikūn may dislike it."

### A verse that occurs three times in the Qurʾān

This sentence is found in three places in the Qurʾān, and their endings differ:

| Place | Closing |
|---|---|
| al-Tawba 9/33 | **وَلَوْ كَرِهَ ٱلْمُشْرِكُونَ** |
| **al-Ṣaff 61/9** | **وَلَوْ كَرِهَ ٱلْمُشْرِكُونَ** |
| al-Fatḥ 48/28 | **وَكَفَىٰ بِٱللَّهِ شَهِيدًا** ("God suffices as a witness") |

The two closings look to two different pieces of work: one nullifies the reaction of the other side, the other shows the witness to the claim.

### لِيُظْهِرَهُۥ — the root ظ-ه-ر and a disagreement

Root **ظ-ه-ر**. This root was analysed at al-Mumtaḥana 60/9: its concrete sense is **the back** (*ẓahr*), and from it two branches issue — **to be visible** (the side that turns to the front) and **to prevail** (to set one's back against, to back someone, to come out on top). The form *ẓāharū* (to back someone) was treated there.

Here the word is in the *ifʿāl* form: **أَظْهَرَ**. And there are two readings of its meaning:

| Reading | Its meaning | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **To make prevail, to bring to victory** | The "coming out on top" branch of the root; the particle *ʿalā* stating superiority. |
| **2** | **To bring out into the open, to make distinct** | The "being visible" branch of the root; the widespread meaning of the word *iẓhār*. On this reading: to make the content of the religion clear and distinct over against the other religions. |

These two readings bring with them the question of **what kind** of superiority the superiority is. Several tendencies on this subject are transmitted in the classical commentaries: superiority in respect of proof and argument; actual dominion; and a promise concerning the end of time.

**In this commentary I adopt no preference among them**, and I have a ground: throughout history this verse has been made a support for political programmes, and per the method (`STYLE.md`) this commentary takes no side in current politics. What the wording of the verse says — that a sending has a purpose — must be kept separate from interpretations of how that purpose will be realised. The first belongs to the text, the second to interpretation.

**عَلَى ٱلدِّينِ كُلِّهِۦ** — "over the whole of religion". *al-Dīn* is singular and definite; together with *kullih* it comes to mean "everything that is religion".

### The link within the sūra: the return of the root ظهر

This root will come back in the **last verse** of the sūra: *fa-aṣbaḥū **ẓāhirīn*** (61/14) — "they came to prevail".

That is, the sūra sets up a promise in the ninth verse and, in the fourteenth, gives with the same root **an example realised in history**. Promise and example are bound together by the same word.

And one further cross-link: in the preceding sūra in the order of the muṣḥaf, at al-Mumtaḥana 60/9, the same root was used **of the other side** — *ẓāharū ʿalā ikhrājikum*, "they backed your being driven out". The same root, then, names the deed of both sides in two neighbouring sūras. The question is who stands behind whom, and who comes out on top.

## 61/10 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ عَلَىٰ تِجَٰرَةٍ تُنجِيكُم مِّنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū hal adullukum ʿalā tijāratin tunjīkum min ʿadhābin alīm*

"O you who believe! Shall I show you a trade that will save you from a painful punishment?"

### Word order: not a command but an offer

The most striking side of the verse is its mood. It could have run: "Believe, and strive in the way of God." The imperative. That is not what happens.

In its place comes a **question**: **هَلْ أَدُلُّكُمْ** — "shall I show you?"

And this question is of the same family as the question in the second verse of the sūra, but its function is the reverse. In the second verse the question was a reproach; here the question is an **invitation**. With its two questions the sūra does two different pieces of work: one seats the addressee opposite himself, the other shows him a door.

The consequence of the mood of offer is this: the addressee faces not a command but a **proposal**. To accept is his own decision. And precisely for this reason the grammatical subtlety in the next verse (below) becomes more meaningful.

### أَدُلُّكُمْ — to show

Root **د-ل-ل**: to show the way, to point out, to make known the sign that will bring one to a thing.

From the same root come *dalīl* (the sign that brings one to a thing) and *dalāla* (one thing's indicating another). And — in keeping with the context of trade — **دَلَّال** (*dallāl*): the one who shows the goods to the buyer, who acts as broker.

That the verb is from this root establishes the sentence's language of trade from the very start. The speaker does not give a command; he **shows an opportunity.**

### تِجَارَة — the metaphor of trade, and a comparison with al-Baqara

Root **ت-ج-ر**: to buy and sell.

In the section on al-Baqara this metaphor was treated in three separate places. Now the fourth position comes and the table is completed:

| Verse | The description of the exchange | Given | Taken | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Baqara 2/16** | *"They bought misguidance in exchange for guidance"* | Guidance | Misguidance | *"This exchange brought them no profit"* |
| **al-Baqara 2/86** | *"They bought the life of this world in exchange for the hereafter"* | The hereafter | This world | The punishment is not lightened |
| **al-Baqara 2/90** | *"How evil is that for which they sold themselves!"* | **Themselves** | A fabricated pretext | *"Biʾsamā" — how evil!* |
| **al-Ṣaff 61/10-13** | *"A trade that will save you from a painful punishment"* | **Wealth and life** | **Forgiveness, the garden, help, victory** | *"al-fawzu'l-ʿaẓīm"* |

**What the three occurrences in al-Baqara have in common: all of them are losing exchanges.** And as was recorded in that section, the structure common to them all is this: what is lost is not something never possessed; it is something **held in hand and given away**. The badness of the exchange lies not in the intention but **in the price**.

**The occurrence in al-Ṣaff, by contrast, is the reverse: the profitable one.** And its structure is the same — here too there is something in hand (wealth and life) and it is given away.

The Qurʾān, then, uses the same metaphor in both directions, and in both cases the metaphor measures the same thing: **what you exchange for what.** In al-Baqara man took the near and gave away the lasting; here the reverse is proposed.

And in the section on al-Baqara 2/16 this sūra was already pointed to: *"The Qurʾān uses this language often — the khusr (loss) in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr, the address 'shall I show you a profitable trade' in Sūrat al-Ṣaff, all come from the same vein."* That link is completed here.

It is worth dwelling too on **why the metaphor works**. Mecca and Medina were trading cities; the notions of profit, loss, capital and price were part of everyday speech. In using this language the Qurʾān makes an abstraction concrete: the reckoning of the hereafter is translated into the language of a transaction the addressee already carries out every day.

And the most important idea the metaphor carries is this: **every choice is a barter.** To choose one thing is to give up another. The language of trade makes this visible — because in trade there is no such thing as taking without a price.

### تُنجِيكُم — to save

Root **ن-ج-و**. And its concrete sense gives an image: **a raised place, a mound.**

- **نَجْوَة** — high ground that the flood does not reach.
- And from this *najāt*: deliverance. To be saved, in the word's own logic, is **to rise above the level**.
- From the same root comes *najwā* (whispering) as well; according to the classical explanation, because those speaking separate from the group and draw aside.

The idea common to the root: **to separate, to go up, to pass outside the current.**

### And note: the first return of the trade is not profit but deliverance

This is the most noteworthy side of the verse, and it is easily passed over.

An offer of trade is normally commended by its **profit**: "you will gain this much". This verse does not do that. What is named as the first property of the trade is **its saving from punishment**.

That is, the addressee's starting condition is assumed to be not that of a merchant with capital in hand but that of someone in **debt or in danger**. The profit will come later (verses 12-13); first, the exit from loss.

This looks to the same place as the point established in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr: *"Man is truly in loss"* — that is, the starting condition is loss, and the exception comes afterwards.

**عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ** — *alīm*, from the root ا-ل-م: causing pain. And the adjective is on the *faʿīl* pattern and here carries the sense of *muʾlim* (pain-causing).

## 61/11 — تُؤْمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ وَتُجَٰهِدُونَ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ بِأَمْوَٰلِكُمْ وَأَنفُسِكُمْ ذَٰلِكُمْ خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

*Tuʾminūna billāhi wa rasūlihi wa tujāhidūna fī sabīlillāhi bi-amwālikum wa anfusikum, dhālikum khayrun lakum in kuntum taʿlamūn*

"You believe in God and His messenger; you strive in the way of God with your wealth and your lives. That is better for you, if you but knew."

### A grammatical subtlety: the imperfect in place of the imperative

The finest choice of word order in the sūra is here.

The expected mood is the **imperative**: *āminū wa jāhidū* — "believe and strive". And indeed this is the meaning. But the verse does not use the imperative; it uses **the imperfect, the indicative of the present**: *tuʾminūna… wa tujāhidūna…*

There are several views on the explanation of this choice:

| View | Analysis | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1 (the most widespread)** | The sentence is **the explication** of the word *tijāra* in the preceding verse. It is the predicate of an implied subject: "(That trade is this:) you believe and you strive." | The sentence is not a command but a description. |
| **2** | The sentence has the sense of a command; in Arabic, giving a command in the indicative is a known technique. | A command **stronger** than the imperative. |
| **3** | A variant reading is transmitted in which the verbs are read in the imperative. | The command is then explicitly established. |

With regard to the third view: it is stated that such a reading is transmitted; since I am not certain to which authority it is ascribed, **I give no name.**

**The subtlety in the second view is worth dwelling on.** In Arabic — and in many languages — a command given in the indicative is more binding than one in the imperative. The reason is this: the imperative states something **that is wanted done**; the indicative states something **that is being done**. This is the difference between "do this" and "you do this". In the second, the business is described as though it had already happened, and no choice is left before the addressee — because the choice has been buried inside the description.

English uses this construction too: "You're here at nine tomorrow morning." That is a command, but it is not in the imperative; and precisely for that reason it is the more absolute.

**And this choice must be read together with the mood of offer in the preceding verse.** The tenth verse had asked a question: *"shall I show you?"* A door had been opened to the addressee. The eleventh verse continues as though the addressee had accepted: you believe, you strive.

That is, the sūra carries the account on **without waiting** for the addressee's acceptance. This is not pressure; it is an expression of **confidence**. The offer was made, and without asking what the answer will be, the continuation of the transaction is described.

I present this reading as my own inference.

### بِأَمْوَٰلِكُمْ وَأَنفُسِكُمْ — the order

Wealth first, life second. This order is established in the Qurʾān (al-Tawba 9/111 and elsewhere).

Why wealth first? Several explanations are given in the classical commentaries; the most widespread is this: wealth is the thing more widely possessed and is the first step of sacrifice — everyone has a life, but giving one's life is not always required, whereas giving wealth is continuous.

A second observation may be added, and it is especially apt in the context of this sūra: **there are many who say they are ready to give their lives and do not give their wealth.** Wealth, that is, is the field in which the distance between word and deed is most often seen.

And the second verse of the sūra took exactly this distance as its subject. Having said "you do not do what you say", the sūra, in enumerating what is asked to be done, puts **the item hardest to talk about first**.

I present this link as a reading; it is not a transmitted report.

### ذَٰلِكُمْ خَيْرٌ لَّكُمْ إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ

**خَيْر** — the word can mean both "good" and "better"; here a comparison is implied: better than what? In the language of trade the answer is plain — better than holding on to it.

**إِن كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ** — "if you know". The conditional clause adds a caveat about knowledge to the sentence.

And this caveat agrees exactly with the metaphor of trade: **whether an exchange is profitable is a matter of knowledge.** The merchant who does not know the price cannot see the profit; the one who does not know the value of the goods sells cheap. The verse marks as the reason for refusing the offer not ill will but **not knowing the value**.

At al-Baqara 2/16 it was said of the result of the exchange that *"it brought no profit"*; here the seeing of the profit is tied to knowledge. Two ends of the same metaphor.

## 61/12 — يَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ ذُنُوبَكُمْ وَيُدْخِلْكُمْ جَنَّٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ وَمَسَٰكِنَ طَيِّبَةً فِى جَنَّٰتِ عَدْنٍ ذَٰلِكَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ

*Yaghfir lakum dhunūbakum wa yudkhilkum jannātin tajrī min taḥtiha'l-anhāru wa masākina ṭayyibatan fī jannāti ʿadn, dhālika'l-fawzu'l-ʿaẓīm*

"(If you do so) He will forgive you your sins and admit you into gardens beneath which rivers flow, and into goodly dwellings in the gardens of ʿAdn. That is the great triumph."

### Grammar: why is يَغْفِرْ in the jussive?

The first verb of the verse is **يَغْفِرْ**, and its ending has come **in the jussive** (without a vowel). In Arabic this is the sign showing that a verb is the answer to a condition.

So where is the condition? **It has not been stated.** There is an elided condition in the sentence: "(if you do this) He forgives."

And the consequence is this: **the indicative sentence of the eleventh verse acquires here, retrospectively, the function of a condition.** That is, the grammar itself confirms that the preceding verse has the sense of a command or a condition.

This is a piece of evidence that settles the discussion above: had the sentence *tuʾminūna wa tujāhidūna* been a mere statement, a jussive verb would not have come after it. The jussive verb shows that a demand precedes it.

The three verses thus form a single structure: the offer (10) → the price (11) → the return (12).

### Three items of return

| # | Return | Its character |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **مَغْفِرَة** — the forgiveness of sins | The cleansing of the past |
| 2 | **جَنَّٰت تَجْرِى** — gardens with rivers | A place |
| 3 | **مَسَٰكِن طَيِّبَة فِى جَنَّٰتِ عَدْنٍ** — goodly dwellings in the gardens of ʿAdn | **Settlement** |

The order is meaningful: first the wiping out of the debt, then the giving of the place, then a position in that place in which one will **come to rest**.

**غَفَرَ** — root غ-ف-ر: **to cover, to close over.** And its concrete source: *mighfar* — the war-helmet that covers the head. Forgiveness, then, is not treating the sin as non-existent but **covering it over**.

And there is a striking closeness here: **the root ك-ف-ر is also "to cover."** This was analysed at al-Baqara 2/6. The two roots state the same deed but their objects differ: *kufr* covers over **the truth**, *ghufrān* covers over **the sin**. The same movement, in two different directions.

### مَسَٰكِنَ طَيِّبَةً — and its link with the sūra

**مَسْكَن** — root س-ك-ن: **to come to rest, to become motionless, to grow still.**

Derivatives: *sukūn* (motionlessness), *sakīna* (inner stillness), *sākin*, and **مِسْكِين** (*miskīn* — the idea in the root of the word: one in a condition of being unable to move, unable to rise from his place).

A *maskan*, then, is not a "house"; it is **the place where one comes to rest**. At the centre of the word is the ending of movement.

**And in the context of the sūra this choice is worth noting.** What was there throughout the sūra? Standing in rank, fighting, striving, spending wealth and life, carrying out a trade, help and victory. All of it is **movement**.

And what is given in return is **coming to rest**.

I present this link as a reading; but the choice of word supports it. In describing the garden a word such as "palace", "pavilion" or "station" could have been used. The word used is the one whose root is "motionlessness".

**طَيِّبَة** — root ط-ي-ب: pleasant, clean, agreeable to the heart. The Qurʾān uses this root of food (*ṭayyibāt*), of speech (*al-kalimu'ṭ-ṭayyib*) and of life (*ḥayātan ṭayyiba*, al-Naḥl 16/97) alike. The idea common to the word: **acceptable, settling well within.**

**عَدْن** — root ع-د-ن: to stay permanently in a place, to settle. From the same root comes **مَعْدِن** (*maʿdin*, a mine) — the place of a mine is fixed, it cannot be moved. *Jannātu ʿadn*, then: the gardens of permanence.

### ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ

**فَوْز** — root ف-و-ز. And the root has an interesting double aspect: both **to be saved** and **to attain what one desires**.

From the same root comes **مَفَازَة**, and this word is used both for "a place of deliverance" and — strikingly — for **"the desert"**. One of the classical explanations is this: because coming out of the desert alive is a deliverance, the dangerous crossing has been named with an optimistic name.

The two aspects of the word overlap with the content of the verse: in the tenth verse the first property of the trade was **to save** (*tunjīkum*); here at the close comes *fawz* — both deliverance and gain. The exchange is thereby completed.

## 61/13 — وَأُخْرَىٰ تُحِبُّونَهَا نَصْرٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَفَتْحٌ قَرِيبٌ وَبَشِّرِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ

*Wa ukhrā tuḥibbūnahā, naṣrun mina'llāhi wa fatḥun qarīb, wa bashshiri'l-muʾminīn*

"And one further thing that you will love: help from God and a near victory. And give good news to the believers."

### وَأُخْرَىٰ — "and another"

The sentence is incomplete; a word has been elided. The analyses: *wa lakum ukhrā* ("there is one further thing for you") or *wa tijāratun ukhrā* ("one further trade"). Both come out at the same place.

The structure is this: the twelfth verse had enumerated the return belonging to **the hereafter**. This verse adds the one belonging to **this world**.

### تُحِبُّونَهَا — a caveat that must not be passed over

And this is the finest point of the verse.

In defining this second return the verse adds a relative clause: **تُحِبُّونَهَا** — **"which you love".**

That is, the text knows what a person loves more, and it **records this explicitly.** A person does not describe the garden as "the thing I love"; it is distant, it has not been experienced, it is desired by being imagined. Victory and conquest, by contrast, belong to the present, are tangible, are wanted at once.

The verse neither denies this fact nor censures it.

**But it puts it in order.** And note the ordering:

| Order | Return | Time | The person's interest |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Forgiveness, the garden, the dwellings (61/12) | The hereafter | Distant |
| **2** | Help and a near victory (61/13) | This world | **Loved** (*tuḥibbūnahā*) |

The text places what is loved more in **second** position, and does not shrink from saying that it is loved more.

This is a good example of the Qurʾān's attitude towards worldly desires: the desire is not treated as non-existent, not suppressed, not censured. **It is put in its place.** And this is a far harder piece of work than denying the desire; because a desire that has been put on record is more easily managed than one that has not.

And one further observation: the sūra uses a verb of feeling in three separate places, and the three of them draw a map:

| Verse | Verb | Who | Towards what |
|---|---|---|---|
| 61/3 | **مَقْتًا** | God | Towards saying and not doing (revulsion) |
| 61/4 | **يُحِبُّ** | God | Towards those who fight in rank (love) |
| 61/13 | **تُحِبُّونَهَا** | **You** | Towards help and victory (love) |

The sūra says first what God is revolted by, then what He loves, and at the end what the addressee loves. Three sentences describe a two-sided relationship.

### نَصْر — help

Root **ن-ص-ر**: to help. And the concrete use of the root is striking: **the rain's coming to parched ground.**

- **نُصِرَتِ ٱلْأَرْض** — rain fell on the land, the land was helped.
- **نَصَرَهُ** — he helped him.

At the centre of the root is this: **what comes from outside to what does not suffice on its own.** Rain is what the soil cannot produce; it comes from outside and puts the soil in a condition to do its own work.

This root sense explains the caveat the word carries in the verse: **نَصْرٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ** — "help **from God**". The caveat may look unnecessary; help is from Him in any case. But in the logic of the root the caveat is apt: help, by definition, comes from outside, and its source must be specified.

And this word will return in the next verse as **أَنصَار**. The sūra sets up help first as something received (13) and then as something given (14).

### فَتْح — victory

Root **ف-ت-ح**: to open. From the same root come *miftāḥ* (a key), *al-Fattāḥ* (the opener — one of the names of God), and *fātiḥa* (an opening).

The word's sense of "victory" comes from here: the opening of the gates of a city. Conquest, then, in the word's own logic, is not a destroying but an **opening**.

In the section on al-Baqara 2/89 another form of this root was treated: *yastaftiḥūna* — "to ask for a victory", that is, to demand superiority and triumph. What was recorded there was this: what was awaited was really not the prophet but the superiority; when the prophet came and the superiority passed to another, the expectation lost its meaning.

Here, by contrast, the victory stands on the side of **what is promised, not what is demanded** — and in return for a price.

**قَرِيب** — "near". A caveat about time is placed on the promise. And this caveat completes the language of trade in the offer: in an exchange the term matters. A long-term return (the garden) and a short-term return (the victory) are offered together.

### وَبَشِّرِ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ — the shift in the direction of address

Throughout the sūra the address has been plural: *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū*, *taqūlūna*, *tuʾminūna*. Now suddenly a **singular imperative** comes: **بَشِّرِ** — "give good news".

This is an instance of *iltifāt* (a shift in the direction of address), and the addressee is plainly the Prophet.

Its function is this: the offer was made to the community, but the task of **conveying** the good news is given to one person. Towards its close, that is, the sūra sets up a distribution of duties: the community will carry out the trade, the messenger will carry the good news.

And the root of the verb *bashshir* — as was analysed in the section on al-Muddaththir, ب-ش-ر, whose centre is **the skin** — is the same as the *mubashshiran* of the sixth verse. Jesus had given good news; now the same verb is commanded to the addressee of this sūra. The root comes back at the two ends of the sūra.

## 61/14 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ كُونُوٓا۟ أَنصَارَ ٱللَّهِ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū kūnū anṣāra'llāhi kamā qāla ʿĪsa'bnu Maryama li'l-ḥawāriyyīna man anṣārī ila'llāh, qāla'l-ḥawāriyyūna naḥnu anṣārullāh, fa-āmanat ṭāʾifatun min banī Isrāʾīla wa kafarat ṭāʾifah, fa-ayyadna'lladhīna āmanū ʿalā ʿaduwwihim fa-aṣbaḥū ẓāhirīn*

"O you who believe! Be the helpers of God — just as Jesus son of Mary said to the disciples, 'Who are my helpers on the way to God?' and the disciples said, 'We are the helpers of God.' Thereupon a group of the Children of Israel believed and a group denied. And We supported those who believed against their enemy, and they came to prevail."

### كُونُوٓا۟ — not "do" but "be"

The last command of the sūra comes with a verb that must be read carefully: the imperative of the verb **كَانَ** — "be".

And this carries the whole matter of the sūra one step higher.

In the second verse the sūra had spoken of a gap: the distance between **saying** and **doing**. Now a third step is added: **being.**

| Step | Verb | Its character |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **قَوْل** — to say | Declaration |
| 2 | **فِعْل** — to do | Action |
| 3 | **كَوْن** — to be | **Identity** |

This distinction was treated in detail in the section on al-Kāfirūn. There, by way of that sūra's grammatical subtlety, this point was established: **not to do** a thing is a decision — decisions can be taken again, and slacken when one tires; **not to be someone who does** a thing belongs to identity and does not require deciding afresh each time.

The last command of al-Ṣaff is exactly at this level. It does not say "help"; it says **"be helpers"**. What is asked for is not a deed but an attribute.

And this is the most fitting answer given to the problem in the second verse of the sūra: not doing what one says appears where the word does not turn into a deed. The way to close this gap is not to exert oneself more but **for what is said to turn into identity**. For a thing that has become part of who you are, no gap remains between word and deed.

I present this reading as my own inference.

### أَنصَارَ ٱللَّهِ — "the helpers of God"

At first sight the expression carries a difficulty: does God have need of help?

The answer is plain, and the Qurʾān itself gives it. The Qurʾān explains this expression elsewhere:

> *"O you who believe! If you help God, He will help you and make your feet firm."* (Muḥammad 47/7)

That is, "helping God" is helping His **cause**, His religion, His messenger. This is the explanation common to the classical commentaries.

And what is striking is this: the expression has been left in its unabbreviated form. "The helpers of God's religion" could have been said; it is not. This brevity of the expression does something: it establishes the address of the help **directly**. No intermediary is mentioned.

Recall the sense of the root **نَصَرَ**: "what comes from outside to what does not suffice on its own". Read by way of this sense of the word, the expression carries a tension — and the tension is intentional. What is in the next verse is how far the word makes a person liable.

### مَنْ أَنصَارِىٓ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ — the particle "ilā"

There is an unusual particle in Jesus' question: **إِلَى ٱللَّهِ**.

What would have been expected is "*maʿallāh*" (with God) or "*fillāh*". *Ilā*, by contrast, is a particle of **direction**: "towards…".

There are two readings:

| Reading | Analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | *Ilā* here has the sense of *maʿa* (with, together with) | "Who are my helpers who will be with me on the road to God?" |
| **2** | *Ilā* is in its original function: it states direction | "Who are my helpers as I go **towards God**?" |

The second reading makes the sentence more alive: help is not standing still in a fixed place and giving support; it is **walking together in a direction**. The question is not a search for a partisan but a search for a companion on the road.

I present this distinction as a reading; both analyses occur in the classical sources, and I adopt no firm preference between them.

### ٱلْحَوَارِيُّونَ — the disciples

Root **ح-و-ر**. And there is more than one view on the origin and the meaning of this word. I enumerate them all and adopt no preference among them:

| View | Its basis | The *ḥawārī* on this view |
|---|---|---|
| **Whiteness** | *Ḥawar* — pure whiteness. And *ḥāriya*/*ḥawwār*: one who washes clothes and makes them white. | Either those who wear white garments, or "those who cleanse, who purify". |
| **Turning** | *Ḥāra* — he turned. From the same root *muḥāwara* (conversation — the word turning and coming back), *miḥwar* (the axis of turning). | Those who **turn** the people towards the truth; or those who turn about a centre. |
| **The sharp distinction in the eye** | *al-Ḥawar*: the marked separation of the black of the eye from its white. | The select, the ones singled out. |

It has also been said that the word rests on a non-Arabic origin and entered Arabic later. **On this matter I do not speak with certainty.**

In the classical commentaries the most widespread explanations run in the direction of "those who cleanse, who purify" and "select helpers, sincere friends".

The function of the word in this sūra, however, is clear: **the group that answers a call.** And the answer they give repeats the wording of the call exactly: *man anṣārī* → *naḥnu anṣārullāh*. The answer, that is, meets the question exactly — without diminishing it, without entering a caveat, without putting forward a condition.

Considered together with the second verse of the sūra, this answer is meaningful in a further way: **the word spoken is exactly the thing asked for.** And the continuation of the verse shows that the word turned into a deed.

### فَـَٔامَنَت طَّآئِفَةٌ … وَكَفَرَت طَّآئِفَةٌ — the division

And now the link with Sūrat al-Bayyina.

In the section on al-Bayyina the paradox at the centre of that sūra was analysed: *"Those who were given the Book divided only **after** the bayyina had come to them"* (98/4). The division, that is, is not the result of ignorance but something that comes after the knowledge.

al-Ṣaff 61/14 sets up the same structure: a call was made, an answer was given — and **then** the community split in two.

| | **al-Bayyina 98/4** | **al-Ṣaff 61/14** |
|---|---|---|
| What comes first | The *bayyina* (the proof) came | A call was made, the disciples answered |
| What happened afterwards | *تَفَرَّقَ* — they divided | *فَـَٔامَنَت طَّآئِفَةٌ … وَكَفَرَت طَّآئِفَةٌ* |
| The common point | **Division comes after the proof** | |

And in the section on al-Bayyina the reading preferred for *munfakkīn* looked in this direction as well: when the *bayyina* comes the mass **comes apart**; it becomes clear who stands where.

**طَآئِفَة** — root ط-و-ف: to go about, to turn around something (*ṭawāf* is from the same root). A *ṭāʾifa* is a group, a body of people. The idea of "going about" in the root of the word explains how the word comes to mean a moving part that has separated from a whole.

**مِّنۢ بَنِىٓ إِسْرَٰٓءِيلَ** — and note the particle **مِن** here: "a group **of** the Children of Israel". No wholesale ruling is issued, that is; the division takes place within a community.

This looks to the same place as the long analysis made of the particle *min* in the section on al-Bayyina; and it does the same work as the *minhum* caveat at al-Mumtaḥana 60/7, in the preceding sūra in the order of the muṣḥaf. Per the method (`STYLE.md`), no wholesale ruling is issued about a group in this commentary — and here the text itself does the same.

### فَأَيَّدْنَا — We supported

Root **أ-ي-د**. And the sense of the root is **power, strength**; the word *ayd* is used both for "hand" and for "power".

The Qurʾān uses this root in a striking place: *"And the heaven We built with power (bi-aydin)"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/47).

**تَأْيِيد** — to strengthen, to give support. And note: this is not "We gave victory". The verse does not say *naṣarnā*, it says *ayyadnā* — that is, He did not fight in their place; **He increased their strength.** This must be read together with the *naṣr* of the thirteenth verse: both are a contribution coming from outside, but the work is still being done.

### فَأَصْبَحُوا۟ ظَٰهِرِينَ — the return of the root

**أَصْبَحَ** — root ص-ب-ح: to become morning. And in Arabic the verb is used in the sense of "to come into a state, to wake into that state". So in saying "they came to prevail" there is, in the word's own logic, an image of **coming out into morning**: the night ended, the situation became clear.

**ظَٰهِرِينَ** — and here the root of the ninth verse comes back: **ظ-ه-ر.**

In the ninth verse a purpose had been set up: *li-yuẓhirahu ʿala'd-dīni kullih* — "to make it prevail over all religion". Now, in the last word of the sūra, the same root returns as **a history that has been realised**: *fa-aṣbaḥū ẓāhirīn*.

The sūra, that is, closes a forward-looking promise (9) with a backward-looking example (14). With the same word. It is shown that what was promised has once already come about.

And one further cross-link: at al-Mumtaḥana 60/9 the same root had been used **of the other side** — *ẓāharū ʿalā ikhrājikum*. Two neighbouring sūras name the position of both sides with the same root.

### The closing of the sūra and its opening

One last observation. The sūra is framed by two perfect verbs:

- **61/1:** *سَبَّحَ* — "it declared God free of all imperfection". Existence has done its own work.
- **61/14:** *فَأَصْبَحُوا۟ ظَٰهِرِينَ* — "they came to prevail". A community has done its own work.

Both are completed. And between the two stands an addressee who has not yet completed his own work — the addressee to whom the second verse puts its question.

I present this reading as my own inference.

## The sūra as a whole: a gap and its closing

The skeleton of the sūra is built on a single concept: **the gap.**

| Verse | Where the gap lies | Word |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *(none)* — existence has done its own work | **سَبَّحَ** |
| 2-3 | **Between word and deed** | **مَقْت** |
| 4 | Its closed state: a structure without gaps | **مَّرْصُوص** |
| 5 | Between knowledge and behaviour | **وَقَد تَّعْلَمُونَ** |
| 6 | Between proof and acceptance | **سِحْرٌ مُّبِين** |
| 7 | Between the word and its source | **ٱفْتَرَىٰ** |
| 8 | Between effort and target | **بِأَفْوَٰهِهِم** |
| 10-13 | The price of the closing and its return | **تِجَارَة** |
| 14 | The final state of the closing: to be | **كُونُوٓا۟** |

The sūra first diagnoses the gap inside (2-3), then shows it in history (5-6), then shows it on the other side (7-8). The same fault, that is, is repeated in three separate places, in three separate forms:

- **Inside:** the right word, the missing deed.
- **In history:** the right knowledge, the contrary behaviour.
- **Outside:** the wrong word, the counterfeit source.

The common denominator of the three is this: **the breaking of the bond between declaration and reality.** The sūra treats this disconnection not as a moral fault but as a **structural problem** — and for this reason it gives its solution too by way of a comparison with a structure: a building sealed with lead.

## al-Mumtaḥana and al-Ṣaff: two neighbouring sūras

These two sūras, which come one after the other in the order of the muṣḥaf, carry several elements in common. These are recorded not as a claim about "naẓm" (compositional order) but as an observation:

| | **al-Mumtaḥana 60** | **al-Ṣaff 61** |
|---|---|---|
| The main matter | **Whose rank you stand in** | **Whether or not you stand in the rank** |
| The key root | **و-ل-ي** (*walāya*) | **ص-ف-ف** (the rank) |
| A shared root | **ظ-ه-ر** — *ẓāharū* (60/9): the other side's backing | **ظ-ه-ر** — *li-yuẓhirahu* (61/9), *ẓāhirīn* (61/14) |
| A shared pair of names | *al-ʿAzīzu'l-Ḥakīm* (60/5) | *al-ʿAzīzu'l-Ḥakīm* (61/1) |
| A shared caveat | *minhum* (60/7): no wholesale ruling | *min banī Isrāʾīl* (61/14): no wholesale ruling |
| The exemplary figure | **Abraham** and those with him | **Moses**, **Jesus** and the disciples |
| A shared structure | A community's relation with **what is outside** | A community's consistency **within itself** |

Read together, the two sūras form a whole: al-Mumtaḥana orders a community's **boundary**, al-Ṣaff the **inner fabric** of that same community. And the assumption common to both is this: a consistent boundary towards the outside is possible only if there is a consistent fabric inside.

I present this as a reading; I claim no relation of revelation between the two sūras.

## What this says to the present

**First: the cost of speech.** The second and third verses of the sūra touch the most widespread habit of the present. To **declare** a truth is far easier and far more visible than to live it; and the declaration is satisfying enough that it can take the place of doing. As was recorded in the section on al-Baqara 2/44: "Expounding takes the place of doing. Indeed, the better the expounding is done, the stronger the substituting effect becomes."

al-Ṣaff takes this one step further: the matter is not only the insufficiency of the declaration; the declaration itself generates a **debt**. To say a thing produces the obligation to do it. This is what must be thought about before speaking.

**Second: the physical form of trust.** The comparison in the fourth verse holds for any joint undertaking today as well. A team is strong not in proportion to the sum of its members' qualities but in proportion to **the soundness of the bond between them**. And the bond is established by the keeping of the words given. As the distance between word and deed grows inside a community, that community falls apart even if no one at all is ill-intentioned — because no one can reckon that anyone else will hold his position.

**Third: the growth of the small angle.** The root ز-ي-غ in the fifth verse names a known mechanism of the moral life: deviation does not begin with a break, it begins with an **inclination**. And the inclination grows as the distance lengthens. This is the detail added to the analysis set up for the seal at al-Baqara 2/7: the cause of the locking is not one great decision but small bendings repeated. For an angle to become noticeable a good deal of road must be travelled; and at that point turning back has become hard as well.

**Fourth: disproportion of scale.** The image of the eighth verse — putting out a light with the mouth — describes an attitude often seen today too: supposing that one can do away with a thing **by talking**. Denying a fact does not annihilate it; contradicting a truth loudly enough does not change it. The verse's stance here is worth noting in itself: the effort of the other side is not censured, **its scale is shown.** This is a more effective answer than anger.

**Fifth: the putting of desire on record.** The *tuḥibbūnahā* caveat in the thirteenth verse is a piece of honesty rarely seen in the language of morality. The text knows what its addressee loves more and does not shrink from saying so; but neither does it change the ordering. To deny a desire does not annihilate it, it only hides it; a desire put on record, by contrast, can be managed. This is an instructive example both for personal discipline and as a way of addressing a community.

**Sixth: from doing to being.** The last command of the sūra — *kūnū* — asks not for a change of behaviour but for a change of identity. And this is the only lasting answer that can be given to the problem in the second verse. To try to hold a behaviour in place continuously by force of will is wearying and sooner or later slackens; once that behaviour has become part of who you are, the burden disappears. The sūra says this not as a piece of advice but by changing the mood of the verb.

## Where this section does not speak with certainty

At several points throughout the text a language of certainty was deliberately not used; the reader needs to know them:

- **The occasion of revelation.** Two different accounts transmitted for verses 2-3 were conveyed; no preference was adopted between them and neither was presented as the definite occasion of revelation. No ruling was issued on the degrees of soundness of the reports.

- **The relation of the perfect form at 61/1 to the rest of the sūra.** This link was marked as my own reading, not as a transmitted report.

- **The use of *nikāḥu'l-maqt* at 61/3.** This naming, belonging to the period of the Jāhiliyya, was given with the caveat "it is transmitted that"; that the word occurs at al-Nisāʾ 4/22, on the other hand, rests directly on the text of the verse.

- **The explanation of *marṣūṣ* at 61/4 as "sealed with lead".** It was stated that this explanation is made in the classical commentaries; the root's relation to *raṣāṣ* (lead) was conveyed within that same frame. The information about the building technique was given as a general historical record; no scientific claim was loaded onto the verse.

- **The relation of 61/4 to 61/2-3** (that one who does not keep his word cannot hold the rank) was marked as my own inference; the verse does not establish this link explicitly.

- **Which text the good news of Aḥmad at 61/6 points to.** **No claim at all was established on this matter**, and the ground for that was written plainly. The classical approach was conveyed as a report; no comparison was made upon any external text, because it cannot be verified.

- **Whether the *Aḥmad* pattern at 61/6 is to be read in the direction of the agent or of the patient.** The two readings were given in a table; no preference was adopted.

- **The referent of the pronoun *jāʾahum* at 61/6** (Jesus, or the messenger announced). Two readings were given; no firm preference was adopted.

- **The function of the *lām* in the construction *yurīdūna li-yuṭfiʾū* at 61/8.** Two views were given in a table; the second was found the more interesting, but it was written that it is not binding.

- **The observation made on the *nūr* / *nār* distinction at 61/8** was explicitly marked as **an observation about the language**; it was written that it is not a claim about physics.
