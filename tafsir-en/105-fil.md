# 105. Sūrat al-Fīl

Revealed in Mecca; one of the early sūras. Five verses.

The sūra steps outside the Qurʾān's customary manner of narration. When the Qurʾān speaks of the past it generally names distant peoples: ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Noah (Nūḥ), Pharaoh. Here, instead, it points to an event that took place in **the addressee's own city, in the lifetime of his own fathers, and still fresh in oral memory**. The proof is not fetched from far off; it is drawn out of the ground the addressee stands on.

There is also this. Sūrat al-Fīl does not stand like a sentence complete in itself. Its close (`fa-jaʿalahum ka-ʿaṣfin maʾkūl`) ends at a point, but the Sūrat Quraysh that comes after it begins with a **lām** — "for…". How these two sūras are joined is one of the oldest debates in exegesis, and it will be handled in detail at the head of Sūrat Quraysh. For now it is enough to keep this in mind: this sūra does not look as though it was built to be read on its own.

---

## The historical frame: what we know, and from where

What is most often confused in this sūra is the boundary between **what the Qurʾān says and what the reports relate**. Let us draw that boundary first, and then move to the verses.

**The whole of what the text of the Qurʾān says is this:**

- There was a community called "the companions of the elephant".
- They had a *kayd* (a hidden plan), and God brought that plan to nothing.
- Birds were sent upon them.
- The birds were pelting them with stones of "sijjīl".
- In the end they became like straw eaten up.

**What the Qurʾān does not say:** The commander's name is not mentioned. Where the army came from is not said. What the target was — to destroy the Kaʿba, or something else — is not expressly stated. The number of the elephants, the year of the event, the kind of the birds, the number of the dead: none of it is there. Not even the name of the city is named.

All of this comes from **the biographical and historical reports**. These reports are not worthless; but they are not of the same degree of certainty as the text of the Qurʾān, and they diverge from one another as well.

### What the reports relate

The narrative gathered in Ibn Isḥāq's Sīra (in the transmission of Ibn Hishām) and in al-Ṭabarī's History runs, in its main lines, as follows: in Yemen, under Abyssinian (Aksumite) rule, there was a governor and commander named **Abraha**. He had a great church built at Ṣanʿāʾ; he wanted to turn the Arabs, who went to Mecca for the pilgrimage, towards that building. When the church was defiled by an Arab he grew angry and marched to destroy the Kaʿba. There was an elephant (or elephants) in his army. The army was halted near Mecca; the birds came; the army broke up, and Abraha died on the way back.

The same cluster of reports carries other details as well: ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib's meeting with Abraha and his asking for the camels that had been taken from him; Abraha's saying, "I have come to destroy the Kaʿba, and you ask me about camels?"; ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib's answer, "The camels are mine; the House has an Owner, and He will protect it"; the elephant's kneeling down towards Mecca and refusing to advance; the elephant's name being Maḥmūd. These make up **the literary core of the narrative** and are supported by pre-Islamic poetry as well. But at the level of detail the reports diverge from one another; for that reason I give them all here with the note that this is how they are transmitted, not with a claim to historical certainty.

### What the inscriptions and the external sources confirm

Modern research confirms part of this picture independently:

**The Abyssinian occupation of Yemen is a fact, and it is dated.** In the first quarter of the sixth century the kingdom of Ḥimyar held sway in Yemen, and its king had adopted Judaism. A bloody assault on the Christian community at Najrān (around 523) is recorded in both Syriac and Greek sources. After this — with Byzantine support as well — the Aksumite king Kaleb (Ella Aṣbeḥa) crossed the Red Sea and took Yemen; this is around 525. Yemen thus passed under Abyssinian administration. This event has been connected with the story of the *aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd* (the companions of the trench) in Sūrat al-Burūj; the connection is present in most of the classical exegetes, but the Qurʾān does not establish that link explicitly.

**Abraha is a historical figure.** His name occurs not only in the Islamic sources but in stone inscriptions found in South Arabia. One of these is a long text describing the repair of the dam at Maʾrib (the inscription researchers refer to as CIH 541; dated to around 548), and in it Abraha's titles, the repair of the dam, a church at Maʾrib and the embassies that came to him are mentioned. Another is the Murayghān inscription, which describes a military expedition directed at central Arabia (around 552). That Abraha mounted an expedition from Yemen northward into the interior of Arabia is thus established by inscription.

The Byzantine historian Procopius, moreover, speaks of a ruler named "Abramos" who held power in Yemen in the middle of the sixth century, and writes that the emperor Justinian tried to persuade him to move against Persia, and that he did not keep his promises. This shows that Abraha was not merely a local figure but stood inside the contest between the two great powers of the period.

**The larger picture in the background:** in the sixth century there was a long rivalry between Byzantium and Sasanian Persia over the Red Sea–Indian Ocean trade routes. Byzantium had built up an ally in the south by way of Christian Aksum; Persia had tried to bring Yemen within its own sphere (and indeed in the decades after Abraha the Sasanians entered Yemen). In this picture Mecca stood in the intermediate zone that neither empire directly ruled, at the crossing of the caravan routes and around a religious centre. In this context the march of a governor of Yemen to the north is an economic and strategic move as much as a religious one. That Sūrat Quraysh, which comes after this sūra, speaks of trade is therefore no coincidence.

### The question of the date: when is the "Year of the Elephant"

Here one must be honest, because this point is genuinely disputed.

In the Islamic tradition the "Year of the Elephant" (*ʿām al-fīl*) has been used like the starting point of a calendar and has been equated with the year of the Prophet's birth. Converted into the Common Era, this equation falls at about **570**.

Against this, the dates the inscriptions give create a tension: the inscription describing Abraha's northern expedition is dated to around 552, and there is no clear evidence that Abraha was still in power long after that date. For this reason some modern researchers hold that the affair of the elephant may have occurred at a date earlier than 570, and that the traditional equation may have been constructed afterwards. Others say that the Murayghān expedition and the affair of the elephant were different expeditions.

We do not have a decisive resolution of this debate. It would be right to say this much: **the event itself and the existence of Abraha stand on solid historical ground; the exact year is disputed.** I do not give a year I am not sure of as though it were certain.

One further point should be added: this debate does not affect the sūra's value as proof. The sūra is not giving a history lesson; it is reminding its addressees of an event they knew. There is no record at all of the Meccans being able to say "no such thing happened" — and we are speaking of a community that objected to every claim the Qurʾān made. That silence is the strongest indication that the event was beyond dispute in the memory of Mecca.

## 105/1 — أَلَمْ تَرَ كَيْفَ فَعَلَ رَبُّكَ بِأَصْحَابِ الْفِيلِ

*A lam tara kayfa faʿala Rabbuka bi-aṣḥābi'l-fīl*
"Have you not seen what your Lord did to the companions of the elephant?"

### "Have you not seen" — a question put to one who did not see

The sūra's first difficulty is in its first words. According to the traditional dating the Prophet **was born** in the year this event took place; that is, he did not see it. For those who accept an earlier dating he had not yet been born. What, then, does "have you not seen?" mean?

The answer lies in the range of the Arabic verb **رأى (raʾā)**. This verb expresses seeing with the eye, but it also expresses knowing, grasping, coming to a conviction. English has the same extension: when we say "you see that this will not work", no one is speaking of the eye. In Arabic the usage is more firmly settled; indeed, for the usage that expresses seeing with the heart there is a separate grammatical behaviour (it takes two objects).

There is decisive evidence for this from within the Qurʾān. The same form is used for events no one could have seen with the eye:

- *"Have you not seen what your Lord did to ʿĀd?"* (al-Fajr 89/6) — the people of ʿĀd are centuries earlier than the addressees.
- *"Have you not seen those who went out from their homes in their thousands, in fear of death?"* (al-Baqara 2/243) — a very old, indeed an indeterminate, event.

So *a lam tara* is a formula the Qurʾān uses to say "look at this thing everyone knows". The source of the knowledge is not the eye but **widespread and undisputed report.** Its equivalent in today's language is: "Do you not remember that famous affair?"

There is a second reason as well. It is transmitted in the reports that people who survived the event crippled were seen begging in Mecca for years afterwards. If this report is sound, then "seeing" rests partly on an actual seeing as well as on a metaphor: if not the event itself, then its traces could still be seen. I transmit this detail as a report; I make no claim to certainty.

### كَيْفَ — "how", not "what happened"

The point to be noticed: the question is not **"what happened"** but **"how did He do it"**. In Arabic *kayfa* asks after the manner — that is, the occurrence of the event is taken as granted, and what is asked about is its form.

Rhetorically this is a very strong construction. It obliges the addressee to accept the event; the only thing opened to discussion is its detail. Had the sūra said "Do you know that your Lord did something to the companions of the elephant?", the door would have been left open to denial. When it says "Have you not seen how He did it?", the event is given, and what is asked to be thought about is **the manner**.

### رَبُّكَ — "your Lord"

The agent is stated openly, and it does not say "God"; it says **"Rabbuka"** — *your Lord*.

Two things are done at once:

First, the owner of the act is assigned. The Meccans knew that event, but they were able to read it as "the Kaʿba protected itself", "the blessedness of the House protected it". The sūra cuts this off: the one who acted is **the Lord**. Not the House, but the Owner of the House.

Second — and this is finer — the address is to the Prophet, who at that moment was in a position in Mecca of being alone, powerless and mocked. The expression "your Lord" builds a consolation: *the One who stands by you is the One who once halted a whole army.* The sūra is a sentence of assurance as much as it is a historical narrative. That the Qurʾān uses the address "rabbuka" most in the early Meccan sūras, where the Prophet is being supported, bears out this reading (al-Ḍuḥā 93/3, 5; al-Inshirāḥ 94/8, for example).

### أَصْحَابُ الْفِيل — "the companions of the elephant"

Root *ṣ-ḥ-b*: to accompany something, to be together with it, not to part from it. *Ṣāḥib* means a companion on the road; *ṣuḥba* is from the same root, "being together".

The Qurʾān names communities with this form very often, and it **ties the naming to the one thing that defines that community**: *aṣḥāb al-kahf* (the companions of the cave), *aṣḥāb al-ukhdūd* (the companions of the trench), *aṣḥāb al-sabt* (the companions of the sabbath), *aṣḥāb al-janna* (the companions of the garden).

There is a diminishment in the naming here, and it is deliberate. The commander of the army is not mentioned, his state is not mentioned, the imperial connection is not mentioned. They are named **by an animal**. This community, which in history called itself "the Abyssinian army", "the forces of the governor of Yemen", passes into the Qurʾān's record as "those beside the elephant". What they trusted in became their name.

This is a method the Qurʾān uses often: to name a person by what he leans on. When the outcome appears, the name turns into irony — the elephant not only failed to save them, it became the one thing left of them in memory.

### الْفِيل — the elephant itself

That the word passed into Arabic from Persian (*pīl*) is a view widely accepted among the lexicographers. The elephant is not an animal of Arabia; neither is the word.

The function of the elephant in this event has to be understood. In sixth-century Arabia the elephant is less an instrument of war than an instrument of **display**. The elephant that walks before the army through a region whose people had seen camels and horses but never an elephant is an element of propaganda: *we hold a power you do not know.* In today's language, a show of force for the sake of deterrence.

The whole logic of the sūra is built here. The largest animal was brought; what halted it was a bird. The scale is inverted, and this inversion is the sūra's main argument: **power is not measured by size.**

### Context — the place of the sūra in the muṣḥaf

Immediately before Sūrat al-Fīl comes Sūrat al-Humaza, and there the man *"who amasses wealth and counts it over and over, supposing that his wealth will make him immortal"* is described (al-Humaza 104/2-3). That is, the **individual** illusion of power.

Sūrat al-Fīl describes the **collective** form of the same illusion: an army, an elephant, imperial backing. The end of both is the same. And the Sūrat Quraysh that follows says where real security comes from. The three sūras together build a chain of argument: wealth cannot protect, an army cannot protect; the one who protects is **the Lord of the House**.

## 105/2 — أَلَمْ يَجْعَلْ كَيْدَهُمْ فِي تَضْلِيلٍ

*A lam yajʿal kaydahum fī taḍlīl*
"Did He not bring their scheme to nothing?"

### A second question, in the same form

The verse again begins with a negative question. In Arabic a negative question (*a lam*) is in fact a strong affirmation: "did He not do it?" = "of course He did, and you know it too." It is the same thing we do in English with "and did he not do exactly that?"

That the two verses come one after the other in the same form shows that the two are **two faces of a single event**: the first verse shows the event from outside ("what He did"), the second gives the inner logic of the event ("He brought their plans to nothing").

### كَيْد — the scheme

Root *k-y-d*. In the dictionary: to try to do something secretly and by an indirect route; to lay a trick; to prepare an arrangement by which the other party will be harmed without noticing it.

The word's most decisive property is **secrecy**. An open attack is not called *kayd*; *kayd* is a calculated, planned mechanism that the other side cannot see. For this reason the Qurʾān generally uses the word *kayd* for the arrangement the strong set against the weak.

A question arises here: if Abraha marched openly with his army, where is the hidden plan? The answer the classical exegetes give is reasonable: the *kayd* is not the march of the army but **the calculation behind the intention.** According to the report, on the surface there was a matter of building a temple and a centre of pilgrimage; the real target was to do away with Mecca's position in the region. That is, there was as much a **strategy** as a military movement, and a strategy is by definition hidden.

The root's other occurrences in the Qurʾān open the horizon:

- *"Thus did We contrive for Joseph (Yūsuf)"* (Yūsuf 12/76) — here *kayd* is ascribed to God and is not negative; it means "the fine measure that turns their calculation back upon them".
- *"They are devising a scheme, and I am devising a scheme"* (al-Ṭāriq 86/15-16) — these two verses are like the general statement of the principle of Sūrat al-Fīl.

The measure that follows from this is: what the Qurʾān makes a matter of discussion is not the evil plan itself but **in whose hand the plan will come to its end.** Making a plan is not forbidden; what is said is that the final verdict on a plan does not lie with man.

### تَضْلِيل — being brought to nothing

Root *ḍ-l-l*: to lose the way, to stray, to fail to find the target. *Ḍalāl* is going astray; *ḍāll* is one who strays.

The word here is on the **tafʿīl** pattern (*taḍlīl*), and in Arabic this pattern conveys causation: not to stray but **to make stray**; not to be lost but **to cause to be lost**.

To render it "being brought to nothing" is right, but it misses the picture in the word. The real picture is this: a plan is like a traveller who has set out towards his destination. *Taḍlīl* is to make that traveller lose his way. The plan is not destroyed, it is **put off its road.** The army was not repulsed, nor was it annihilated — it failed to arrive. The difference between the two fits exactly the form of the event the sūra describes.

### فِي تَضْلِيلٍ — "inside a bringing to nothing"

Here is the real point in the word order. The verse does not say "He brought their scheme to nothing"; it says **"He placed their scheme *inside* a bringing-to-nothing"**. The verb is *jaʿala* (to make, to place), the particle is *fī* (inside).

In Arabic *fī* conveys containment: a vessel, a taking-in, an enclosing. That is, the plan has been **set inside** the state of coming to nothing; no blow fell upon it from outside, it fell into a condition that surrounded it from beginning to end.

The meaning of this is: the plan did not break down at some one point, **it did not work at any point.** The march was in vain, the preparation was in vain, the elephants were in vain. Coming to nothing is not a stage of the plan but its element.

There is an indefiniteness (tanwīn) too: *taḍlīlin*, "a bringing to nothing". In Arabic indefiniteness here conveys magnification (*taʿẓīm*): not an ordinary one, but one hard to describe.

### What this says to the present

The measure the two verses build together is this: **the size of the preparation is no guarantee of the outcome.**

This can be read as a sentence of religious consolation; but it is at the same time an observable rule of history. In military history it is not rare for a superior force to be caught on a small variable that was not reckoned with: climate, disease, logistics, terrain. How Abraha's expedition ended is disputed; that the expedition ended and failed to reach its target is not.

What the sūra does is to assign an agent to this fact. It is possible to read it as "coincidence" as well; the sūra reads it otherwise, and it gives the ground for its reading in the verses that follow.

## 105/3 — وَأَرْسَلَ عَلَيْهِمْ طَيْرًا أَبَابِيلَ

*Wa arsala ʿalayhim ṭayran abābīl*
"He sent upon them birds, flock upon flock."

### أَرْسَلَ — He sent

Root *r-s-l*: to let loose, to send, to let flow. From the same root *rasūl* (a messenger who is sent), *risāla* (the office of messenger), *mursal* (sent).

The choice of the word is meaningful. It does not say "the birds came"; it says they **were sent**. That is, the birds moved not by their own decision but by a command. The subject of the verb continues from the preceding verse: *Rabbuka*.

The same root is used in the Qurʾān for the sending of the wind and of punishment as well. This shows how the Qurʾān sees natural events: nature is not a mechanism answerable to itself but **an army under orders.** *"None knows the hosts of your Lord but He"* (al-Muddaththir 74/31).

### عَلَيْهِم — "upon them"

The particle *ʿalā* conveys coming from above and bringing under pressure. In the Qurʾān punishment is sent "upon them", while mercy is generally expressed with other particles. A small particle draws the direction of the event: from above downward.

There is one more inversion here. The army was coming on foot, over the ground. The answer came from the sky. The battle was not given on the plane the army commanded.

### طَيْرًا — birds

Root *ṭ-y-r*: to fly. *Ṭayr* can be used in Arabic both as a singular and as a collective noun; here it is in the plural or generic sense.

The verse does not give the **kind** of the birds. Various descriptions circulate in the reports (that they resembled swallows, that they were sea birds, that they were black or green); these contradict one another and have no counterpart in the text of the Qurʾān. What the sūra is concerned with is not the species of the birds but that the birds are **birds**: the smallness of what is set against the elephant.

### أَبَابِيل — flock upon flock

This is the most debated word in the sūra.

**On the meaning there is broad agreement:** groups coming one after another, troop by troop, flock upon flock. The birds came not as a single mass but in waves that followed one another. The best rendering in English is a repetition: "flock upon flock", "troop after troop" — because in English too repetition conveys a scattered multiplicity.

**The debate is over the singular.** Three positions are found in the classical linguistic sources:

| View | Ground |
|---|---|
| It has no singular; the word is used directly with a plural sense | The famous saying ascribed to al-Farrāʾ: "I have not heard a singular for it." Arabic has plural forms with no singular. |
| Its singular is *ibbāla* | *Ibbāla* means a bundle or sheaf bound together; a cluster of birds too was seen as a kind of bundle |
| Its singular is a form such as *ibbīl* or *ibbawl* | Weaker proposals transmitted in the dictionaries |

The importance of this debate lies not in the technical detail but here: **the word itself is an unfamiliar word even within Arabic.** The Qurʾān does not use an ordinary expression for "a crowd of birds"; it chooses a rare word that leaves a mark on the ear. The sound-texture of the sūra bears this out as well: the rhyme *abābīl* / *sijjīl* / *maʾkūl*, with its long *ī* and *ū* sounds, makes the stretching out and spreading of the event audible.

A grammatical note: the word *abābīl* comes in the verse as a **diptote** (*ghayr munṣarif*), that is, it takes no tanwīn. This indicates that the plural is in one of the highest plural patterns of Arabic, the one called *muntahā al-jumūʿ* — it carries an emphasis of multiplicity within itself too.

### The subtlety in the word order: singular "ṭayr", plural "abābīl"

*Ṭayran* is indefinite and singular-generic in structure, while its adjective *abābīl* is plural. In Arabic a generic noun can take a plural adjective; but here a visual effect arises: there is a single "birdness" in the sky, and yet that birdness comes piece by piece, wave upon wave. It is the same picture we build in English when we say "the sky was all bird, flock upon flock".

## 105/4 — تَرْمِيهِم بِحِجَارَةٍ مِّن سِجِّيلٍ

*Tarmīhim bi-ḥijāratin min sijjīl*
"They were pelting them with stones of sijjīl."

### تَرْمِي — pelting

Root *r-m-y*: to throw, to hurl, to take aim and throw. The same root is used for the *ramy* (the throwing of stones) in the rite of pilgrimage.

The verb is in the **imperfect**, the present-continuous form. All the preceding verses were in the past tense: *faʿala*, *yajʿal*, *arsala*. Here the tense changes.

This is a known technique of Arabic narration: at the most critical moment of an event one passes from the past tense into the present, and the scene is **brought before the eye**. We do the same in English: "He got up, he went to the door, and there she is…" Here the Qurʾān leaves off narrating the event and begins to show it. The addressee is no longer receiving news; he is watching the scene.

The verb is feminine singular because its subject, *ṭayr*, is a generic noun; in Arabic generic nouns can be used with a feminine singular verb.

### حِجَارَة — stones

The plural of *ḥajar*. Ordinary stone. Not an extraordinary object but the best-known, the cheapest, the most widespread of substances.

The series of contrasts is completed here: **elephant ↔ bird**, **army ↔ flock**, **weapon ↔ stone.** The sūra puts no superior instrument into the other side's hand. Had it done so, one could have said "they were defeated by a stronger army". It does not.

### سِجِّيل — sijjīl

The origin of the word has been disputed since the classical period.

**The first view — it is a borrowing from Persian.** It is said that the words *sang* (stone) and *gil* (clay) combined and were Arabised: "stone-clay", that is, stone hardened out of baked clay. This view is widely transmitted in the classical exegetical and lexicographical tradition, and most modern linguists incline in this direction as well. Put together with the preceding word, an interesting picture emerges: in this sūra of five verses two words (*fīl* and *sijjīl*) are counted as being of Persian origin. That the Qurʾān uses words that have entered the language from outside has to do with its addressees already knowing those words; the relation between Persia and Yemen and the Hijaz makes such a passage unremarkable.

**The second view — it is from the Arabic root *s-j-l*.** From this root comes *sijill* (a written record, a register). On this view *sijjīl* means "what has been written", that is, **recorded, settled by decree**; as though it were written upon the stones whose lot each was. There is a usage in the Qurʾān that supports this reading: *"The day We shall roll up the sky as a sijill rolls up the written sheets"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/104).

**The third — a combining reading.** Some accept both senses: baked clay as substance, decreed as verdict. This is not impossible in language, but it is forced; caution is called for.

**On which side is the Qurʾān's own testimony?** The word occurs in three places in the Qurʾān, and in all three together with "stone":

- Hūd 11/82 — the people of Lot (Lūṭ): *"We rained upon them stones of sijjīl, piled one upon another"*
- al-Ḥijr 15/74 — again the people of Lot: *"and We rained upon them stones of sijjīl"*
- al-Fīl 105/4 — here

What is decisive is this: **the same event is described elsewhere with a different word.** Of the people of Lot it is said at al-Dhāriyāt 51/33 *"stones of clay"* (ḥijāratan min ṭīn). That is, within the Qurʾān itself an equation *sijjīl* ≈ *ṭīn* (clay) is established. This markedly supports the first view — the baked-clay reading. The Qurʾān's exegesis by the Qurʾān gives a clear result here.

The same connection does one more thing: it binds Sūrat al-Fīl and the story of Lot to one another through a single word. Both events end with an intervention from the sky at the moment when a community supposed its own secure order to be indestructible.

### The question of the size of the stones

It is transmitted in the reports that the stones were the size of a chickpea or a lentil, and that each bird carried three stones (one in its beak, two in its claws). None of these details is in the Qurʾān; it is material of report, and it is not uniform within itself either. The force of the narrative comes in any case not from size but from the difference of scale.

## 105/5 — فَجَعَلَهُمْ كَعَصْفٍ مَّأْكُولٍ

*Fa-jaʿalahum ka-ʿaṣfin maʾkūl*
"So He turned them into straw eaten up."

### فَ — the particle of result

The *fāʾ* at the head conveys "immediately after" and "as a result of this". No process, no struggle, no counter-move is narrated in between. The stones were thrown — the outcome.

There is no battle scene at all in the sūra. The army does not resist, does not flee, does not fight. This is a verdict the narration establishes by its own manner: the other side found no opportunity to be the subject of the event.

### جَعَلَ — to turn into

This verb had occurred in the second verse as well: *"He **placed** their scheme inside a bringing-to-nothing"*. The same verb is used at two different points of the sūra, and both convey a transformation. The plan was put into a state; then those who made the plan were put into a state. The same hand does both.

### عَصْف — the stalk of the crop, straw

Root *ʿ-ṣ-f*. What the word describes: the part of the crop left over apart from the grain — stalk, leaf, husk, chaff. The dry residue left in the field after the harvest and scattered about by the wind.

From the same root comes *ʿāṣif*: a violent wind that sweeps things away. This word occurs in the description *"a violent wind"* (Yūnus 10/22). The root carries the idea of being scattered within it.

Its clearest occurrence in the Qurʾān is in Sūrat al-Raḥmān: *"the grain with its stalks and leaves, and the fragrant plants"* (al-Raḥmān 55/12). There *ʿaṣf* is the worthless part beside the grain. Even where it is being counted as a favour, the grain and the stalk are kept apart.

This is the first layer of the comparison: an army — with its elephants, its cavalry, its imperial backing — is likened to **the dry stalk left in a field.** Not the grain, which has an inside, but the husk, which has only an outside.

### مَأْكُول — eaten

Root *ʾ-k-l*: to eat. *Maʾkūl* is the passive participle: that which has been eaten.

The construction here brings the comparison down one step further: not merely "like straw", but "like **eaten** straw". The classical exegetes are found to explain this expression in two ways:

**The first:** fodder that an animal has eaten, digested and passed out. That is, a residue chewed once more, worked over, now good for nothing at all. On this reading the sūra would be describing the scattered state of the bodies.

**The second:** a crop eaten out from within by worm or insect. Seen from outside the ear still stands and looks whole; but it has been emptied inside. Touched by the hand, it falls apart.

The second reading sits more sharply on the situation the event describes, and accords better with the sūra as a whole. For what the sūra has been working on from the beginning is **the difference between apparent size and real durability**. The elephant looks large; the army looks imposing; the ear of grain stands upright. In all three there is a mismatch between the outward appearance and the emptiness within.

I offer this not as a preference adopted but as a strong reading; neither interpretation runs against the text.

### The sound-texture of the sūra

Look at the rhyme of the five verses: *fīl* — *taḍlīl* — *abābīl* — *sijjīl* — *maʾkūl*. Four end with a long *ī*, the last with *ū*. A long vowel stretches the sound out and weights its close; the vowel of the last verse changes and so brings the reader to a stop. In short sūras the sound is a carrier of the meaning; so it is here.

## What happened? — the reports, the naturalistic readings, and the limit

There is a debate that has revived in the modern period around this sūra, and it must be conveyed honestly.

A report occurs in the classical exegetical material: it is said that in that year **smallpox** (*judarī*) and **measles** (*ḥaṣba*) were seen for the first time in the Arabian peninsula. This report is found in the early sources; it is a report whose chain and reliability have to be assessed separately.

In the modern period **Muḥammad ʿAbduh**, in his commentary on the juzʾ ʿamma, took up this report and voiced the following possibility: the birds that were sent may have been carriers of disease, and "stones of sijjīl" may be understood as the particles or microbes that spread the disease. This interpretation was spread abroad through Rashīd Riḍā and was much debated in the twentieth century.

Let us set the views side by side:

| Reading | Ground | Weak point |
|---|---|---|
| The literal reading: birds really came, and really threw stones | The wording of the verse is plain: *ṭayr*, *ḥijāra*, *tarmī*. The Qurʾān gives no sign of figurative speech here. The extraordinariness is in any case the reason for the event. | It establishes no connection with the record of disease in the reports |
| The naturalistic reading: an epidemic | It takes account of the record of disease in the reports; historically it offers a plausible mechanism for the breaking up of the army | In the verse **the agent is the birds** and the verb is "to throw". A disease does not throw stones. It strains the text considerably. Moreover, turning "bird" into microbe rests on no usage of the word in Arabic at all |
| The combined reading: the birds came, the stones struck, and afterwards an epidemic spread as well | It leaves both data in place | The Qurʾān does not speak of a subsequent epidemic; this is information added from outside the text |

**My assessment** — and I record that it is not binding: the wording of the verse supports the literal reading. The subject of the verb *tarmīhim* (they pelt them) is plainly the birds; to turn this sentence into the description of an epidemic would be to ask more of the grammar than it allows. On the other hand, the concern of those who defend the naturalistic reading deserves to be taken seriously: no event the Qurʾān relates is a "magic" outside the working of nature; there are always causes. But not knowing what the cause was and determining the cause in defiance of the text are not the same thing.

This too should be added: the argument of the sūra stands on either reading. The sūra does not make the **physical mechanism** of the event its proof; it makes the **failure to reach the target** its proof.

## The central question of the sūra: why was the House of an idolatrous city protected?

The theological difficulty here is plain and should not be passed over. At the time of the event there were idols in the Kaʿba. The people of Mecca were inside a religious order that the Qurʾān would afterwards criticise severely. What, then, was protected?

Several answers stand one upon another:

**First: what was protected was not the Meccans but the House.** The sūra does not say "what your Lord did to you"; it says "what He did to the companions of the elephant". The Meccans are not the heroes of the event but its witnesses. It is transmitted in the reports that the Meccans withdrew to the hills and watched the event; if this report is sound, then the narrative itself keeps them in a passive position.

**Second: the House does not belong to those who use it at a given moment.** The Qurʾān says that the Kaʿba has a foundation reaching back to Abraham (Ibrāhīm): *"The first House established for people is the blessed House at Mecca"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/96). The House is a trust in the hands of the people of a period; when its people are corrupted, the House is not abolished. The misuse of a trust does not void the trust itself.

**Third: protection is not approval.** The Qurʾān says this explicitly afterwards. While the Meccans were claiming to be the protectors of that House, this came: *"Why should God not punish them, when they bar people from the Sacred Mosque? They are not its guardians; its guardians are only those who are mindful of God"* (al-Anfāl 8/34). That is, the protection seen in the affair of the elephant was not a confirmation given to the religious condition of Mecca — and it is the Qurʾān itself that says so.

**Fourth — and historically the most concrete: the protection is for the sake of the future.** Had the Kaʿba been destroyed, Mecca's regional position and the commercial order that position provided would have collapsed. The city in which the Prophet was to be born, in which revelation was to come down, in which the first community was to be formed, would have broken up. That Sūrat Quraysh comes immediately after works this out openly: the House was protected → trade continued → the city stayed standing. Sūrat Quraysh adds a ruling to the end of this chain: *then let them serve the Lord of this House.*

That the Prophet himself made this connection is seen in the hadith sources. It is transmitted that in the sermon he gave on the day of the conquest of Mecca, while speaking of the inviolability of Mecca, he said, *"God held back the elephant from Mecca, and gave His Messenger and the believers mastery over it"* (it is found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim). That is, the conquest has been read as the continuation of the affair of the elephant: in both cases it was not an army that determined the fate of the city.

There is a further report: it is transmitted that at al-Ḥudaybiya, when the Prophet's camel knelt down and would not rise and the Companions said "she has turned stubborn", he said, *"She has not turned stubborn; the One who held back the elephant has held her back too"* (al-Bukhārī). This shows how living a memory the sūra was in the language of the first community: an animal's refusal to walk had become a sign now recognised.

## What this says to the present

**First: the sūra says that power and outcome are not the same thing.** Abraha held in his hand every advantage of the period: the backing of an empire, an organised army, a war animal never seen in the region, an economic and religious strategy. The side that stood against him had nothing at all — the Meccans did not even try to resist. The lesson the sūra gives is not "the weak always win"; there is no such rule, and the Qurʾān gives no such promise. The lesson it gives is this: **the whole of the reckoning is not in the hand of the one who reckons.** This is at once a warning to the strong and a support for the powerless.

**Second: there is an "elephant" in every age.** The elephant was for that day an unheard-of technology — a power brought out before the addressee, which appeared impossible to answer. Every period has its own elephant: capital, weaponry, information, scale. What the sūra says is not that these are unimportant but that they are **not final**. What has never been seen is not unbeatable.

**Third: making the sacred an instrument of trade is an old business.** In the form the reports relate it, the starting point of the event is an attempt to turn the traffic of pilgrimage from one centre to another. That is, belief was seen as an item of revenue. This motive is one that recurs in the history of institutions and of religious centres, and it takes no side — in the sūra that follows, the Qurʾān puts the same question to the Meccans themselves: what are you confusing this House with, its revenue or its Lord?

**Fourth: the sūra counts looking at history as a method of knowledge.** The question "have you not seen" points to the past as a source of knowledge. The Qurʾān expects of its addressee not that he demand a miracle but that he **think about an event that has already happened**. The proof is not something promised in the future but a trace left behind.
