# 65. Sūrat al-Ṭalāq

Twelve verses. It is accepted as having come down in the Medinan period. It takes its name from the verb in its first verse: **ṭalāq** — divorce.

This is one of the densest **legal** texts in the Qurʾān. Verses 226-242 of Sūrat al-Baqara handle the same subject in a wider frame; Sūrat al-Ṭalāq narrows onto a small field and goes into detail there: **when** a divorce is to be pronounced, **how its period is to be counted**, **where the woman is to live** throughout that period, **who pays what**, and **how the process is to be closed**.

But the sūra is not a pure legal text. Between the rulings there are sentences placed that look as though they had no direct bearing on the ruling — and some of the most quoted sentences in the whole Qurʾān are exactly these: *"Whoever guards himself against God, He opens for him a way out"*, *"He provides for him from where he does not reckon"*, *"God will bring ease after hardship"*. These sentences generally circulate torn from their contexts. One of the things this section will do is put them back where they came from — because where they came from does not change what they say, but it does **make them heavier.**

## What the sūra is doing

It is possible to say what the sūra does in a single sentence: **it takes a separation out of being an event left to the whim of the parties and turns it into a process that is counted, witnessed, and has a known address.**

It does this by three separate mechanisms:

**First: time.** The divorce does not finish in a moment. It is begun at a determined time (*li-ʿiddatihinna*), counted through a determined period (*aḥṣu'l-ʿidda*), and until that period is out the parties are not held to be severed from one another. A person's decision in a moment of anger does not take effect at that moment.

**Second: place.** The woman is not put out of her house, and she does not leave it herself. That is, for the whole period the two parties remain under one roof, or at least at one address. This makes the period be lived **in fact** and not merely on the calendar.

**Third: record.** When the period ends, the decision — whether to continue or to separate — is made final before two just witnesses. A private matter is thus turned into a record open to the public.

The three mechanisms have a single common aim, and the sūra states it itself at the end of the first verse: **لَا تَدْرِى لَعَلَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحْدِثُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ أَمْرًا** — "you do not know; it may be that God will bring about something new after this."

That is, the reason for drawing the process out is that a human being **does not know the future.** The one deciding cannot know at that moment whether his decision is right. So the law buys him time against his own ignorance.

The second half of the sūra goes somewhere else entirely: towns destroyed, a severe reckoning, and at the very end seven heavens. At first sight this transition looks disconnected. It is not; as I shall show below, there is a single word that binds the two halves together: **أَمْر.**

## How we shall read a legal text

There is a question of method here that I must be open about from the start.

This sūra states rulings. And upon these rulings a wide body of jurisprudence has been built over fourteen centuries: the kinds of *ṭalāq*, the distinction between revocable and irrevocable, what the waiting period is to be in which circumstances, upon whom and when and in what amount lodging and maintenance become obligatory, whether the witnessing counts as a condition or as a recommended act. All of these debates are real and none of them will be settled here.

**This commentary does not issue legal rulings.** For a ruling to be acted upon in a given matter, the works of jurisprudence must be consulted. The work to be done here is a different one:

- To show **the logic of the regulation**: what this rule prevents, whom it protects, which harm it forestalls.
- To open up **the words**: which root was chosen, what the unchosen alternative was, what the pattern adds.
- **Not to conceal the disagreement:** where the views have parted, to say that they have parted, but not to take a side.

And one further note: **I shall not give the names of those who hold a view where I am not sure of it.** Sentences such as "according to the Ḥanafīs it is thus" can be written only when one is genuinely certain; otherwise a view is ascribed to a school that never held it. For this reason I shall in most places say "according to one view… according to another view…". This is not an evasion; it is what the truthfulness rule of the method (`STYLE.md`) requires.

**One further warning, from the opposite direction.** This sūra is a text that has fallen into the middle of modern debates, and it is read under a two-sided pressure: one side takes it as a document to be defended, the other as a document to be indicted. Both make the same mistake — they make the text answer a question other than its own.

The work to be done here is this: **to describe what the regulation does and what it assumes.** In seventh-century Arabia a divorced woman had no independent economic standing: no order in which she could hold property in her own name, no source of income, no institution before which she could claim her right. This holds in large measure for all the Mediterranean and Near Eastern societies of that period. To understand what a legal text does requires reading it against **the situation that preceded it** and against **the alternatives available in its own environment**. I shall do that. But I shall not draw from that reading the conclusion that the text endorses every present-day application — because the text is not the application.

## The name of the sūra

**الطَّلَاق (al-Ṭalāq).** Taken from the verb in the first verse. It is transmitted that in the sources the sūra is also called **سُورَةُ النِّسَاءِ الْقُصْرَى** — "the short Sūrat al-Nisāʾ" — since Sūrat al-Nisāʾ too carries a large part of the rulings concerning women, and this sūra was counted its short counterpart. That this naming was in use in the early period is recorded in the sources.

One point about the name: the sūra bears the name "divorce", but the greater part of its content is about **how divorce is to be delayed** and **how the divorced woman is to be protected**. That is, the name of the sūra states its subject; it does not state its thesis.

## Medinan

There is no serious disagreement in the sources about the sūra's having come down in the Medinan period. There are internal data pointing to this as well:

- That it contains detailed legal regulation. Rulings at this density are not found in the Meccan sūras.
- That it treats matters — witnessing, maintenance, lodging — that presuppose the institutional order of a community.
- That the greater part of the address is in the form *yā ayyuha'l-nabiyy* and *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū*.

**Occasion of revelation.** Various events are transmitted in the sources for the sūra as a whole or for particular verses. Since I am not sure of the soundness of these reports or of their agreement with one another, I do not set down any particular event here. The sūra's own text already shows its context: an environment in which divorce was in fact practised within a community, and in which that practice stood in need of regulation.

## The structure of the sūra

The sūra divides into two halves, and the second half settles over the first like a frame.

| Part | Verses | Subject | Language |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I. Ruling** | 1-7 | The time of the divorce, the counting of the waiting period, remaining in the dwelling, witnessing, maintenance, the wage for suckling | Imperative, second person plural |
| **II. Warning** | 8-10 | The end of the towns that went out from the command; address to *ūlu'l-albāb* | Perfect, narrative |
| **III. Close** | 11-12 | The messenger, the verses, the bringing out from darkness into light; and seven heavens | Imperfect, cosmic scale |

And between the parts there is a fourth layer scattered through the first part: **the sentences of *taqwā*.** In the sūra the pattern *"whoever guards himself against God…"* occurs three times (in verses 2, 4 and 5; and also in verses 1 and 10 in the imperative, *ittaqu'llāh*). Each time it comes immediately after a ruling and attaches a return to that ruling:

| Place | Promise |
|---|---|
| **65/2-3** | *makhrajan* (a way out) + *yarzuqhu min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasib* (provision from where he does not reckon) |
| **65/4** | *min amrihi yusrā* (ease in his affair) |
| **65/5** | *yukaffir ʿanhu sayyiʾātih* + *yuʿẓim lahu ajrā* (the covering of misdeeds and the enlarging of the reward) |

This is the architecture of the sūra: **beside every ruling a promise has been set.** And all the promises are tied to the same condition — *man yattaqi'llāh*. As we shall see verse by verse below, this is no accidental repetition; it has to do with the real problem the sūra is trying to solve.

**The problem is this:** how is a person to be brought to obey a rule that appears to be against his own interest? At the moment of divorce the man facing the lawgiver is angry, his relations with the other party have broken down, and what is laid upon him — keeping the woman in the house, paying maintenance, waiting out a period — appears to him as a cost. The sūra sets something against that cost. What it sets there is not a sanction (there is no penal ruling in the sūra); it is a **promise of return.**

And the scene of destruction in the second part attaches exactly here: the breaking of the rule does not go unpunished, but the address of the punishment is not a court.

## 65/1 — يَا أَيُّهَا ٱلنَّبِىُّ إِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ ٱلنِّسَآءَ فَطَلِّقُوهُنَّ لِعِدَّتِهِنَّ وَأَحْصُوا۟ ٱلْعِدَّةَ وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ رَبَّكُمْ لَا تُخْرِجُوهُنَّ مِنۢ بُيُوتِهِنَّ وَلَا يَخْرُجْنَ إِلَّآ أَن يَأْتِينَ بِفَٰحِشَةٍ مُّبَيِّنَةٍ وَتِلْكَ حُدُودُ ٱللَّهِ وَمَن يَتَعَدَّ حُدُودَ ٱللَّهِ فَقَدْ ظَلَمَ نَفْسَهُ لَا تَدْرِى لَعَلَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحْدِثُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ أَمْرًا

*Yā ayyuha'l-nabiyyu idhā ṭallaqtumu'l-nisāʾa fa-ṭalliqūhunna li-ʿiddatihinna wa aḥṣu'l-ʿidda, wa'ttaqu'llāha rabbakum, lā tukhrijūhunna min buyūtihinna wa lā yakhrujna illā an yaʾtīna bi-fāḥishatin mubayyina, wa tilka ḥudūdu'llāh, wa man yataʿadda ḥudūda'llāhi fa-qad ẓalama nafsah, lā tadrī laʿalla'llāha yuḥdithu baʿda dhālika amrā*

"O Prophet! When you divorce women, divorce them towards their waiting period, and count the period. Guard yourselves against God, your Lord. Do not put them out of their houses, and let them not go out — unless they commit a clear indecency. These are the limits of God; and whoever transgresses the limits of God has wronged himself. You do not know; it may be that God will bring about something new after this."

The longest verse of the sūra, and its whole programme is here. It contains six separate rulings or qualifications. I shall take them in order.

### Word order: singular address, plural command

The verse opens with a grammatical oddity, and it is something that ought to be noticed at once.

**يَا أَيُّهَا ٱلنَّبِىُّ** — "O Prophet". Singular, definite, to one person.

**إِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ** — "when you divorce". **Plural.**

That is, the sentence calls out to one person and then immediately issues a ruling to a community. In the classical works of grammar several explanations are given for this construction:

| Explanation | Account |
|---|---|
| The address is to the Prophet, the ruling to the community | The Prophet is the representative of the community and the one addressed; what is said to him is said through him to everyone. The most widespread explanation. |
| The Prophet too falls within the ruling | He is included in the plural; he is not being set apart, he is being set in front. |
| The address announces the weight of the ruling | A ruling could have begun directly with "O you who believe". That it begins by calling upon the Prophet points to the importance of the matter. |

All three are defensible and they do not exclude one another. I want to add this — as my own reading: **the whole sūra advances by this double structure.** The first verse begins with a singular address, continues with plural imperatives, and at the end **returns to the singular**: *lā tadrī* — "you do not know". That is, the verse draws a circle. The rule is for everyone, but what is said at the end is said to each person one by one. I shall take up the reason for this in the final section.

It is also transmitted in the sources that there is a singular variant reading of the verse, *"O Prophet! When you divorce women…"*. The existence of this reading is on record; since I am not sure to which imām it belongs, I give no name. And since it does not substantially change the meaning, I do not dwell on it here either.

### طَلَّقَ — ṭalāq

The word that carries the name of the sūra. The root is **ط ل ق (ṭ-l-q)**, and the concrete sense of the root sits strikingly ill with what the English word "divorce" calls to mind.

**The centre of the root: to untie a bond, to let loose, to set free.**

- **طَلِيق (ṭalīq)** — one who has been set free; one released from captivity or from a bond.
- **إِطْلَاق (iṭlāq)** — releasing, setting loose. The derivative *muṭlaq* (unrestricted, unbound) belongs here as well.
- **نَاقَةٌ طَالِق (nāqa ṭāliq)** — a she-camel whose tether has been untied and which has been let out to pasture. This is the most concrete use of the word: the animal's rope is untied and the animal is left free to go.
- **طَلْقُ الْوَجْه (ṭalqu'l-wajh)** — open-faced, cheerful. The face's untying from its tension.
- **طَلْق** — the pangs of childbirth. The connection the lexicographers give: birth is the child's **being untied and parted** from the mother's body.

**And here there is something worth pausing over.** In Arabic the name of divorce is not **breaking** but **untying**. The word describes not a demolition but a **setting free.**

This has two consequences, and both need to be recorded:

**First — what the word assumes.** For a thing to be "let loose", it must first have been bound. The word *ṭalāq* assumes a **bond** behind it, and the name of that bond is set down explicitly in the Qurʾān: **مِيثَاقًا غَلِيظًا** — "a solemn covenant" (al-Nisāʾ 4/21). The same expression is used in the Qurʾān for the covenant taken from the prophets as well (al-Aḥzāb 33/7). That is, marriage is counted at the level of the word as a weighty contract; and *ṭalāq* is not the **annulment** of that contract but its **untying**.

**Second — the manner the word imposes.** The one who performs *ṭalāq* is the one who **sets the other free.** He is not disposing of a piece of property, he is not driving off an enemy; he is untying a bond. This choice of word agrees directly with the two qualifications *bi-maʿrūf* in the rest of the sūra (verse 2): both the keeping and the letting go are to be done "with the good that is known".

I offer this as a moral inference, not as a ruling that follows necessarily from the dictionary sense of the word. Languages do not always remember the origins of their words; but the Qurʾān had alternatives before it when it chose this word — *faskh*, *qaṭʿ* (to cut), *farq* — and in the second verse it will use one of them (*fāriqūhunna*).

**Pattern: تَفْعِيل (tafʿīl).** *Ṭallaqa* is Form II. This form generally conveys causation or intensity: *ṭalaqa* (he became free) → *ṭallaqa* (he set free, he let loose). That is, the subject of the verb is the party that sets the other free.

### إِذَا طَلَّقْتُمُ ٱلنِّسَآءَ — "when you divorce"

**إِذَا** — in Arabic this is used for conditions whose occurrence is certain or strongly probable; *in* conveys possibility (this distinction was recorded in `073-muzzemmil.md` and in `094-insirah.md`).

The use of *idhā* here is an acknowledgement: **divorce will occur.** The text does not position it as a deviation, an exception, a thing that ought not to be. It takes it as a situation that will occur and regulates it.

This is the basic posture of making law, and it is not always to be seen in religious texts. A text either forbids an act it does not approve of or ignores it. The Qurʾān here takes a third way: it does not forbid the act, but neither does it **leave it unattended.**

### فَطَلِّقُوهُنَّ لِعِدَّتِهِنَّ — "divorce them towards their waiting period"

The first ruling of the verse and one of its most debated expressions. The difficulty lies in the letter **لِ (lām)**.

**Why is it difficult?** Because in Arabic the particle *li-* covers a wide field: belonging, purpose, time, cause, direction. And which of these it is here is not said. The sentence does not say "divorce them during their waiting period" (it does not say *fī ʿiddatihinna*); it says "**for**/**towards** their waiting period".

The readings proposed in the classical commentaries:

| Reading | Meaning | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **A lām of time** | Divorce them at a time at which the waiting period can be counted; that is, at a moment at which the period can begin at once. | In Arabic *li-* conveys time: usages such as *"qitālin li-awwalihi"*. The most widespread reading. |
| **A lām of purpose** | Divorce them so that a waiting period may be kept; that is, let the divorce be made in such a way that a period of waiting follows it. | The purposive sense of the root. In agreement with the command *wa aḥṣu'l-ʿidda* that follows in the verse. |
| **A lām of direction (istiqbāl)** | Directed towards their waiting period, so as to come to face the period. | A fine variant of the preceding. |

All three readings come out at the same result in practice: **a divorce cannot be pronounced at a random moment; it is pronounced at a time at which the period can begin.** The difference between them is a technical point of grammar; it does not change the ruling.

**But which time is this time?** Here we are in the field of jurisprudence, and it will not be settled here. It is transmitted in the sources that there is wide acceptance of the view that the divorce should be pronounced at a time when the woman is not menstruating and when she has not been approached during that period of purity; a divorce pronounced in observance of this is called *ṭalāq sunnī*, one pronounced without it *ṭalāq bidʿī*. The details of this distinction, whether a *bidʿī* divorce is to be counted valid, and the consequences attached to that, have been disputed. **This commentary adopts no preference here.**

But something can be said about the **logic** of the regulation, and it needs to be said:

**The condition of time separates the decision from the moment of anger.** To set a condition of time is to insert a compulsory interval between the moment the decision is taken and the moment it is carried out. The man cannot divorce at the moment he is angry; he has to wait for the suitable time. And the waiting tests the decision itself.

This is the functional counterpart of the mechanism modern law calls a *cooling-off period*. Today the divorce law of many countries has similar intervals, and the ground given is the same: to prevent an irreversible decision from being taken in a passing state of feeling. I record this parallel not as a **claim** but as a **comparison** that makes the function of the regulation visible.

**A second function: the determinability of the period.** If the divorce could be pronounced at any time at all, when the waiting period began and when it would end would be open to dispute. To fix the time is to fix the period as well. And the next command comes precisely on top of this.

### وَأَحْصُوا۟ ٱلْعِدَّةَ — "and count the period"

One of the most important words of the sūra is here.

**عِدَّة — root ع د د (ʿ-d-d).** To count; number, tally. From the same root: *ʿadad* (number), *taʿdād* (a counting), *ʿidda* (a counted quantity), *maʿdūd* (numbered).

The word is rendered "waiting period"; but what it says in Arabic is narrower and sharper: **the counted period.** There is no idea of waiting in it; there is the idea of **counting**.

**This root was treated in `104-humeze.md`, and a finding was made there.** I do not repeat that analysis; a short reminder is enough: in treating the verb *ʿaddadahu* (he kept counting it) in Sūrat al-Humaza, this was recorded — **"number makes uncertainty measurable; it draws out of an uncontrollable field a controllable figure."** There this was the pathology of the man counting his wealth over and over: the counting did not remove the real uncertainty, it merely stood in its place.

**Here the same mechanism works in the opposite direction**, and this is one of the most striking contrasts between the two sections:

| | **al-Humaza 104/2** | **al-Ṭalāq 65/1** |
|---|---|---|
| Who counts | The owner of the wealth, in his own favour | The parties, as a rule requires |
| What is counted | Property | A period |
| What the counting serves | Its owner's inner unease — and does not remove it | The removal of uncertainty — and does remove it |
| Result | A false sense of security (*yaḥsabu*) | The prevention of arbitrariness |

That is, counting is in itself neither a good nor a bad operation. **What is counted, and in whose favour it is counted**, determines it.

**And what does the counting prevent here?** Concretely, this: one party's lengthening or shortening the period at his own pleasure.

In an order in which the period is not counted, this happens: the man says "the waiting period is over", the woman says "it is not over"; the man says "I have not divorced you yet", the woman says "you have". Where there is no number, every word carries as much weight as the power of the one saying it. And in that order the balance of power is known.

**Number puts measure in the place of power.** This is one of the most basic functions of law, and here it is done with a single word.

**أَحْصُوا۟ — root ح ص ي (ḥ-ṣ-y).** And the choice of this word is further striking, because in Arabic *ʿuddū* could also have been said for "count" (from the same root, directly).

*Iḥṣāʾ* is akin to the word **ح ص ى (ḥaṣā)**: **pebble.** The lexicographers record that the concrete sense of the root is "to count with pebbles" — before the number system and writing were widespread, counting was done with small stones; for each unit a stone was set aside.

The sense that comes out of this is **counting completely, one by one, skipping none**. *Iḥṣāʾ* is not "to know approximately"; it is **to take an inventory**.

The Qurʾān uses this word in the same way elsewhere too — in the sense of a thing's being recorded exactly, with no part of it left outside.

That is, the command is not "wait roughly a period"; it is **"count them one by one"**. And the verb is a plural imperative: *aḥṣū* — count. Who is to count is not specified. This indeterminacy is meaningful: the task of counting has not been consigned to one party.

### وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ رَبَّكُمْ — "guard yourselves against God, your Lord"

**Root و ق ي (w-q-y).** This root was analysed in full in `002-bakara.md` in connection with 2/2; I do not repeat that analysis. A short reminder: at the centre of the root there is not **fear** but **guarding** — *wiqāya* (protection, a shield), *muttaqī* (one who guards himself). The *muttaqī* is not the timid man but the careful one.

What is new here is **where the word has been placed.**

The command comes immediately after two technical rulings (time + counting). That is, this order is set up:

1. Divorce at the suitable time.
2. Count the period.
3. **Guard yourselves against God.**

The third item is the overseer of the first two. For the first two rulings had — in this period — no institutional oversight. Who will count the period? Who will check that he has counted rightly? There is no judge, no register, no record of persons.

***Taqwā* is put in the place of oversight.** And this is the first instance of a structure that will be repeated to the end of the sūra: beside the ruling an inner mechanism is placed that holds the ruling up.

I record this as an observation from the point of view of **the sociology of law**: in orders with a low capacity for sanction, the working of rules depends in large measure on internalised norms. That almost all the legal passages of the Qurʾān close with a divine name or a qualification of *taqwā* has to do with this structure. This is not a "proof"; it is a finding about how the text itself works.

**رَبَّكُمْ** — and note: it does not say "guard yourselves against God" and stop; it adds **"your Lord"**. The word *rabb* means the one who nurtures, who raises, who watches over (the analysis was made in `001-fatiha.md`). That is, the authority to be guarded against is at the same time the authority that looks after. The function of the addition is to take the command out of being a threat and to tie it to a relationship.

### لَا تُخْرِجُوهُنَّ مِنۢ بُيُوتِهِنَّ وَلَا يَخْرُجْنَ — "do not put them out of their houses; and let them not go out"

The most concrete ruling of the sūra, and the one that says the most.

**And the first thing to be noticed is a pronoun: بُيُوتِهِنَّ — "THEIR houses".**

The house is ascribed to the woman. And that in the very middle of the period in which the marriage is coming to an end. Had the man been speaking of his own house, *buyūtikum* would have been said; had it been left neutral, *al-buyūt* would have been said. It is not said.

**What this ascription means has been debated**, and there are two main readings:

| Reading | Account |
|---|---|
| **Not ownership but residence** | The house is not the woman's property; it is the place she lives in, the place assigned to her. In Arabic it is ordinary for a place to be ascribed to the one who lives in it. |
| **An expression of legal assignment** | The pronoun announces that for the duration of the waiting period the dwelling is the woman's right; the man cannot put her out of it, because for this period it is now hers. |

The two readings do not exclude one another; the second is the legal consequence of the first. **The result in any case is the same: throughout the waiting period the woman's right to be there takes precedence over the man's power to put her out.**

**What does the regulation prevent?** Something very concrete: **the woman's finding herself homeless from one moment to the next.**

And to see the weight of this the conditions of that period must be recalled:

- A woman normally had no independent dwelling or income.
- A divorced woman would return to her father's house or to a relative. That door was not always open; her family might be dead, far away, or poor.
- The options before a woman with no shelter were extremely narrow in that social order.

That is, the ruling prevents **a second blow arriving at the most defenceless moment.** The divorce is already a loss; the ruling forbids the loss of shelter from being added on top of it.

**A second function: the process's being lived in fact.** If the woman remains in the house, the parties remain inside the same life for the whole period. For the possibility spoken of in the next sentence (*lā tadrī laʿalla'llāha yuḥdithu…*) to be able to come about, the parties must not have been physically severed from one another. **The reversibility of the separation depends on the preservation of the place.** Two people who have gone apart cannot make peace — they have no place in which to do it.

I offer this as an inference, but the verse's own word order supports the inference: the ruling about the dwelling and the sentence "it may be that God will bring about something new" stand **inside the same verse**, and side by side at that.

**وَلَا يَخْرُجْنَ — "and let them not go out".** The second half of the prohibition is directed at the woman.

This symmetry must not be passed over. The ruling is not a one-sided protection; it lays **an obligation on both parties**:

- The man **cannot put her out** — his power is limited.
- The woman **does not go out** — her freedom is limited.

The ground of both is the same: that the process should run to its end. If the woman could go out whenever she wished, the period would in fact be emptied of meaning; if the man could put her out whenever he wished, the period would not turn into a protection.

Views are transmitted in the classical sources to the effect that the woman may go out during this period for necessary needs, and the extent of this has been debated. I do not go into the detail of that debate.

**There is a disagreement about the scope of the ruling**, and it would not be right to conceal it:

| Question | Divergence of view |
|---|---|
| Which divorce does *lā tukhrijūhunna* cover? | According to one view only the revocable divorce; according to another view the irrevocable one as well. |
| Are lodging and maintenance owed in an irrevocable divorce? | According to one view both; according to another only lodging; according to another neither is owed if she is not pregnant. |

This disagreement is real and has been treated at length in the literature of jurisprudence. **I adopt no preference here.** What I can say is this: the wording of the verse draws no distinction; the distinction has been built with other evidence and other reports. This is a finding, not a ruling.

### إِلَّآ أَن يَأْتِينَ بِفَٰحِشَةٍ مُّبَيِّنَةٍ — the exception

The verse sets an exception to the ruling about the dwelling: *"unless they commit a clear indecency."*

**فَاحِشَة — root ف ح ش (f-ḥ-sh).** The sense of the root: **an ugliness that exceeds measure, an evil that passes the bound.** *Fuḥsh* is a thing's exceeding the limit in ugliness; in Arabic an excessively high price is also called *fāḥish*. That is, the word announces not the kind of the evil but its **degree**: not an ordinary fault, but what is openly and excessively ugly.

**مُبَيِّنَة** — root **ب ي ن (b-y-n)**. This root was analysed in full in `098-beyyine.md`; I do not repeat the analysis given there. A reminder: at the centre of the root there is **the space between two things**, and from this both the sense "to separate" and the sense "to be evident" arise. *Mubayyina*: what is plain, what stands there in a way that can be told apart.

The function of the adjective here is narrow and important: **the exception is not open to suspicion.** Had it said "indecency" and stopped, every allegation would have opened the door of the exception. The qualification *mubayyina* makes an allegation insufficient: there must be something evident, beyond dispute.

**But what is this *fāḥisha*?** Several explanations are transmitted in the classical commentaries:

| Explanation | Account |
|---|---|
| Adultery | One of the most widespread uses of the word in the Qurʾān. |
| Severe discord towards the people of the house, injury by word and conduct | It rests on the sense of the root, "ugliness that passes the bound"; it does not confine the word to the sexual field. |
| The act of leaving the house without permission and without right | On this reading the exception is tied to the violation of the ruling. |

These explanations are present in the sources and I adopt no preference among them. The dictionary sense of the word is not proper to the sexual field; but a part of the uses in the Qurʾān are in that field. The text does not specify here.

**One thing, however, must be said plainly:** how this exception is to be operated — who is to establish it, by what procedure — is not in the verse. And this gap is a place open to abuse. An exception clause, when no procedure of establishment is laid down, can be widened so far as in effect to abolish the ruling. It is for exactly this reason that the literature of jurisprudence has tried to narrow the scope of the exception. I record this as an observation; not as a ruling.

### وَتِلْكَ حُدُودُ ٱللَّهِ — "these are the limits of God"

**حَدّ — root ح د د (ḥ-d-d).** The concrete sense: **the dividing line between two things, the edge of a piece of land, the place where a thing ends.**

The derivatives of the root confirm this picture:
- **حَدِيد (ḥadīd)** — iron. According to the lexicographers the connection is the sharpness of iron and its cutting apart.
- **حَدَّاد (ḥaddād)** — blacksmith; and at the same time gatekeeper, warder — the one who holds the boundary.
- **مَحْدُود** — bounded.
- **حِدَّة** — sharpness.
- **تَحْدِيد** — delimiting, defining. (The derivative *taḥdīd*, "delimitation", belongs here.)

The choice of the word states the character of the ruling: **the *ḥudūd* are not things one is asked to do; they are lines one is asked not to cross.**

This distinction is fine but real. A "command" tells you what you are to do; a "limit" tells you how far you may go and leaves you free within. What the sūra sets is the second: divorce is not forbidden, the **edges** of divorce are drawn.

Accordingly the same word occurs again and again in the middle of the divorce rulings in Sūrat al-Baqara as well (al-Baqara 2/229-230). Two sūras do the same work with the same word.

**وَمَن يَتَعَدَّ حُدُودَ ٱللَّهِ فَقَدْ ظَلَمَ نَفْسَهُ** — "whoever transgresses the limits of God has wronged himself."

**يَتَعَدَّ — root ع د و (ʿ-d-w):** to pass the bound, to go beyond, to trespass. From the same root *ʿaduww* (enemy — the one who violates the boundary), *ʿudwān* (aggression), *iʿtidāʾ*.

**and فَقَدْ ظَلَمَ نَفْسَهُ** — this is the crux of the sentence.

The expected sentence would have been: "whoever transgresses the limits has wronged **his wife**." After all, what is being regulated is a ruling that protects the woman's right.

The verse does not say this. It says **"has wronged himself"**.

If one looks at the use of this expression in the Qurʾān, the pattern is widespread and it does this work: **it turns the real address of the harm from the victim to the doer.** When the rule is broken the first party harmed is of course the other side; but the verse brings forward the loss on the account of the one who breaks it.

The practical function of this is as follows — and I write it as my own reading: **the person who breaks the rule has for the most part already accepted the harm to the other party.** His relationship is broken as it is; the other's loss will not stop him. The sentence therefore carries the reckoning elsewhere — to the loss of the one who breaks it. And this is perhaps the only effective thing that can be said to a person on bad terms with the other side.

**ظُلْم** is from the root **ظ ل م (ẓ-l-m)**: darkness; and from this "to put a thing somewhere other than the place it belongs to". *Ẓulm* is the disturbing of a balance. A person's wronging himself is his disturbing his own balance.

### لَا تَدْرِى لَعَلَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحْدِثُ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ أَمْرًا — the heart of the sūra

After six rulings the verse suddenly shifts into another language. And this last sentence is the **ground** of everything that came before it.

First the word order.

### Word order: the shift from plural to singular

The whole verse was plural: *ṭallaqtum, ṭalliqū, aḥṣū, ittaqū, lā tukhrijū*. Five verbs, all second person plural.

And in the last sentence, suddenly: **لَا تَدْرِى** — "**you** do not know". Second person **singular**.

In Arabic rhetoric this shift is called **iltifāt** (a shift in the direction of address), and it is a technique often used in the Qurʾān. But not every iltifāt does the same work; two different functions were recorded earlier in this body of work (drawing the reader onto the stage in `104-humeze.md`, handing the criticism to its owner in `080-abese.md`). The function here is a third, and it is this:

**The rule is set upon the community; the ignorance is said to each single person.**

A rule of law addresses everyone — "when you divorce, do thus". But the sentence "you do not know" cannot be said to a community. Who does not know what is the affair of each single person. The sentence takes the ground of the rule out of being a general principle and carries it **into the head of the man making the decision.**

And sharper still: the sentence is said at the moment when the man is **most certain.** A person who has resolved on divorce is at that moment certain of his decision; had he not been certain he would not have made it. The verse enters exactly there: *lā tadrī.*

### يُحْدِثُ — "brings about"

**Root ح د ث (ḥ-d-th).** The centre of the root: **a thing's coming to be that was not there before, being new.**

- **حَدِيث (ḥadīth)** — new; and speech, news. Both are from the same root: a piece of news is what is new. The term *ḥadīth* (a saying of the Prophet) comes from here as well.
- **حَادِث / حَادِثَة (ḥāditha)** — what happens, what comes about; an event.
- **مُحْدَث** — what has come about afterwards, newly arisen.
- **إِحْدَاث (iḥdāth)** — to bring forth something new.
- **حَدَاثَة (ḥadātha)** — newness, youth. (The word now used to render "modernity" is from this root as well.)

**And the choice of this word changes what the sentence says.**

The verse does not say "it may be that God will bring back the former state". It does not say *yurjiʿu* (he returns), it does not say *yuʿīdu* (he restores). It says **يُحْدِثُ**: **he brings about something new.**

The difference is not trivial. The expected possibility is the parties' "making peace and returning to what was". The word does not say this. What it says is wider and more realistic: a situation may arise that **was not there before**.

Why is this more realistic? Because in a broken relationship "returning to what was" is not possible in any case. A couple cannot, after a fracture has come between them, return to what preceded the fracture. The only place they can go is **a new situation** — either a different agreement, or a changed attitude, or (not out of the question) a cleaner form of the separation itself.

The verse does not say which it will be. **أَمْرًا** — indefinite, without the article: "a matter". What it is is not specified.

**And the function of the indefiniteness here is not magnification but indeterminacy.** The sentence gives no promise. It does not say "you will be reconciled". It says only this: **you do not know what will happen.**

### لَعَلَّ — "it may be"

And this word sets the tone of the sentence.

**لَعَلَّ** in Arabic conveys *tarajjī* (hoping). There is no certainty; there is possibility.

Had the verse said "God will bring about a new situation", the period would have been a waiting room: the outcome is settled already, only let the time come. *Laʿalla* prevents this. The outcome is not settled. The period does not guarantee the outcome.

**Then what is the ground of the period?** Not the outcome, but the **possibility.** The door is held open not because it is known that someone will come through it; **because the probability of coming is not zero.**

This sets the logic of the regulation on a very clear footing:

> The law here does not command an outcome. It **preserves a possibility.**

And the manner in which the possibility is preserved is wholly material: the period will be counted, the woman will remain in the house. That is, the text does not give an exhortation such as "try to make peace"; it **establishes an order in which making peace remains physically possible.** An exhortation is recommended, an order is imposed. The verse chooses the second.

### The logic of the regulation: a law built upon ignorance

I want to gather up the structure that comes out of this verse as a whole, because the rest of the sūra is built upon it.

The verse does this:

1. It **does not forbid** divorce.
2. But it **takes it out of being an operation completed in a moment.**
3. It makes the period **countable** — so that neither party can define the period in his own favour.
4. It **keeps the woman in place** — so that the period is in fact lived and a return remains physically possible.
5. And it grounds all of this in **a human being's not knowing his own future.**

The fifth item carries the first four. And what is striking is this: **the ground is not rested on the goodness or the morality of the parties.** The verse does not say "treat one another well, and perhaps you will be reconciled". It says: *you do not know.*

That is, the support of the regulation is not a virtue but an **epistemic limit**: a human being's knowledge about the rightness of his own decision is incomplete.

And this is a fairly solid ground on which to make law. Rules that rest on virtue do not work on those who are not virtuous. A rule that rests on ignorance works on everyone — because no one knows the future.

### What this says to the present

**One. Irreversible decisions and time.**

The mechanism the verse sets up — putting a compulsory interval between an irreversible decision and its being carried out — is today a deliberate design principle in many fields. Withdrawal periods in consumer law, notice periods in labour law, the divorce waiting periods of many countries, even the "undo" button in software interfaces all carry the same idea: **a decision's remaining reversible raises the quality of the decision.**

The ground is the same too: the state of mind at the moment of decision is not a good indicator of the long-term rightness of the decision. The verse says this as *lā tadrī*.

I record this not as a "miracle" but as a parallel that makes the function of the regulation visible.

**Two. The protective side of "counting".**

Today "measuring" is generally taken to be a cold and technical business; in human matters the intrusion of measure looks like a defect. The verse shows the opposite direction: **where there is no measure, power fills the gap.**

The counting of a period, the keeping of a record, the finding of a witness — these are not formalities that chill a relationship; they are the only support in the hand of the weaker party. In every order in which a word is not a document and a period is not a number, who is in the right is determined by who is strong.

**Three. The tendency of exception clauses to expand.**

The qualification noted above on *illā an yaʾtīna bi-fāḥishatin mubayyina*, though it looks like a narrow technical point, points to a general problem: **an exception whose procedure of establishment is not laid down can eat the rule.**

This is a known weakness in legal texts, and it meets us in the reading of religious texts as well. To read an exception placed upon a protection broadly and the protection narrowly reverses the work the text does. That the verse has added the adjective *mubayyina* is the first dam set against that expansion.

## 65/2 — فَإِذَا بَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ فَارِقُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ وَأَشْهِدُوا۟ ذَوَىْ عَدْلٍ مِّنكُمْ وَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلشَّهَٰدَةَ لِلَّهِ ذَٰلِكُمْ يُوعَظُ بِهِۦ مَن كَانَ يُؤْمِنُ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْءَاخِرِ وَمَن يَتَّقِ ٱللَّهَ يَجْعَل لَّهُۥ مَخْرَجًا

*Fa-idhā balaghna ajalahunna fa-amsikūhunna bi-maʿrūfin aw fāriqūhunna bi-maʿrūf, wa ashhidū dhaway ʿadlin minkum wa aqīmu'l-shahādata li'llāh, dhālikum yūʿaẓu bihi man kāna yuʾminu bi'llāhi wa'l-yawmi'l-ākhir, wa man yattaqi'llāha yajʿal lahu makhrajā*

"When they reach the end of their term, keep them in the recognised good way or part from them in the recognised good way. Call to witness two just persons from among you, and hold the testimony upright for God. With this, admonition is given to whoever believes in God and the Last Day. And whoever guards himself against God, He opens for him a way out."

### بَلَغْنَ أَجَلَهُنَّ — "when they reach the end of their term"

**أَجَل — root أ ج ل (ʾ-j-l).** The sense of the word is not "period" but something more exact: **the end of a period, its due date, its terminal point.**

This is a nicety in Arabic that has to be attended to. *Ajal* is used both for the period itself and for the moment at which the period ends; but its central sense is **the end**. The word is commonly met in only one of its uses, the appointed time of death; but in Arabic it serves for the maturity of a debt as well — *daynun muʾajjal*, a deferred debt.

And precisely this commercial use is meaningful here: **the waiting period is a term of maturity.** It falls due on a determined date, and when it falls due a decision must be made. The commercial-legal language of the sūra frames the process of divorce like a relation of debt: a counted period, a maturity, two witnesses, and a record.

**بَلَغْنَ — root ب ل غ (b-l-gh):** to arrive at a place, to reach, to attain. From the same root *balāgh* (delivery, conveying a message), *bāligh* (one who reaches; one come of age), *balāgha* (a word's reaching its target). The root's second occurrence in this sūra is in verse 3: *inna'llāha **bālighu** amrih* — there what reaches is God's command, here what is reached is a term of maturity.

The verb is **feminine plural**: *balaghna* — "they (the women) reached". That is, it is the women who reach the end of the period; and this prepares the party addressed in the next sentence.

### فَأَمْسِكُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ أَوْ فَارِقُوهُنَّ بِمَعْرُوفٍ — two ways, one measure

Architecturally the most beautiful place in the verse is here.

**أَمْسَكَ — root م س ك (m-s-k).** The concrete sense: **to hold firmly by hand, to grip, not to let go.** From the same root *misk* (musk, a scent that holds and stays), *imsāk* (holding; abstaining from food in the fast), *mustamsik* (one who holds on).

**فَارَقَ — root ف ر ق (f-r-q).** To separate, to divide. This root was taken up in `098-beyyine.md` in connection with *tafarraqa* (to be divided); what was recorded there was this: at the centre of the root there is **a whole's being parted into pieces.** *Farq*, *firqa*, *furqān*, *tafriqa* are from the same root.

**The difference of pattern matters.** *Fāriqa* is of the **مُفَاعَلَة (mufāʿala)** form — like *tafāʿul* this form too carries reciprocity, but in a different manner: *mufāʿala* announces that the act issues from one party and is directed at the other and meets a response. *Qātala* (he fought with someone), *sāfara* (he travelled — he parted from a place).

That is, the verse does not say "separate them" (*farriqūhunna*); it says **"part from them"**, "part mutually". The separation is set up not as a one-sided operation but as a movement that covers both parties. This is an observation about the grammar; I draw no legal conclusion from it.

**And now the main matter: بِمَعْرُوفٍ, twice.**

Look at the structure of the sentence:

> *fa-amsikūhunna* **bi-maʿrūf** — *aw* — *fāriqūhunna* **bi-maʿrūf**

Two options are offered and **the same qualification is attached to both of them**. This is a symmetry that must not be passed over.

What one would expect is that the qualification be attached only to the second option: "keep them; or, if you are going to separate, separate well." For the risk of behaving badly is higher in the separation. The verse does not do this. **Keeping too has a measure.**

And the reason for this is worth thinking about. A man can do harm by **keeping** his wife too: not divorcing her and leaving her in suspense, holding her in a position neither married nor divorced, neither letting her go nor taking her in. This was a known form of wrong in that period, and the Qurʾān forbids it explicitly elsewhere:

> **وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِّتَعْتَدُوا۟**
> *"Do not keep them in order to do harm, so as to transgress against them."* (al-Baqara 2/231)

There too the same verb (*tumsikūhunna*) is used and harmful keeping is named. Al-Ṭalāq 65/2 expresses the same matter from its positive side: if you are keeping, keep *bi-maʿrūf*.

**That is, the verse oversees both doors.** Neither staying nor going is a free field. The measure of both is the same and comes from outside.

### مَعْرُوف — "the known good"

**Root ع ر ف (ʿ-r-f).** To know, to recognise — but a particular kind of knowing: **to recognise what has been met with before.** *Maʿrifa* is not learning for the first time but recognising. From the same root *ʿirfān*, *taʿrīf* (introducing), *ʿurf* (the conduct known and accepted in a society), and the day of *ʿArafa* (the meeting/being known at ʿArafāt).

**مَعْرُوف** is a passive participle: **known, recognised.** That is, it does not mean "good"; it means **"what everyone knows to be good"**.

This distinction carries the whole force of the word. The verse does not leave the measure of goodness to the conscience of the parties; it ties it to **the common knowledge of the community.**

Why? Because at the moment of divorce the parties' own measures are not trustworthy. Everyone finds his own conduct reasonable. The qualification *maʿrūf* carries the measure outside: what you do is not what you find reasonable; it is **the good that those around you recognise, know, and have a name for.**

And this has a concrete consequence: *maʿrūf* is a measure that can be **demonstrated**. "I behaved as I felt like behaving" is not defensible; "I behaved in the way everyone knows" is either true or not, and someone looking on from outside can decide.

**The second face of the word.** The opposite of *maʿrūf* in the Qurʾān is **مُنْكَر (munkar)**: from the root *n-k-r* — **unrecognised, strange, found odd.** That is, in the moral vocabulary of the Qurʾān good is named "familiar" and evil "unfamiliar". Evil is what does not fit the setting, what stands out, what makes people say "what is this now?"

This does not amount to reducing morality to social relativity — the Qurʾān does not define *munkar* merely as "outside custom", it also forbids it. But the choice of the word itself asks that the moral measure be **visible and shared.**

### وَأَشْهِدُوا۟ ذَوَىْ عَدْلٍ مِّنكُمْ — call to witness

The third mechanism of the regulation: **record.**

**شَهِدَ — root ش ه د (sh-h-d).** Its sense has two layers: **to be present** and **to report what one has seen.** The witness is the one who was there at the moment of the event and afterwards conveys it. From the same root *shahāda*, *mashhad* (the place where an event occurred), *shāhid*, *shahīd*, *mushāhada*.

That the root's original sense is "to be present" is important: witnessing is not a transmission of information but **a presence**. The witness becomes part of the event.

**The verb is in the إِفْعَال form (*ashhidū*)** — causative: "**call** to witness", that is, **make** some people witnesses. The command is directed not to those who witness but to the party: have the record set up.

**ذَوَىْ عَدْلٍ** — "two possessors of justice". *Dhawā* is the dual of *dhū* (possessor). The number is **two** and it is given in the dual form.

**عَدْل — root ع د ل (ʿ-d-l).** The concrete sense: **equivalence, the equality of two sides.** *ʿAdl* is the levelling of the two pans of a balance; from the same root *muʿādala* (equation), *taʿdīl* (equalising), *iʿtidāl* (balance), *ʿadīl* (equal, of like value).

That is, "a possessor of justice" is the **balanced** person: the one who does not lean to one side.

**مِّنكُمْ** — "from among you". The witnesses are not from outside but from within the community. This shows that the record is not institutional but **belongs to the congregation**: there is no court, no register; but there is a community.

### The logic of the regulation: what does witnessing do?

To see the function of the ruling about witnessing, it is enough to think what would happen in an order without witnesses.

In an order without witnesses divorce is an entirely private event. When it was done, with what words it was done, how many times it was done — all of it is left to the memory and the assertion of the parties. And when a dispute arises, which of the assertions is to count as valid is determined not by evidence but by **power**.

The witness closes this gap:

- **It fixes the time.** When the divorce occurred, and therefore when the waiting period ended, becomes beyond dispute.
- **It fixes the content.** The defence "that is not what I said" is cut off.
- **And it carries the event from the private into the public.** This is perhaps the most important: an operation a community knows about runs differently from one it does not. The man's conduct now becomes observable — and only by this means does the oversight of the *bi-maʿrūf* qualification become possible.

Two rulings complete one another: *bi-maʿrūf* sets a measure from outside, *ashhidū* makes the application of that measure visible from outside. **Had measure and observation not come together, neither would have been of any use.**

At which moment the witnessing belongs — to the divorce itself, or to the decision to keep or to part at the end of the waiting period — has been debated in the sources; a divergence of view is also transmitted as to whether witnessing in this context is a condition or a recommendation. I do not go into the detail of these disagreements or adopt a preference among them.

### وَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلشَّهَٰدَةَ لِلَّهِ — "hold the testimony upright for God"

And now the verse turns to the witnesses.

**أَقِيمُوا۟ — root ق و م (q-w-m).** This root was treated in detail in `001-fatiha.md` (*mustaqīm*), in `002-bakara.md` at 2/3 (*iqāmatu'l-ṣalāt*) and in `098-beyyine.md` (*qayyima*, *dīnu'l-qayyima*). I do not repeat those analyses. A reminder: the centre of the root is **to stand and to be upright** — both to be without crookedness and not to collapse. **إِقَامَة** (Form IV) is causative: *to hold upright.*

**And the choice of the verb here is striking.** The verse does not say "**bear** witness". It does not say *ishhadū*. It says **أَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلشَّهَٰدَةَ** — "hold the testimony upright".

This is the very verb the Qurʾān uses for prayer: *aqīmu'l-ṣalāt*. In `002-bakara.md` the reason for this verb's being chosen for prayer was treated: prayer is not something "performed" but something **held upright**; because it tends to fall down it needs continual support.

The same logic is set up for testimony. Testimony by its nature tends to lean:

- The witness may know one of the parties.
- Telling the truth may have a price.
- Keeping silent looks easier and less risky than lying.

**That is, testimony left to itself does not stay upright; it leans.** The verb says this.

And the Qurʾān describes this risk of leaning explicitly elsewhere:

> **يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ كُونُوا۟ قَوَّٰمِينَ بِٱلْقِسْطِ شُهَدَآءَ لِلَّهِ وَلَوْ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِكُمْ أَوِ ٱلْوَٰلِدَيْنِ وَٱلْأَقْرَبِينَ**
> *"O you who believe! Be upholders of justice, witnesses for God, even against yourselves or your parents and kinsfolk."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/135)

There too the same root (*qawwāmīn*), the same qualification (*li'llāh*), and the address of the leaning written out plainly: yourself, your parents, your near kin. In the section `070-mearic.md` this verse was taken up in connection with the single public item on that list.

**لِلَّهِ** — "for God". The function of the qualification: the address of the testimony is neither of the parties. The witness is not answerable to the man who called him; nor to the community that summoned him. The address is raised upward — and this is the only thing that frees the witness from the pressure of the parties.

### ذَٰلِكُمْ يُوعَظُ بِهِۦ مَن كَانَ يُؤْمِنُ بِٱللَّهِ وَٱلْيَوْمِ ٱلْءَاخِرِ

"With this, admonition is given to whoever believes in God and the Last Day."

**وَعَظَ — root و ع ظ (w-ʿ-ẓ).** *Waʿẓ*: to counsel someone by reminding him of the outcome. The lexicographers record that within the root there are elements of **softening and of drawing attention to the outcome.**

And the word order of the sentence sets a qualification: **the admonition is given to the believer.**

This is not an exclusion but a finding — and it is the same as the structure of *hudan li'l-muttaqīn* (a guidance for those who guard themselves) at 2/2 in `002-bakara.md`. The solution recorded there holds here too: the call of the text is to everyone; **its being of actual benefit** depends on the person's own posture.

But there is something additional here, and it is proper to the subject: **the effect of the admonition is tied to belief in the hereafter.**

The reason is concrete. Most of the rulings in this sūra have no worldly sanction, or only a weak one. There is no police force to make the man count the period rightly, keep the woman in the house, pay the maintenance in full. The rule works only on the person who **takes into account a reckoning he does not see.**

This is another appearance of the finding made in `107-maun.md`: there it was recorded that the only support for not repelling the orphan is belief in the reckoning — because repelling the orphan has no worldly cost at all. There is a similar structure here: counting the waiting period short, treating the woman narrowly, has no cost. **Where the cost is invisible, the rule stands only by belief.**

### وَمَن يَتَّقِ ٱللَّهَ يَجْعَل لَّهُۥ مَخْرَجًا — "He opens for him a way out"

One of the most quoted sentences of the Qurʾān. And in being quoted it is almost always torn from its context.

Let us look first at the word.

### مَخْرَج — a way out

**Root خ ر ج (kh-r-j).** To go out, to come out. From the same root *khurūj* (exit), *ikhrāj* (putting out), *kharāj* (what comes out of the soil; and tax), *makhraj* (the place of going out; and the point at which a letter issues from the mouth — *makhārij al-ḥurūf*).

**Pattern: مَفْعَل.** In Arabic this pattern is either a **noun of place** (where the act is done), a **noun of time** (when it is done), or a **mīmic verbal noun** (the act itself).

- **On the noun-of-place reading**: *makhraj* = **the place one will go out by**. A concrete door.
- **On the mīmic-verbal-noun reading**: *makhraj* = **going out, exit**. The act itself.

Both readings are possible and both are present in the classical sources. The difference is fine but real: on the first a **place** is promised, on the second a **possibility**.

I think the first reading suits the language of the sūra better, and my ground is the weave of the root we shall see under the next heading. **This is a preference; it is not binding.**

**Its coming indefinite.** Not *al-makhraj* but *makhrajan*. That is, not "the way out" but "a way out". What it is is not said, where it opens onto is not said. The indeterminacy can be read here both as magnification (*"such a way out as…"*) and as real indeterminacy — and the two can stand together.

### The backbone of the sūra: the three positions of the root خ ر ج

**And now the main matter.** Almost everyone who quotes this verse takes it on its own. But the verse is the place where the same root occurs for the third time — and the two previous occurrences change what the sentence says.

The root occurs in the sūra in these three places:

| # | Verse | Expression | Who puts out / goes out | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 65/1 | **لَا تُخْرِجُوهُنَّ** — do not put them out | The man, the woman | Out of the house — **forbidden** |
| **2** | 65/1 | **وَلَا يَخْرُجْنَ** — and let them not go out | The woman, herself | Out of the house — **forbidden** |
| **3** | 65/2 | **يَجْعَل لَّهُۥ مَخْرَجًا** — He opens for him a way out | **God**, for the man | Out of straitness — **promised** |

Read the table. With the same root the sūra first **forbids two goings out**, then **promises one going out.**

And what it says is this:

> Do not set about opening yourself a way out. You cannot open the door — and when you try to, the door you open turns out to be putting the other person into the street. The one who will open the door is another.

This is my own reading, and I do not present it as a transmitted report. But that the root occurs three times, with three different subjects and two opposite rulings, is the text's own datum; and a repetition of this density occurs within too narrow a space (two verses) to be counted an accident.

**And there is a fourth occurrence** — at the end of the sūra:

> **لِّيُخْرِجَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ مِنَ ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ إِلَى ٱلنُّورِ** (65/11)
> *"In order to **bring out** those who believe and do righteous deeds from the darknesses into the light."*

The same root, the same causative pattern (*yukhrija*), and again **a bringing out of which God is the subject.** The act forbidden to the man in the first verse of the sūra (*tukhrijū*), God performs in the last verses — but in an entirely different direction.

That is, the sūra takes a single root and works it from beginning to end:

**Forbidden putting out (from the house) → promised going out (from straitness) → accomplished bringing out (from darkness).**

I record this as the backbone of the sūra.

### The context of the verse: where it is said

Now something must be said about the widespread use of this verse.

The sentence *"whoever guards himself against God, He opens for him a way out"* is generally used as a general consolation for every kind of hardship: work, debt, illness, trouble. This use is not wrong — the wording of the sentence is general, it is built with *man* (whoever) and it is not restricted to a subject. The principle of the tradition of legal theory, *al-ʿibratu bi-ʿumūmi'l-lafẓ*, operates here.

**But something is lost, and what is lost is not trivial.**

The place the sentence comes in is this: two people are separating. The woman cannot be put out of the house. The man is obliged to pay. A period is being waited out. The relationship has broken down, the parties cannot bear one another, and the law keeps them both under the same roof.

**That is, the sentence is said at the moment when the way out looks most closed.** And what it says is tied to what is asked of one at that moment: *man yattaqi'llāh* — whoever obeys the rule.

This connection is what gets lost. Used as a general consolation the sentence turns into: "fear God and your affairs will improve." In its context it says something else:

> **At the moment when the way out looks closed, obey the rule. The way out will open from there.**

The difference is practical. The first is an advice about feeling, the second an advice about conduct. And the second is far harder — because it asks the person to obey the rule at exactly the moment when he will want to break it.

**And one further detail, often passed over:** the promise in the verse does not say that the hardship **will be lifted**. It does not say "He will deliver him from the straitness". It says **"He opens for him a way out"**. The straitness may remain where it is; what is opened is a door. The opening of the door and the emptying of the room are not the same thing.

### وَيَرْزُقْهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَحْتَسِبُ — "He provides for him from where he does not reckon"

This is the beginning of verse 3, but it is the continuation of the sentence of verse 2; for that reason I take it up here.

**حَيْثُ** — an adverb of place: "place, side". *Min ḥaythu* — "from the place where…".

**يَحْتَسِبُ — root ح س ب (ḥ-s-b).** And this root was treated in `104-humeze.md`; I do not repeat that analysis. A reminder — and it falls exactly into place here: **the root carries both the verb "to count" (*ḥasaba*) and the verb "to suppose" (*ḥasiba*)**, and the lexicographers record that beneath the root lies the idea of "assessing a thing according to a determinate reckoning", from which both numerical reckoning and opinion are derived.

**Pattern: إِفْتِعَال (iftiʿāl).** *Iḥtasaba* — to take into account, to reckon up, to expect. What the form adds is that the act is done by the subject **for himself**: the person is making his own reckoning.

That is, *min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasib* is rendered "from where he does not expect"; but what it says literally is sharper: **"from where he has not put into his reckoning."**

And this, combined with the context of the verse, takes on a very definite meaning.

The man in the process of divorce is making a reckoning. He will pay maintenance, provide a dwelling, perhaps a wage for suckling too. These are entries and their sum is known. The rule loads a cost upon him and the resource to meet that cost does not appear in his reckoning.

What the verse says settles exactly here: **the resource will not come out of the reckoning.**

**And the word-weave of the sūra keeps this up across two verses** — this is a striking density:

| Position | Word | Root | What it says |
|---|---|---|---|
| 65/1 | **وَأَحْصُوا۟** | ح ص ي | **Count** the period — this is the reckoning a human being makes |
| 65/3 | **لَا يَحْتَسِبُ** | ح س ب | Provision comes from where he **has not reckoned** |
| 65/3 | **فَهُوَ حَسْبُهُۥ** | ح س ب | God **suffices** him — He settles his account |
| 65/8 | **فَحَاسَبْنَٰهَا حِسَابًا شَدِيدًا** | ح س ب | And in the end the **reckoning** is taken |

**In the sūra the word for reckoning is used for four different tasks:** what a human being must count (the period), what a human being cannot count (the source of provision), the authority that settles a human being's account, and the final reckoning.

I record this weave as an observation. The sūra sets up a language of reckoning and within that language draws how far a human being's reckoning extends: **you can count the period; you cannot count the source.**

## 65/3 — وَيَرْزُقْهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَحْتَسِبُ وَمَن يَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ فَهُوَ حَسْبُهُۥٓ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بَٰلِغُ أَمْرِهِۦ قَدْ جَعَلَ ٱللَّهُ لِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدْرًا

*Wa yarzuqhu min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasib, wa man yatawakkal ʿala'llāhi fa-huwa ḥasbuh, inna'llāha bālighu amrih, qad jaʿala'llāhu li-kulli shayʾin qadrā*

"And He provides for him from where he does not expect. Whoever relies on God, He suffices him. Indeed God brings His command to its end. God has set a measure for everything."

### وَمَن يَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى ٱللَّهِ — tawakkul

**Root و ك ل (w-k-l).** The common use of this word has drifted a good way from what the root says; so one has to begin from the root.

**وَكِيل (wakīl)** — the person **appointed** to carry out someone's business on his behalf. English keeps the same idea in *proxy*, *agent*, *power of attorney*: when a person cannot or will not do a thing himself, he **appoints** someone to conduct it for him.

**تَوَكُّل** is of the *tafaʿʿul* form and conveys reflexivity: **to take someone as one's agent, to consign one's business to another.**

**And here is the thing to be attended to: appointing an agent is an operation.**

To appoint an agent:
1. There must be a **business** in hand.
2. You must be its **owner**.
3. And **you** must give the agency.

That is, *tawakkul* presupposes not the absence of business but its **presence**. A man with no business appoints no agent. This is a consequence that comes out of the structure of the word, and it is the word itself that prevents *tawakkul* from being understood as "doing nothing".

Accordingly the context of the verse confirms it too: the sentence occurs **in the middle of a series of rulings listing things to be done.** Count the period, do not put the woman out, call witnesses, pay the maintenance — and rely. *Tawakkul* does not stand in place of the list; it stands beside the list.

**A qualification.** That *tawakkul* must go together with taking hold of the means is a subject treated at length in the Islamic intellectual tradition, and there is a widely transmitted saying on it — the saying to the bedouin who left his camel untethered, to the effect of "tie it first, then rely". There is dispute about the soundness of this report; for that reason I do not transmit it as a ḥadīth. **The structure of the word already yields the same result** and there is no need to rest on a report for it.

### فَهُوَ حَسْبُهُۥ — "He suffices him"

**حَسْب** — the same root (**ح س ب**) that occurred two words earlier in *lā yaḥtasib*. The word here means **"what is sufficient, what is enough"**.

Its link with the root's sense of "reckoning" is direct: **sufficiency is the result of a reckoning.** A thing's being "enough" is its having reached the required quantity. English covers the two ends of this with two unconnected words — *reckoning* and *enough* — but the Arabic holds them in one root.

**And the two words' occurring this close together sets up a contrast:**

- *lā **yaḥtasib*** — a human being's reckoning **does not suffice**; he cannot compute the source.
- *fa-huwa **ḥasbuh*** — and God **suffices** him.

That is, the verse uses the same root twice in two opposite directions: the insufficiency of a human being's reckoning and the sufficiency of God. I record this as an observation about the language; I do not present it as a connection established in the classical commentaries.

### إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بَٰلِغُ أَمْرِهِۦ — "who brings His command to its end"

**بَالِغ** — root **ب ل غ (b-l-gh)**, which occurred in verse 2 in connection with *balaghna*: to reach, to arrive. *Bāligh* is an active participle: the one who brings, who makes arrive.

**أَمْر** — root **أ م ر (ʾ-m-r)**. The word has two main uses in Arabic, and both occur in this sūra:

1. **Command, order.** (Plural *awāmir*.)
2. **Affair, situation, matter.** (Plural *umūr*.)

English renders the two separately, as "command" and "affair"; in Arabic it is one word. And this unity will become important at the end of the sūra.

The meaning of the sentence: **God brings His affair/His command to its target.** That is, what He decrees does not stop on the road.

And this sentence is the ground of the preceding one (*fa-huwa ḥasbuh*): God's being sufficient is given as the consequence not of His power but of **His bringing His work to completion.**

### قَدْ جَعَلَ ٱللَّهُ لِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدْرًا — "He has set a measure for everything"

**Root ق د ر (q-d-r).** This root was analysed in full in `097-kadr.md`; I do not repeat that analysis. A short reminder: in Arabic the root opens onto **three separate directions of meaning** — (1) **measure, determination, apportioning**; (2) **worth, value, honour**; (3) **narrowness, constriction**. In the discussion of Sūrat al-Qadr it was recorded that these three branches are three faces of the same night.

**Which is it here?** Plainly the first: *qadran* = **measure, a determined quantity.** The sentence says that a measure has been set for everything.

**But this is not the main matter.** The main matter is this:

> **`097-kadr.md`, when looking for evidence for the THIRD branch of the root (narrowness), pointed to a place four verses further on in this sūra.**

The evidence given there was: *"He whose provision has been straitened for him (qudira ʿalayhi rizquh)…"* — **al-Ṭalāq 65/7.**

That is, the first sense of the root occurs at 65/3 and the third at 65/7. **The same root, four verses apart, with two different branches of meaning.**

And the relation between the two occurrences is not accidental; they explain one another:

| | **65/3** | **65/7** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | *qadran* | *qudira ʿalayhi* |
| Branch of meaning | **Measure** (determination) | **Narrowness** (straitening) |
| Its subject | God — "He set a measure for everything" | Passive — "he whose provision is straitened" |
| What it says | Everything has a quantity | Some people's quantity is narrow |

**What comes out when the two are joined is this:** narrowness is not the absence of measure. No one's provision is randomly narrow; **what is narrow is a measure too.** 65/3 sets the general principle, 65/7 brings it down into a man's pocket.

And this is the ground of the ruling at 65/7 — "let him whose hand is straitened spend out of what God has given him". The man is not paying short; **he is paying by his own measure.** And it is not he who determines that the measure is narrow.

I complete this connection from `097-kadr.md` towards this side: there this verse was cited as a lexical proof; here the same word is seen working with two branches of meaning at once inside the same sūra.

**A qualification: the debate over destiny.** The word *qadran* is the word at the centre of the debate over destiny in the literature of theology. I do not enter that debate from this verse, because the verse does not construct a theological thesis; it stands inside a series of rulings, as a sentence meant to bring a person to be content with his own measure. To tear it from its context and make it the proof of a school's position is to lose the work the verse does.

### Context: why here?

These four sentences in verses 2 and 3 — *makhraj*, provision, *tawakkul*, measure — what are they doing in the middle of a legal text?

The answer lies in the **cost** of the rulings.

The rulings the sūra sets are expensive for the party applying them:
- Waiting out the period: the man's not being free at the moment he wishes.
- Keeping the woman in the house: the cost of the dwelling, and the tension.
- Maintenance: money directly.
- The wage for suckling: money again.
- Calling witnesses: the opening of the event to the public.

And none of these has any worldly enforcer. What will happen when the man chooses not to obey the rulings?

**The sūra's answer has two layers.** In the long run there is a reckoning (verses 8-10). But in the short run these four sentences stand: *a way out will be opened for you, provision will come from where you have not reckoned, He suffices you, and your measure is already determined.*

That is, the verses **set up the psychological ground that will carry the cost of the rule.** So long as a person thinks his own resource is limited, he resists sharing his resource with another. When he becomes convinced that the resource is not in his own hand, the resistance lessens.

I record this as an observation: **the sūra does not content itself with imposing a ruling; it also gives the picture of the world that makes obeying the ruling possible.**

### What this says to the present

**One. The least examined side of the most quoted verse.**

The sentence *"whoever guards himself against God, He opens a way out for him"* circulates on signboards, in messages, in words of consolation. Put into its context, what it says goes beyond consolation:

This sentence is said at a moment **when breaking the rule looks like a way out.** For the man the easiest way out is to put the woman out of the house. The cheapest way out is to cut the maintenance. The quickest way out is not to wait the period.

The verse closes those ways out and in return promises another door. That is, the function of the sentence is not to soothe; it is **to close the short cut and show an alternative.**

The side of this that looks to the present is direct and not confined to divorce: a person under pressure sees, most of the time, that he can get out by breaking the rule. And most of the time he is right — in the short run he really does get out. The claim of the verse is that that way out is not a way out.

**Two. The limit of making a reckoning.**

*Min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasib* — "from where he has not reckoned". This expression does not reject making a reckoning; it says that the **scope** of the reckoning is limited.

Its practical counterpart is this: in making a decision we add up all the entries in our hand, and if the sum does not come out we say "it cannot be done". The sum really does not come out. But within the sum there are only the entries **we know**.

This is not a ground for not deciding — on the contrary, the verse commands a decision (obeying the rule). What it says is narrower: **the sum's not closing does not show that the road is closed.**

**Three. The word "suffices".**

*Fa-huwa ḥasbuh* — "He suffices him". In present-day speech "to suffice" is mostly the voicing of a lack: it does not suffice, it does not stretch, there is none left.

What the verse sets up is not a claim about satisfaction but a claim about **a source**. And two different anxieties have to be distinguished: "is what I have enough?" and "is there something behind me?" are separate questions. The first is a question of quantity, the second a question of relationship. The sentence answers the second and does not answer the first — accordingly the amount of the maintenance will be regulated separately in the following verses.

## 65/4 — وَٱلَّٰٓـِٔى يَئِسْنَ مِنَ ٱلْمَحِيضِ مِن نِّسَآئِكُمْ إِنِ ٱرْتَبْتُمْ فَعِدَّتُهُنَّ ثَلَٰثَةُ أَشْهُرٍ وَٱلَّٰٓـِٔى لَمْ يَحِضْنَ وَأُو۟لَٰتُ ٱلْأَحْمَالِ أَجَلُهُنَّ أَن يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ وَمَن يَتَّقِ ٱللَّهَ يَجْعَل لَّهُۥ مِنْ أَمْرِهِۦ يُسْرًا

*Wa'llāʾī yaʾisna mina'l-maḥīḍi min nisāʾikum ini'rtabtum fa-ʿiddatuhunna thalāthatu ashhurin wa'llāʾī lam yaḥiḍn, wa ulātu'l-aḥmāli ajaluhunna an yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunn, wa man yattaqi'llāha yajʿal lahu min amrihi yusrā*

"Those of your women who have despaired of menstruation — if you are in doubt — their waiting period is three months; and so too those who have not yet menstruated. And those who are with child: their term is that they lay down their burden. And whoever guards himself against God, He grants him ease in his affair."

### How the verse is to be read: completing the coverage

At first sight this verse looks technical and narrow. Read that way, something is missed, and I want to say it first.

In Sūrat al-Baqara the general rule for the waiting period had been given: **ثَلَٰثَةَ قُرُوٓءٍ** — three *qurūʾ* (al-Baqara 2/228). This is a measure that works for women who menstruate; it ties the period to the woman's own bodily cycle.

**But this measure does not fit everyone.** There are three groups that fall outside the measure:

1. Those who have ceased to menstruate — the cycle has ended.
2. Those who have not yet menstruated — the cycle has not begun.
3. Those who are pregnant — the cycle is suspended.

The general rule cannot be applied to these three groups. And because it cannot, the women in these three groups **fall outside the rule** — that is, without a waiting period, therefore without a term, therefore without protection.

**The verse closes exactly this gap.**

For this reason to read the verse as a passage of "rulings about menstruation" is to miss the work it does. The work the verse does is this: **to complete the coverage.** A separate measure is set for every case the general rule could not reach, so that no woman can be left outside on the ground that "there is no ruling for my case".

And the consequence of this is concrete: a woman with no waiting period has no right to lodging, no right to maintenance, and no possibility of return. **The period is the bearer of the whole protection.** Giving everyone a period means giving everyone a protection.

I offer this as a proposed reading. The wording of the verse completes the coverage; that the **purpose** of this completion is protection is my own inference — but the rest of the sūra (lodging, maintenance, the wage for suckling) supports the inference.

### The words

**ٱلَّٰٓـِٔى (allāʾī)** — the feminine plural of *alladhī*: "the women who…". It is transmitted in the sources that the word is read in the forms *allāʾī*, *allāʾi* and *allā*; since the variant reading does not change the meaning I do not dwell on it and I give no imām's name.

**يَئِسْنَ — root ي أ س (y-ʾ-s):** to give up hope. The derivatives *yaʾs* (despair) and *maʾyūs* (one who has despaired) are from this root. The verb here names a bodily condition by its psychological counterpart: to have "given up hope" of menstruation.

This manner of naming is striking. The verse does not give a physiological definition (it sets no age limit, it specifies no period); it takes as its measure **the woman's own judgement about her own condition.** Given the resources of knowledge in that period this is a practical choice; and at the same time it ties the measure to the person being measured.

**ٱلْمَحِيض — root ح ي ض (ḥ-y-ḍ).** The concrete sense of the root: **to flow, to overflow.** The word is on the *mafʿil* pattern and can be read both as a verbal noun and as a noun of time/place.

**إِنِ ٱرْتَبْتُمْ — "if you are in doubt"**

**Root ر ي ب (r-y-b):** doubt, a suspicion that unsettles. This root was taken up in `002-bakara.md` in connection with *lā rayba fīh* at 2/2: *rayb* is not an ordinary lack of information but the doubt that **disturbs a person, that keeps working at him inside.**

**And the expression *in irtabtum* here is the most debated place in the verse.** Doubt about what?

| Reading | Account |
|---|---|
| Doubt about what the ruling is | "If you do not know what the waiting period of women in this condition is to be" — that is, the verse is answering a question. |
| Doubt as to whether the woman has ceased to menstruate | "If you are not sure whether it has ceased or not" — a difficulty of establishing the fact. |
| Not a condition, but the noting of the fact | *In* here does not set up a real condition; it announces that the situation is in fact so. |

These readings are present in the classical sources. **I adopt no preference among them**, because the verse does not state the object of the doubt and none of the three readings runs against the wording of the verse.

**وَٱلَّٰٓـِٔى لَمْ يَحِضْنَ — "those who have not menstruated"**

The clause is added on top of the preceding ruling: their waiting period too is three months. The construction is grammatically elliptical (the *ruling* is not repeated; it is understood from the previous clause).

**There is a disagreement about the scope of this expression** and it would not be right to conceal it. The question debated in the sources is this: does the expression cover only those who have not yet menstruated on account of youth, or also adult women who for some reason do not menstruate?

| View | Ground |
|---|---|
| Only those of a young age | That the clause is coupled to the expression *yaʾisna* (those who have ceased); the two together describe the two ends of the cycle. |
| Also adults who for some reason do not menstruate | The generality of the wording; *lam yaḥiḍna* states no reason. |

This disagreement is real and has been treated at length in the literature of jurisprudence; and how the waiting period of a woman whose menstruation has ceased for some reason is to be computed is a separate matter of debate. **I do not settle it here.** This commentary does not issue legal rulings; for a ruling to be acted upon, the works of jurisprudence must be consulted.

**One further warning.** Debates unrelated to its subject have been conducted over this verse and are being conducted today. The wording of the verse specifies no age, commands no practice and contains no encouragement; it sets a **measure of computation**. To make a text say what it does not say — in whichever direction — is not exegesis. Where the verse is silent I am silent.

### وَأُو۟لَٰتُ ٱلْأَحْمَالِ أَجَلُهُنَّ أَن يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ — those with child

**حَمْل — root ح م ل (ḥ-m-l):** to load, to carry. *Ḥaml* is the load carried; and from this, pregnancy. From the same root *ḥammāl*, *ḥamūla*, *taḥammul* (bearing a load), *ḥamīl*.

The choice of the word sets up an image of itself: pregnancy is named as **a load carried**. And the end of the waiting period with the same image: **يَضَعْنَ** — from the root *w-ḍ-ʿ*, **to lay down, to put down, to set down.** *Waḍʿu'l-ḥaml* is the laying down of the load.

That is, the verse names birth not as "giving birth" but as **"laying down the load"**. This is the ordinary usage of Arabic and I draw no special meaning from it; but the word's standing side by side with *ajal* (term of maturity) carries on the language of **load–term–payment** that the whole sūra carries.

**The logic of the ruling.** The pregnant woman's waiting period is tied not to months but to childbirth. This sometimes lengthens the period a great deal (a divorce at the beginning of the pregnancy), sometimes shortens it a great deal (a divorce days before the birth).

Why? Because one of the functions of the waiting period is **that lineage not be confused** — that to whom the child belongs should not remain uncertain. In pregnancy this uncertainty does not exist in any case; the child is there. The period is kept not for the removal of an uncertainty but for **the completion of the birth.**

And this has a consequence, written out plainly in the next verse of the sūra: maintenance is to be paid to the pregnant woman until the birth (65/6). That is, tying the waiting period to the birth means extending the protection to the birth as well.

### وَمَن يَتَّقِ ٱللَّهَ يَجْعَل لَّهُۥ مِنْ أَمْرِهِۦ يُسْرًا

The second *taqwā* sentence in the sūra. And it needs to be compared with the previous one (65/2-3), because the promise changes.

| | **65/2-3** | **65/4** |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | *man yattaqi'llāh* | *man yattaqi'llāh* (the same) |
| Promise | *makhrajan* + *yarzuqhu min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasib* | *min amrihi yusrā* |
| What it answers | Constriction and lack of resource | **The difficulty of the affair** |

That is, the two promises answer different hardships: the first **the absence of a way out** and **the absence of resource**, the second **the arduousness of the affair**.

And the context of the second confirms this: verse 4 lists cases that are hard to compute. Ceased to menstruate, never menstruated, pregnant — each of these is a state that creates difficulty as to how the period is to be reckoned. And immediately after these the verse says "He grants him ease in his affair".

**يُسْر — root ي س ر (y-s-r).** Ease. The account the lexicographers give of the concrete sense of the root is this: *yusr* is a thing's being fluent, effortless, unobstructed. From the same root *maysara* (breadth, ease), *taysīr* (making easy), *yasīr* (easy, little).

**مِنْ أَمْرِهِۦ** — "of his affair". The word *amr* occurs here for the fourth time in the sūra, and I shall take that up separately in the closing section.

**To whom does the pronoun refer?** There are two readings: (a) "of his affair", that is, of the person's own affair; (b) "of His command", that is, of God's command — on this reading the ease comes from the divine command itself. Both readings are present in the sources; the first appears better suited to the context, but this is a preference and it is not binding.

**And a connection: `073-muzzemmil.md` 73/20.** There, in treating the lightening of the ruling, this was recorded: *"the lightening is offered not as a concession but as a direction"* and *"the act is preserved, the measure is made elastic."*

Here the same structure is set up by a different route. In al-Muzzammil the command **itself** was being lightened (*fa'qraʾū mā tayassara* — the same root, *y-s-r*). Here the command is not lightened; **ease is promised** to the one who applies the ruling. Two sūras produce two different solutions with the same root: one makes the load smaller, the other supports the one carrying it.

I record this as an observation. That the two texts use the same root is the text's datum; the distinction drawn between them is mine.

## 65/5 — ذَٰلِكَ أَمْرُ ٱللَّهِ أَنزَلَهُۥٓ إِلَيْكُمْ وَمَن يَتَّقِ ٱللَّهَ يُكَفِّرْ عَنْهُ سَيِّـَٔاتِهِۦ وَيُعْظِمْ لَهُۥٓ أَجْرًا

*Dhālika amru'llāhi anzalahu ilaykum, wa man yattaqi'llāha yukaffir ʿanhu sayyiʾātihi wa yuʿẓim lahu ajrā*

"That is the command of God; He has sent it down to you. And whoever guards himself against God, He covers his misdeeds for him and enlarges his reward."

### ذَٰلِكَ أَمْرُ ٱللَّهِ — an intervening heading

A short verse, and it serves as a closure: a seal upon everything counted up to here.

**ذَٰلِكَ** — a demonstrative of distance. In `107-maun.md` two functions of this demonstrative were recorded (distancing/belittling, and visibility). Here there is a third function: **gathering.** The demonstrative gathers all the rulings before it into a single indication.

**أَمْرُ ٱللَّهِ** — and here the **first** sense of the word (command, order) is prominent. But the second sense in Arabic (affair, situation) stands behind it as well and will come forward at the end of the sūra.

**أَنزَلَهُۥٓ إِلَيْكُمْ** — "He has sent it down to you."

This expression is striking. The Qurʾān generally uses the verb "to send down" for revelation, for the Book, for a verse. Here it is used for **a legal regulation**.

That is, the sūra puts the computation of the waiting period and the ruling about the dwelling into the category of "what has been revealed". This is a claim about the source of the ruling: **these are not custom, not policy, not advice.** They are in the same class as what has been sent down.

**Root ن ز ل (n-z-l).** The difference between the two patterns of this root (*anzala* / *nazzala*) was taken up in `002-bakara.md` at 2/23 and in `097-kadr.md`; I do not repeat that analysis. Here the **إِفْعَال** form is used: *anzala* — sending down all at once, in a single act.

**إِلَيْكُمْ** — "towards you". The particle *ilā* conveys direction: what is sent down has an address, and the address is the one addressed.

### يُكَفِّرْ عَنْهُ سَيِّـَٔاتِهِۦ — "He covers his misdeeds"

**كَفَّرَ — root ك ف ر (k-f-r).** This root was treated in `002-bakara.md` and in `076-insan.md`; I do not repeat those analyses. A reminder: the concrete sense of the root is **to cover, to close over** — a farmer is called *kāfir* because he covers the seed with earth.

**And here the same root is used in reverse.** *Kufr* is a person's covering over a truth he has seen. *Takfīr* (in this pattern) is God's covering over the misdeeds of a servant.

That is, **the same act, in two different subjects, yields two opposite results:**

| Subject | What is covered | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A human being | The truth | *Kufr* — destroying |
| God | The servant's misdeeds | Forgiveness — saving |

This is one of the clearest symmetries in the Qurʾān's use of roots. Covering is in itself neither good nor bad; **what is covered** determines it. (The same structure was present between *ʿaddada* in `104-humeze.md` and *iḥṣāʾ* in this sūra as well: counting too was a neutral operation.)

**عَنْهُ** — "from him". The particle *ʿan* conveys removal: the misdeeds are taken off the servant and covered over.

**سَيِّئَة — root س و أ (s-w-ʾ):** to be evil, to be ugly. From the same root *sūʾ* (evil), *aswaʾ* (worse), *sawʾa* (the part that must be covered). The word is the opposite of *ḥasana* (a good deed) and the two often occur together in the Qurʾān.

### وَيُعْظِمْ لَهُۥٓ أَجْرًا — "and enlarges his reward"

**أَجْر — root أ ج ر (ʾ-j-r).** And this word belongs to the commercial language of the sūra: *ajr* is **the wage paid in return for work done**. From the same root *ijāra* (hire), *ajīr* (a hired worker), *ujra* (fee).

The choice of the word is meaningful: the return is named not as a favour but as **a payment that has been earned.** And the same root will be used one verse later for a payment between human beings: **فَـَٔاتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ** (65/6) — "give them their wages."

**This is one of the most striking word-pairings of the sūra.** Two verses apart, the same root on two levels:

- 65/5: God enlarges the servant's **reward**.
- 65/6: The man gives the woman who suckles her **wage**.

That is, the idea of payment works in two directions at once: from above downward and from person to person. And the word order sets the second immediately after the first.

I record this as an observation; I do not present it as a connection established in the classical commentaries. But that the two words are from the same root and stand this close together is the text's own datum.

**يُعْظِمْ — root ع ظ م (ʿ-ẓ-m):** to be great. The concrete sense of the root is **bone** (*ʿaẓm*); greatness is derived from the load-bearing of the skeleton. The *tafʿīl* form is causative here: to enlarge.

## 65/6 — أَسْكِنُوهُنَّ مِنْ حَيْثُ سَكَنتُم مِّن وُجْدِكُمْ وَلَا تُضَآرُّوهُنَّ لِتُضَيِّقُوا۟ عَلَيْهِنَّ وَإِن كُنَّ أُو۟لَٰتِ حَمْلٍ فَأَنفِقُوا۟ عَلَيْهِنَّ حَتَّىٰ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ فَإِنْ أَرْضَعْنَ لَكُمْ فَـَٔاتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ وَأْتَمِرُوا۟ بَيْنَكُم بِمَعْرُوفٍ وَإِن تَعَاسَرْتُمْ فَسَتُرْضِعُ لَهُۥٓ أُخْرَىٰ

*Askinūhunna min ḥaythu sakantum min wujdikum wa lā tuḍārrūhunna li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinn, wa in kunna ulāti ḥamlin fa-anfiqū ʿalayhinna ḥattā yaḍaʿna ḥamlahunn, fa-in arḍaʿna lakum fa-ātūhunna ujūrahunn, wa'tamirū baynakum bi-maʿrūf, wa in taʿāsartum fa-saturḍiʿu lahu ukhrā*

"Lodge them where you dwell yourselves, according to your means. Do not do them harm so as to press them into straits. If they are with child, spend on them until they lay down their burden. If they suckle for you, give them their wages. Consult one another between you in the recognised good way. And if you make things hard for one another, another woman will suckle for him."

The most detailed ruling-verse of the sūra. It contains four separate regulations: the dwelling, the prohibition of harm, maintenance in pregnancy, the wage for suckling. And at the end two sentences of procedure.

### أَسْكِنُوهُنَّ — "lodge them"

**Root س ك ن (s-k-n).** And the choice of this root is the most beautiful thing in the verse.

**The concrete sense of the root is not to be sheltered but to stop, to remain still, to subside.**

- **سُكُون (sukūn)** — stillness. In Arabic grammar a letter's carrying no vowel is also *sukūn*: the letter stops, it does not move.
- **سَكِينَة (sakīna)** — inner calm, the settling of the heart. It occurs in several places in the Qurʾān (e.g. al-Baqara 2/248; al-Fatḥ 48/4).
- **مِسْكِين (miskīn)** — the poor man. The connection the lexicographers give: poverty leaves a person **motionless**; the *miskīn* is the one whom need has nailed to the spot.
- **مَسْكَن (maskan)** — the place one dwells, a house.
- **سَكَن** — what gives repose, the place taken refuge in.

**That is, in Arabic "house" means "the place where one stops".** The name of shelter comes from the stopping of movement.

And this changes what the verse is doing. The command is not "give them a house"; it is **"make them stop, leave them at rest, keep them in their place"**. To leave a person homeless is to leave them **in motion** — looking for somewhere to go, moving house, going from door to door.

The Qurʾān uses the same root in describing the relation of husband and wife as well:

> **وَمِنْ ءَايَٰتِهِۦٓ أَنْ خَلَقَ لَكُم مِّنْ أَنفُسِكُمْ أَزْوَٰجًا لِّتَسْكُنُوٓا۟ إِلَيْهَا**
> *"And among His signs is that He created for you mates of your own kind, that you might **find rest** with them."* (al-Rūm 30/21)

There too *li-taskunū* — the same root. That is, marriage is named, by its very root, as a relation of **coming to rest**.

**And this says something as one reads al-Ṭalāq 65/6:** even as the marriage is ending, the woman's right to "come to rest" does not end. The relationship had been set up for *sukūn*; as the relationship is untied, the material side of *sukūn* — a roof — is preserved.

I offer this as an inference. That the two verses use the same root is the text's datum; the connection between them is mine.

### مِنْ حَيْثُ سَكَنتُم مِّن وُجْدِكُمْ — the measure

Two qualifications are set, and both determine quantity.

**مِنْ حَيْثُ سَكَنتُم** — "where you dwell yourselves". That is, the standard is the man's **own** standard of living. No separate, lower measure is defined for the woman.

This is one of the most practical protections of the regulation. Had a standard been laid down as "a suitable place", what "suitable" was would have remained open to dispute, and the dispute would have been won by the stronger. When the measure is tied to the man's own life, the dispute closes: **wherever you live, there you shall lodge her.**

**مِّن وُجْدِكُمْ** — "according to your means / your capacity".

**وُجْد — root و ج د (w-j-d).** To find. From the same root *wujūd* (existence — "being found"), *wijdān*, *mawjūd*, *ījād*. *Wujd* here means **means, resources, what one has in hand**.

That is, the second qualification limits the first: you shall lodge her where you dwell yourself — **but not beyond what you have.**

**The two qualifications together set up a balance:**
- Lower bound: your own standard. You cannot bring it below that.
- Upper bound: your means. More than that is not asked.

This balance is the application of what the sūra will formulate one verse later (65/7) as a general rule.

### وَلَا تُضَآرُّوهُنَّ لِتُضَيِّقُوا۟ عَلَيْهِنَّ — a prohibition placed upon intention

**And here there is something out of the ordinary from the point of view of legal technique.**

**ضَرَّ — root ض ر ر (ḍ-r-r):** to do harm. *Ḍarar*, *ḍirār*, *muḍārra* are from the same root. The verb here is in the **مُفَاعَلَة** form (*tuḍārrū*), that is, it carries reciprocity: mutual harming, or one party's persistently harming the other.

**لِتُضَيِّقُوا۟** — "**in order to** straiten". The *lām* conveys purpose.

**ضَيَّقَ — root ض ي ق (ḍ-y-q):** to be narrow, to be pressed. *Ḍayyiq* (narrow), *ḍīq* (narrowness), *taḍyīq* (narrowing).

**What is out of the ordinary is this: the prohibition is attached not to the act but to the PURPOSE BEHIND the act.**

The verse does not say "do them no harm" and stop. It says "do them no harm **in order to straiten them**". That is, the object of the prohibition is not a piece of conduct but an **intention.**

This is something rarely met with in legal texts, and the reason is worth thinking about.

**The reason must be this:** the greater part of the harm in this field is done by conduct that, taken item by item, looks **legitimate**. The man does not put the woman out of the house — but he makes the house unusable. He does not cut off the maintenance — but he delays it. He does nothing contrary to the rule, yet he makes her staying there unbearable.

Each of these kinds of conduct cannot be prohibited separately; their number is endless. **The only thing that can be prohibited is their common purpose.**

And the verse does exactly this: instead of enumerating the acts, it prohibits the purpose that describes them all together. *Li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinna* — "in order to press them".

This is the field covered in modern law by the concepts of **abuse of right** and **evasion** (*fraus legis*): keeping to the letter of the rule and defeating its purpose. That the verse has devoted a clause to this I record as a mark of how realistic a picture of the human being the regulation was written with. The text takes into account not the man who will break the rule but **the man who will go around the rule.**

**The Qurʾān sets the same qualification elsewhere on the same subject:**

> **وَلَا تُمْسِكُوهُنَّ ضِرَارًا لِّتَعْتَدُوا۟** *"Do not keep them in order to do harm, so as to transgress against them."* (al-Baqara 2/231)

There too the same root (*ḍirār*) and again a *lām* of purpose (*li-taʿtadū*). The two verses set up the same mechanism for two different acts: in keeping and in lodging.

### فَأَنفِقُوا۟ عَلَيْهِنَّ حَتَّىٰ يَضَعْنَ حَمْلَهُنَّ — maintenance in pregnancy

**نَفَقَ — root ن ف ق (n-f-q).** The concrete sense of the root is interesting: **to be used up, to run out; and a thing's being current coin, finding a ready market.** *Nafaqa* is what is used up — that is, wealth spent. From the same root *nafaq* (an underground passage, a tunnel — entering at one end and coming out at the other), *munāfiq* (the connection the lexicographers give: the field-mouse's burrow with two mouths; the one who enters by one door and leaves by another).

**Infāq**, in Form IV: to bring wealth out, to consume it, to spend it. That is, wealth that is spent is wealth that is **used up**; the opposite of what is hoarded.

**عَلَيْهِنَّ** — "upon them". The particle *ʿalā* here conveys obligation: the spending is in their favour and upon the man as a debt.

**There is a disagreement about the scope of the ruling.** The question debated in the sources: is maintenance owed to a woman who is not pregnant and who has been separated by a divorce that is definitively closed to return? This clause of the verse plainly commands maintenance for those who are pregnant; whether the conclusion follows from this that it is not owed for those who are not pregnant is disputed. **I do not settle it.**

From the point of view of the logic of the regulation this can be said: **that the maintenance has been tied to pregnancy protects the child directly.** The nourishment of the unborn child passes through the nourishment of the mother; and if the mother has no income, the payment must come from the father. It was for this reason too that the period was tied not to months but to the birth (65/4).

### فَإِنْ أَرْضَعْنَ لَكُمْ فَـَٔاتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ — the wage for suckling

**رَضَعَ — root ر ض ع (r-ḍ-ʿ):** to suck. *Raḍāʿ*, suckling; *murḍiʿa*, the woman suckling; *raḍīʿ*, a foster-brother.

**And what is striking here is the way the sentence is built.**

**فَإِنْ** — a particle of condition, conveying **possibility**. That is, suckling is **not commanded** to the woman. It does not say "let them suckle"; it says "if they suckle".

**لَكُمْ** — "**for you**". That is, the suckling is positioned not as the woman's own business but as **a service performed on the man's behalf**.

**فَـَٔاتُوهُنَّ أُجُورَهُنَّ** — "give them **their wages**". And the word is *ajr*: a wage. Not a kindness, not a gift, not an assistance — **a payment.**

When the three elements are joined, the structure that emerges is this: **a divorced woman's suckling of the child is defined as paid work.**

To see what this regulation does one must think of the alternative. Had suckling been counted the woman's **duty**, there would have been no return for it. The woman would both suckle the child and find her livelihood elsewhere. And for a divorced woman to find another source of livelihood was, in that order, all but impossible.

The verse cuts this off in two strokes:
1. It **does not make suckling obligatory** (*fa-in arḍaʿna* — "if they suckle").
2. If she suckles, it **attaches a wage** (*ujūrahunn*).

That is, the woman is granted a choice, and if she chooses, a payment is made.

**A qualification:** whether suckling is legally obligatory upon the woman, in which cases a wage becomes due, and how the wage is to be determined have been debated in detail in jurisprudence. I do not enter these debates and I issue no ruling. What I have shown here is **the frame set up by the words the verse uses**: the particle of condition, the qualification *lakum*, and the word *ajr*.

Sūrat al-Baqara treats the same subject more broadly (al-Baqara 2/233), and there too it is recorded that the wage for suckling is to be given *bi'l-maʿrūf*, that no one is to be held liable beyond his capacity, and that mother and father are not to harm one another through the child. The two sūras set up the same frame.

### وَأْتَمِرُوا۟ بَيْنَكُم بِمَعْرُوفٍ — "consult one another in the recognised good way"

**ٱئْتَمِرُوا۟ — root أ م ر (ʾ-m-r)**, in the **إِفْتِعَال (iftiʿāl)** form. And this pattern conveys reciprocity here: **to command one another, to exchange commands with one another** — that is, **to consult, to take counsel, to decide together.**

**The choice of the word is extremely meaningful.**

Throughout the sūra the word *amr* had always come **from above**: *amru'llāh* (65/5), *amri rabbihā* (65/8), *bālighu amrih* (65/3). The command was something given.

Here, in a single place, the word becomes **horizontal**. Two people "command" one another — that is, they take each other's view reciprocally.

And this is asked of a couple who have separated. Their relationship is over, hostility between them is likely, and there is a child in the middle. The verse asks them **to decide together** about the child.

**بِمَعْرُوفٍ** — and again the same qualification. The word occurs in the sūra for the third time (twice in verse 2, once here). The measure does not change: *maʿrūf* in keeping, *maʿrūf* in parting, *maʿrūf* in consulting about the child.

### وَإِن تَعَاسَرْتُمْ فَسَتُرْضِعُ لَهُۥٓ أُخْرَىٰ — if you cannot come to terms

**تَعَاسَرْتُمْ — root ع س ر (ʿ-s-r)**, in the **تَفَاعُل (tafāʿul)** form.

And the *tafāʿul* form has been taken up several times in this body of work: in `102-tekasur.md` with *takāthur*, in `083-mutaffifin.md` with *tanāfus*, in `002-bakara.md` at 2/148 with *istibāq*. I do not repeat the finding made there; a reminder: **tafāʿul conveys reciprocity (*mushāraka*)** — the act goes back and forth between two parties.

**تَعَاسُر**, in this pattern: **to make things hard for one another, to render the business difficult mutually.**

That is, the verse does not exactly say "if you cannot come to terms". What it says is sharper: **"if you make things hard for one another."** Failure to agree is a passive state; *taʿāsur* is an active act. And the pattern does not lay the fault on one side — it says it is **mutual**.

**And compared with the other instances of the *tafāʿul* pattern in this body of work, a table emerges:**

| Word | Place | What is done mutually | Ruling |
|---|---|---|---|
| **تَكَاثُر** | al-Takāthur 102/1 | To outdo one another in abundance | Censured |
| **تَنَافُس** | al-Muṭaffifīn 83/26 | To vie with one another for what is precious | Commanded |
| **اسْتِبَاق** | al-Baqara 2/148 | To get ahead of one another in good works | Commanded |
| **تَعَاسُر** | al-Ṭalāq 65/6 | To make things hard for one another | Noted — and its consequence announced |

The fourth row stands somewhere different from the others: the verse neither commands *taʿāsur* nor forbids it. **It only states its consequence.**

**فَسَتُرْضِعُ لَهُۥٓ أُخْرَىٰ** — "another woman will suckle for him."

And the tone of this sentence has to be dwelt on, because it is easily misread.

The sentence can be read as a threat: "if you do not agree, I shall find someone else." But the construction does not fit this:

- The verb announces **a passive outcome**, not a sanction. *Sa-turḍiʿu* — "will suckle". Who will have her suckle, who will arrange it, is not said.
- The pronoun **لَهُۥ** — "**for him**". To whom does it refer? To the child or to the father. Both readings are possible. If it refers to the child, then at the centre of the sentence stands the child's need.
- And most important of all: the sentence **states the solution, not the punishment.** When agreement cannot be reached the child does not go hungry; another arrangement comes into play.

That is, what the verse does is this: **it takes the disagreement out of being a dead end.** It accepts from the outset the possibility that the parties will not agree and leaves a road open for that possibility.

This is another mark of the realism of the regulation. The text **hopes** that the parties will consult *bi-maʿrūf*, but does not **assume** it. The hope is met by a command (*wa'tamirū*), the reality by a condition (*wa in taʿāsartum*).

**And the party who loses is thereby determined as well.** Parties who do not agree lose the payment relation: the woman cannot take the wage, the man is obliged to pay someone else. This is a practical pressure pushing both towards agreement — and the verse sets it not as a punishment but as the natural consequence of the matter.

### What this says to the present

**One. A prohibition placed upon intention.**

The qualification *li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinna* targets the cluster of conduct that in present-day language might be called "systematically making life unbearable". The common feature of these kinds of conduct is that, taken one by one, none of them counts as a breach of a rule.

This is a pattern that holds outside divorce as well: at work, in a family, in any asymmetry of power. To obey a rule is not the same thing as to protect what the rule protects. That the verse has devoted a clause to purpose recognises this difference.

**Two. The paying of care work.**

*Fa-ātūhunna ujūrahunna* — the definition of suckling as work done for a wage touches on the question of making care work visible.

Here one must be careful and I shall not force an inference: the verse says nothing about care work within a marriage. What it says is narrow: **care that continues after the marriage has ended cannot be expected without return.**

Even this narrow finding is meaningful to record, because expecting it without return is the default attitude. The verse breaks that default and makes payment the rule.

**Three. Leaving a way out for disagreement.**

What the sentence *wa in taʿāsartum* does — accepting the possibility of disagreement from the outset and opening a road for it — is one of the basic principles of good regulation.

A rule that assumes the parties will act in good faith collapses when there is no good faith. The verse does both at once: it commands good faith (*wa'tamirū bi-maʿrūf*), but it also leaves a way out for the bad case. And the aim of that way out is not the right of the parties but **the feeding of the child.**

## 65/7 — لِيُنفِقْ ذُو سَعَةٍ مِّن سَعَتِهِۦ وَمَن قُدِرَ عَلَيْهِ رِزْقُهُۥ فَلْيُنفِقْ مِمَّآ ءَاتَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ لَا يُكَلِّفُ ٱللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا مَآ ءَاتَىٰهَا سَيَجْعَلُ ٱللَّهُ بَعْدَ عُسْرٍ يُسْرًا

*Li-yunfiq dhū saʿatin min saʿatih, wa man qudira ʿalayhi rizquhu fa'l-yunfiq mimmā ātāhu'llāh, lā yukallifu'llāhu nafsan illā mā ātāhā, sa-yajʿalu'llāhu baʿda ʿusrin yusrā*

"Let him who has ample means spend out of his ampleness. And let him whose provision has been straitened for him spend out of what God has given him. God does not burden any soul with more than He has given it. God will bring ease after hardship."

One of the densest verses of the sūra. A rule of maintenance, a general principle and a promise — the three one after another.

### سَعَة and قُدِرَ — thinking of money as a space

The verse names wealth and poverty with two words, and **both are words of place.**

**سَعَة — root و س ع (w-s-ʿ):** to be wide, to take up room, to have room for. From the same root *wāsiʿ* (wide), *tawassuʿ* (widening), *wusʿ* (capacity, the field of what one can manage), *ittisāʿ*.

The concrete sense of the root is **width** — the room a space can hold. *Dhū saʿa*, "possessor of width": literally, **the man with room around him.**

**قُدِرَ — root ق د ر (q-d-r).** The third branch of meaning of the root, as recorded in `097-kadr.md`: **narrowness, constriction.** *Qadara ʿalayhi* — He straitened it for him, He restricted it.

And there it was already **this verse** that was shown as the proof of that branch. I treated the relation between the root's two occurrences in this sūra at 65/3; here I only recall the result: **what is narrow is a measure too.**

**That the verb is passive** is also important: *qudira* — "was straitened". Who straitened it is not said; but the continuation of the verse (*mimmā ātāhu'llāh*) shows the agent.

This changes the man's position. Poverty is named not as the man's failure but as the **measure given** to him. The practical consequence of this is: the man who is to give little is not in a position that ought to make him ashamed. And a man who is not ashamed gives what he can give; an ashamed man runs away.

**The two words together set up the sūra's language about money: wealth is not a quantity but a SPACE.** One man stands in a wide space, another's space has been narrowed. And the spending is done out of that space.

This picture also joins with the expression *li-tuḍayyiqū* (in order to straiten) one verse earlier — there too straitening was something the man could do to the woman. Not the same root (**ض ي ق** and **ق د ر**), but the same picture: **narrowing.** The thing the man is forbidden to do to the woman (to straiten) occurs one verse later as a thing God may do to the man. I record this as an observation; the roots of the two words are different and I claim no relation of root from it.

### مِّن سَعَتِهِۦ / مِمَّآ ءَاتَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ — the particle "min"

And here a finding established earlier in this body of work falls exactly into place.

**In `002-bakara.md` at 2/3**, in treating *wa mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn*, this was recorded — I do not repeat it, I recall it: *"The verse does not say 'they spend what We have given them', it says 'out **of** what We have given them'. In Arabic min here conveys partitivity: a portion of it. That is, they are not being asked to give away everything they have."*

**In al-Ṭalāq 65/7 the same particle occurs twice:**

- **مِّن سَعَتِهِۦ** — "out **of** his ampleness"
- **مِمَّآ ءَاتَىٰهُ ٱللَّهُ** — "out **of** what God has given him"

Both are *min*, both partitive.

And its function in this context is slightly different from that in al-Baqara, and more concrete. There the matter was the measure of spending. Here the matter is **the payability of the maintenance**:

Had the verse said "let him spend his ampleness", the ruling would have been inapplicable — no one can give his whole substance in maintenance. The particle of partitivity makes the ruling **capable of being carried out.**

And just as this makes payment possible, it also **makes evasion impossible.** The defence "I have nothing" is invalid so long as he has something in hand: the particle *min* commands that a portion of whatever is in his hand be given. Zero is in no case an answer.

### لَا يُكَلِّفُ ٱللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا مَآ ءَاتَىٰهَا — and the difference from al-Baqara 2/286

The verse passes from the rule of maintenance to a general principle. And this principle occurs in another place in the Qurʾān in almost the same wording — **but a single word of it is different**, and the difference is not trivial.

> **al-Ṭalāq 65/7:** لَا يُكَلِّفُ ٱللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا **مَآ ءَاتَىٰهَا**
> *"God does not burden any soul with more than **what He has given it**."*

> **al-Baqara 2/286:** لَا يُكَلِّفُ ٱللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا **وُسْعَهَا**
> *"God does not burden any soul with more than **its capacity**."*

The construction is identical: the same verb (*yukallifu*), the same negation, the same object (*nafsan*), the same particle of exception (*illā*). The only thing that changes is the content of the exception.

| | **al-Baqara 2/286** | **al-Ṭalāq 65/7** |
|---|---|---|
| Measure | **وُسْعَهَا** — its capacity, what it can manage | **مَآ ءَاتَىٰهَا** — what has been given to it |
| Root | و س ع — width, being able to hold | أ ت ي — to come, to bring |
| What it looks to | What the person **can bear** | What has **been given** to the person |
| Context | General obligation (commands, prohibitions, responsibility) | Maintenance — money |

**The difference is real and must be handled carefully.**

*Wusʿ* is a word of **capacity**: a person's power to bear. The measure is the person's endurance.

*Mā ātāhā* is a word of **inventory**: what has actually been given to the person. The measure is what the person has in hand.

And the two are not the same thing. A person **can bear** more than what he has in hand — by borrowing, by cutting into his own share, by going hungry. That is, the measure of *wusʿ* is wider than the measure of *mā ātā*.

**That Sūrat al-Ṭalāq has chosen the narrower measure agrees with its context.** The subject is money, and money is measured not by endurance but by quantity. A man cannot, by being told "you are able", be loaded with a payment that is not in his hand.

I record this difference **as an observation**. The wording of the two verses is different, and this is the text's datum. But I **do not draw** from it the conclusion that the two principles conflict or that one restricts the other — these are two measures set in different contexts, and I have no certain knowledge of how this difference has been handled in the classical sources. What I record is the existence of the difference of wording, and that each wording suits its own context.

**كَلَّفَ — root ك ل ف (k-l-f).** And the concrete sense of this root explains the weight of the term: **to take on a task with difficulty, with hardship.** *Kulfa* is trouble; *takalluf* is forcing oneself; *mukallaf* is a person upon whom a troublesome task has been laid.

That is, the word **taklīf** carries within itself an acknowledgement of difficulty. A command is not an easy thing; it is a load. And the verse draws the boundary of that load.

This belongs to the same family as the structure at 73/20 in `073-muzzemmil.md`. The finding recorded there was: *"the act is preserved, the measure is made elastic."* Here it is the same: the command of maintenance is not removed; its amount is adjusted to the person.

And another expression of the same principle is in the neighbouring sūra: **فَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ مَا ٱسْتَطَعْتُمْ** — *"So guard yourselves against God as far as you are able"* (al-Taghābun 64/16). Three texts — al-Baqara, al-Ṭalāq, al-Taghābun — say the same thing with three different words: obligation is bound to capacity.

### سَيَجْعَلُ ٱللَّهُ بَعْدَ عُسْرٍ يُسْرًا — the soundest cross-finding in the corpus

And now we come to the most important place in this section.

**In `094-insirah.md`, in treating al-Inshirāḥ 94/5-6** — *fa-inna maʿa'l-ʿusri yusrā* / *inna maʿa'l-ʿusri yusrā*, "with hardship there is **together** an ease" — the most debated question of that sūra was the particle *maʿa*: why not "after" but "together with"?

And there a piece of evidence was given showing that the choice was deliberate. What was presented as the strongest of the arguments was this:

> *"And there is a decisive piece of evidence that this is a choice: **the Qurʾān says 'baʿda' in another place.** … The same two words (ʿusr and yusr), with a different particle. That is, the Qurʾān knows how to say 'after' and says it when it is called for. In al-Inshirāḥ it does not. This is the soundest datum showing that the choice of particle is deliberate — because the comparison is made from within the Qurʾān itself, and not by an assumption from outside."*

**That "other place" is here.**

Now we can complete the connection from the other end. The two sentences side by side:

> **al-Inshirāḥ 94/5-6:** فَإِنَّ **مَعَ** ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا · إِنَّ **مَعَ** ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا
> **al-Ṭalāq 65/7:** سَيَجْعَلُ ٱللَّهُ **بَعْدَ** عُسْرٍ يُسْرًا

The same two words, two different particles. And the difference of particle is not the only difference — there are three further differences, and all three point in the same direction.

### Difference 1: the frame of the sentence

**In al-Inshirāḥ the sentence is a nominal sentence.** *Inna maʿa'l-ʿusri yusrā* — there is no verb. In Arabic a nominal sentence conveys **fixity** (constancy, permanence); it is not tied to time. That is, the sentence is a **statement of a state**: with hardship there is ease. This was so yesterday, it is so today, it will be so tomorrow.

**In al-Ṭalāq the sentence is a verbal sentence** and the verb is **in the future**: *sa-yajʿalu* — "He will make, He will bring about".

The **سَ (sīn)** at the head is in Arabic the particle of *tanfīs*, and it carries an imperfect verb into the future. It is said to convey a nearer future than *sawfa*, but its real function is to remove indeterminacy: the act will certainly happen.

That is:

| | **al-Inshirāḥ** | **al-Ṭalāq** |
|---|---|---|
| Frame | Nominal sentence | Verbal sentence |
| Time | Timeless — a continuing state | Future — *sa-yajʿalu* |
| Subject | None (there is no verb) | **God** — written out explicitly |
| What it says | A **state** | A **promise** |

**And the difference comes out from here.** Al-Inshirāḥ announces a fact; al-Ṭalāq gives a word. The first says "it is so", the second says "I shall do it".

**The subject's being written out explicitly** is meaningful as well: *sa-yajʿalu'llāh* — "**God** will bring it about". In al-Inshirāḥ there was no subject, because what was being stated was a state. Here the name of one who promises appears, because a promise has no existence without an owner.

### Difference 2: definiteness

**In al-Inshirāḥ:** *al-ʿusr* (**definite**) — *yusran* (**indefinite**).
**In al-Ṭalāq:** *ʿusrin* (**indefinite**) — *yusran* (**indefinite**).

This difference touches the very centre of a debate taken up in `094-insirah.md`. As recorded there, there is an argument much repeated in the tradition: in Arabic, when a definite noun is repeated the two are the same thing, and when an indefinite noun is repeated the two are separate things. On this rule the two occurrences of *al-ʿusr* in al-Inshirāḥ are the same hardship, and the two occurrences of *yusran* are two distinct eases — and from this the conclusion is drawn that "one hardship cannot prevail over two eases".

**Al-Ṭalāq 65/7 is a place where this argument does not operate**, and the reason is simple: there is no repetition here. The sentence is said once and both words are indefinite.

What is the function of the indefiniteness here? Two readings are possible:

| Reading | Account |
|---|---|
| **Generalisation** | "An ease after a hardship" — any hardship, any ease. The rule is general. |
| **Magnification (taʿẓīm)** | "Such an ease as…" — the indefiniteness announces that the extent cannot be drawn. |

Both are defensible; I adopt no preference. But this must be recorded: **the indefiniteness takes the sentence out of being proper to this man and this hardship of his.** The verse does not say "after this straitness of yours". The rule covers this man too, but it is not restricted to him.

### Difference 3: context — a promise attached to a bill

And the third difference is the most concrete distinction between the two.

**The context of al-Inshirāḥ is a consolation.** The sūra counts up that the breast of the one addressed has been opened, that his load has been taken down, that his name has been raised; then it sets the rule. That is, the sentence comes after favours that have been given.

**The context of al-Ṭalāq is a debt.** What stands immediately before the sentence, in order:

1. Let him who has ample means give out of his ampleness.
2. **Let him whose provision has been straitened** give out of what God has given him.
3. No one is liable for more than what he has been given.
4. **God will bring ease after hardship.**

That is, the one addressed by the sentence is **a man with too little money to pay.** He is obliged to pay maintenance to the woman he has divorced; perhaps a wage for suckling too. He does not have enough in hand. The verse first lightens his load (give out of what you have, more is not asked), then gives him this word.

**And the sentence has a second addressee, most of the time unseen:** the woman who is to live on that money. Her maintenance is tied to the means of a man who cannot pay. Her straitness comes from the man's straitness.

That is, the promise is said to both parties at once: to the one who pays and to the one who waits for payment.

**I record this as a finding: one of the best-known sentences of consolation in the Qurʾān has been attached to the back of a bill.** This does not lessen the weight of the sentence — it increases it. Because the consolation is given not where consolation is easiest, but at the moment when a person is thinking about the money that will go out of his hand.

### What the two sentences say together

To gather up:

> **Al-Inshirāḥ** says that there is something **inside** the hardship. Timeless, without a subject, a statement of a state. What falls to the person is **to look**.
>
> **Al-Ṭalāq** says that there will be something **after** the hardship. In the future tense, with a subject, a promise. What falls to the person is **to wait** — but not to wait idly: the period of waiting is the period in which the maintenance is paid.

The two sentences do not conflict. They do two different jobs and are said to two different situations. And as was recorded in `094-insirah.md`, the difference between them is **practical**: "The one who waits stops; the one who knows it is alongside looks."

**And here we can make an addition from that section towards this side.** In al-Inshirāḥ "looking" was asked; in al-Ṭalāq waiting is asked — but while waiting something is **done**. The man does not sit while he waits for the ease; he pays out of what he has. That is, the *baʿda* of al-Ṭalāq is not an empty interval: **the inside of the interval has been filled with a ruling.**

I record this as my own reading.

**And finally, a note about method.** This comparison between two verses is one of the soundest cross-findings in this body of work, and the reason for its soundness is this: **the comparison is made not by an assumption from outside but from within the text itself.** To claim that the choice of a particle is deliberate is normally an unprovable claim — to say "it could have been said otherwise" is not enough. Here, however, the text has itself said the "otherwise". The two verses are each other's control group.

### What this says to the present

**One. The man who cannot pay and the man who does not pay.**

The most practical distinction of the verse is here. *Qudira ʿalayhi rizquh* — the man whose provision has been straitened is not outside the rule; the rule binds him **by his own measure.**

This distinction stands in the same place today. A person who cannot meet an obligation and a person who does not meet it are not the same thing; and an order that does not distinguish the two punishes the first and leaves the second free to hide behind the first.

The verse's solution closes both: because the measure is tied to **what has been given**, much is asked of one to whom much has been given; little is asked of one to whom little has been given; and **there is no case in which nothing at all is asked.**

**Two. The placing of a promise after a task.**

Where the sentence *sa-yajʿalu'llāhu baʿda ʿusrin yusrā* has been placed matters as much as the sentence itself.

The promise comes not **after a description of the hardship** but **after the thing asked of one while in the hardship.** That is, the order is: fulfil your obligation — then the promise.

This says something about how consolation works. To tell a person "it will pass" makes him do nothing. What the verse does is different: it first says what can be done (give out of what you have), then adds the promise. The promise does not stand in the place of the action; it rides on top of the action.

**Three. The distinction between "what is given" and "what can be borne".**

The difference of wording between al-Baqara 2/286 and al-Ṭalāq 65/7 carries a real counterpart in a matter to do with money.

When "what he is able for" is asked of a person, the limit is indeterminate — because a person can nearly always be pushed a little further. When "what has been given to him" is asked of a person, the limit becomes measurable.

And a measurable limit protects both debtor and creditor: the debtor cannot be pushed until he is exhausted, and the creditor cannot be put off with the defence "I am not able".

## 65/8 — وَكَأَيِّن مِّن قَرْيَةٍ عَتَتْ عَنْ أَمْرِ رَبِّهَا وَرُسُلِهِۦ فَحَاسَبْنَٰهَا حِسَابًا شَدِيدًا وَعَذَّبْنَٰهَا عَذَابًا نُّكْرًا

*Wa kaʾayyin min qaryatin ʿatat ʿan amri rabbihā wa rusulihi fa-ḥāsabnāhā ḥisāban shadīdan wa ʿadhdhabnāhā ʿadhāban nukrā*

"And how many a town went out from the command of its Lord and His messengers; and We called it to a severe reckoning and punished it with an unheard-of punishment."

### The transition: why does a legal text tell of destruction?

The sharpest turn of the sūra is here. For seven verses the waiting period, the dwelling, maintenance, the wage for suckling have been spoken of. In the eighth verse, suddenly, towns destroyed.

This transition looks disconnected and requires explanation. The explanations proposed in the classical commentaries, and what I want to add:

| Explanation | Account |
|---|---|
| **A general threat** | To recall the end of one who goes out from the command; to set a sanction behind the rulings. The most widespread explanation. |
| **Enlargement of scale** | To put disobedience in a small field (a household) in the same class as disobedience in a large field (a town). |
| **A statement of seriousness** | To prevent the subject from being counted "private life" and made light of. |

**And there is a word that binds these explanations to one another: أَمْر.**

### أَمْر — the backbone word of the sūra

The word in **eight** places in the sūra, and each time on a different scale:

| # | Verse | Expression | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 65/1 | *yuḥdithu baʿda dhālika **أَمْرًا*** | A new situation that may arise inside a house |
| 2 | 65/3 | *inna'llāha bālighu **أَمْرِهِ*** | God's affair reaching its target |
| 3 | 65/4 | *yajʿal lahu min **أَمْرِهِ** yusrā* | Ease in a person's affair |
| 4 | 65/5 | *dhālika **أَمْرُ** llāhi anzalahu ilaykum* | A ruling sent down |
| 5 | 65/6 | *وَأْتَمِرُوا۟ baynakum bi-maʿrūf* | Two people consulting one another |
| 6 | 65/8 | *ʿatat ʿan **أَمْرِ** rabbihā* | The command a town went out from |
| 7 | 65/9 | *wabāla **أَمْرِهَا** … ʿāqibatu **أَمْرِهَا*** | The outcome of that town's own affair |
| 8 | 65/12 | *yatanazzalu'l-**أَمْرُ** baynahunn* | The command descending among seven heavens |

**Eight occurrences, four scales:** the inside of a household, a person's affair, the fate of a town, and the working of the cosmos. All with the same word.

And this arises from the two senses of the Arabic word *amr* (command / affair) being gathered in a single word. English cannot carry this unity; each time it must say either "command" or "affair", and the link breaks.

**The work the sūra does may be described thus:** by naming the divorce rulings *amr*, the cause of destruction *amr*, and the working of the cosmos *amr*, it makes the three parts of the same order. Whether a man pays his wife maintenance and the annihilation of a town stand under the same word.

**I record this as an observation, not as a thesis.** The number of the word's occurrences in the sūra and their distribution are the text's data; the expression "parts of the same order" that I draw from them is my reading. *Amr* is a very common word in the Qurʾān and not every concentration need mean a deliberate weave. But given the length of this sūra (twelve verses) eight occurrences are striking, and the last of them (65/12) standing in the sentence that closes the sūra does not look like an accident.

### عَتَتْ — "went out from the command"

**Root ع ت و (ʿ-t-w).** Its sense: **to exceed the bound, to run wild, to stiffen, not to bow.** For the concrete sense of the root the lexicographers give "a thing's drying out and hardening, becoming unbendable" — like a dried branch that will not bend and breaks.

From the same root *ʿātī* (the insolent), *ʿutuww* (insolence), *ʿitāʾ* (obstinacy).

And the word's kinship with **ط غ ي (ṭ-gh-y)** is at the level of meaning, not at the level of root: *ṭughyān* is to overflow (treated at 2/15 in `002-bakara.md`), whereas *ʿutuww* is to harden and not bend. The one overflows the vessel, the other refuses to bend.

The particle **عَنْ** — "from". *ʿAtā ʿan*: to run wild by **going out** from the command. The particle shows the going out.

**قَرْيَة — root ق ر ي (q-r-y):** to gather, to bring together. *Qarya* is the place where people gather — village, town, city. The word does not announce size; it announces gathering.

**And note: the verbs are feminine.** *ʿAtat*, *ḥāsabnāhā*, *ʿadhdhabnāhā*, *dhāqat* — all refer back to *qarya* and are feminine. The town is spoken of like a person: it went out, it was called to reckoning, it saw punishment, it tasted. This is the ordinary usage of Arabic (*qarya* is feminine), but its effect is this: a community is handled as a single agent.

### فَحَاسَبْنَٰهَا حِسَابًا شَدِيدًا — the reckoning

**حَاسَبَ — root ح س ب**, in the **مُفَاعَلَة (mufāʿala)** form. And the pattern is meaningful here: *mufāʿala* conveys reciprocity. *Muḥāsaba* is the settling of accounts between two parties — not one party's calling the other to account, but **the account's being settled reciprocally.**

And this root occurs in the sūra for the third time. I recall the table I set up at 65/2-3: *lā yaḥtasib* (does not reckon), *ḥasbuh* (suffices him), and now *ḥāsabnāhā* (We called to reckoning).

**The sūra had set up a language of reckoning and here it closes it.** The period a human being counts, the source a human being cannot count, the authority that suffices a human being — and at the very end, the place where a human being's account is settled.

**حِسَابًا شَدِيدًا** — an absolute object: the repetition of the verbal noun of the verb. In Arabic this construction conveys intensification. "We called to reckoning, and to a severe reckoning at that."

**شَدِيد — root ش د د (sh-d-d):** to bind tight, to harden. *Shadda* (the sign of doubling in Arabic), *shidda*, *ashadd* are from the same root. The concrete sense of the root is **the tightness of a bond.**

### عَذَابًا نُّكْرًا — "an unheard-of punishment"

**نُكْر — root ن ك ر (n-k-r).** And this word stands in direct opposition to **مَعْرُوف** in verses 2 and 6.

The sense of the root: **not to recognise, to find strange, to find odd.** *Munkar* (unrecognised, evil), *inkār* (not to recognise), *nakira* (the indefinite noun — the unrecognised), *tankīr*.

**And the opposition is set up inside the sūra:**

| Word | Root | Place | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **مَعْرُوف** | ع ر ف | 65/2 (twice), 65/6 | The **known** good — the measure of conduct |
| **نُكْر** | ن ك ر | 65/8 | The **unrecognised** punishment — unheard of, its like unknown |

The sūra names the good "known" and the penalty "unknown". And this says more than a play on words: **the measure of conduct is familiar, no one can say I did not know; its return is not familiar, no one can foresee what will happen.**

I record this as an observation. The opposition of the two words is established in Arabic; the meaning I draw from their standing in these positions in the same sūra is my own reading.

## 65/9 — فَذَاقَتْ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهَا وَكَانَ عَٰقِبَةُ أَمْرِهَا خُسْرًا

*Fa-dhāqat wabāla amrihā wa kāna ʿāqibatu amrihā khusrā*

"So it tasted the burden of its affair, and the outcome of its affair was loss."

### ذَاقَتْ — "it tasted"

**Root ذ و ق (dh-w-q):** to taste. And the choice of the verb is striking: for the punishment it is not "it saw", "it met with", "it suffered"; it says **"it tasted"**.

The verb of tasting is the most **direct** of the senses. Seeing happens at a distance, hearing happens at a distance; tasting requires contact. And what is tasted enters into the one who tastes.

The Qurʾān often uses this verb for punishment, and it has an effect: the punishment is described not as a blow coming from outside but as **something taken inside.**

**And a second effect:** tasting gives knowledge. One who has tasted a thing has **learned** it. That is, the verse names the punishment at the same time as an acquiring of knowledge — something previously unknown (*nukr*) has now been tasted.

### وَبَال — "burden"

**Root و ب ل (w-b-l).** The concrete sense of the root: **what is heavy and harmful.** *Wābil* is heavy rain in large drops (it occurs at al-Baqara 2/264-265). *Wabīl* is what is heavy and hard to digest; *wabāl* is the heavy and harmful consequence of an act.

The lexicographers record that at the centre of the root lies **weight**: the rain's falling in large drops, a food's lying heavy, being caught under the consequence of an act — all from the same idea.

English reaches for the same image when it speaks of bearing the consequences of an act, or of a thing being on a man's own head: what is meant is a weight to be carried.

**وَبَالَ أَمْرِهَا** — "the burden of its affair". That is, the weight does not come from outside; it is the weight of **its own affair**. The punishment is described as the weight of the very thing that was done.

And this construction occurs in this form in several places in the Qurʾān — in this body of work it will meet us in the neighbouring sūra as well: **فَذَاقُوا۟ وَبَالَ أَمْرِهِمْ** (al-Taghābun 64/5). Two sūras use the same expression; one tells of past communities, the other of a town.

### عَٰقِبَة and خُسْر

**عَاقِبَة — root ع ق ب (ʿ-q-b):** heel; and from this, "to come behind, to follow after". *ʿĀqiba* is what **comes behind** a thing — its outcome.

That the word derives from the heel is meaningful: the outcome is the thing that walks behind the deed. The one walking does not see it, but it is always there.

**خُسْر — root خ س ر (kh-s-r):** harm, loss. It is a commercial word: the diminution of capital. This root was treated in full in `103-asr.md`; I do not repeat that analysis.

And the word's use here is the closing of the **commercial-legal language** the sūra has been building from the start: *ajal* (term), *iḥṣāʾ* (counting), *shahāda* (record), *ajr* (wage), *ḥisāb* (reckoning), and at the very end *khusr* (loss).

The sūra has been set up like a trading ledger: the period was counted, the payment was made, the witness was called, the account was settled, the balance came out.

## 65/10 — أَعَدَّ ٱللَّهُ لَهُمْ عَذَابًا شَدِيدًا فَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ يَٰٓأُو۟لِى ٱلْأَلْبَٰبِ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ قَدْ أَنزَلَ ٱللَّهُ إِلَيْكُمْ ذِكْرًا

*Aʿadda'llāhu lahum ʿadhāban shadīdā, fa'ttaqu'llāha yā ūli'l-albābi'lladhīna āmanū, qad anzala'llāhu ilaykum dhikrā*

"God has prepared a severe punishment for them. So guard yourselves against God, O you who possess the kernel and have believed. God has sent down to you a reminder."

### أَعَدَّ — and the root's third turn

**Root ع د د (ʿ-d-d).** From the same root-family as *ʿidda* and *aḥṣū* in the first verse of the sūra.

**أَعَدَّ**, in Form IV: **to prepare.** Its link with the root's sense of "counting" is direct — to prepare a thing is to count it up and complete it, to make it lack nothing. The distance English keeps between *preparing* and *counting* does not exist in Arabic.

**And the root follows a path in the sūra:**

| Place | Word | Who does it | What is counted/prepared |
|---|---|---|---|
| 65/1 | **عِدَّة** | — | The woman's waiting period |
| 65/1 | **أَحْصُوا۟** (a near sense) | A human being | The command to count that period |
| 65/2 | **ذَوَىْ عَدْلٍ** (a separate root: ع د ل) | A human being | Two equal witnesses |
| 65/10 | **أَعَدَّ** | **God** | The punishment |

At the beginning of the sūra a human being counts a period; at the end God has prepared a return. I record this as an observation; and I note as well that the word *ʿadl* comes from a separate root, so that no meaning may be drawn from the resemblance of sound.

### يَٰٓأُو۟لِى ٱلْأَلْبَٰبِ — "O you who possess the kernel"

**لُبّ — root ل ب ب (l-b-b).** And the concrete sense of this word is important: ***lubb* is the core, the inside, the kernel of a thing.** Not the shell of the walnut but its inside; the stone inside the flesh of the date.

In Arabic there are several words for the mind and each holds a different side:
- **عَقْل (ʿaql)** — its root is "to bind" (the camel's tether: *ʿiqāl*). The mind is what holds and reins in.
- **فُؤَاد (fuʾād)** — the heart; in its root there is "heat, kindling".
- **لُبّ (lubb)** — **the kernel.** The side of the mind that is not shell but core.

That is, *ūlu'l-albāb* are not "clever men"; they are **those who grasp the kernel**. Those who do not stay at the shell, who go down into the inside of the matter.

**And the placing of the address here is striking.**

Throughout the sūra the address had been *yā ayyuha'l-nabiyy* and second-person plural imperatives. Here, towards the end of the sūra, the one addressed is given a **quality**: those who grasp the kernel.

Why here? Because this verse is the place where the two parts are joined: on one side the rulings of the household, on the other the towns destroyed. **To see the link between these two requires looking not at the shell but at the kernel.** To draw a connection between a rule of maintenance and the collapse of a civilisation seems meaningless to one who stays on the surface.

I record this as my own reading.

**ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟** — "those who have believed". The adjective restricts *ūlu'l-albāb*. That is, the address is not to all who possess a mind, but to those of them who have believed.

This can be read as an exclusion; but it has a finer function. The verse sets belief and the possession of *lubb* side by side — and it enters the same family as the question recorded in `098-beyyine.md`, of knowledge's remaining incomplete where it does not issue in a result. The mind that goes down to the kernel and belief are ascribed in the same sentence to the same person.

### قَدْ أَنزَلَ ٱللَّهُ إِلَيْكُمْ ذِكْرًا — "He has sent down a reminder"

**ذِكْر — root ذ ك ر (dh-k-r):** to mention, to remember, to give voice to. This root was treated together with the differences of pattern in the sections `087-ala.md` and `088-gasiye.md`; I do not repeat it.

**Its coming indefinite** — not *al-dhikr* but *dhikran*: "a reminder". The indeterminacy can be read here as magnification.

**And this expression opens the next verse.** What is *dhikran*? Verse 11 answers: **رَّسُولًا** — a messenger.

## 65/11 — رَّسُولًا يَتْلُوا۟ عَلَيْكُمْ ءَايَٰتِ ٱللَّهِ مُبَيِّنَٰتٍ لِّيُخْرِجَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ مِنَ ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ إِلَى ٱلنُّورِ وَمَن يُؤْمِنۢ بِٱللَّهِ وَيَعْمَلْ صَٰلِحًا يُدْخِلْهُ جَنَّٰتٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَآ أَبَدًا قَدْ أَحْسَنَ ٱللَّهُ لَهُۥ رِزْقًا

*Rasūlan yatlū ʿalaykum āyāti'llāhi mubayyinātin li-yukhrija'lladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilu'l-ṣāliḥāti mina'l-ẓulumāti ila'l-nūr, wa man yuʾmin bi'llāhi wa yaʿmal ṣāliḥan yudkhilhu jannātin tajrī min taḥtiha'l-anhāru khālidīna fīhā abadā, qad aḥsana'llāhu lahu rizqā*

"A messenger; he recites to you the clear verses of God — in order to bring out those who believe and do righteous deeds from the darknesses into the light. And whoever believes in God and does what is righteous, He admits him into gardens beneath which rivers flow, abiding there for ever. God has indeed made his provision good."

### رَّسُولًا يَتْلُوا۟ — the link with Sūrat al-Bayyina

**The first three words of this verse are almost identical in structure with a verse treated in `098-beyyine.md`:**

> **al-Bayyina 98/2:** رَسُولٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ **يَتْلُوا۟** صُحُفًا مُّطَهَّرَةً
> **al-Ṭalāq 65/11:** رَّسُولًا **يَتْلُوا۟** عَلَيْكُمْ ءَايَٰتِ ٱللَّهِ مُبَيِّنَٰتٍ

The shared elements: **رسول** (a messenger), **يتلو** (recites, imperfect — continuity), and the qualification of what is recited.

And the work the two verses do in common is the same as well: **the explaining of an abstract noun by a person.**

The structure recorded in `098-beyyine.md` was this: verse 1 says *al-bayyina*, the reader asks "what is it?", verse 2 answers — a messenger. And the finding made there was: **the *bayyina* is not a text but a person.**

**In al-Ṭalāq the same structure is repeated:** verse 10 says *dhikran*, verse 11 opens it — *rasūlan.* That is, the "reminder" sent down is again described as a person.

And the grammatical problem in the two verses is the same as well: what is *rasūlan*? The classical analyses list possibilities like those in al-Bayyina — it may be an appositive substitute for *dhikran*, a substitute of inclusion for it, the object of an elided verb (*arsala rasūlan*), or *dhikran* may itself mean the messenger. Which of these analyses is preferred does not substantially change the meaning; in all of them the messenger is identified with the reminder sent down, or is named together with it.

**يَتْلُوا۟ — root ت ل و.** This root was treated in full in `098-beyyine.md`; I do not repeat it. A reminder: the root's original sense is not to read but **to come after, to follow**; reading is derived from the letters' following one another. And the finding recorded there holds here too: **the one who recites is the one who goes after the text** — he follows not his own order but the text's.

**مُبَيِّنَٰت — root ب ي ن.** Again a root analysed in full in `098-beyyine.md`; I do not repeat it. The word here is an adjective of the verses: **making clear, distinguishing.**

And a detail: the **مُبَيِّنَة** (*fāḥishatin mubayyina*) in the first verse of the sūra and the **مُبَيِّنَات** in this verse are the same word. The sūra uses the same adjective first for the plainness of an offence, then for the plainness of the verses.

This is an observation. The function of the word is the same in both places: **to leave no room for doubt.** The offence must be plain if the exception is to operate; the verse must be plain if no excuse is to remain.

### لِّيُخْرِجَ — the root's last turn

**And here the backbone of the sūra closes.**

I extend the table I set up at 65/2:

| # | Verse | Expression | Subject | Whom/what he brings out | From where | To where | Ruling |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 65/1 | *lā **tukhrijū**hunna* | The man | The woman | From her house | Outside | **Forbidden** |
| 2 | 65/1 | *wa lā **yakhrujna*** | The woman | Herself | From her house | Outside | **Forbidden** |
| 3 | 65/2 | *yajʿal lahu **makhrajā*** | God | A door for the man | From constriction | Unspecified | **Promised** |
| 4 | 65/11 | *li-**yukhrija**…* | God (through the messenger) | Those who believe | From the darknesses | **Into the light** | **Accomplished** |

The root occurs four times, and each time the subject, the direction and the ruling change.

**What the series says is this:** a human being has no power to put out (1), nor any power to go out on his own (2). A going out is promised (3) and comes about (4) — but in both cases the subject is God, and in the last case the place to be gone to is stated as well.

That is, the sūra closes one door in the first verse and opens another door in the last verses. And the difference between the two doors is not only who opens them; it is **where they open onto.** The door the man would have opened gave onto the street; the other door onto the light.

**I record this as the backbone of the sūra and I state that it is my own reading.** The four occurrences of the root and the distribution of the subjects are the text's data; the line of meaning drawn between them is mine.

**مِنَ ٱلظُّلُمَٰتِ إِلَى ٱلنُّورِ** — "from the darknesses into the light".

And the asymmetry of number here is consistent throughout the Qurʾān and is striking: **the darknesses are plural (*ẓulumāt*), the light singular (*nūr*).** The Qurʾān builds this construction in the same way in many places; it does not build the reverse (from lights into darkness).

In the classical commentaries the reason given for this is: the ways of straying are many, the right way is one. This explanation is reasonable and agrees also with the structure built in `001-fatiha.md` in connection with the singularity of the word *ṣirāṭ*.

### وَمَن يُؤْمِنۢ بِٱللَّهِ وَيَعْمَلْ صَٰلِحًا — and the closing ring of the sūra

The structure of the sentence is the same as that of the *taqwā* sentences in the first half of the sūra: *man* + condition + return. But the condition has changed: not *man yattaqi'llāh* but *man yuʾmin bi'llāhi wa yaʿmal ṣāliḥan.*

**قَدْ أَحْسَنَ ٱللَّهُ لَهُۥ رِزْقًا** — "God has indeed made his provision good."

And this is the last ruling-sentence of the sūra. The word **رِزْق** occurs in the sūra for the third time:

| Place | Expression | What |
|---|---|---|
| 65/3 | *wa **يَرْزُقْهُ** min ḥaythu lā yaḥtasib* | Provision coming from where he does not expect |
| 65/7 | *wa man qudira ʿalayhi **رِزْقُهُ*** | Straitened provision |
| 65/11 | *qad aḥsana'llāhu lahu **رِزْقًا*** | Provision made good |

**Three positions, three states:** unexpected, narrow, and good. And the arrangement follows a path — the sūra opens the question of provision with an indeterminacy (where it will come from is not known), tests it in the middle with a straitness, and closes it at the end with a sufficiency.

**And note: the provision in the last verse occurs in the context of the Garden.** That is, the sūra has opened the question of money as a worldly entry and closed it with its counterpart in the hereafter. The man paying maintenance is told where what he has paid will stand.

I record this as an observation.

## 65/12 — ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ سَبْعَ سَمَٰوَٰتٍ وَمِنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ مِثْلَهُنَّ يَتَنَزَّلُ ٱلْأَمْرُ بَيْنَهُنَّ لِتَعْلَمُوٓا۟ أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ وَأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَدْ أَحَاطَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عِلْمًا

*Allāhu'lladhī khalaqa sabʿa samāwātin wa mina'l-arḍi mithlahunn, yatanazzalu'l-amru baynahunna li-taʿlamū anna'llāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr, wa anna'llāha qad aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā*

"God is the one who created seven heavens, and of the earth their like. The command descends among them — so that you may know that God has power over everything, and that God has encompassed everything in knowledge."

### Why does a sūra of divorce end here?

The most unexpected move of the sūra. For eleven verses the waiting period, the dwelling, maintenance, the reckoning; and in the last verse, suddenly, seven heavens.

This transition can be explained, and the explanation rests on a single word: **يَتَنَزَّلُ ٱلْأَمْرُ.**

I gave the eight occurrences of the word *amr* in the sūra above (in the discussion of 65/8) in the form of a table. **The last of them is here.**

And what it does is this: the command set inside a household at the beginning of the sūra and the command descending among the layers of the cosmos are **named with the same word.**

> At what time a man is to divorce his wife, which period he is to count, how much he is to pay — these are *amru'llāh* (65/5).
> And *al-amr* descends among seven heavens (65/12).

**The two are the same word.** In English one is rendered "ruling", the other "command" or "affair", and the link breaks. In Arabic it does not break.

That is, the verse closes all the rulings of the sūra with a movement that changes the scale: **the order inside the house and the order of the cosmos are the two ends of the same command.**

I offer this as a reading. That the word is the same is the text's datum; binding the two ends together is my inference.

### لِتَعْلَمُوٓا۟ — the ground

And the verse states its own ground itself: *li-taʿlamū* — "so that you may know".

That is, no cosmology is being recounted. **A piece of knowledge is aimed at**, and what that knowledge is is given in two items:

1. **أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ** — He has power over everything.
2. **أَنَّ ٱللَّهَ قَدْ أَحَاطَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عِلْمًا** — He has encompassed everything in knowledge.

**And these two items turn back to the first verse of the sūra.**

65/1 had ended thus: **لَا تَدْرِى** — "**you do not know**."
65/12 ends thus: **قَدْ أَحَاطَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عِلْمًا** — "**He has encompassed everything in knowledge**."

The sūra opens with a human being's ignorance and closes with God's encompassing knowledge.

**And this explains the whole structure of the sūra.** In the first verse a period was set because the human being was told "you do not know". In the last verse it is said who does know. Every ruling between the two fills **the distance between the one who does not know and the one who knows.**

I record this as the soundest internal link of the sūra, and I state that it is my own reading. That the two expressions stand at the two ends of the sūra is the text's datum.

**قَدِير** — root **ق د ر**, in the sūra for the third time. At 65/3 *qadran* (measure), at 65/7 *qudira* (was straitened), here *qadīr* (having power). Two of the three branches of meaning recorded in `097-kadr.md`, and the sense of power, side by side in the same sūra.

**أَحَاطَ — root ح و ط (ḥ-w-ṭ):** to turn about, to encompass. *Ḥāʾiṭ* (a wall), *iḥāṭa*, *muḥīṭ* (encompassing; and the ocean). The concrete sense of the root is **to enclose about** — as a garden is enclosed by a wall.

**عِلْمًا** — a specifying accusative: "in respect of knowledge". It determines from which side the encompassing is. That is, the encompassing is not by power but **by knowledge.**

### وَمِنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ مِثْلَهُنَّ — a place to be silent about

I shall **not speak with certainty** about one expression in the verse, and I shall write the reason.

*"And of the earth their like"* — this expression does not say what is like what. Like in number? In structure? In manner of creation?

Different explanations have been transmitted in the classical commentaries on this, and no definite preference has been made among them. In some explanations likeness in number comes forward, in others likeness in creation.

**And I shall not insert a piece of modern cosmology here.** This is the plain rule of the method (`STYLE.md`): no hunting for scientific miracles, no forcing a piece of modern knowledge onto a verse. The verse gives no description of the structure of heaven and earth; it describes an **encompassing** and a **descent**. The verse's own sentence says this too: *li-taʿlamū* — what is to be known is not cosmography but power and knowledge.

Where the text is silent I am silent.

### يَتَنَزَّلُ — the pattern

**Root ن ز ل**, in the **تَفَعُّل (tafaʿʿul)** form. This form conveys here continuity and repetition: *yatanazzalu* — "it keeps descending", "it descends by degrees".

In `097-kadr.md` the form of the same root in the *tafʿīl* pattern (*nazzala*) was treated, and it was recorded there: **descending piece by piece, in repetition.** The *tafaʿʿul* form too carries a similar continuity, but in a reflexive tone.

And the verb's coming in the imperfect is meaningful: the descent is **not a finished event but a continuing state.** It is not said that the command came down once and was over; it is coming down.

This says something when one looks back at the rulings of the sūra: if the command is continually descending, obedience to it is a continual business too. It is not an obligation discharged once and closed.

### بَيْنَهُنَّ — "among them"

**Root ب ي ن.** Analysed in full in `098-beyyine.md`; I do not repeat it. A reminder: at the centre of the root there is **the space between two things**, and in Arabic *bayn* announces both the space between and the separation.

And the word's use here carries an echo within the sūra: at 65/6 **وَأْتَمِرُوا۟ بَيْنَكُم بِمَعْرُوفٍ** — "consult **between you** in the recognised good way."

Two *bayn*s: one the consultation between two people, the other the descent among seven layers. The same word, in the same sūra, on two scales.

I record this as an observation. *Bayn* is a very common word and I draw no structural claim from this proximity; but because it stands in the same direction as the change of scale the sūra sets up with the word *amr*, I found it worth recording.

## The weave of the sūra

Throughout the sūra several roots turn round in a regular way, and in turning they change meaning. Gathering these shows how the sūra is built.

### First weave: the root of GOING OUT (خ ر ج)

The backbone of the sūra. Four occurrences, given in the form of a table in the discussions of 65/2 and 65/11. In summary:

> **Forbidden putting out (from the house) → forbidden going out (from the house) → promised going out (from constriction) → accomplished bringing out (from darkness into light).**

The root occurs in the two sentences of which a human being is the subject as a **prohibition**, and in the two sentences of which God is the subject in the state of **promise and accomplishment**.

### Second weave: the root of RECKONING (ح س ب) and counting

| Word | Verse | Root | Who does it | What |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **أَحْصُوا۟** | 65/1 | ح ص ي | A human being | Count the period completely |
| **لَا يَحْتَسِبُ** | 65/3 | ح س ب | A human being | Cannot compute the source |
| **حَسْبُهُۥ** | 65/3 | ح س ب | God | Suffices him |
| **حَاسَبْنَٰهَا** | 65/8 | ح س ب | God | Settled the account |

The sūra sets up a language of reckoning and draws the limit of a human being's reckoning: **you can count the period; you cannot count the source; and in the end your account is settled.**

And this says something when compared with the *ʿaddada*–*yaḥsabu* pair in `104-humeze.md`. There counting and supposing came from the same root and the man who counted was making a false reckoning. Here counting is commanded, but what is to be counted is determined: **not your own property, but the right of the one before you.**

### Third weave: the root of COMMAND (أ م ر)

Eight occurrences, four scales. The table was given in the discussion of 65/8. The sūra names the order of the household, a person's affair, the fate of a town and the working of the cosmos with the same word; and it closes the sūra with the cosmic occurrence of that word.

### Fourth weave: NARROWNESS and WIDTH

The sūra thinks of money and means with words of **place**:

| Word | Root | Verse | What |
|---|---|---|---|
| **لِتُضَيِّقُوا۟** | ض ي ق | 65/6 | The straitening the man is **forbidden** to do to the woman |
| **سَعَةٍ / سَعَتِهِۦ** | و س ع | 65/7 | Width — means |
| **قُدِرَ عَلَيْهِ** | ق د ر | 65/7 | The straitening of provision |
| **يُسْرًا** | ي س ر | 65/4, 65/7 | Ease — unobstructedness |
| **عُسْرٍ** | ع س ر | 65/7 | Hardship |
| **تَعَاسَرْتُمْ** | ع س ر | 65/6 | To make things hard for one another |

**And within this table there is a symmetry:** the thing the man is forbidden to do to the woman (*to straiten*) occurs one verse later as a thing God may do to the man (*qudira ʿalayh*). The roots are different and I claim no relation of root from this; but the picture is the same, and the sūra uses both two verses apart.

### Fifth weave: the KNOWN and the UNKNOWN

| Word | Root | Verse | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **مَعْرُوف** | ع ر ف | 65/2 (×2), 65/6 | The **known** good — the measure of conduct |
| **مُبَيِّنَة / مُبَيِّنَٰت** | ب ي ن | 65/1, 65/11 | **Plain** — leaving no room for doubt |
| **لَا تَدْرِى** | د ر ي | 65/1 | You **do not know** |
| **لَا يَحْتَسِبُ** | ح س ب | 65/3 | The place he has **not put into his reckoning** |
| **نُّكْرًا** | ن ك ر | 65/8 | The **unrecognised**, unheard-of punishment |
| **لِتَعْلَمُوٓا۟** | ع ل م | 65/12 | So that you may **know** |
| **أَحَاطَ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عِلْمًا** | ع ل م | 65/12 | He encompassed everything in **knowledge** |

**This is the densest weave of the sūra.** And what it says is this:

> **What you must do is known (maʿrūf, mubayyināt). What will happen is not known (lā tadrī, lā yaḥtasib, nukr). And there is one who knows everything (aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā).**

Three layers: **the plainness of the duty, the closedness of the future, the encompassing of knowledge.**

And the whole law of the sūra is built between these three. A person knows what he is to do, does not know what will happen, and obeys the rule because he does not know.

I record this as the main structure of the sūra and I state that it is my own reading.

### Sixth weave: the sentences of TAQWĀ

The pattern *man yattaqi'llāh* three times, the imperative *ittaqu'llāh* twice. Five occurrences, spread over the first ten verses of the sūra:

| Place | Form | The ruling it is attached to | Return |
|---|---|---|---|
| 65/1 | *wa'ttaqu'llāha rabbakum* (imperative) | Time + counting | — |
| 65/2 | *man yattaqi'llāh* | Keeping/parting + witnessing | *makhrajan* |
| 65/4 | *man yattaqi'llāh* | The computations of the waiting period | *min amrihi yusrā* |
| 65/5 | *man yattaqi'llāh* | The whole of the ruling | *yukaffir* + *yuʿẓim ajrā* |
| 65/10 | *fa'ttaqu'llāh* (imperative) | After the warning of destruction | — |

**The structure is regular:** two imperatives at the two ends of the sūra; three conditional promises in the middle. And the three hardships the three promises answer are distinct: **absence of a way out, the difficulty of the affair, past faults.**

## What this says to the present

**One. The process itself is a protection.**

This is the most transferable finding of the sūra, and it is not confined to divorce.

**Binding a decision to a period** is done not in order to delay the decision but to raise the quality of the decision. And the benefit of the delay does not consist only in the possibility of the parties "making peace": through the period the emotional intensity falls, information increases, and the decision is reconsidered from a different state of mind.

That the verse has set this not as a recommendation but as a **rule** is important. A recommendation is not listened to at the moment it is most needed — that is, when the person is angry. A rule is listened to, because it does not need to be listened to; it is applied.

**Two. The measure coming from outside.**

The qualification *bi-maʿrūf* does not leave the measure of goodness to a person's own judgement. The side of this that looks to the present is plain.

When a relationship breaks down, each of the parties finds his own conduct reasonable and the other's excessive. This arises not from bad faith but from position: everyone knows his own reasons, and does not know the other's.

The measure's coming from outside opens this dead end. That the sentence "it was enough as far as I was concerned" is counted invalid is what ends the argument.

**Three. A prohibition placed upon purpose.**

*Li-tuḍayyiqū ʿalayhinna* — the qualification "in order to press them" forbids going around the rule.

This is a problem that holds today at every level. It is possible to obey a rule to the letter and destroy the thing the rule protects, and most of the time this is the commonest form of violation. That the verse has forbidden this separately shows that the regulation knows the human being realistically: the text takes into account not the man who will break the rule but **the man who will use the rule.**

**Four. The paying of care.**

*Fa-ātūhunna ujūrahunna* — the definition of suckling as work done for a wage says that care continuing after the relationship has ended cannot be expected without return.

The present-day counterpart of this narrow finding is direct: after a separation the care of the child falls on one of the parties and is generally carried as an invisible cost. The verse makes that cost visible and gives it a name: *ajr* — a wage.

**Five. Verses torn from their contexts.**

This sūra carries the sentences of the Qurʾān that are most torn from their contexts. *"He opens a way out for him"*, *"He provides for him from where he does not expect"*, *"an ease after a hardship"* — all three circulate as words of consolation.

Put back into their contexts, the work all three do is the same: **to make an obligation bearable.**

And the note about method that comes out of this holds generally: the wording of a sentence is general and its general use is legitimate (*al-ʿibratu bi-ʿumūmi'l-lafẓ*); but **where the sentence comes** shows how much weight is to be given to it. That a sentence of consolation was said in the middle of a table of accounts, where there is least room for consolation, shows that the sentence is not cheap.

**Six. The legal consequence of not knowing.**

And finally the most original side of the sūra.

*Lā tadrī* — "you do not know". This sentence is not a counsel of humility; it is **the ground of a rule of law.** From a person's not knowing the future the conclusion is drawn that that person must wait a determined period.

The side of this that looks to the present has to do with how irreversible decisions are made. To make an irreversible decision at the moment when our knowledge is most incomplete — that is, immediately after the event — is the commonest mistake. And the mistake arises not from the wrongness of the decision but from **the wrongness of the knowledge at the moment the decision was made.**

The sūra's solution is not an exhortation but a mechanism: count the period, stay in place, call a witness. All three do the same work — they carry the decision outside the moment in which it was made.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

Per the method (`STYLE.md`), I list plainly the places where certain language has deliberately not been used throughout the text:

- **None of the legal rulings has been settled.** The distinction between *ṭalāq sunnī* and *bidʿī* and the consequences of a *bidʿī* divorce, whether *lā tukhrijūhunna* covers the irrevocable divorce, whether lodging and maintenance are owed in an irrevocable divorce, whether the witnessing is a condition or a recommendation and to which moment it belongs, the obligatoriness of suckling and the fixing of the wage — in all of these the existence of the disagreement was recorded and no preference was adopted. **This commentary does not issue legal rulings; for a ruling to be acted upon, the works of jurisprudence must be consulted.**
- **No name of a school and no name of an imām was given.** The views were transmitted in the form "according to one view / according to another view". The reason: not to ascribe a view to the wrong school. I have written no ascription of which I was not sure.
- **No occasion of revelation was written.** Various events are transmitted in the sources for the sūra as a whole or for particular verses; since I am not sure of the soundness of these reports or of their agreement with one another, I have transmitted none of them.
- **The lām in لِعِدَّتِهِنَّ.** Three readings were given in the form of a table (time, purpose, direction); no preference was adopted. All three come out at the same practical result.
- **The scope of فَاحِشَة مُبَيِّنَة.** Three explanations were given; no preference was adopted. The verse does not specify. It was also recorded that the procedure for establishing the exception is not in the verse and that this is a place open to abuse — this is an observation, not a ruling.
- **The doubt in the expression إِنِ ٱرْتَبْتُمْ.** Three readings were given; no preference was adopted.
- **The scope of وَٱلَّٰٓـِٔى لَمْ يَحِضْنَ.** Two views were given in the form of a table; it was not settled. It was also recorded plainly that the wording of the verse specifies no age, commands no practice and contains no encouragement; where the text is silent, silence was kept.
- **The pattern of مَخْرَجًا.** As to whether it is a noun of place or a mīmic verbal noun, the first reading was preferred, but it was stated that this is a preference and that it is not binding.
- **The pronoun in مِنْ أَمْرِهِۦ** (65/4). Two readings were given; the first was found better suited to the context but was offered as a preference.
- **The syntax of رَّسُولًا** (65/11). The possibilities of the classical analyses were listed; none was preferred, because they do not substantially change the meaning.
- **وَمِنَ ٱلْأَرْضِ مِثْلَهُنَّ.** No explanation was preferred as to what is like what, and no piece of modern cosmology was inserted. Per the method's (`STYLE.md`) prohibition of scientific-miracle hunting, silence was kept where the text is silent.
- **No imām's name was given for the variant readings.** The existence of the singular reading at 65/1 and of the forms *allāʾī* at 65/4 is on record in the sources; since I am not sure to which imāms they belong, no name was written, and since they do not change the meaning they were not dwelt on.
- **The report concerning *tawakkul*, "tie it first, then rely",** was not transmitted as a ḥadīth; it was stated that there is dispute about its soundness, and it was recorded that the structure of the word itself yields the same result.
- **The difference of wording between al-Baqara 2/286 and al-Ṭalāq 65/7** was recorded as an observation. The conclusion that the two principles conflict or that one restricts the other was **not drawn**; I have no certain knowledge of how this difference has been handled in the classical sources.
- **No theological debate was entered into by way of قَدْرًا (65/3).** The verse does not construct a thesis about destiny; tearing it from its context and making it the proof of a school's position was avoided.
- **My own inferences.** The following are my reading, are not transmitted reports, and are not binding: the finding of "the backbone of the sūra" drawn from the four occurrences of the root خ ر ج; the table drawn from the four positions of the root of reckoning; the reading "parts of the same order" drawn from the eight occurrences of the word *amr*; the meaning drawn from the *maʿrūf*–*nukr* opposition; the frame set up between *lā tadrī* at 65/1 and *aḥāṭa bi-kulli shayʾin ʿilmā* at 65/12; the connection made with al-Rūm 30/21 by way of the root *sakana*; the two layers of the word *ajr* at 65/5 and 65/6; the reading of 65/4 as "completing the coverage"; and the whole of the headings "What this says to the present". These distinctions were stated one by one within the text.
