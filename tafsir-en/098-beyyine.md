# 98. Sūrat al-Bayyina

Eight verses. It stands in the belt of short sūras, but in length and in sentence structure it separates itself from the sūras around it: the verses are longer, the sentences more complex, the weave of rhyme looser. Its first verse is one of the most disputed sentences in the Qurʾān.

## What the sūra is doing

The sūra advances in three steps, and at its middle stands a paradox.

**The first step:** humanity was in a locked condition; what was to open this lock was the **bayyina** — the clear proof that distinguishes — (verses 1-3). And this bayyina is not a book, not a text, not a miracle; it is **a human being who reads.**

**The second step — the paradox:** the bayyina came. And people divided precisely *after* the bayyina came (verse 4). The division, that is, is not the result of ignorance; it is something that follows upon knowledge.

**The third step:** and over what did they divide? What was commanded was exceedingly little: service rendered sincerely, turning in a single direction, the prayer, the alms (verse 5). The subject of the division was something too plain to divide over.

Then the sūra sets two tableaux one upon the other: the worst and the best of created beings (verses 6-7). And at the very end, above the description of the Garden, it places something higher than it: **mutual contentment** (verse 8).

## The names of the sūra

It is transmitted that in the sources it is called by several names: **al-Bayyina** (the key word of the first verse), **Lam yakun** (its opening words), **al-Qayyima** (from the expression *dīnu'l-qayyima* at the end of the fifth verse), and **Ahl al-kitāb**. In the early period it was widespread for sūras to be called by some striking word inside them; this multiplicity is the trace of that.

It is striking that two of the names answer to the sūra's two main concepts: *Bayyina* (the proof that comes) and *Qayyima* (the upright stance that proof establishes). These two words carry the sūra.

## Meccan or Medinan?

I do not speak with certainty here, because the sources differ.

| View | Ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **Medinan** | The People of the Book being mentioned within a polemical frame and side by side with the mushrikūn; the alms being mentioned explicitly; the theme of "the division of those given the Book" overlapping with the disputes at Medina. In most lists of the order of revelation it is counted Medinan. | There was contact with the People of the Book at Mecca too; moreover the word "alms" occurs in Meccan sūras as well, and there it has less the sense of an institutional rate than of purifying and giving in general. |
| **Meccan** | The sūra's main subject is belief and the ground of prophethood; there is not a single sentence conveying a legal ruling. It is transmitted that in some lists of revelation it is counted Meccan. | The sūra's cast of addressees (People of the Book and mushrikūn together) and the length of its verses stand nearer to the Medinan manner. |

It can be said that the majority call it Medinan; but this is not an undisputed consensus. In my judgement the distinction does not change how the sūra is understood: what the sūra describes is not a period but a mechanism — the proof comes, people divide. This preference is not binding.

**The occasion of revelation.** I know of no sound and widely accepted report of an occasion of revelation, either for the whole sūra or for any particular verse of it. For that reason I write no occasion here. The sūra's own content already shows its context: a setting in which communities that had been given a Book and mushrikūn without a Book were present together, and a new messenger had appeared.

## A report: the sūra being recited to Ubayy b. Kaʿb

According to a report found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the Prophet said to Ubayy b. Kaʿb, "God has commanded me to recite *Lam yakuni'lladhīna kafarū* to you." Ubayy asked, "Did He mention me to you by name?"; when the answer was yes, he wept.

I transmit the report not as material for exegesis but to show what weight the sūra carried in the early period. I do not quote its wording verbatim; I give its sense — there are small differences among its variants.

## 98/1 — لَمْ يَكُنِ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُواْ مِنْ أَهْلِ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ وَٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ مُنفَكِّينَ حَتَّىٰ تَأْتِيَهُمُ ٱلْبَيِّنَةُ

*Lam yakuni'lladhīna kafarū min ahli'l-kitābi wa'l-mushrikīna munfakkīna ḥattā taʾtiyahumu'l-bayyina*

"Those who deny, from the People of the Book and from the mushrikūn, were not going to be parted (unloosed) until the bayyina (the clear proof) came to them."

This rendering is provisional. How the sentence is to be rendered is itself the dispute we shall take up below.

### First, let us see the difficulty

There is no point in concealing that this verse is difficult. In the exegetical tradition the hardness of this sentence is pointed out plainly, and the exegetes have proposed markedly different solutions. The difficulty comes from three places:

**1. "Munfakkīn" — parted from what?** The verb has no object. In Arabic *infikāk* is always *from* something: a thing is unloosed from a thing, parted from it. The verse does not say what that thing is.

**2. Is "ḥattā" really a limit?** The sentence "they were not going to be parted until the bayyina came" implies that they were parted when the bayyina did come. But verses 4 and 6 of the sūra say that even after the bayyina came a part of them remained in denial. So the limit does not work as cleanly as it looks.

**3. The tense of the sentence.** *Lam yakun* negates the past; *ḥattā taʾtiya* is a construction that looks to the future. The sentence carries two tenses at once.

These three difficulties are the reason for the multiplicity of views about the verse. Below I shall give first the words, then the proposed solutions in tabular form.

### مُنفَكِّين — munfakkīn

The most loaded word in the sūra. Root **ف ك ك (f-k-k)**.

**The concrete sense of the root: the loosing of a bond.** *Fakk* is the parting of two things held tight from one another, the slackening of a bond, the opening of a lock. The root's concrete uses in Arabic show this picture clearly:

- **فكّ العقدة** — to untie a knot.
- **فكّ الخاتم** — to break open a seal.
- **فكّ الرهن** — to redeem a pledge; that is, to pay the debt and free the pledged property from its bond.
- **فكّ الأسير** — to release a captive, to loose his bonds.
- **الفَكّ** — the jaw. It takes this name because the two jaws part from one another and open.

The same root occurs in the Qurʾān in this famous place: *"And what will make you know what the steep pass is? The freeing of a neck (fakku raqaba)"* (al-Balad 90/12-13). There *fakk* is the freeing of a slave from his bond. At the centre of the root stand **the lock and the release.**

**The pattern: Form VII.** *Munfakk* is the active participle of the verb *infakka* and belongs to Form VII. In Arabic the basic function of this form is **muṭāwaʿa**: it conveys the *result* of the Form I verb. *Kasara* (he broke) → *inkasara* (it broke). *Fakka* (he unloosed) → *infakka* (it came unloosed).

This is a point easily passed over, and it carries the whole sense of the verse: **infikāk is not a work a person does; it is a result that happens to him.** The verse does not say "they were not going to loose themselves"; it says "they were not going to be loosed". To be loosed, a looser from outside is needed. And the rest of the sentence names that looser: *ḥattā taʾtiyahumu'l-bayyina.*

The lock, that is, does not open from the inside. This is the sūra's thesis about prophethood: a human being cannot bring himself out of that condition; something that comes from outside is needed.

**A grammatical subtlety: the second use of munfakk.** In Arabic the pattern *mā infakka* / *lā yanfakku* is placed among the **defective verbs of continuance** (*afʿālu'l-istimrār*), with the sense of "he kept on, he did not leave off, he stayed just so": *mā infakka yafʿalu* — "he did not stop doing it, he kept on doing it." In this use the verb requires a predicate: what he kept on doing must be stated.

In the verse there is no such predicate. The grammarians therefore divide in two:

- The word is used here as **complete** (*tāmm*): it means "parted / unloosed", it requires no predicate, and what they are parted from is understood from the context.
- The word is used as **defective** (*nāqiṣ*) and its predicate has been elided: "they had not left off being…" — the elided part is filled in by the context.

Both analyses have been defended in the classical sources, and in practice they come out at the same gap: something has not been said, and the reader is asked to complete it.

### The proposed solutions

I have tried to collect the explanations proposed for "munfakkīn" in the classical commentaries in a way that keeps them distinguishable from one another. I present none of them as certainly correct.

| # | Reading | The sense of the sentence | Its strong side | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **They were not going to be parted from their denial** | They were fixed in denial; the one thing that would tear them from it was the coming of the bayyina. | The commonest explanation. It reads *ḥattā* as a natural limit. | There were those who did not part from it although the bayyina came (verses 4 and 6). The limit looks not to have been reached in fact. |
| 2 | **They were not going to be parted from one another** | The People of the Book and the mushrikūn stood as a single bloc in denial; what would part them from one another is the bayyina. | It coincides exactly with the sense of "distinguishing" in the root of the word bayyina: what separates is the proof. | There is no expression of reciprocity in the verse, such as *baʿḍuhum ʿan baʿḍ*. |
| 3 | **They were not going to be parted from their own state, from the condition they were in** | A general description of a situation: the order they were in would not have come apart of itself. | The reading that requires the fewest assumptions; it leaves what is elided indeterminate. | Being indeterminate, it says little. |
| 4 | **They were not going to be abandoned, left to themselves** | God does not leave them without a proof; they were not to be called to account before the argument reached them. | An exact fit with al-Isrāʾ 17/15: *"We do not punish until We have sent a messenger."* It is tied to the principle of justice. | It moves a little away from the root's sense of "coming unloosed"; the sense "to be left" may be found forced. |
| 5 | **They were not going to go back on their word** | They were saying that they awaited a messenger; they said "if he comes we shall follow him", and they were insistent on this promise. When the bayyina came they broke their word. | It connects perfectly with verse 4: the division comes after the proof. It loads the sentence with an irony. | The textual ground for this claim is not in this verse but in others (e.g. Fāṭir 35/42, al-Anʿām 6/157). It is an indirect reading. |
| 6 | ***Ḥattā* is not a real limit; it conveys impossibility** | As one says "until the raven turns white": they were never going to be parted. | It removes the problem of the limit's not having been reached. | This usage exists in Arabic but it is rare; and the rest of the sūra says the bayyina *did in fact come*. The weakest possibility. |

**My judgement and its ground.** Thinking the second and the fourth readings together seems to me the most coherent, and my ground is in the words themselves:

*Infikāk* is a *muṭāwaʿa* — it requires a looser from outside. And *bayyina*, by its root, is that which **distinguishes** (we shall see this under the next heading). The two ends of the sentence, then, describe the same operation: on one side an undissolved mass, on the other a proof that distinguishes. When the proof comes the mass comes apart — but "coming apart" here does not mean "being saved"; it means **separating out.** It becomes clear who stands where. And indeed the rest of the sūra does exactly this: in verses 6 and 7 there are two separate masses.

On this reading the paradox of verse 4 is resolved as well. Before the bayyina came there was no division, because there was nothing to divide — everyone was inside the same indeterminacy. When the bayyina came, division *appeared*. The "they were not going to be parted" of verse 1 and the "they divided" of verse 4, that is, do not contradict one another; the second is the realisation of the first.

I offer this as a preference, not as a transmitted report. It is not binding, and the other readings above are defensible too. The verse's difficulty is not removed by this preference.

### أَهْلِ ٱلْكِتَٰب — the People of the Book

**Root أ ه ل (a-h-l):** to belong to a place or to a thing, to form a closeness and a familiarity with it. *Ahl* is a person's household, his family. An *ahlī* animal is one accustomed to living alongside human beings (the opposite of the wild). *Taʾahhul* is to marry. *Ahliyya* is aptitude for a task, being the man for it.

At the centre of the word stand **belonging and familiarity.** This is why the expression "the People of the Book" says more than "those who read the Book" or "those who have a Book": **the household of the Book.** Those who live in the same house as the Book, who are no strangers to it.

This is a standing granted in the expression itself. In naming this community the Qurʾān does not choose a belittling word; on the contrary, it chooses a word of closeness. And the sūra's later accusation will grow heavier exactly from here: it is not the stranger who divides, but the one inside the house.

**Whom does it cover in the Qurʾān?** Predominantly Jews and Christians. The Qurʾān also names these two communities as *alladhīna hādū* and *al-naṣārā*; the Sabians are mentioned as a separate name in some lists (al-Baqara 2/62, al-Māʾida 5/69, al-Ḥajj 22/17). The bounds of the coverage of the expression "the People of the Book" — particularly as regards the Magians — have been disputed in the legal literature; I do not enter that dispute here, because the sūra's subject is not legal coverage.

### ٱلْمُشْرِكِين — the mushrikūn

**Root ش ر ك (sh-r-k):** to be a partner, to share. From the same root come *sharīk* (a partner), *sharika* (a company), *mushtarak* (held in common), *ishtirāk* (participation), *tashrīk* (association). There is no bad sense in the root — partnership is an ordinary relation in trade and in ownership. The word grows heavy only when it is said *to whom the partnership is ascribed*.

A *mushrik* is one who associates a partner. The Qurʾān generally uses this without naming its object, because the object is understood: one who associates a partner with God.

The point to note: the mushrikūn were not people who denied God. The Qurʾān records in several places that they accepted God as creator (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/61-63, al-Zumar 39/3). *Shirk* is not the erasing of God; it is the setting of another beside Him. *Partnership* is a different operation from denial — and the language itself says so.

### The two groups counted apart — and named together

The sūra's first verse on the one hand **separates** the two groups, and on the other **joins** them. Both must be seen at once.

**It separates:** the sentence could have said "the deniers" and passed on. It does not. It gives two separate names and puts a *wāw* between them. The Qurʾān maintains this distinction systematically:

- The food of the People of the Book is lawful, and that of the mushrikūn is not mentioned within this frame; marriage with chaste women of the People of the Book is permitted (al-Māʾida 5/5). There is no such ruling for the mushrikūn (al-Baqara 2/221).
- The People of the Book come from within a tradition of prophethood and a revelation. Of the mushrikūn the Qurʾān uses the expression "a people to whom no warner came before" (e.g. Yāsīn 36/6, and in the context of al-Sajda 32/3).
- In addressing the People of the Book the Qurʾān calls out *"yā ahla'l-kitāb"* — that is, it takes them as interlocutors, speaks with them, looks for common ground: *"O People of the Book! Come to a word held in common between us"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/64).

The distinction, then, is real, and the Qurʾān does not erase it.

**It joins:** but this verse gathers the two into a single sentence, under a single attribute. What is the common point? Not the having or not having of a Book; **their position over against the bayyina.** Both are inside the same lock, and the same thing will loose them both.

This is a silent but important claim of the sūra: possessing a Book does not automatically keep a person in the right place. The Book is not a guarantee but a trust. Having the text in hand and doing what the text requires are not the same thing — and verse 4 will say exactly this.

### The particle "min" — the most critical word in this verse

The verse does not say *"the People of the Book and the mushrikūn denied"*. It says this:

> **الذين كفروا مِن أهل الكتاب والمشركين**
> "**those who deny** from the People of the Book **and** from the mushrikūn"

The particle **مِن (min)** here conveys the partitive in Arabic: **a part of a whole.** In "I ate of the bread", *min* indicates not the whole of the bread but a portion of it.

The subject of the sentence, then, is not "the People of the Book" but "*the denying portion* of the People of the Book". The attribute comes before the identity: *alladhīna kafarū* is said first, and *min ahli'l-kitāb* bounds it. Even the grammatical order shows where the ruling is attached.

This is the place where the methodological rule to which this text is bound is applied directly: **the verse issues no wholesale ruling about a community; it describes an attribute.** Whoever carries that attribute is the one the verse is speaking of.

And the textual evidence for this is not one particle only. The Qurʾān sets out the balancing statement about the same community plainly:

> **لَيْسُوا۟ سَوَآءً ۗ مِّنْ أَهْلِ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ أُمَّةٌ قَآئِمَةٌ يَتْلُونَ ءَايَٰتِ ٱللَّهِ ءَانَآءَ ٱلَّيْلِ وَهُمْ يَسْجُدُونَ**
> *"They are not all one. Among the People of the Book there is an upright (kāʾima) community that recites the signs of God in the watches of the night, prostrating themselves."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/113)

The first two words of this verse — *laysū sawāʾan*, "they are not all one" — directly forbid generalising. What follows (3/114-115) says that this community believes in God and in the Last Day, enjoins good and forbids wrong, and vies in good works, and it declares that *"no good they do will be left without its reward"*.

Two verbal details tie this verse tightly to Sūrat al-Bayyina:

- **أُمَّةٌ قَآئِمَةٌ** — *ummatun qāʾima*: "a community that stands upright, that is straight". This is from the same root (*q-w-m*) as the **qayyima** of al-Bayyina 98/3 and 98/5. In describing the good part within the People of the Book, that is, the Qurʾān uses this sūra's key root.
- **يَتْلُونَ** — *yatlūna*: "they recite". This is the same verb as the **yatlū** of al-Bayyina 98/2. The work the messenger does is done by the *qāʾim* part of the People of the Book as well.

Moreover Āl ʿImrān 3/199 says that among the People of the Book there are those who believe in God, in what was sent down to you and in what was sent down to them, and that their reward is with their Lord.

Conclusion: verses 1 and 6 of Sūrat al-Bayyina are not about "the People of the Book" but about "those who deny from among the People of the Book". If we do not enter this qualification, what we have read is not the verse but a corrupted copy of it.

### ٱلْبَيِّنَة — bayyina

The word that carries the sūra's name. The root is **ب ي ن (b-y-n)**, and it is one of the most interesting roots in Arabic.

**The centre of the root: the space between two things.** The root's most concrete product is the word **بَيْن (bayn)**: "between". But in Arabic *bayn* does two contrary works at once — it conveys both **the space between** and **the parting**. *Dhāta baynikum* (al-Anfāl 8/1) means "the state that is between you"; *yawmu'l-bayn* is "the day of parting". The lexicographers count words of this kind under the heading *aḍdād* (words that carry a sense and its opposite together).

The reason for this is simple: what lies *between* two things is at once what binds them to one another and what separates them from one another. Without the interval, two things are not two.

**How does one pass from this to the sense of "clarity"?** Directly. A thing becomes visible only when it is **separated** from what surrounds it. The mountain within the mist is not seen; when the mist disperses the mountain *is separated out* and *appears*. In Arabic the same root covers both separating and appearing:

- **بَانَ / يَبِينُ** — it separated; it appeared, it came into view.
- **أَبَانَ** — he separated; he made clear.
- **بَيَّنَ** — he explained, he distinguished. *Tabyīn*.
- **بَيَان** — clear expression, well-ordered speech. *"He taught him clear expression"* (al-Raḥmān 55/4).
- **تِبْيَان** — full exposition. *"We have sent down the Book to you as a tibyān for everything"* (al-Naḥl 16/89).
- **مُبِين** — perfectly clear. *Kitābun mubīn.*

**Its traces in Turkish.** *Beyan etmek* ("to declare"), *beyanname* ("a declaration"), *tebyin*, *beynelmilel* ("international" — literally, *between* nations) — all from this root. English has no word taken from it. And there is no etymological connection between the Turkish *belli olmak* ("to become evident") and the Arabic *bayyin*, though in sense they fall in the same place.

**The form of the word بَيِّنَة.** *Bayyina* is the feminine of the adjective *bayyin*. The *fayʿil* pattern (as in شديد، قوي، بيّن) conveys intensification: "perfectly clear". In time the word ceased to be an adjective and became a noun — the thing it qualified was dropped and the adjective took its place (*al-ḥujjatu'l-bayyina* → *al-bayyina*).

**Its sense:** *bayyina* is **the proof that distinguishes, and that is clear because it distinguishes.** It is rendered into English as "clear proof"; but that rendering misses the word's function. A bayyina is not merely *clear*; it is what **opens.** It opens the space between two things and makes visible which is which.

### The legal sense of bayyina

The word's technical sense in Islamic law lights this up further.

In jurisprudence **bayyina** means the **proof** brought forward in a suit — and especially a **witness**. The famous maxim runs: *al-bayyinatu ʿala'l-muddaʿī wa'l-yamīnu ʿalā man ankara* — "the proof (bayyina) falls to the claimant, the oath to the one who denies." This maxim is very widely used in the legal literature and is also transmitted as a hadith; its chain and its wording are disputed, and for that reason I transmit it here not as a hadith but as a settled legal maxim.

The logic of the maxim is this: a claim is what seeks to break the standing indeterminacy in its own favour; and what is to break an indeterminacy must be something that **distinguishes.** A witness does exactly this — he stands as a third element between the words of the two parties and opens the space between them.

Now go back to the verse. The verse recounts prophethood with a legal word. This is no accident; the sūra's whole frame is the frame of a **lawsuit**:

- There is a claim (the oneness of God and the sending of a messenger).
- There is a denial (the two groups of verse 1).
- And a **bayyina** is produced by the claimant.

This frame also explains the Qurʾān's understanding of prophethood: a prophet is not a force that compels people; he is **a proof submitted to a court.** The proof is submitted, and the outcome is left to the judge. And indeed the Qurʾān sets this principle out plainly: *"There is no compulsion in religion; the right way has become distinct from error"* (al-Baqara 2/256) — and the verb used there is *tabayyana*, that is, the same root.

**And one verse gathers the whole matter into a single sentence:**

> **لِّيَهْلِكَ مَنْ هَلَكَ عَنۢ بَيِّنَةٍ وَيَحْيَىٰ مَنْ حَىَّ عَنۢ بَيِّنَةٍ**
> *"So that whoever perishes may perish upon a clear proof, and whoever lives may live upon a clear proof."* (al-Anfāl 8/42)

The bayyina makes both outcomes possible. It is what saves, and what condemns. The proof's work is not to determine the sides; it is **to make the decision one's own.** After the proof has come, no one can say "I did not know".

### Word order

A few points:

**1. The sentence begins with a negative.** The sūra — and so a text of this length — opens with *lam yakun*. It is not common for a sūra in the Qurʾān to open with a negation. The effect is this: the text begins by saying that something *is not*, and at the very first word the reader is looking at an incomplete picture. The bayyina comes as what fills that lack.

**2. The bayyina is at the end of the sentence.** The verse begins with a long subject phrase (*alladhīna kafarū min ahli'l-kitābi wa'l-mushrikīn*), then comes the predicate (*munfakkīn*), and at the very end comes the solution (*al-bayyina*). The verse sets up the problem with its whole weight and puts the solution last. The rhythm of the sentence is a scaled-down model of the sūra's architecture.

**3. The definite article.** Not *bayyinatun* but *al-bayyina*. Definite. That is: not "some proof" but **the known, the expected, the determinate proof.** The article itself implies that this proof was a familiar notion to those addressed. The next verse will define it.

## 98/2 — رَسُولٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ يَتْلُواْ صُحُفًا مُّطَهَّرَةً

*Rasūlun mina'llāhi yatlū ṣuḥufan muṭahhara*

"A messenger from God; reciting purified pages."

### The bayyina is not a text but a person

This is the sūra's most important move, and it needs to be dwelt on.

The preceding verse named what was to open humanity's lock: *al-bayyina*. The reader now waits to learn what it is. The expected answers might have been these:

- **A book.** "The bayyina is the Book that is sent down."
- **A miracle.** This is generally what peoples ask of their prophets.
- **A proof, an argument.** A rational demonstration.
- **A sign, a token.** Some indication from the sky.

The verse says none of these. It says: **رَسُولٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ** — a messenger from God. That is, **a human being.**

A human being is sent as the proof. The demonstration of the largest claim in history is a person who walks, speaks and reads.

The consequences of this are heavy:

**First: the proof and its carrier cannot be separated.** A book is independent of its carrier — whoever brings it, it is the same book. But if the bayyina is a person, then how that person lives becomes a part of the proof. The Prophet's character is not an addition but the body of the proof. The Qurʾān says this plainly elsewhere: *"Assuredly, in the Messenger of God there is a fine example for you"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/21).

**Second: the proof unfolds in time.** A book is there all at once. A person is known only with time. The bayyina's being a person means that the proof is submitted *not in a moment but across a lifetime.* Twenty-three years is the period over which the proof is produced.

**Third: the proof answers back.** A book does not answer a question; a person does. That the greater part of the Qurʾān came down inside a conversation — the frequency of the pattern "they ask you about such and such; say…" — has to do with this.

**Fourth, and the finest: the proof is fragile.** A human being tires, falls ill, dies. To choose a human being as the bayyina is to place the proof inside fragility. The Qurʾān records this objection too: the mushrikūn say "what kind of messenger is this, who eats food and walks about in the markets" (al-Furqān 25/7). The objection is reasonable — and the Qurʾān does not refuse it; it turns the proposition round: the messenger's being human is not the proof's defect but its condition. *"Say: I am only a human being like you; it is revealed to me"* (al-Kahf 18/110).

### Word order: what is رَسُولٌ doing?

There is a point that needs explaining on grammatical grounds: *al-bayyina* is feminine, *rasūl* masculine. How are the two joined to one another?

The classical analyses set out these possibilities:

| Analysis | Its sense |
|---|---|
| *Rasūl* is the **appositive substitute** for *al-bayyina* (an explanatory term standing in its place) | "Until the bayyina — that is, a messenger from God — came." The substitute follows the sense; since bayyina carries the sense of "proof, argument", it can be treated as masculine. |
| *Rasūl* is an **explicative apposition** | The same result; only the technical name differs. |
| It is the predicate of an elided subject: *huwa rasūlun* | "He is a messenger from God." A new sentence is framed, and the verse is read as the answer to a question: *What is the bayyina? — A messenger from God.* |

The third analysis is especially fine, because it leaves a silent question between the verses. Verse 1 gives a name, the reader asks "what is it?", verse 2 answers. This is the same technique we saw in the first two verses of Sūrat al-Māʿūn: the question is answered without being asked.

### رَسُول — rasūl

**Root ر س ل (r-s-l).** The concrete sense of the root: **to let a thing go, to leave it to its own flow, to send it off.** *Irsāl* is sending; *irsālu'sh-shaʿr* is letting the hair down, undoing its braid and leaving it loose. *Rasl* is the slow, even walk of a camel. The words *rasl* and *risl* carry the sense of "without haste, at its own pace" — close to the English "deliberateness".

*Rasūl* is on the *faʿūl* pattern, and this pattern can be read both actively and passively. The word covers both **the person sent** and **the message sent**; in Arabic *rasūl* has sometimes been used directly in the sense of "message". This doubleness is meaningful: the messenger is not something separate from the message he carries.

**مِّنَ ٱللَّه** — "from God". This qualification is necessary, because *rasūl* is a neutral word; anyone at all can have a messenger. The verse states the source. And note: not "God's messenger" (*rasūlu'llāh*, a construct) but "a messenger **from** God" (*min*, a preposition). The difference is fine but real: the construct conveys belonging, *min* conveys **the point of departure**. The stress falls not on whom the messenger belongs to but on **where he comes from**. The most important property of a proof is its source.

### يَتْلُواْ — reciting

**Root ت ل و (t-l-w).** The proper sense of the root is **not to read, but to come after, to follow, to go behind.** *Talāhu* — he followed him. *Al-tālī* — the one that comes next. *Tilw* — what stands immediately behind a thing.

This concrete sense of the root is used plainly in the Qurʾān: *"By the moon when it follows it"* (al-Shams 91/2) — *wa'l-qamari idhā talāhā*. The moon comes after the sun.

**Where, then, does the sense of "reading" come from?** Directly from the idea of following. Reading is the following of letters and words one upon another. The reader goes after the text; he follows not his own order but the order of the text.

This must be the reason the word was chosen. Arabic also has *qirāʾa* for "reading" (*q-r-ʾ*: to gather, to bring together — to gather letters into a word). There is a difference between the two: *qirāʾa* stresses the gathering, *tilāwa* **the following**.

In reciting, then, the messenger is himself a follower. He does not govern the text; he goes after it. This is the necessary complement of the observation that "the bayyina is a person" — the person is the proof, but he is not the proof's source. The person is following something larger than himself.

**The mood of the verb.** *Yatlū* is imperfect: continuance and renewal. Not "he read once" but "one who keeps on reading". The messenger is defined not by an event but by **a continuous act.** His identity is the work he does.

And this verb was used in Āl ʿImrān 3/113 of the *qāʾim* part of the People of the Book as well: *yatlūna āyāti'llāhi ānāʾa'l-layl*. "Reciting", that is, is present on both sides. The sūra will criticise the People of the Book not for failing to read but for dividing after they had read.

### صُحُفًا — pages

**Root ص ح ف (ṣ-ḥ-f).** A *ṣaḥīfa* is a broad flat surface written upon; it may be leather, papyrus or parchment. Its plural is *ṣuḥuf*.

From the same root comes **مُصْحَف (*muṣḥaf*)**: that whose pages have been gathered together. The word's present-day use — the copy of the Qurʾān between two covers — comes directly from this root. In modern Arabic *ṣaḥāfa* (the press, journalism) and *ṣaḥafī* (a journalist) are derived from the same root as well; in all of them there is the idea of the written leaf.

**Its coming indefinite.** Not *al-ṣuḥuf* but *ṣuḥufan*. The indefiniteness here may be read as magnification (*taʿẓīm*): "such pages as these". The indefiniteness also shows that what is meant is not a particular bound volume — a revelation still being read, not yet complete.

**Ṣuḥuf in the Qurʾān.** The word occurs in several contexts:
- Of earlier revelations: *"in the pages of Abraham (Ibrāhīm) and Moses (Mūsā)"* (al-Aʿlā 87/19; also al-Najm 53/36-37).
- Of the registers of deeds: *"When the pages are spread open"* (al-Takwīr 81/10).
- And most importantly, in almost the same expression as this verse, in Sūrat ʿAbasa (below).

### مُّطَهَّرَة — purified

**Root ط ه ر (ṭ-h-r):** cleanness, being far from filth. From the same root: *ṭahāra*, *ṭahūr* (that which cleanses), *muṭahhar*, *taṭhīr*.

**The pattern: a passive participle, from Form II.** *Muṭahhara* = "cleansed", **passive**. This is a point not to be passed over: the pages are *not* clean, they have been **cleansed**. They did not become clean of themselves; there is one who cleansed them.

Moreover Form II (*ṭahhara*) conveys intensification and repetition: to cleanse from end to end, thoroughly. That this form was chosen rather than *ṭahara* (Form I) implies that the cleansing was not a single operation but a comprehensive one.

**Cleansed of what?** The verse does not say. The explanations proposed in the classical commentaries:

| Explanation | What it rests on |
|---|---|
| Cleansed of **falsehood, of lying, of contradiction** | A reading concerned with the content. In accord with al-Nisāʾ 4/82, "they would have found much contradiction in it". |
| Cleansed of **any admixture of the satans** | al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/210-212: *"The satans did not bring it down… they are kept far from the hearing."* The cleanness of revelation's channel of transmission. |
| Cleansed and guarded from **addition and subtraction** | al-Ḥijr 15/9: *"We have sent down the Reminder, and We are its guardian."* |
| Guarded from **the touch of unclean hands** | al-Wāqiʿa 56/79: *"None touch it but the purified."* Note: there the purified are **those who touch** (*muṭahharūn*), here **the pages**. |
| **Exalted, held in honour** | A reading together with ʿAbasa 80/13-16 (below). |

These explanations are not alternatives to one another but different faces of the same cleanness. The common point is this: **the channel between the text and its source is clean.** Nothing has been mixed in along the way, and nothing has been lost from it.

**The Qurʾān's exegesis by the Qurʾān — ʿAbasa 80/13-16.** The same word occurs there, in the same construction:

> **فِى صُحُفٍ مُّكَرَّمَةٍ ۝ مَّرْفُوعَةٍ مُّطَهَّرَةٍ ۝ بِأَيْدِى سَفَرَةٍ ۝ كِرَامٍۭ بَرَرَةٍ**
> *"In pages held in honour; exalted, purified; by the hands of scribe-envoys; noble and dutiful."* (ʿAbasa 80/13-16)

There the pages have three attributes: *mukarrama* (made honoured), *marfūʿa* (raised), *muṭahhara* (purified). All three are passive participles; all three are passive. The pages do not earn their own worth; they do not exalt themselves; they do not cleanse themselves. All three operations were done from outside.

This parallel shows that the *muṭahhara* of al-Bayyina 98/2 is not an attribute standing alone but a part of an established pattern of description.

**And there is this too: the language of ritual purity is applied to paper.** *Ṭahāra* is in Islam primarily a bodily and legal category — ablution, washing, purification from filth. To use this word of a text is a transfer of category: the text too has a cleanness, and this cleanness is something that can be spoiled. And indeed the Qurʾān speaks, with regard to a part of the earlier texts, of those who write with their own hands and say "this is from God" (al-Baqara 2/79). But note: the accusation there is directed not at the whole of a community but at those who commit a particular act — the verse begins *"woe to those who…"*, and describes an attribute.

## 98/3 — فِيهَا كُتُبٌ قَيِّمَةٌ

*Fīhā kutubun qayyima*

"In those pages there are upright writings/books."

### فِيهَا — inside the pages

The verse consists of three words and adds one further layer: the messenger recites the pages, and *inside* the pages there are books.

This nesting is striking. The expected arrangement would be: book → page. That is, the book the larger thing and the page its part. The verse says the reverse: **inside the pages there are books.** The page contains, the book is contained.

How is this to be understood? There are several explanations:

| Explanation | Comment |
|---|---|
| *Kutub* here means "written ordinances, the things that have been written" | In Arabic *kitāb* covers not the physical volume only but also **what has been written**. In the pages there are written ordinances. The most linguistic explanation. |
| *Kutub* is the content of the earlier Books | The new pages contain the substance of the earlier revelations. In keeping with the sūra's thesis of continuity (verse 5 will support this). |
| *Kutub* means "written decrees, ordinances" (*maktūbāt*) | The sense in the pattern *kutiba ʿalaykum* ("it has been written upon you"). |
| The plural is there to enlarge the scope | Not one single book but the load that many books carry. |

All of these are reasonable and they do not exclude one another. The common result: **the pages are not thin but laden.** To look at, a few leaves; in content, a library.

### كُتُب — books

**Root ك ت ب (k-t-b).** The concrete sense of the root is not to write but **to join, to attach one thing to another, to stitch.** *Katabtu'l-siqāʾ* — I stitched the waterskin, I closed its mouth. A *katība* is a military unit gathered into one body.

Writing attaches to this root here: writing is the work of **stitching** letters and words to one another. That the Arabic script is joined strengthens this picture further.

The root's second great use: **kutiba ʿalā** — "it was written upon", that is, it was made obligatory, made binding. *"Fasting has been written for you"* (al-Baqara 2/183); *"Retaliation has been written for you"* (al-Baqara 2/178).

The link between the two senses lies in the root's proper sense: **to write is to bind.** To put something into writing is to fix it, to stitch one side to the other. A spoken promise can come undone; what is written binds. This is why the same root covers both "writing" and "obligation".

And this throws light on the verse's sense here: the *kutub* inside the pages are not merely information to be read; they are what **binds.**

### قَيِّمَة — qayyima

The sūra's second key word, and the root that builds its weave, begins here.

**Root ق و م (q-w-m).** The full analysis of this root was made in other sections: *mustaqīm* (the straight path) at al-Fātiḥa 1/6, and *iqāma* (holding the prayer upright) at al-Baqara 2/3. I do not repeat the basic account given there; I confine myself to this reminder: the centre of the root is **standing, and being upright** — both being without crookedness and not collapsing.

What is new here is the word's **pattern**.

**The قَيِّم pattern.** *Qayyim* is on the *fayʿil* pattern (originally *qaywim*). In Arabic this pattern conveys **intensity and continuance**. A *sayyid* is not one lightly so (*hayyin*) but one who is a master continuously; and *qayyūm* is a still more intense pattern of the same root (as a name of God at al-Baqara 2/255: *al-Ḥayyu'l-Qayyūm*).

The word *qayyim* in Arabic has a sense that opens in three separate directions, and all three fit the verse:

**1. Straight, without crookedness.** Carrying no distortion within itself. The cleanest evidence for this from within the Qurʾān is this:

> **ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنزَلَ عَلَىٰ عَبْدِهِ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ وَلَمْ يَجْعَل لَّهُۥ عِوَجًا ۜ ۝ قَيِّمًا**
> *"Praise be to God, who sent down the Book upon His servant and left in it no crookedness; straight (kayyimen)."* (al-Kahf 18/1-2)

There *qayyim* is set as the opposite of the word *ʿiwaj* (crookedness) immediately before it. So *qayyim* = without crookedness.

**2. One who sees to another's affairs, who holds up, who watches over.** In Arabic the *qayyim* of an orphan or of a property is the person who watches over it; the borrowed word lives on in Turkish as *kayyum*, still a legal term in this sense. On this reading *kutub qayyima* are books that are not merely straight in themselves but that **make others straight.** Not standing upright, but holding upright.

**3. Valuable.** In Arabic *qīma* is "where a thing stands", what it comes to in exchange. English has no word taken from this root; the Turkish *kıymet* ("value") and *kıymetli* ("valuable") are borrowed straight from it. On this reading *qayyima* becomes "valuable, precious".

The three senses complete one another: a thing can hold another upright only if it stands upright itself; and it is valuable only because it holds upright.

**The feminine form.** *Kutub* is a broken plural, and in Arabic broken plurals may be treated as feminine singulars. This is why its adjective comes in the feminine as *qayyima*. It is a grammatical necessity; it carries no load of meaning. But it has a side effect: in this form the word joins, in sound and in shape, with the expression *dīnu'l-qayyima* at the end of verse 5.

### The sum of the three verses: the anatomy of the proof

The first three verses open the bayyina layer by layer:

| Layer | What is said | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| **Al-bayyina** (1) | The clear proof that distinguishes | The proof's function: to separate |
| **Rasūlun mina'llāh** (2) | A messenger from God | The proof's form: a person |
| **Yatlū** (2) | Reciting | The proof's act: following and transmitting |
| **Ṣuḥufan muṭahhara** (2) | Purified pages | The proof's channel: clean |
| **Kutubun qayyima** (3) | Upright writings | The proof's content: without crookedness, and straightening |

Note the order: **function → person → act → instrument → content.** The sūra proceeds from the outermost inwards. First it says what the proof is for, then who it is, then what it does, then with what it does it, and last what it carries.

And there is something these three verses do **not** recount: a miracle. In the description of the bayyina no marvel whatever is mentioned. A man, pages, writings. What is submitted as the proof is not a wonder that can be displayed but **a text that can be read, and a life that carries it.**

## 98/4 — وَمَا تَفَرَّقَ ٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُواْ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ إِلَّا مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا جَآءَتْهُمُ ٱلْبَيِّنَةُ

*Wa mā tafarraqa'lladhīna ūtu'l-kitāba illā min baʿdi mā jāʾathumu'l-bayyina*

"Those who were given the Book did not fall into division except after the bayyina had come to them."

### The sūra's sharpest observation

This verse is one of the most striking sentences in the sūra — perhaps, on this subject, in the Qurʾān. And its force is hidden in an adverb of time: **مِنۢ بَعْدِ** — "after".

The common assumption is this: people fall into division because they do not know. If knowledge is lacking, opinions multiply; if knowledge comes, opinions converge. This assumption is both intuitive and consoling, because it offers a remedy: more knowledge.

The verse refuses this assumption. The division arose not **before** the proof came but **after** it came.

And the sentence does not merely *say* this; it **locks** it with the grammar. The construction is *mā … illā* — negation + exception. In Arabic this construction sets up **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*), which comes into English as "only, none but, in no other way". The sentence, that is, does not say "they divided after the proof came as well"; it says **"their dividing happened at no other time, but only after the proof came"**.

The division has a single window of time, and that window opens after the moment knowledge arrives.

### Why does it happen so?

The verse does not state the cause. But the other places where the Qurʾān frames the same sentence do state it — we shall come to that shortly. First let us look at the mechanism itself; the three points below are my own reading, not a transmitted report:

**First: before the proof comes there is nothing there to divide.** If a community knows nothing at all about a matter, it cannot divide over it either. Division requires a *claim*. When the bayyina comes a definite claim appears — and a definite claim makes a definite stance towards it unavoidable. Indeterminacy is unifying, because it forces no one to say anything.

**Second: the proof creates a cost.** A person who does not know need do nothing. A person who knows must either comply or refuse; both exact a price. When a human being stands between a truth that has a cost and an evasion that has none, he mostly produces an interpretation that will legitimate the evasion. **The multiplication of interpretations begins exactly here.**

**Third: knowledge becomes a possession.** When a Book is given to a community, it becomes for that community not merely a guide but an **identity.** Knowledge that has become an identity is thereafter defended; and what is defended becomes the subject of division. To carry the Book is a different work from doing what the Book requires — and in time the first can take the place of the second.

### The Qurʾān's exegesis by the Qurʾān: the other versions of the same sentence

This expression does not stand alone in the Qurʾān. Almost the same sentence is framed in several places, and each time one more piece is added. Setting them side by side fixes what the verse says:

| Place | Verb | When | Cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Bayyina 98/4** | *tafarraqa* (they divided) | *min baʿdi mā jāʾathumu'l-bayyina* | — not stated |
| **al-Baqara 2/213** | *ikhtalafa* (they fell into disagreement) | *min baʿdi mā jāʾathumu'l-bayyināt* | *baghyan baynahum* |
| **Āl ʿImrān 3/19** | *ikhtalafa* | *min baʿdi mā jāʾahumu'l-ʿilm* | *baghyan baynahum* |
| **al-Shūrā 42/14** | *tafarraqū* | *min baʿdi mā jāʾahumu'l-ʿilm* | *baghyan baynahum* |
| **al-Jāthiya 45/17** | *ikhtalafū* | *min baʿdi mā jāʾahumu'l-ʿilm* | *baghyan baynahum* |

Three results follow from the table:

**1. The pattern is fixed.** *Mā … illā min baʿdi mā jāʾahum …* The Qurʾān uses this sentence like a formula. The fixed part of the formula: the division is always *afterwards*.

**2. "Bayyina" and "ʿilm" stand in for one another.** In some places what comes is *al-bayyina* / *al-bayyināt*, in others *al-ʿilm*. The bayyina, that is, is the **knowledge** that comes. This also settles the sense of the word in verse 1.

**3. Sūrat al-Bayyina does not state the cause — the others do.** In all four parallel verses the same expression stands: **بَغْيًۢا بَيْنَهُمْ** (*baghyan baynahum*).

### بَغْيًۢا بَيْنَهُمْ — "out of the insolence between them"

This expression must be looked at separately, because it fills the place Sūrat al-Bayyina leaves silent.

The root of the word **بَغْي** is **ب غ ي (b-gh-y)**: to want, to seek, to demand. *Bughya* and *bughya* are the thing sought; *ibtighāʾ* is seeking (*ibtighāʾa wajhi'llāh* — seeking the face of God). In the root's first sense there is no evil.

But in Arabic the same root opens in a second direction: **to want past the limit, to demand more than one's own share, insolence.** A *bāghī* is one who attacks without right. The point of transition is this: when the act of seeking oversteps its limit it turns into wrongdoing. To want is legitimate; **to want more than one's due** becomes *baghy*.

**And now the real point:** the second word of the expression is **بَيْنَهُم** — "between them". This is **from the same root** as **بَيِّنَة** (*b-y-n*).

The sentence, then, says this: the **bayyina** came to them, and they committed *baghy* in the **bayn** between them. Two products of the same root, in the same sentence, at contrary works:

- **Bayyina** is what opens the darkness between. It separates and it lights.
- **Baynahum** is the distance between them. *Baghy* enters there and turns the distance into enmity.

The proof came to light up the "between"; people turned the "between" into a battlefield. This is a verbal relation that actually stands in the text of the Qurʾān; it is not a forced reading but the weave of the root itself.

**And its meaning is heavy:** the cause of the division is not a lack of knowledge. The knowledge was there. The cause is **baghy** — wanting more than one's own share, the search for superiority, envy, the appetite for standing first. The division is not an intellectual accident but **a moral choice.**

### al-Baqara 2/213 — where the matter is set out in full

This verse is the text that builds the background of al-Bayyina 98/4. Because it is long I take it in pieces:

> **كَانَ ٱلنَّاسُ أُمَّةً وَٰحِدَةً فَبَعَثَ ٱللَّهُ ٱلنَّبِيِّـۧنَ مُبَشِّرِينَ وَمُنذِرِينَ وَأَنزَلَ مَعَهُمُ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ بِٱلْحَقِّ لِيَحْكُمَ بَيْنَ ٱلنَّاسِ فِيمَا ٱخْتَلَفُواْ فِيهِ**
> *"Mankind were a single community. Then God raised up the prophets as bearers of good news and as warners, and with them He sent down the Book with the truth, so that it might judge between people in that over which they had fallen into disagreement."*

> **وَمَا ٱخْتَلَفَ فِيهِ إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُوهُ مِنۢ بَعْدِ مَا جَآءَتْهُمُ ٱلْبَيِّنَٰتُ بَغْيًۢا بَيْنَهُمْ**
> *"And none fell into disagreement over it but those who had been given it — and that after the clear proofs had come to them, out of insolence between them."*

> **فَهَدَى ٱللَّهُ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُواْ لِمَا ٱخْتَلَفُواْ فِيهِ مِنَ ٱلْحَقِّ بِإِذْنِهِۦ**
> *"Then God, by His leave, guided those who believed to the truth over which they had fallen into disagreement."*

This verse adds three things to al-Bayyina 98/4:

**1. Disagreement occurs twice, but it is not the same disagreement.**
- The first disagreement: *"that over which people had fallen into disagreement"* — the Book was sent down **to resolve** it.
- The second disagreement: *"the disagreement of those who had been given the Book"* — this arose **after** the Book was sent down.

What was sent as the remedy, that is, became the subject of a second disease. The Book came to end the disagreement, and became **the address of a new one.** This is a bitter fact the verse does not conceal.

**2. Those who divided are "those who were given the Book".** Not everyone. Not the crowd sunk in ignorance. Precisely those who received the Book. Responsibility and division gather in the same place.

**3. The remedy is not knowledge but guidance.** *"Fa-hada'llāhu'lladhīna āmanū … bi-idhnih."* The remedy for a division that arises after knowledge has come is not more knowledge — because the problem was not a lack of knowledge in the first place. The verse gives the remedy as *guidance* and *leave*. That is, the setting straight of the will.

This marks out in advance the mistake most often made in an age of information: seeking a cure by knowledge for a division born of knowledge. If the division is not a problem of knowledge, knowledge will not solve it.

### أُوتُواْ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ — "those who were given the Book"

Here the sūra changes the naming, and the change is meaningful.

- In verse 1: **أَهْلِ ٱلْكِتَٰب** — the people of the Book. An expression conveying belonging; closeness and familiarity.
- In verse 4: **ٱلَّذِينَ أُوتُواْ ٱلْكِتَٰبَ** — those to whom the Book **was given**. A passive verb; a state of having taken delivery.

The first conveys a condition, the second an event. And the passive voice, without saying who the subject is, implies that there is a **giver**: the Book is not theirs, it was given to them. It was entrusted.

This is the ground of the accusation that follows immediately: **to divide over what has been entrusted** is a different thing from an ordinary difference of opinion. A man who divides over his own property owes an account to no one; a man who divides over a trust owes an account to the one who entrusted it.

**The root أ ت ي.** *Ūtū* is the passive of *ītāʾ* (Form IV); the root is **أ ت ي (a-t-y)**: to come. And *ītāʾ* is "to bring, to give". Keep this root in mind, because within the sūra it is used three times, in three different directions:

- **تَأْتِيَهُم** (98/1) — the bayyina **comes** to them.
- **جَآءَتْهُم / أُوتُوا** (98/4) — the bayyina **came** to them, the Book **was given** to them.
- **يُؤْتُواْ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ** (98/5) — let them **give** the alms.

The same root flows first from God towards man, and is then asked to flow from man towards man. The one who receives will give. This is a real verbal weave standing inside the sūra.

### تَفَرَّقَ — to divide

**Root ف ر ق (f-r-q):** to separate two things from one another, to open the space between them.

The root's derivatives are very widespread in Arabic: *farq* (a distinction), *firāq* (parting), *farīq* (a party, a group), *firqa* (a company, a faction), *iftirāq* (separating from one another), *tafriqa* (dividing). And most important of all: **فُرْقَان (*furqān*)** — that which separates; one of the names of the Qurʾān.

There is a tension here worth dwelling on: from the same root come both **furqān** (a separating that is praised) and **tafriqa** (a separating that is blamed). Where is the difference?

**Furqān** separates the true from the false — it opens the space between *right and wrong*.
**Tafriqa** separates people from one another — it opens the space between *person and person*.

The first is necessary; the second is a disaster. And the sūra's tragedy lies exactly here: what came so that it might separate the true from the false (the bayyina, the furqān) became the cause of people being separated from one another (tafriqa). **They divided on the wrong axis.**

**The pattern: Form V.** *Tafarraqa* is the *muṭāwaʿa* of *farraqa*, and in this form there is usually **gradualness** (coming about by degrees) and **reciprocity**. The division did not happen in a moment; it happened slowly, each pushing the other, by degrees.

**The Qurʾān's view of division.** The Qurʾān blames *tafriqa* consistently:

- *"Hold fast, all together, to the rope of God, and do not divide (wa lā tafarraqū)"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/103).
- *"You have nothing to do with those who split their religion into pieces and became factions"* (al-Anʿām 6/159).
- *"Do not be like those who divided and fell into disagreement after the clear proofs had come to them"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/105) — this verse turns the content of al-Bayyina 98/4 directly into a **warning**: what happened to them can happen to you.

The last item is important, and it is the foundation of what the sūra says to the present: the Qurʾān recounts "the division of those given the Book" not as the story of some community in the past but as **a mechanism that can repeat.** The one addressed by the warning is everyone who has received a Book.

### A note on balance

If this verse is read in order to belittle the People of the Book, it has been turned upside down. The sūra's own logic does not permit this, because:

1. The verse describes an **attribute**: "those who were given the Book". Whoever has received a Book falls under that description.
2. Āl ʿImrān 3/105 lays down the same act as a prohibition for Muslims. What is recounted, that is, is not a story about "them" but a human condition.
3. Āl ʿImrān 3/113 must be recalled once more: *"They are not all one."*

The verse's function is diagnosis, and it comes before accusation: **division after knowledge has come is the structural risk of communities that have a Book.** From the moment a community receives a Book it has taken on this risk as well.

## 98/5 — وَمَآ أُمِرُوٓاْ إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُواْ ٱللَّهَ مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ حُنَفَآءَ وَيُقِيمُواْ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَيُؤْتُواْ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ ۚ وَذَٰلِكَ دِينُ ٱلْقَيِّمَةِ

*Wa mā umirū illā li-yaʿbudu'llāha mukhliṣīna lahu'd-dīna ḥunafāʾa wa yuqīmu'ṣ-ṣalāta wa yuʾtu'z-zakāta; wa dhālika dīnu'l-qayyima*

"Yet they had been commanded only this: to serve God, making the religion purely His, as ḥanīfs, and to hold the prayer upright, and to give the alms — nothing else. That is the upright religion."

### The sentence's function: shrinking the subject of the division

To read this verse cut away from the one before it destroys its force. Note the order:

- Verse 4: **They divided.**
- Verse 5: **Yet what was commanded was only this.**

The verse comes, that is, not as a "definition of religion" but as **an objection.** The *wāw* at the head of the sentence works not as a conjunction but almost as a mark of astonishment: *you divided — yet only this much was asked.*

And the sentence is again built with the structure of **restriction**: **مَآ … إِلَّا** — "they were commanded only …, and nothing else." Verse 4 locked the time of the division with a restriction; verse 5 locks the content of the command with a restriction. The two verses use the same grammatical instrument in two different places.

**The picture that emerges is this:** the command was small, the division was large. What was commanded was five items; what was divided over cannot be counted. The sūra uses this disproportion as an accusation.

This is the sūra's most practical observation, and it is true today as well: **divisions mostly arise not in the command itself but in what has gathered around the command.** How a text is to be understood, who has understood it rightly, who is authorised, what a given detail requires — the quarrel breaks out over these. The plain ruling of the text is mostly not even the subject of the quarrel.

### أُمِرُوٓاْ — "they were commanded": to whom?

The verb is passive; who gives the command is not said (it is understood), but **to whom it was given** is not clear either. The views:

| Whom | Ground | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Those who were given the Book** (the subject of verse 4) | The nearest context. A pronoun refers back to the nearest antecedent. | In their own Books too this was what was commanded. A claim of continuity in the substance of the religions. |
| **The two groups of verse 1** (the People of the Book and the mushrikūn) | Verses 1 and 6 name the same group; the sūra sets up a frame. | The command is the same for everyone. |
| **All human beings** | The source and the content of *the command* are universal. | The widest reading. |

The first reading is the strongest as regards context, and it produces the most striking result: **the same thing had been commanded to them as well.** This verse, that is, makes a claim about their own Books — it says that the substance has not changed.

This claim is framed elsewhere in the Qurʾān too: *"He has laid down for you as religion what He enjoined upon Noah (Nūḥ), and what We have revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham, Moses and Jesus (ʿĪsā): hold the religion upright, and do not divide in it"* (al-Shūrā 42/13). There too the warning against division comes immediately after; and the verb used there, *aqīmu'd-dīn*, is again from the root *q-w-m*.

### The five items

The verse reduces the religion to four acts. Note the order:

| # | Item | What kind of thing | Its direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **ليعبدوا الله** — service to God | The fundamental orientation | Upward |
| 2 | **مخلصين له الدين** — making the religion purely His | Inward purification (unmixedness of intention) | Inward |
| 3 | **حنفاء** — as ḥanīfs | The setting of a direction (turning back from crookedness) | Direction |
| 4 | **ويقيموا الصلاة** — holding the prayer upright | A regular act | Upward |
| 5 | **ويؤتوا الزكاة** — giving the alms | Transfer | Sideways |

In fact the two circumstantial elements (2 and 3) qualify the first item; the main verbs, that is, are three: **service, prayer, alms.** And this triad is a core repeated many times over in the Qurʾān.

Note the architecture — the arrangement we saw in Sūrat al-Māʿūn is here as well: **the vertical bond (the prayer) and the horizontal bond (the alms) are named together.** Neither on its own is counted sufficient. And what is placed before the two of them is the intention that makes both meaningful.

### لِيَعْبُدُواْ ٱللَّهَ — service to God

**Root ع ب د (ʿ-b-d).** The word's abstract sense is well known: service, submission, worship. But the root's concrete use is more illuminating.

The lexicographers record that **طريق مُعَبَّد (*ṭarīqun muʿabbad*)** means **a road levelled, softened, its roughness gone** because it has been trodden over and over. *Taʿbīd*, that is, is the taming of a surface by continual use.

This picture says what worship does better than any definition can: **worship is a person's being levelled by being walked over and over.** Not a submission made once; a wearing and a softening formed by repetition. The going of hardness, of resistance, of roughness.

I transmit this as a point about the language; it carries no weight as a ruling, but it shows the image behind the word.

**The particle lām.** *Li-yaʿbudū* — "so that they may serve". This is the ***lām* of purpose**: it conveys reason and aim. The verse does not say "service was commanded to them"; it says **"they were commanded so that they might serve"**. A fine difference: the command itself is not the aim but what leads to the aim. Commands have a purpose, and the purpose is service.

### مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ — making the religion purely His

The analysis of the concept of **ikhlāṣ** was made in the section on Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ (the root *kh-l-ṣ*: to be freed of admixture, to become pure; *khāliṣ* milk, *khulāṣa*). I do not repeat what was said there. Three points are new here:

**1. What is purified is not the self but the religion.** The sentence does not say "purifying themselves"; it says **"making the religion purely His"**. The object of *ikhlāṣ*, that is, is *al-dīn*. This takes *ikhlāṣ* out of being a matter of feeling and makes it a matter of **direction**: removing all others from the religion's addressee.

What does it mean for a religion to be "mixed"? For other addressees to enter into it. For part of the prayer to be done for God and part for the onlooker; for part of a supplication to be directed truly to Him and part to one's own image. *Ikhlāṣ* is the straining out of this admixture.

**2. لَهُ has been brought forward.** The expected order would be *mukhliṣīna'd-dīna lah*. The verse has pulled *lahu* forward: **مُخْلِصِينَ لَهُ ٱلدِّينَ**. In Arabic an element brought forward from its normal place acquires **restriction**. So not "making the religion His" but **"making the religion His alone, and no other's"**. The centre of the sentence's weight falls on the addressee.

**3. The form is a circumstantial qualifier.** *Mukhliṣīn* is an active participle and stands in the position of a *ḥāl* (an element conveying a circumstance). *Ikhlāṣ*, that is, is not an item separate from service; it is **the condition in which service stands.** Service without *ikhlāṣ* is not an item missing from the list — it is outside the definition.

**Parallel usages in the Qurʾān.** This construction occurs almost word for word in several places in the Qurʾān: *"Say: I have been commanded to serve God, making the religion purely His"* (al-Zumar 39/11); also al-Zumar 39/2, 39/14 and Ghāfir 40/14, 40/65. What is striking is that at al-Zumar 39/11 too the verb is *"I have been commanded"* — the same construction as *umirū* at al-Bayyina 98/5. The core of what is commanded is given in both places in the same words.

### حُنَفَآءَ — as ḥanīfs

One of the richest words in the sūra.

**Root ح ن ف (ḥ-n-f):** to incline in a direction, to lean, to turn.

The root's concrete sense comes from an unexpected place: according to the lexicographers **الأَحْنَف (*al-aḥnaf*)** is a person whose feet turn inwards — whose soles face one another as he walks. The by-name of the well-known Companion Aḥnaf b. Qays is from this word.

The root, then, first names **a deviation**: a slipping to one side out of the normal stance.

**How, then, does this root come to mean "the true religion"?** Because inclining has no direction of its own — it depends on what one inclines from and towards what. By the period of the Qurʾān's revelation the word had taken on this particular sense: **one who turns his face from idolatry and inclines towards the religion of Abraham.** The deviation is a deviation from deviation itself: turning back from a crooked stance is, because it is a turning, still an "inclining", but it is towards the straight.

**A sister root: ج ن ف (j-n-f).** In Arabic these two roots face one another with a difference of a single letter, and they carry opposite values. *Janaf* too is inclining, but inclining **towards injustice**: *"Whoever fears that the testator has inclined to some injustice (janaf) or to sin…"* (al-Baqara 2/182).

The two roots tie the same movement (inclining) to two opposite directions: *ḥanaf* to the straight, *janaf* to the crooked. No single English word carries the difference; perhaps the gap between "turning towards" and "swerving" comes nearest.

**The ḥanīf and Abraham.** The Qurʾān uses this word insistently together with Abraham. And this is a use critical for precisely this sūra:

> **مَا كَانَ إِبْرَٰهِيمُ يَهُودِيًّا وَلَا نَصْرَانِيًّا وَلَٰكِن كَانَ حَنِيفًا مُّسْلِمًا وَمَا كَانَ مِنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ**
> *"Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian; but he was a ḥanīf, one submitted, and he was not of the mushrikūn."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/67)

Now set this verse beside al-Bayyina 98/1. In the first verse of al-Bayyina there were two groups: **the People of the Book** and **the mushrikūn**. And Āl ʿImrān 3/67 places Abraham **outside both of them**: neither Jew, nor Christian, nor mushrik.

This is no accident. *Ḥanīf* is the **third position** in the divided tableau the sūra sets up — or rather, the position **before** the division. The division was recounted in verse 4; being a *ḥanīf*, in verse 5, is the call to return to what came before the division.

This is the word's function: **not the name of a camp but the name of a direction.** To say "I am a ḥanīf" is not to enrol in a group but to state a bearing. And precisely for this reason the sūra sets it in the middle of a text that recounts the forming of camps.

Other usages: *"Say: rather, [we follow] the religion of Abraham, as a ḥanīf; and he was not of the mushrikūn"* (al-Baqara 2/135); *"I have turned my face, as a ḥanīf, towards Him who created the heavens and the earth; and I am not of the mushrikūn"* (al-Anʿām 6/79).

Note the verb in the last example: *wajjahtu wajhī* — "I turned my face". Being a ḥanīf is an act of turning the face. The root's sense of "inclining" stands here as a bodily movement.

**Its coming in the plural.** In the verse there is not *ḥanīfan* (singular) but **حُنَفَآءَ** (plural). The reason is simple: the subject is plural. But a side sense arises: being a ḥanīf is not a one-man heroism; it can be the shared stance of a community. Abraham was a ḥanīf on his own; by using the plural the verse says that this can be multiplied.

### وَيُقِيمُواْ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ — holding the prayer upright

The concept of *iqāma* was treated at al-Baqara 2/3: from the root *q-w-m*, "to raise a thing to its feet, to set it up, to hold it standing"; the difference between it and *adāʾ* (settling a debt); the prayer being not a duty struck off a list but a structure held upright. I do not repeat what was said there.

Two points are new here:

**1. The verb is governed by the lām of purpose.** *Li-yaʿbudū … wa yuqīmū … wa yuʾtū* — all three verbs stand under the same *lām*, in the subjunctive. The three are therefore not separate commands but **three faces of a single command**. The sentence does not say "let them serve, and let them also pray"; it says "they were commanded so that they might serve, hold the prayer upright and give the alms". Prayer and alms are not items appended alongside service; they are the body of service itself.

**2. This verse is the second knot in the sūra's q-w-m weave.** *Yuqīmū* (98/5) is from the same root as *qayyima* (98/3) and *al-qayyima* (98/5). I shall come back to this at the end of the sūra.

### وَيُؤْتُواْ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ — giving the alms

**Root ز ك و (z-k-w).** This root has two senses, and both are alive:

**1. To grow, to increase, to be blessed with increase.** *Zakā'z-zarʿ* — the crop grew. The shooting up of the plant.
**2. To be cleansed, to be purified.** *Tazkiya* is purifying. *"He who purifies his soul has prospered"* (al-Shams 91/9). *Nafsan zakiyya* — a wholly pure soul (al-Kahf 18/74).

How do the two senses stand together? In farming the answer is visible: a plant grows only when it has been **cleared of weeds.** Cleanness is the condition of growth. Taking away is the road to increase.

The word's becoming the name of a financial obligation is an application of this logic: the taking of a part out of one's property is named both the **cleansing** and the **increase** of that property. The word itself, that is, says that what is given is not a loss.

**A curious pairing inside the sūra.** In verse 2 the pages were **مُطَهَّرَة** (purified). In verse 5 property is named with **زَكَاة** (purifying). Two different words for cleansing, for two different objects:

- The text's cleanness: done from outside, passive (*muṭahhara*). The servant does not perform that cleansing; it is given to him.
- The property's cleansing: performed by the servant's own hand (*yuʾtū*). It waits to be given.

The sūra, then, receives one cleanness and gives another. What is received is a wholly clean text; what must be given is a cleansed property.

### وَذَٰلِكَ دِينُ ٱلْقَيِّمَةِ — "that is the upright religion"

The verse's seal. And its grammar is disputed.

**The problem is this:** *dīnu'l-qayyima* looks like a construct: "the religion of the qayyima". But *qayyima* is an adjective, not a noun. How can a thing be put in construct with its own adjective?

The classical explanations:

| Explanation | Its unfolding | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **The construct of the qualified with its qualifier** (*iḍāfatu'l-mawṣūf ilā ṣifatih*) | Another way of saying *al-dīnu'l-qayyim*. Arabic has instances of it, such as *masjidu'l-jāmiʿ*, *dāru'l-ākhira*. | "The upright religion." |
| **There is an elided qualified noun: *al-milla*** | *Dīnu'l-millati'l-qayyima* — "the religion of the upright community." | It adds a social dimension. |
| **The elided qualified noun: *al-kutub*** | A reference to *kutubun qayyima* in verse 3: "the religion of those upright books." | It makes a link within the sūra; the most elegant reading. |
| ***Al-qayyima* is here a verbal noun** | In the sense of *qiyām* / *istiqāma*: "the religion of the upright stance". | A conceptual reading. |

The third explanation is the one I find most striking: in verse 3 it was said *kutubun qayyima*; verse 5 may be repeating the same word and tying the sentence back to it. In that case the verse is saying: **"This is the religion of those upright books."** The four items enumerated in verse 5, that is, are the content of the books spoken of in verse 3. The sūra closes a ring within itself.

I offer this not as a preference but as a possibility. All four explanations have been defended in the classical sources, and the difference in sense is not great.

**ذَٰلِكَ — the far demonstrative.** Not "this" but "that". In Arabic the far demonstrative conveys here **magnification** (*taʿẓīm*): one points into the distance in order to raise the rank of what is being said. The same pattern will come again in the sūra's last verse: *dhālika li-man khashiya rabbah.*

### The Qurʾān's exegesis by the Qurʾān: al-Rūm 30/30-32

The strongest parallel to this verse is in Sūrat al-Rūm, and the parallel is extraordinarily close. Let us give the three verses in order and compare them with al-Bayyina:

> **فَأَقِمْ وَجْهَكَ لِلدِّينِ حَنِيفًا ۚ فِطْرَتَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلَّتِى فَطَرَ ٱلنَّاسَ عَلَيْهَا ۚ لَا تَبْدِيلَ لِخَلْقِ ٱللَّهِ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ ٱلدِّينُ ٱلْقَيِّمُ وَلَٰكِنَّ أَكْثَرَ ٱلنَّاسِ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ**
> *"So set your face towards the religion, as a ḥanīf. [Follow] the fiṭra of God, upon which He created mankind. There is no changing the creation of God. That is the upright religion (al-dīnu'l-qayyim); but most people do not know."* (al-Rūm 30/30)

> **مُنِيبِينَ إِلَيْهِ وَٱتَّقُوهُ وَأَقِيمُواْ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَلَا تَكُونُواْ مِنَ ٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ ۝ مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ فَرَّقُواْ دِينَهُمْ وَكَانُواْ شِيَعًا ۖ كُلُّ حِزْبٍۭ بِمَا لَدَيْهِمْ فَرِحُونَ**
> *"Turning to Him; and be mindful of Him, and hold the prayer upright, and be not of the mushrikūn — of those who split their religion apart and became sects, each party rejoicing in what it holds."* (al-Rūm 30/31-32)

The table of comparison:

| Element | al-Bayyina 98 | al-Rūm 30 |
|---|---|---|
| Ḥanīf | *ḥunafāʾa* (5) | *ḥanīfā* (30) |
| The upright religion | *dīnu'l-qayyima* (5) | *al-dīnu'l-qayyim* (30) |
| The prayer | *yuqīmu'ṣ-ṣalāta* (5) | *aqīmu'ṣ-ṣalāta* (31) |
| The mushrikūn | *al-mushrikīn* (1, 6) | *al-mushrikīn* (31) |
| Division | *tafarraqa* (4) | *farraqū dīnahum … shiyaʿan* (32) |
| The demonstrative | *wa dhālika* (5) | *dhālika* (30) |

Six elements out of six coincide. The two sūras frame the same argument in almost the same words: **being a ḥanīf → the upright religion → the prayer → not dividing.**

And Sūrat al-Rūm adds two things al-Bayyina does not say:

**1. The fiṭra.** The claim that this religion is not something put upon a person afterwards but that it accords with his creation. This is not in al-Bayyina; the stress there falls on the **command** (*mā umirū*).

**2. The psychology of division.** *"Kullu ḥizbin bimā ladayhim fariḥūn"* — "each party rejoices in what it holds." This sentence is the explanation of al-Bayyina 98/4. Why does division persist? Because **each piece is content with its own piece.** The pain of the division is not felt by those who are divided; on the contrary, each side counts what it holds sufficient and correct. The choice of the word *faraḥ* (rejoicing) is merciless: the division has become not a source of grief but a source of pride.

Reading these two verses together with al-Bayyina 98/4-5 completes the sūra's diagnosis: knowledge came → division followed out of baghy → each party rejoiced in its own share → yet what was commanded was four items.

## 98/6 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُواْ مِنْ أَهْلِ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ وَٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ فِى نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَآ ۚ أُوْلَٰٓئِكَ هُمْ شَرُّ ٱلْبَرِيَّةِ

*Inna'lladhīna kafarū min ahli'l-kitābi wa'l-mushrikīna fī nāri jahannama khālidīna fīhā; ulāʾika hum sharru'l-bariyya*

"Those who deny, from the People of the Book and from the mushrikūn, are in the fire of Hell, abiding therein. Those are the worst of created beings."

### The sūra closing upon itself

The first half of the verse is a **word-for-word repetition** of the first half of verse 1: *alladhīna kafarū min ahli'l-kitābi wa'l-mushrikīn*. The one difference is the particle at its head: in verse 1 *lam yakun*, here *inna*.

This repetition is not a weakness of style but a **frame**. The sūra opens with the same subject phrase and gives its ruling with the same subject phrase. What has happened in between is this: the bayyina came to them (1-3), they divided (4), yet the command was plain (5). Now the ruling is given (6).

The verse, that is, takes the sentence of verse 1 and **adds its conclusion.** The three verses in between are the ground of that conclusion.

### The particle "min" must be recalled again here

Because this verse carries the sūra's heaviest ruling, it is the place that must be read with the most care.

The subject of the sentence is **not "the People of the Book and the mushrikūn".** The subject is this: **"those who deny, from the People of the Book and from the mushrikūn."** Here too the particle *min* is partitive — a part of a whole.

My reason for repeating this: throughout history this verse has been torn from its context and used as a ruling directed at the whole of a community. The text does not give that.

What the text actually says:
1. The ruling is attached to **the attribute of denying** — not to membership.
2. In the sentence the attribute comes **before** the identity: *alladhīna kafarū* first, *min ahli'l-kitāb* after.
3. The same Qurʾān says of the same community *"they are not all one"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/113), and declares that those among them who believe have their reward with their Lord (al-Baqara 2/62; al-Māʾida 5/69; Āl ʿImrān 3/199).

To read one verse while disregarding other verses of the same Book is not exegesis.

And this must be added: verse 7 of the sūra, in describing the good side, **uses no group name at all.** We shall see this shortly; but it needs saying already, because it determines how verse 6 is to be read. In describing the bad side the sūra gives a group name, *but gives it with a narrowing*; in describing the good side it gives no group name whatever. This asymmetry is deliberate.

### فِى نَارِ جَهَنَّم — in the fire of Hell

**نَار (*nār*)** — root **ن و ر (n-w-r)**: light, brightness. An interesting root: from the same root come both *nūr* (light) and *nār* (fire). The common point is that both are things that make visible, things that shine. The difference is this: *nūr* gives light without burning, *nār* burns while it gives light.

**جَهَنَّم (*jahannam*)** — the origin of this word is disputed. Some of the lexicographers hold that it derives from an Arabic root (*jahm* — a sullen, forbidding face; or *jihinnām* — a deep well). Others tie it to an origin outside Arabic; it is connected with the name *Ge-Hinnom* (the Valley of Hinnom) near Jerusalem. I cannot speak with certainty as to which view is correct; both have their defenders. The word's use and sense in the Qurʾān, however, are not disputed: it is the name of the abode of punishment.

In the verse **جَهَنَّمَ** comes as a diptote (it takes no tanwīn) — this is explained by the lexicographers either on the ground of proper-name status plus foreignness, or of proper-name status plus feminine gender. The first ground supports the view that the word is counted foreign in origin.

### خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَا — and an important asymmetry

**Root خ ل د (kh-l-d):** to remain for a long time, to go on, to continue unchanged. *Khuld* is continuance; *mukhallad* is one made to abide.

In Arabic *khulūd* does not have to mean absolute eternity; it can also convey a stay of very long duration. This is why mountains are called *khālid* — a mountain is not everlasting, but on a human scale it abides.

**Now let us look at the asymmetry.** In this sūra *khālidīn* occurs twice:

| Verse | Expression | Addition |
|---|---|---|
| **98/6** (the Fire) | *khālidīna fīhā* | — |
| **98/8** (the Garden) | *khālidīna fīhā **abadā*** | *abadā* is present |

For the Garden *abadā* (for ever) has been added; for Hell it has not. This is a real difference standing inside the sūra, and it has been noticed.

**But it would not be right to draw a general conclusion from it.** For elsewhere the Qurʾān uses the word *abadā* of Hell as well:

- *"…[He guides them] only to the road of Hell; they will abide therein for ever"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/169).
- *"Whoever disobeys God and His Messenger, for him is the fire of Hell; they will abide therein for ever"* (al-Jinn 72/23).

The absence in this sūra, then, is not a rule across the Qurʾān. The reason for the choice in this sūra is, in my judgement, a matter of style: verse 8 is **a description of reward**, and every element of the description is given with magnification (*jannātu ʿadn*, *tajrī min taḥtihā'l-anhār*, *abadā*, *riḍwān*). Verse 6, by contrast, is short and decisive; it conveys not a description but a ruling.

This is a preference; it is not binding. In the tradition of *kalām* long debates have been held over the everlastingness of Hell; that exceeds the frame of this text, and it cannot be rested on the absence of one word in a single verse.

### شَرُّ ٱلْبَرِيَّةِ — the worst of created beings

**شَرّ (*sharr*)** — in Arabic it means both "evil" and, as an elative, "worse / worst". Its original form was *asharr*; because it was so much used the hamza dropped away (the same holds for *khayr*: originally *akhyar*). So the *sharr* here is not "bad" but **"the worst"**, and the construct that follows makes this certain.

**ٱلْبَرِيَّة (*al-bariyya*)** — the sūra's most striking choice of word. In the Qurʾān it occurs **only in these two verses** (98/6 and 98/7). Nowhere else at all.

**There are two paths as to its root, and both are defended in the classical sources:**

**1. ب ر أ (b-r-ʾ) — to create.** From this root comes **al-Bāriʾ**, one of the names of God (al-Ḥashr 59/24: *al-Khāliqu'l-Bāriʾu'l-Muṣawwir*). On this reading the word's original form was *barīʾa*, the hamza softened into a *yāʾ*, and *bariyya* = **"created beings"**.

The second sense of the same root gives one pause: *bariʾa mina'sh-shayʾ* — **to be far from and clear of a thing, to be quit of it**. From this comes *barāʾa* (being clear, acquittal; the other name of Sūrat al-Tawba). The connection between creating and "being quit of" may be explained by an idea of **separating** at the centre of the root: to create is to separate a thing out of nothingness and bring it forth. I record this connection not as a settled etymology but as a reasonable relation between the two wings of the root.

**2. ب ر ي (b-r-y) — to whittle, to pare.** *Barā'l-qalam* — to whittle the pen, to cut the point of a reed pen. *Burāya* is the shaving that comes off in the whittling. On this reading *bariyya* = **"the whittled ones, the shaped ones"**.

An explanation is also transmitted in which some lexicographers tie it to the word *al-barā* (earth): *bariyya* = those created from earth. I transmit this with caution.

**The variant reading.** Some of the imams of the readings have read this word with the hamza: **ٱلْبَرِيئَة** (*al-barīʾa*). This reading ties the word directly to the root *b-r-ʾ* (to create) and settles the first derivation. I do not go into the detail of which imam holds which reading; but the difference of reading is itself recorded in the sources, and it does not change the sense — it only removes the hesitation about the root.

### Why "bariyya"? A question of scale

The verse could have said "the worst of people" (*sharru'n-nās*). It does not.

*Bariyya* covers **all created beings**: human beings, animals, jinn, every creature known and unknown. The scale of the comparison has been drawn out to its maximum.

This changes the weight of the ruling. To say "the worst of people" is to make a ranking within humanity. To say "the worst of created beings" is to make **a ranking within being** — and in that ranking a human being who was given a Book falls below every being that received no Book at all.

The Qurʾān uses this scale elsewhere too, and there the comparison is made openly: *"They are like cattle; indeed, they are further astray"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/179; a similar expression at al-Furqān 25/44). And the ground given in that verse is exactly this sūra's subject: *"They have hearts with which they do not understand; they have eyes with which they do not see; they have ears with which they do not hear."* The instrument is there; the use is not.

### Why is this ruling so heavy?

The verse does not state its ground, but the context does. And this is the sūra's most important moral conclusion:

**The weight of the ruling is proportional to the quantity of light received.**

These people are not persons who heard nothing. The *bayyina* came to them (1-3). To a part of them a Book was given (4). What was commanded was exceedingly clear (5). And in spite of this they denied.

What makes them "the worst", that is, is not the raw weight of the acts they committed; it is **the position of those acts over against knowledge.** What is done without knowing and what is done knowingly are not the same thing.

And this principle has an unnerving corollary — one that looks directly at this sūra's present-day reader:

> **This criterion threatens most the one who has the most knowledge.**

The sūra recounts this as though it were a story about "them", but its mechanism is universal. Whoever has a Book in his hand, whoever is addressed by the bayyina, whoever knows what he ought to do, stands under this criterion. To use the verse in order to rank other people is to settle into exactly the position the verse warns against.

## 98/7 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُواْ وَعَمِلُواْ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ أُوْلَٰٓئِكَ هُمْ خَيْرُ ٱلْبَرِيَّةِ

*Inna'lladhīna āmanū wa ʿamilu'ṣ-ṣāliḥāti ulāʾika hum khayru'l-bariyya*

"Those who believe and do righteous deeds — those are the best of created beings."

### Complete symmetry

These two verses are one of the cleanest patterns of opposition in the Qurʾān. Let us set them side by side:

| | **98/6** | **98/7** |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ | إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ |
| Verb | كَفَرُوا۟ | ءَامَنُوا۟ وَعَمِلُوا۟ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ |
| Note of identity | مِنْ أَهْلِ ٱلْكِتَٰبِ وَٱلْمُشْرِكِينَ | **— none —** |
| Outcome | فِى نَارِ جَهَنَّمَ خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَا | (in verse 8) |
| Seal | أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمْ **شَرُّ** ٱلْبَرِيَّةِ | أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمْ **خَيْرُ** ٱلْبَرِيَّةِ |

The pattern is the same, the places of the words are the same, the sealing sentence is the same letter for letter — only one word changes: *sharr* / *khayr*.

Paired patterns of this kind are a technique the Qurʾān uses often, and their function is this: **to leave no third place between the two options.** When the same sentence pattern is framed twice, the reader is obliged to look for himself in one of the two.

### But within the symmetry there is an asymmetry

Look at the table once more. The third row is empty.

**Verse 6 bounds its subject with two names:** *min ahli'l-kitābi wa'l-mushrikīn*. The deniers, that is, are named with a statement of the source they come from.

**Verse 7 gives no name at all.** Not "from the believers", not "from such and such a community", not "from this umma". Only two verbs: **they believed** and **they did righteous deeds.**

This emptiness is the sūra's most important silent declaration, and it says two things:

**1. The good side has not been closed to a group.** In describing the bad side the source was stated; in describing the good side it was not. The state of "being the best", that is, cannot be secured by a membership, nor barred by one. The door is opened not by a name but by an act.

**2. The measure is not identity but conduct.** Even in verse 6 what carried the ruling was the verb *kafarū* (the identity merely bounded it); and in verse 7 there is nothing at all besides the act. In determining the sides the sūra uses a single measure, and that measure is not a name.

This is the rule in `USLUP.md` in the form in which it emerges from the text itself: **what the verse enumerates is an attribute, not a name.**

### ءَامَنُواْ وَعَمِلُواْ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتِ

This pair is one of the Qurʾān's most frequently repeated patterns, and its analysis was made in the section on Sūrat al-ʿAṣr (the root *ṣ-l-ḥ*; its opposite *fasād*; its kinship with *iṣlāḥ* and *ṣulḥ*; the definite plural form of *al-ṣāliḥāt*). I do not repeat it here.

Two points are to be added here:

**1. Both verbs are in the perfect.** *Āmanū*, *ʿamilū*. One would have expected a nominal sentence, or a pattern conveying continuance, to be used for belief. The use of the perfect stresses that the thing has **actually happened**: not an intention but something done and finished. Belief has been given, and the deed has been done.

**2. The order is binding.** First belief, then deed. The Qurʾān never breaks this order. The reason is that a deed acquires meaning only when it is tied to an orientation — this is also the work done by the qualification *mukhliṣīna lahu'd-dīn* in verse 5. The same act, with a different orientation, becomes a different thing.

### What the two verses say together

How can a created being be "the worst of created beings", or "the best"? This places the human being at an extraordinary point in the ranking of being: **both the lowest and the highest step are open to him.**

The Qurʾān states this doubleness of extremes plainly elsewhere: *"We created man in the fairest form; then We reduced him to the lowest of the low"* (al-Tīn 95/4-5) — and there too the exception comes immediately after: *"except those who believe and do righteous deeds."* The same exception, in the same words.

The cause of this doubleness is the sūra's whole subject: **the bayyina.** Having received knowledge makes a human being able to rise to the highest and to fall to the lowest. A being that has received no knowledge neither rises nor falls; it is fixed. The possibility of rising and of falling is born after the proof has been received.

## 98/8 — جَزَآؤُهُمْ عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ جَنَّٰتُ عَدْنٍ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَآ أَبَدًا ۖ رَّضِىَ ٱللَّهُ عَنْهُمْ وَرَضُواْ عَنْهُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ خَشِىَ رَبَّهُۥ

*Jazāʾuhum ʿinda rabbihim jannātu ʿadnin tajrī min taḥtihā'l-anhāru khālidīna fīhā abadā; raḍiya'llāhu ʿanhum wa raḍū ʿanh; dhālika li-man khashiya rabbah*

"Their reward, with their Lord, is gardens of ʿAdn beneath which rivers run; they abide therein for ever. God is well pleased with them, and they are well pleased with Him. That is for whoever holds his Lord in awe."

### The structure of the verse

The sūra's longest and most laden verse. It consists of five pieces, and each piece rises above the one before it:

1. **جزاؤهم عند ربهم** — the place of the reward: with their Lord.
2. **جنات عدن** — the name of the reward: gardens to settle in.
3. **تجري من تحتها الأنهار … خالدين فيها أبدا** — the quality of the reward: water and continuance.
4. **رضي الله عنهم ورضوا عنه** — the summit of the reward: mutual contentment.
5. **ذلك لمن خشي ربه** — the condition of the reward: awe.

What must be noticed is **where** the fourth piece stands. It comes *after* the description of the Garden. Having enumerated the gardens, the rivers and the everlastingness, the sūra stops and adds one thing more, as though to say "but here is the real thing".

### جَزَآء — reward

**Root ج ز ي (j-z-y):** to requite, to pay, to stand in the place of, to suffice.

The root has an interesting side: *jazāʾ* is a **neutral** word in Arabic. It can mean both reward and punishment; it is "the giving of what is deserved". In Turkish the borrowed word narrowed and only the negative side remained (*ceza*, "punishment"). English splits the two sides across two unconnected words, reward and penalty, though the older "requital" still spans both. In the verse it is the positive side that is used.

The root's second sense — **to suffice, to stand in another's place** — is more interesting still: *"And guard yourselves against a Day when no soul will pay anything on behalf of another (lā tajzī nafsun ʿan nafsin shayʾan)"* (al-Baqara 2/48). There *jazāʾ* has the sense of "standing in another's place, sufficing on his behalf".

When the two senses are put together this comes out: *jazāʾ* is what is the exact **counterpart** of a thing, what answers to it, what **covers** it. Neither more nor less.

### عِندَ رَبِّهِمْ — "with their Lord"

Before saying what the reward is, the verse says **where it is.** This order is no accident.

**عِند (*ʿinda*)** is in Arabic an adverb of place, but it mostly conveys not physical location so much as **nearness and presence**. Like the English "at", "with", "in the keeping of".

And what it says here is this: the reward is not waiting in a storehouse. **It is beside the giver.** To reach the reward is the same movement as reaching the one who gives it.

This expression is repeated in the Qurʾān like a formula, and the places where it recurs are telling:

- *"…whoever believes in God and the Last Day and does righteous work, for them is their reward **with their Lord**"* (al-Baqara 2/62; al-Māʾida 5/69). These verses occur in the context of the People of the Book and the Sabians — a balance within the Qurʾān as to how al-Bayyina 98/6 is to be read.
- *"Do not suppose those killed in the way of God to be dead; rather they are alive, provided for **with their Lord**"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/169).

The analysis of the word **رَبّ** was made in the section on al-Fātiḥa; I do not repeat it here. Let me only draw attention to the pronoun: *rabbihim* — "**their** Lord". And again at the end of the verse: *rabbah* — "**his** Lord". The possessive pronoun is used twice. The relation is not abstract but owned.

### جَنَّٰتُ عَدْن — the gardens of ʿAdn

**جَنَّة (*janna*)** — root **ج ن ن (j-n-n):** to cover, to conceal, to hide.

This is one of the most productive roots in Arabic, and its derivatives show very well what the word says:

- **جَنِين (*janīn*)** — the child in the womb; because it is covered.
- **جِنّ (*jinn*)** — beings hidden from sight.
- **مَجْنُون (*majnūn*)** — a person whose mind is covered over.
- **جُنَّة (*junna*)** — a shield; the thing one hides behind for protection.
- **جَنَان (*janān*)** — the heart; because it is hidden inside the breast.
- **أَجَنَّ اللَّيْلُ (*ajanna'l-layl*)** — the night covered. In the Qurʾān: *"When the night covered him…"* (al-Anʿām 6/76).

And a **janna** is a garden whose trees are so dense that they **cover** the ground. The word, that is, is not the general name for a garden; it is the name of a garden of a certain density. The shade is inside the word.

In a language spoken in the desert one must consider what this description means: uncovered ground is ground open to the sun and to drought. Covered ground is protected ground. The word *janna* does not describe a landscape; it describes **a protection.**

**عَدْن (*ʿadn*)** — root **ع د ن (ʿ-d-n):** to settle in a place, to stay, to stand fixed.

The best-known derivative of this root is still in use today: **مَعْدِن (*maʿdin*)**, a mine. English has no word from this root; the Turkish *maden* is this Arabic word itself. A mine is the place where an ore has **settled and been fixed** inside the earth. This is the sense of the root: a thing standing in its own place, not to leave it.

So *jannātu ʿadn* = **gardens that are settled in.** Not lodged in, but settled in. Not a temporary halt but a dwelling.

The choice of this word is meaningful, because from beginning to end the sūra had been recounting **placelessness**: an undissolved mass (1), division (4), the splitting into factions. What is given to people at the end of the sūra is **a place.** And the root of the word says that this place will never be left again.

### تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ — beneath which rivers run

The Qurʾān's most frequently repeated description of the Garden. Three details:

**1. "min taḥtihā" — from beneath.** The water runs not over but under. Not a stream passing beside the garden; a network of water circulating **beneath** the garden. The water, that is, is not an element of the scenery but the **ground**. What the garden stands on is water.

This detail speaks in the language of an arid country. There the continuance of a garden depends not on the water one sees but on the water lying beneath. Water that is seen evaporates; the water below remains.

**2. أَنْهَار (*anhār*)** — root **ن ه ر (n-h-r).** In Arabic the word *nahr* is properly **not the water itself but the bed the water runs in** — a channel opened out wide. In the sense of the root there is "breadth, opening out". From the same root comes *nahār* (daytime); some of the lexicographers explain this by "the broad spreading of light". I transmit this connection with caution; it is not undisputed.

**3. The verb is imperfect: تَجْرِى.** "Running". Continuance. The description is not a frozen picture but something in movement.

### خَٰلِدِينَ فِيهَآ أَبَدًا

For *khālidīn* see the note at verse 6. The added word here:

**أَبَدًا (*abadan*)** — root **أ ب د (a-b-d):** time continuing without a break, a duration that has no end. In Arabic the word is generally used in negative sentences with the sense of "never" (*lā afʿaluhū abadan* — I shall never do this); in an affirmative sentence it becomes "for ever".

*Khālidīna fīhā* already conveyed continuance. The addition of *abadan* closes off every possibility of a limit within that continuance. The two words together set up the concept known in the tradition of *kalām* as everlasting abiding.

### رَّضِىَ ٱللَّهُ عَنْهُمْ وَرَضُواْ عَنْهُ — mutual contentment

The sūra's summit. And one of the most striking sentence structures in the Qurʾān.

**رِضًى (*riḍan*)** — root **ر ض و / ر ض ي (r-ḍ-w / r-ḍ-y):** to be well pleased, to approve, to accept, to be content.

The word's opposite is **سَخَط (*sakhaṭ*)** — displeasure, anger, refusal to accept. The Qurʾān sets out this opposition plainly: *"Is one who follows the good pleasure of God like one who has drawn upon himself God's displeasure (sakhaṭ)?"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/162).

*Riḍā* is not merely "approving". Within it there is **finding sufficient**: the one who is content seeks nothing else. This is why *riḍā* is less a feeling than **a stance** — the stopping of the search.

**The structure of the sentence.** Two separate clauses, the same verb, the subjects exchanged:

| | Subject | Verb | Particle | Object |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ٱللَّهُ | رَضِىَ | عَنْ | هُمْ |
| 2 | (هُمْ) | رَضُوا۟ | عَنْ | هُ |

Each clause is the other's image in a mirror. The same verb, the same particle, opposite directions.

**To see why this matters so much, consider the version that does not exist:**

*"God is well pleased with them."* — On its own this too is a reward, and a complete one. The verse could have ended here.

But it does not end. The second half is added: **"and they are well pleased with Him."**

This addition has three separate consequences:

**1. The servant's attitude is entered in the record.** The verse says that a created being can have an *attitude* towards his Creator, and that this **matters.** This is an unusual thing in religious language. What is generally expected of the servant is obedience, acceptance, submission; but "being well pleased" is different from these. Obedience is possible while unwilling; contentment is not.

**2. It is implied that the contrary is possible.** A thing is praised only because it is possible. If the verse counts the servant's contentment with God a part of the reward, then **not being content** is possible too. The Qurʾān does not conceal this:

- *"And among people is one who serves God upon an edge: if good touches him he is at rest in it; but if a trial comes he turns about on his face"* (the sense of al-Ḥajj 22/11). This person is not content; he is **gratified** — and gratification is conditional.
- al-Tawba 9/58-59 recounts those who are pleased if they are given a share of the alms and angry if they are not, and then says: *"If only they had been content with what God and His Messenger gave them."*

*Riḍā*, then, is not an automatic by-product of belief. It is a separate achievement.

**3. The order is meaningful.** **God's good pleasure** comes first, and the servant's after. This order cannot be reversed, because the servant's contentment is an **answer**. A person becomes content when he knows that he is found pleasing. The stillness comes first from outside.

### Why is contentment after the Garden, and higher than the Garden?

The verse described the Garden: gardens, rivers, everlastingness. Then it added contentment. Why this order?

Because **a gift does not close the distance between giver and receiver.** You may give someone a very valuable thing and the coldness between you may persist. A gift is an object; *riḍā* is a **relation.**

The Qurʾān sets this hierarchy out plainly in one place. In Sūrat al-Tawba, immediately after the Garden has been described, this sentence comes:

> **وَرِضْوَٰنٌ مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ أَكْبَرُ ۚ ذَٰلِكَ هُوَ ٱلْفَوْزُ ٱلْعَظِيمُ**
> *"And a good pleasure (riḍwān) from God is greater. That is the mighty triumph."* (al-Tawba 9/72)

**"Is greater"** — than what? Than the gardens and the dwellings enumerated just before. The verse makes the comparison itself, and sets contentment above them.

al-Bayyina 98/8 says the same by a different route, by sequence: it finishes the description, and then adds contentment.

**And there is this too:** the setting of "being well pleased" as the summit of the reward redefines the aim of service as well. If the place to be reached is a state of contentment, then everything done up to that point — prayer, alms, sincerity, being a ḥanīf — is not an activity of **earning a reward** but an activity of **forming a relation.** The reward is the relation itself.

This is at the same time a measure that keeps piety from turning into creditorship: a creditor does not look for contentment, he looks for his due. Where contentment is the goal, the language of accounting collapses.

### The same structure in another place

The Qurʾān frames this two-sided contentment in one more place, this time as two *adjectives*:

> **يَٰٓأَيَّتُهَا ٱلنَّفْسُ ٱلْمُطْمَئِنَّةُ ۝ ٱرْجِعِىٓ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكِ رَاضِيَةً مَّرْضِيَّةً**
> *"O soul at rest! Return to your Lord, well pleased and well pleasing."* (al-Fajr 89/27-28)

There two patterns of the same root come side by side: **رَاضِيَة** (an active participle: the one who is content) and **مَّرْضِيَّة** (a passive participle: the one with whom one is content). What is framed in al-Bayyina with two *verbs* is framed in al-Fajr with two *adjectives*.

And in another place the servant's reaching a state of contentment is set down as a promise, as the last point of the giving: *"Your Lord will give to you, and you will be content"* (al-Ḍuḥā 93/5). Contentment, that is, is marked again and again in the Qurʾān as **the last stop**; not an intermediate stage.

### ذَٰلِكَ لِمَنْ خَشِىَ رَبَّهُۥ — "that is for whoever holds his Lord in awe"

The sūra's last sentence. And a conditional one.

All that description — gardens, rivers, everlastingness, mutual contentment — ends with a qualification. The qualification is not a group name; it is again an **attribute**: *li-man khashiya rabbah* — "for whoever holds his Lord in awe."

The sentence does not say "for the believers". It does not say "for such and such an umma". It says **مَنْ (*man*)** — "whoever". The most open-ended relative pronoun in Arabic. Here too, as in verse 7, the sūra defines the door not by a name but by an act.

### The difference between خَشْيَة and خَوْف

**خَشِىَ** — root **خ ش ي (kh-sh-y).**

Arabic has more than one word for fear, and the lexicographers distinguish between them. The distinction most treated is that between *khawf* and *khashya*:

| | **خَوْف (*khawf*)** | **خَشْيَة (*khashya*)** |
|---|---|---|
| Its source | The harm itself; an expected evil | **Knowing the greatness** of what is feared |
| What it requires | Sensing the danger is enough | It requires knowledge; it requires acquaintance |
| Its content | Avoidance, flight | Reverence, a shiver, a sense of exaltation |
| In whom it occurs | In everyone | In the one who knows |
| Its direction | Moving away from harm | A stance towards what is exalted |

This distinction is widely treated in the classical linguistic and exegetical literature. In summary: **khawf comes from the harm of the thing feared; khashya comes from the thing itself.** A person feels *khawf* before an animal he does not know; a person feels *khashya* before a great man he does know.

**The Qurʾān itself makes this connection.** The verse tying awe to knowledge is plain:

> **إِنَّمَا يَخْشَى ٱللَّهَ مِنْ عِبَادِهِ ٱلْعُلَمَٰٓؤُاْ**
> *"Only those of His servants who have knowledge hold God in awe."* (Fāṭir 35/28)

*Innamā* here conveys restriction: **only.** This is the connection the Qurʾān makes between awe and knowledge — awe is what knowledge amounts to in the feelings.

**A caution: khashya must not be confused with khushūʿ.** **خَشْيَة** (*kh-sh-y*) and **خُشُوع** (*kh-sh-ʿ*) are different roots, and in transliteration they fall very close to one another. *Khushūʿ* is lowering oneself, submission, stillness (al-Muʾminūn 23/2: *"those who are humble in their prayers"*). *Khashya* is a reverent fear resting on knowledge. They are not from the same root.

### Why is the sūra's last word "awe"?

This is the sūra's most elegant close, and I do not think it accidental.

**The sūra opened with a knowledge:** *al-bayyina* — the distinguishing proof that comes from outside.
**The sūra closes with a state produced by knowledge:** *khashya* — the mark that knowledge leaves in a person.

What happens in between is what knowledge does in a person. The same bayyina had produced two different results: one part divided (4), one part believed and did deeds (7). Where did the difference arise?

The last verse gives the answer: **in awe.** Where knowledge does not turn into awe, knowledge turns into division. Knowledge on its own does not set a direction; what turns it in one direction is the knower's attitude towards what he knows.

And this sets up an exact opposition with the *baghy* of verse 4:

- **Baghy** — taking the knowledge and using it to enlarge one's own share. Knowledge becomes an instrument of superiority.
- **Khashya** — taking the knowledge and growing smaller. Knowledge becomes an instrument of knowing oneself.

The same bayyina, two attitudes, two results. The sūra's whole argument gathers in this opposition.

**رَبَّهُۥ — "his own Lord".** Note the possessive pronoun in the last word. Awe is not towards an abstract high power; it is towards a person's **own** Lord. The relation is singular and owned. Having recounted the dividing of crowds, the sūra ends by coming down to a single person.

## The sūra as a whole

### The architecture

Eight verses, advancing in four moves:

| Section | Verses | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **The problem and the solution** | 1-3 | Humanity was in a state that would not come undone; what will undo it is the bayyina — and the bayyina is a person. |
| **The paradox** | 4 | The bayyina came, and the dividing happened *after it*. |
| **The plainness of the command** | 5 | Yet what was commanded was five items. The subject of the division was too plain to be worth dividing over. |
| **Two outcomes** | 6-8 | Two groups, two rulings on the same pattern; and at the end, something above the reward: contentment. |

This structure has a peculiarity: **the sūra does not tell a story, it sets up a mechanism.** There is no narrative, no name, no event. What is recounted is a process repeated every time between a proof and a human being.

### The two families of roots that weave the sūra

Looking at the sūra's vocabulary, one sees that it gathers into two groups. This is a striking concentration.

**The first family: the roots of SEPARATING.**

| Word | Root | Verse | The sense of the root |
|---|---|---|---|
| **مُنفَكِّين** | ف ك ك | 1 | The loosing of a bond, the opening of a lock |
| **ٱلْبَيِّنَة** | ب ي ن | 1, 4 | The space between two things; distinguishing; clarity |
| **تَفَرَّقَ** | ف ر ق | 4 | To part, to divide |

Three separate roots, three separate modes of parting. The sūra's first half is woven from end to end with words of separation.

And these three roots say different things:
- **f-k-k**: the loosing of a bond — the parting that *ought* to happen. The opening of the lock is good.
- **b-y-n**: distinguishing — separating *the true from the false*. This is the proof's work.
- **f-r-q**: division — the parting of *person from person*. This is a disaster.

The sūra's tragedy is how these three become confused with one another: the distinguishing proof that came to open the lock turned into a cause of people being separated from one another. **The parting happened on the wrong axis rather than the right one.**

**The second family: the root of STANDING UPRIGHT.**

| Word | Verse | Its function |
|---|---|---|
| **قَيِّمَة** (kutubun qayyima) | 3 | The books' quality: without crookedness, and straightening |
| **يُقِيمُوا** (yuqīmu'ṣ-ṣalāta) | 5 | The servant's act: holding upright |
| **ٱلْقَيِّمَة** (dīnu'l-qayyima) | 5 | The religion's quality: upright |

The root *q-w-m* occurs three times in the sūra, in three different shapes: once as the attribute of **the Book**, once as the act of **the servant**, once as the attribute of **the religion**. The same root, that is, travels from the Book to the servant and from the servant to the religion.

This is not an accidental repetition. The sense of the root — **to stand upright, to be without crookedness** — forms the opposite pole of the whole antithesis the sūra sets up:

> **Division is a horizontal scattering. *Qiyām* is a vertical gathering.**

To a scattering tableau (*tafriqa*) the sūra shows a single remedy: standing upright (*qiyām*). And it gives the concrete counterpart of it as well — *holding the prayer upright.* The prayer is the place where the root turns from a word into an act. The Book is "qayyima", the religion is "qayyima"; and the servant does "yuqīm" — he holds upright.

This is why one of the sūra's names is **al-Qayyima**.

### A third weave: coming and giving

A smaller but real connection: the root **أ ت ي** occurs twice in the sūra, and its direction changes.

- **تَأْتِيَهُم** (1) — the bayyina *comes* to them.
- **جَآءَتْهُم** (4) — the bayyina *came* to them (this is a different root, *j-y-ʾ*, but the same movement).
- **يُؤْتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ** (5) — they *give* the alms.

Receiving and giving, two directions of the same root. The sūra begins by recounting something that comes from God and goes on by asking for something that must go out from man. **What comes will go.**

### What the sūra does not say

The place in this sūra most open to abuse is verses 1 and 6. Two wrong readings must be explicitly shut out.

**The first wrong reading: "the People of the Book are, as a body, deniers."** The text does not give this:

1. In both verses the particle **مِنْ** is present, and it conveys the partitive: a part of a whole.
2. The attribute (*alladhīna kafarū*) comes *before* the identity in the sentence; the ruling is attached to the attribute.
3. The same Qurʾān says *"they are not all one"* and declares that among them there is **"an upright community"** that prostrates itself in the night (Āl ʿImrān 3/113-115).
4. The same Qurʾān says that those who believe and do righteous deeds — from whatever community they may be — have their reward with their Lord (al-Baqara 2/62; al-Māʾida 5/69).
5. And verse 7 of this sūra, in describing the good side, **uses no community name at all.**

The verse describes an attribute; whoever carries that attribute is the one the verse is speaking of. This is the methodological rule to which this text is bound, and here it is confirmed by the text's own grammar.

**The second wrong reading: "the division is other people's story."** In verse 4 the sūra recounts "those who were given the Book". And the Qurʾān lays down the same act, in the same words, as a **prohibition** for its own addressees:

> *"Do not be like those who divided and fell into disagreement after the clear proofs had come to them."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/105)

98/4, then, is not a historical narrative but a **description of a risk.** And the one addressed by the risk is everyone who has received a Book. A community that ascribes a Book to itself, the moment it reads this verse as being about other people, has fallen into exactly the mistake the verse describes.

## What this says to the present

**One. Knowledge does not produce agreement.**

Verse 4 of the sūra is an observation more than amply tested today: division after knowledge has come. We live in a period in which access to shared knowledge is easier than it has ever been in history — and reaching a shared judgement has not become easier. Two groups looking at the same data mostly arrive at two opposite conclusions, and each supports its own conclusion with the data.

The explanation given by the verse and by its parallels is this: **the cause of the division is not a lack of knowledge but baghy.** That is, the use of knowledge to enlarge one's own position. When we meet a piece of knowledge, the real question we ask is mostly not "is this true?" but "what does this mean for me?" When the second question takes over the first, knowledge stops being an arbiter and becomes a weapon.

The practical consequence is this: heaping more knowledge onto a disagreement born of knowledge does not work. al-Baqara 2/213 places the remedy elsewhere — in guidance, that is, in the setting straight of the will.

**Two. The proof's being a person.**

The bayyina is not a text but a human being. What this says to the present is direct: **whoever carries an idea is a part of that idea's proof.**

This is both a possibility and a burden. The possibility: no argument can do what a convincing life does. People can refute a thesis; they cannot refute a life. The burden: for the same reason, the carrier's inconsistency wounds what he carries. If the one who carries the proof is corrupted, the proof takes harm as well.

And it contains a warning: everyone who counts himself the representative of a cause is, without noticing it, adding a document to that cause's file of evidence. Which document it is, is left to his own choice.

**Three. The plainness of the command, the complexity of the argument.**

Verse 5. What this verse says is disquieting: what was commanded was four items. The division arose not in the command itself but around it.

Its counterpart today is plain to see. The greater part of religious argument is not about the core that is agreed upon but about the interpretations, the allegiances and the rivalries that have gathered around the core. Nobody argues about whether the prayer should be performed; the argument is elsewhere.

This does not mean that all disagreement is unnecessary — some disagreements are real and necessary. What the sūra says is narrower: **the disproportion between the size of the division and the plainness of the command shows that the cause of the division is not the command.**

**Four. The measure of "the worst" threatens most those who know most.**

Verse 6. The logic of this verse — that the weight of the ruling is proportional to the light received — makes it impossible to use the verse as a certificate of superiority. If the criterion is tied to knowledge, then the one who stands beneath the criterion is the one who has the most knowledge.

This is the antidote to the mistake most often made in one's relation to religious texts: always reading the harsh verses outwards. The sūra is built so as to prevent this — by giving the good side no name at all in verse 7, it allows no one to find himself there automatically.

**Five. Contentment — a relation, or an accounting?**

Verse 8. This verse places at the summit of the reward not an object but a relation. Its counterpart today has to do with how piety is constituted.

One kind of piety works wholly in the language of accounting: what has been done, what has been earned, what is lacking, what makes up for it. This language is not wrong — the Qurʾān uses the language of reckoning too. But left on its own it misses something: **in an accounting the two parties are not obliged to be pleased with one another.** The debt closes; no relation is formed.

The goal the verse sets is not the closing of the account but the two parties being pleased with one another. This changes the meaning of everything that is done: a deed becomes not a payment but an attempt at drawing near.

And its second half — *"and they are well pleased with Him"* — touches the matter of a person's being at peace with his own life. For a person to be content with God means to be at peace with what has befallen him. This is not approving of everything; it is coming to the conviction that nothing is ownerless and nothing is without meaning. The reason the Qurʾān counts this a *reward* is that it is not easy.

**Six. Awe's connection with knowledge.**

The last verse ties the reward to "whoever holds his Lord in awe"; and Fāṭir 35/28 ties awe to those who know. Putting the two together yields this: **knowledge remains incomplete where it does not issue in anything.**

What this says to the present has to do with how religious knowledge accumulates. Accumulating knowledge is easier than being changed by it. Knowing a subject and that knowledge leaving a mark in a person are separate things; and without the second, the first becomes exposed to the condition described in verse 4 of the sūra — dividing by means of knowledge.

The connection between the sūra's end and its beginning is exactly this: from outside comes the **bayyina**; if **khashya** forms inside, the knowledge has found its place; if it does not form, the same knowledge turns into **baghy** and divides.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

Per the method (`USLUP.md`), I list explicitly the places throughout the text where definite language was deliberately not used:

- ***Munfakkīn* in verse 1.** What the word expresses a parting from is one of the unsettled questions in the history of exegesis. Six readings were given in tabular form; none was presented as certainly correct. I stated my own preference (a combination of the second and the fourth readings) plainly as a preference and with its ground; it is not binding.
- **Whether the sūra is Meccan or Medinan.** It is disputed; it can be said that the majority call it Medinan, but there is no consensus. No preference was stated.
- **The occasion of revelation.** Since I know of no sound and widely accepted report of an occasion of revelation, none was written at all.
- **The report about Ubayy b. Kaʿb.** I stated that it is found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim; I did not quote its wording verbatim, I gave its sense. There are differences among its variants.
- **The maxim *al-bayyinatu ʿala'l-muddaʿī*.** It is also transmitted as a hadith, but its chain and its wording are disputed; for that reason it was transmitted not as a hadith but as a settled legal maxim.
- **The names of the sūra.** They were given as they are transmitted in the sources; nothing definite was said about how early any one of the names came into use.
- **What مُطَهَّرَة means.** Five explanations were given in tabular form; none was preferred. Because the word is not explained in this context, a definite preference would be without ground.
- **The grammar of the construct دِينُ ٱلْقَيِّمَةِ.** Four explanations were given. I brought the third forward (the reference to *kutub* in verse 3) as "a possibility", not as a preference.
- **The root of the word ٱلْبَرِيَّة.** Two derivations (*b-r-ʾ* and *b-r-y*) were given; it was stated that the variant reading supports the first, but no definite ruling was issued. The explanation from *al-barā* (earth) was transmitted with a note of caution.
- **No imam's name was given for the variant reading.** The existence of the reading with the hamza is recorded in the sources; since I am not certain to which imam it belongs, no name was written.
- **The origin of the word جَهَنَّم.** The views of an Arabic and of a non-Arabic origin were transmitted; no preference is adopted.
- **The derivational connection between نَهَار and نَهْر.** It was ascribed to some of the lexicographers, and the qualification "it is not undisputed" was entered.
- **The خَالِدِينَ / أَبَدًا asymmetry.** The difference inside the sūra is real, and it was shown; but no conclusion about the duration of Hell was drawn from it, because elsewhere the Qurʾān uses *abadan* of Hell as well (al-Nisāʾ 4/169; al-Jinn 72/23). The debate in *kalām* was not entered.
- **My own inferences.** The three points under the heading "why does it happen so" in verse 4, the interpretation of the relation of root between *bayyina* and *baghy–bayn*, the reading of the sūra's three roots of separating together, and the whole of the section "What this says to the present" are my own reading; they are not transmitted reports and they are not binding. This distinction was stated within the text.
