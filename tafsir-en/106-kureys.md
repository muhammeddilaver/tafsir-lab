# 106. Sūrat Quraysh

Revealed in Mecca. Four verses.

One of the shortest sūras in the Qurʾān, and one of the most tightly built. Its four verses are in fact **a single sentence**: a statement of cause (1–2), a command (3), and a ground for the command (4). The main element of the sentence stands in the middle, in the imperative; the two verses before it prepare it, the one verse after it supports it. An architecture this clear is rare in a text this small.

The subject of the sūra is, unusually for the Qurʾān, **economic**. It speaks directly of trade, of the caravan, of seasonal travel, of food supply and of safety on the road. It names these and then makes a single demand: serve the owner of this order.

---

## The link between two sūras: the lām at the head of لِإِيلَافِ

The sūra opens with a particle that does not begin sentences in Arabic: the **lām of the genitive**. Meaning "for…", "because of…", this particle must grammatically attach to something before or after it. It cannot hang alone.

This occurs at the head of no other sūra in the Qurʾān, and it produced one of the oldest debates in exegesis.

| View | Reading | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| It attaches to Sūrat al-Fīl | *"…and He made them like chewed-up straw — **so that the order of Quraysh might continue**"* | The lām is a genitive particle and must attach to a verb; the nearest verb is in the preceding sūra. The subjects of the two sūras (the protection of Mecca / the livelihood of Mecca) complete one another |
| It attaches to the following verse | *"**Because of** the order of Quraysh … let them serve the Lord of this House"* | The lām attaches to the verb *fa'l-yaʿbudū* in the third verse; the intervening *fāʾ* is the sign of this. The sūra is thus complete in itself |
| It is a lām of astonishment | *"Look at this order of Quraysh!"* | A view ascribed to al-Khalīl b. Aḥmad. Here the lām is not a connector but an opening that expresses wonder |

**Assessment.** The second view has been preferred by the majority because it allows the sūra to stand as an independent text; and indeed there is a basmala between the two sūras, and they are separate sūras in the codex. But the force of the first view is plain: in meaning the two sūras **genuinely complete one another**, and without the first, the second leaves the question "which order?" hanging in the air.

Reading the two together is in fact an old practice. It is transmitted that in the codex of Ubayy b. Kaʿb these two sūras were written together without a basmala between them. This report does not establish that the two are a single sūra — the present arrangement of the Qurʾān is fixed by mass transmission, and there is a basmala between them — but it does show how strongly the link was felt in the first generation.

**My preference in this commentary:** they are two separate sūras, but they build **a single argument**. Sūrat al-Fīl gives the fact (the city was protected); Sūrat Quraysh gives the consequence and the ruling (therefore trade continued; so serve the one who protected it). This preference is not binding; the sūra is intelligible on either reading.

## 106/1 — لِإِيلَافِ قُرَيْشٍ

*Li-īlāfi Quraysh*
"For the familiar order of Quraysh."

### إِيلَاف — the key word of the sūra

Root: **أ-ل-ف** (alif-lām-fāʾ). One of the most productive roots in the Qurʾān, and one that no single English word can carry.

The concrete sense of the root: **to grow warm towards one another, to become knit together, to grow accustomed, to come together and unite.** A bond that forms between two things over time, built not by force but by repetition.

Its derivatives show how wide the root is:

- **ulfa** — closeness, warming to one another, cohesion.
- **ilf** — the intimate one a person does not part from, the companion he is used to.
- **alifa** (Form I of the verb) — to grow used to something, to warm to it.
- **allafa / īlāf** (Forms II and IV) — causative: **to make things warm to one another, to knit them together, to bring them into one.** The word in the sūra belongs here.
- **taʾlīf** — putting parts together into a whole. This is why authorship is called *taʾlīf* in Arabic: knitting scattered pieces of knowledge into a whole.
- **alf** — the number one thousand. Some lexicographers attach this to the same root: in the Arabic system of numbers, a thousand is the largest collective unit named by a single word; it is many things gathered into one. I record this connection as an observation; there is no firm agreement among the lexicographers.
- **muʾallafa** — those whose hearts are to be knitted together. When the Qurʾān lists the categories of alms it says *"those whose hearts are to be won over"* (al-Tawba 9/60).

**The matter of the pattern.** The *īlāf* of the sūra is the verbal noun of Form IV (*afʿala*), and this form conveys **causation**. The sense is therefore not "the custom of Quraysh" but, exactly, "**Quraysh's having been given a custom**", "their having been made used to an order". The subject of the verb is not Quraysh but the one who accustomed them to that order.

This is the grammatical point that carries the whole logic of the sūra. Quraysh believed they had built their trading network themselves. In its very first syllable the word reverses this: they had been **placed inside** an order that was built.

### The other occurrences of the root in the Qurʾān

This root occurs rarely in the Qurʾān, but wherever it occurs it says the same thing: **knitting hearts together is not a work in human hands.**

- *"Remember the favour of God upon you: you were enemies, and He knit your hearts together, so that by His favour you became brothers"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/103).
- *"He knit their hearts together. Had you spent everything on earth you could not have knit their hearts together; but God knit them together"* (al-Anfāl 8/63).

The second verse is an economic sentence outright: **"had you spent all the wealth on earth"** — that is, cohesion cannot be bought. Sūrat Quraysh is likewise a sūra about trade, and it says the same thing: the very order Quraysh supposed it had built with wealth rested on something that wealth cannot build.

### The semantic field of the word in the sūra

*Īlāf* here covers three things at once, and the exegetes weight them differently:

1. **The cohesion of Quraysh within itself** — the ability of Quraysh to hold together around Mecca in a region marked by tribal fragmentation.
2. **The relationship of trust Quraysh built with neighbouring tribes and states** — the caravan's being treated as inviolable across all the lands it crossed.
3. **Quraysh's having grown accustomed to this order of travel** — an economy that had become seasonal, regular and predictable.

All three are gathered in one word. No single English word carries it; renderings such as "familiar order", "cohesion" or "settled custom" each give one face of it.

### قُرَيْش — Quraysh

This word occurs **only here in the Qurʾān**. That the name of a tribe should be named in a single place, and that in the context of an enumeration of favours, is worth noticing.

The origin of the name is disputed. Three proposals circulate in the classical sources: that it comes from *taqarrush* (to gather, to come together) or from *qars* (gain, earning by trade); that it comes from the name of a sea creature; or that it comes from the nickname of an ancestor. There is no settled conclusion. The first two proposals overlap meaningfully with the subject of the sūra — gathering and trade — but to prefer an origin on the strength of that overlap would be forced. It is enough to say this much: the name itself and the subject of the sūra circle the same two ideas.

The sources of the biographical tradition relate that Quraysh, living scattered around Mecca, were gathered in the centre of the city by **Quṣayy b. Kilāb**, and that the services of the Kaʿba were organised in that period. This is a historical report; it is not in the Qurʾān.

## 106/2 — إِيلَافِهِمْ رِحْلَةَ الشِّتَاءِ وَالصَّيْفِ

*Īlāfihim riḥlata'sh-shitāʾi wa'ṣ-ṣayf*
"Their being accustomed to the journey of winter and of summer."

### The function of the repetition

The word *īlāf* is repeated in two consecutive verses. In Arabic rhetoric such repetition is not idle; it is generally done for **magnification** (*tafkhīm*) and for **explanation**.

The grammarians mostly treat this second *īlāf* as an **appositive substitute** for the first: the first verse names the favour ("the familiar order of Quraysh"), the second describes it ("that is, their being accustomed to these two journeys"). The second verse does not repeat the first; it **fills it in**. The general first, the extent after.

One further point of word order: *riḥlata* comes in the accusative. The reason is that the verbal noun (*īlāf*) works like its own verb and can take an object. The sentence therefore functions like a verbal sentence in the dress of a noun: "his accustoming them to the journey of…". That Arabic can make a verbal noun do this much work is what makes a sūra this short possible.

### رِحْلَة — journey

Root *r-ḥ-l*: to set out, to migrate. **Raḥl** is the saddle put on a camel; **rāḥila** is the riding camel. The concrete core of the word is the tying of a load onto a camel's back.

*Riḥla* is on the *fiʿla* pattern, and this pattern in Arabic conveys **the manner of doing, the form a single performance takes**. What is meant is therefore not an ordinary going but a journey made by a known method, on a settled order. It is close to the English "expedition" in this respect: both the journey and the institutionalised form of that journey.

The word is also singular: one *riḥla* is used for two journeys. This implies that the two are **two legs of a single order** — not separate expeditions but one cycle spread across the year.

### الشِّتَاء وَالصَّيْف — winter and summer

The Qurʾān does not say **where** the journeys went. It gives only their seasons. It is the exegetical and biographical tradition that supplies the directions; the two must be kept apart.

According to the widespread report: **south in winter, to Yemen; north in summer, to Syria.** The geographical logic is clear and can still be verified today: in winter the north (the Syria–Palestine line) is cold and the passes are hard; in summer the south (the Tihāma–Yemen coastal strip) is extremely hot and humid. Splitting the two halves of the year between two directions is not a preference for a caravan but a necessity.

Some reports name other directions as well (towards Abyssinia, towards Iraq, Ṭāʾif). These are less widespread. That the Qurʾān specifies no direction shows that the matter is not geography but **order**: that both halves of the year be productive, that no season pass empty.

### Why Mecca was bound to trade

This is the most concrete background of the sūra, and the Qurʾān itself lays the ground.

When Abraham (Ibrāhīm) left his family there he prayed: *"Our Lord! I have settled some of my offspring in **a valley where nothing grows**, by Your sacred House… So incline the hearts of some people towards them, and provide them with fruits"* (Ibrāhīm 14/37).

This verse is the economic definition of Mecca: **agriculture is impossible.** The valley is rocky, there is no running water, seasonal rainfall is irregular. The basic condition for a settlement to survive — producing its own food — is not available here.

There is only one way for a city to exist in such a place: **to bring things in.** And that requires two things — a network to carry the goods, and a security that protects that network. Mecca had both, and both rested on the same cause: the Kaʿba.

The Qurʾān makes the connection itself:

*"Have We not settled them in a safe sanctuary to which the fruits of all things are gathered and brought?"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/57) — note: not "which they produce" but "which is **brought**". The city is not a centre of production but a point of collection.

*"Have they not seen that We made a safe sanctuary while people are snatched away all around them?"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/67) — this verse states plainly that the safety was a regional exception. The surrounding country is a country of raiding and highway robbery; Mecca is not.

*"God has made the Kaʿba, the sacred House, a **means of standing** for people; and the sacred month too…"* (al-Māʾida 5/97) — the word *qiyām* here means "livelihood, that on which standing depends". The Kaʿba is as much an **economic infrastructure** as a religious building; and the verse names it together with the sacred months, because the two are parts of one system.

### How the system worked

The core of the order the Meccans built is this: throughout the **four months in which fighting was forbidden**, highway robbery and blood feud were suspended across Arabia. In those months fairs were held, the pilgrimage was made, caravans moved. The inviolability of the Kaʿba and of the sanctuary was the ground of that calendar — and Quraysh, standing as the keepers of that inviolability, were indirectly the guarantors of the whole system.

The consequence is this: to attack a caravan of Quraysh was not an ordinary robbery but **the breaking of an order from which everyone benefited**. The safety of the caravan came not from an army but from a shared interest and a religious reverence. This is a fairly pure example of what is called soft power.

The biographical sources add a further layer of agreements: it is transmitted that the leading men of Quraysh — Hāshim and his brothers in particular — made agreements of passage and protection with neighbouring states (the Byzantine governorship of Syria, Abyssinia, Yemen, the Persian/Iraqi side) securing the safety of the caravans, and that these agreements too were called **īlāf**. The symmetrical account in which four brothers are distributed to four directions may have been arranged in a later period; but the existence of such agreements is plausible in terms of how long-distance caravan trade works. I transmit this as a report.

### What modern research records

Honesty requires an addition: the scale of Meccan trade is disputed in modern research.

The classical account pictures Mecca as a great transit centre through which the spice and incense trade of the Indian Ocean was passed northward. This picture has been criticised, particularly from the 1980s onward; the best-known objection is Patricia Crone's *Meccan Trade and the Rise of Islam* (1987). Crone argued that Mecca does not appear as an international centre of trade in the external sources of the period, that the incense route had largely lost its vitality by then, and that Meccan trade was rather a regional activity running on hides, skins and local goods. The thesis has been debated, partly criticised and partly accepted; no settled conclusion has been reached.

**What effect does this have on the sūra?** Surprisingly little. The sūra does not say that Mecca was rich on an imperial scale. What it says is more basic: that these people **could fill their stomachs and could be safe**. Whether the trade was small or large does not change these two outcomes for people living in a valley where nothing grows. The language of the sūra already speaks in terms not of luxury but of the **minimum**: to feed against hunger, to secure against fear.

## 106/3 — فَلْيَعْبُدُوا رَبَّ هَٰذَا الْبَيْتِ

*Fa'l-yaʿbudū Rabba hādhā'l-bayt*
"So let them serve the Lord of this House."

### فَ — the particle of consequence

The *fāʾ* at the head establishes a relation of cause and effect between the two preceding verses and this one. What is enumerated across two verses is a favour; what follows from it is a command.

The logic of the sentence is this: **you owe something in return to one who looks after you.** The sūra does not open this for debate; it takes it as given and moves straight to naming to whom the debt is owed.

### لْيَعْبُدُوا — "let them serve"

The **lām** at the head of the verb is the lām of command (*lām al-amr*). In Arabic a command to the second person is given directly ("do"); a command to the third person is given with this lām ("let him do").

There is a point about address here: the sūra does not say "serve", it says "**let them serve**". The one addressed is not Quraysh directly; it is as though a third eye were looking on from outside. In rhetoric this is approached within the frame of *iltifāt* (a shift in the direction of address). Its effect is this: the situation of Quraysh is set out like a case at law, and the verdict is delivered not to their faces but into the open. This is a heavier position than a direct rebuke — a person hears the verdict about himself in the third person.

**Root *ʿ-b-d*:** service, submission. The concrete sense of the root is a road's being levelled, softened by being trodden over and over; *ṭarīqun muʿabbad* is a road smoothed by much use. Worship, then, is a turning that has been softened by repetition, whose resistance has been broken.

This root has a hidden kinship with the root of *īlāf*: both convey a state that forms **through repetition**. Quraysh were made used to an order of travel (*īlāf*); the return asked of them is likewise a habit (*ʿibāda*). The sūra asks that habit be answered with habit.

### رَبَّ هَٰذَا الْبَيْتِ — "the Lord of this House"

The heart of the sūra is in this construct, and there are two distinct subtleties in it.

**First: not the House, but the Lord of the House.** Quraysh already revered the Kaʿba; they counted themselves its servants and lived on the standing it gave them. The sūra does not reject their attachment; it **corrects its target**: not the building, but the Owner of the building.

This is the exact name of a very common displacement in religious life. A person becomes attached to the **means** of the sacred, and in time begins to take the means for the thing itself. Place, ceremony, title, institution — all exist in order to point somewhere; and all can take the place of what they point to. The correction the sūra makes is a single word: *Rabba*.

**Second: "this" House.** *Hādhā* is a demonstrative, and it indicates what is near — near enough to be pointed at. The sūra is not framing an abstract theological sentence; it is pointing at the building visible from the window of the one addressed.

Its persuasive value is high. Quraysh are not asked to believe something they do not accept; they are asked to **see the consequence of what they do accept**. The argument works in this form: *You already believe this House has a Lord — indeed you relied on Him when Abraha came. Then let your service be to Him too.*

The same demonstrative is used of the city in a nearby sūra: *"and this secure land"* (al-Tīn 95/3). In both, what is indicated stands before the eyes of the one addressed.

**Third: why not "your Lord"?** The sūra could have said "let them serve their Lord". It does not. It builds the link not through the people but **through the House**. The reason is probably this: the identity of Quraysh was bound to that House; their standing, their income, their position against the surrounding tribes came from there. The sūra takes hold of precisely that point of support and pulls upward from it.

### The place of the House in the Qurʾān

*"The first House established for people is surely the one at Bakka, blessed and a guidance for all beings… Whoever enters it is safe"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/96-97).

*"And when We made the House a place of return for people, and a safety"* (al-Baqara 2/125).

In both verses the House is named together with **safety**. The same will happen in the fourth verse of Sūrat Quraysh. The Qurʾān's own definition of the House overlaps with the definition of the favour this sūra gives.

## 106/4 — الَّذِي أَطْعَمَهُم مِّن جُوعٍ وَآمَنَهُم مِّنْ خَوْفٍ

*Alladhī aṭʿamahum min jūʿin wa āmanahum min khawf*
"Who has fed them against hunger and secured them against fear."

### الَّذِي — a relative clause

The verse qualifies the word "Lord" in the preceding verse. That is, immediately after issuing the command to serve, the sūra adds **the ground of the command**. The command is not left bare; its support is shown.

This is a structure the Qurʾān uses often: the call to service is generally tied to a reminder of favour. Service is presented not as an arbitrary demand but as what a relationship requires.

### Two verbs, one structure

There are two verbs in the verse, both on the same pattern (*afʿala*, Form IV — causative):

- **أَطْعَمَ** (*aṭʿama*) — root *ṭ-ʿ-m*: to eat, to taste. *Ṭaʿām* is food, *iṭʿām* is feeding. Hence "he fed", "he satisfied".
- **آمَنَ** (*āmana*) — root *ʾ-m-n*: to be safe, to be without fear. In the causative: "he brought to safety", "he made secure".

The root *ʾ-m-n* is one of the central roots of the Qurʾān: **amn** (safety), **amān** (quarter, inviolability), **amāna** (what is left in trust), **amīn** (trustworthy), and from the same root **īmān** (faith). Faith is thus bound within the language itself to the notion of safety: to believe is to trust something and to be without fear beside it.

The connection here is not idle. When the verse tells Quraysh "He made you *secure*", it also intimates *īmān*, the sister of that word. The worldly safety given to them issues from the same root as the faith asked of them.

### مِنْ جُوعٍ / مِنْ خَوْفٍ — "against hunger", "against fear"

Here is the real point of the word order, and it is easily lost in translation.

Normally the verb "to feed" is used with food: "he fed them bread". Not here. The verse does not say **"he gave them food"**; it says **"he fed them out of hunger"**. The object of the sentence is not the thing given but **the state they were rescued from**.

The same structure appears in the second verb: not "he gave them safety" but "he secured them **against fear**".

The effect is this: the verse does not describe the favour, it describes **what would have been the case without the favour**. The natural state is hunger; the natural state is fear. In a valley where nothing grows these two states are the default. The favour is having been taken out of that default.

The grammarians generally explain the *min* here as expressing cause (*taʿlīl*) or a passage from one state to another (*badaliyya*); both give the same picture.

**The indefiniteness (tanwīn) is meaningful too.** The words "hunger" and "fear" are indefinite: *jūʿin*, *khawfin*. Had they been definite, a particular famine and a particular danger would be understood. Indefinite, the scope widens: hunger of every kind, fear of every kind. Or it may be read as magnification: a severe hunger, a heavy fear.

### The pair in the Qurʾān: hunger and fear

These two words occur **together** elsewhere in the Qurʾān as well, and each time they describe a basic human threshold.

**al-Baqara 2/155** — *"We shall surely test you with something of **fear** and **hunger**, and with loss of wealth, lives and fruits."* Here the two are the first two items of the trial. Note the order: what follows (wealth, life, fruits) is more particular; the first two are the most basic.

**al-Naḥl 16/112** — *"God sets forth the example of a city: it was safe and at ease, its provision coming to it abundantly from every side. Then it was ungrateful for God's favours, so God made it taste the garment of **hunger and fear** for what they had done."*

This verse is the **exact inverse** of Sūrat Quraysh, and it is clear this is no coincidence. Sūrat Quraysh: it was safe + it was provided for. The example in al-Naḥl: it was safe + it was provided for → it was ungrateful → hunger and fear. Most classical exegetes say that the example in al-Naḥl points to Mecca; the Qurʾān does not state this explicitly, but the verbal overlap between the two texts is very close.

Set the two verses side by side and the weight of the command in the third verse becomes visible. "Let them serve" is not a suggestion; its alternative has been described in al-Naḥl.

There is one further subtlety in the expression in al-Naḥl: hunger and fear are named as a **"garment"**. A garment wraps the body, encloses it, is not taken off. Both are thus described not as an incidental hardship but as a state worn continuously.

### The order: why feeding first?

In the verse, feeding is named first and safety second. In Abraham's prayer the order is reversed: *"My Lord, make this a **secure** land and **provide** its people with fruits"* (al-Baqara 2/126).

I transmit this reversal not as a ruling but as an observation: in the order of **asking**, safety comes first, because without safety provision cannot be carried at all. In the order of **giving**, feeding is named first, because it is the more pressing, the more daily, the more concrete. One is the order of the planner, the other the order of the one in need. This is my own reading, not a transmitted report.

### The answer to Abraham's prayer

One further connection must be made. Abraham had asked for two things: **a secure land** and **provision with fruits** (al-Baqara 2/126); he repeats the same prayer in Sūrat Ibrāhīm, and there he explicitly names the barrenness of the valley (Ibrāhīm 14/37).

The last verse of Sūrat Quraysh answers precisely these two items: **He fed** and **He secured**.

This verse is thus the record of the delivery of what was asked for in a prayer made centuries earlier. Quraysh were living inside an order they counted as the result of their own commercial skill; the sūra dates the bill for that order far further back. This also shows the sūra's understanding of history: the present ease of a society is not merely the work of that society.

## The argument the two sūras build together

Let us now set the chain out from beginning to end. Read together, the two sūras yield a closed argument in four steps:

**1. The fact.** An army took the House as its target and did not reach it. (al-Fīl 105/1-5)

**2. The consequence.** The House remained standing; and therefore the inviolability around it, the pilgrimage traffic and the safety of the caravans remained standing too. The *īlāf* of Quraysh — that familiar, regular, secure cycle of trade — continued by this means. (Quraysh 106/1-2)

**3. The ruling.** Then whoever owns this order, let service be to him. (Quraysh 106/3)

**4. The ground.** Because what this order gives you is the two most basic human needs: food and safety. Neither is your own work. (Quraysh 106/4)

The force of the argument lies entirely in its being built from data the one addressed already accepts. No knowledge of the unseen is demanded, no miracle is displayed. A conclusion is reached by way of an event the Meccan knew, an order he lived in, and a meal he ate.

One further detail: the sūra does not ask Quraysh to give up trade. Trade is not criticised, the caravan is not condemned, wealth is not forbidden. The only thing asked is that **the source of the order be addressed correctly**. This is generally how the Qurʾān looks at economic life: what is at issue is not the activity itself but what the activity is attached to.

### The continuation: Sūrat al-Māʿūn

The chain does not end with Sūrat Quraysh. The sūra that follows immediately opens thus: *"Have you seen the one who denies the religion? That is the one who repels the orphan and **does not urge the feeding of the needy**"* (al-Māʿūn 107/1-3).

The word is the same: *ṭaʿām*. A Lord who **feeds** you has just been spoken of to Quraysh, and immediately afterwards the one who does not urge **feeding** is condemned.

The link is plain and carries a hard ruling: **the one who has been fed is under obligation to feed.** Sūrat Quraysh shows where the favour was taken from; Sūrat al-Māʿūn shows where it must flow. Nothing at all comes between the command to serve (106/3) and the obligation to feed the poor (107/3). This sequence shows how closely the early Meccan sūras bind worship to social responsibility.

## What this says to the present

**First: the sūra says that prosperity does not explain itself.** When an order works for a long time it becomes invisible. When the caravans come and go every year, when the roads are safe every year, a person begins to take this for a law of nature. What the sūra does is to **make a working order visible again** — it is not teaching Quraysh something they did not know, it is showing them something they knew but no longer noticed. This is the common fate of every favour that flows without interruption: to go unnoticed.

**Second: the two basic needs are the same today.** "Food security" and "personal safety" — these are the first two items of the modern state and of modern economics as well. Whatever a society's level of prosperity, when these two are shaken everything else becomes meaningless. The sūra reduces everything a society possesses to these two thresholds and calls it to account from there. This is a simplification that does not shift the measure.

**Third: fragility is hidden.** Mecca's trading network worked splendidly; but beneath it lay a valley where nothing grew. Had the system faltered one day, the city would have met hunger within a few weeks. The same holds for most cities today: supply chains, imports, logistics, energy — all invisible so long as they run without interruption, and when they are cut, it becomes clear how fine a line everything was standing on. The sūra's warning is structural as much as moral: **if you do not rest on what you yourself produce, reconsider your relationship with the owner of what you do rest on.**

**Fourth: attachment is a feeling whose address can be confused.** Quraysh were sincerely attached to the Kaʿba. What was wrong was not the attachment itself but where the attachment stopped. This is an error repeated constantly today as well: putting the institution, the flag, the place, the tradition, even religious identity itself, in the place of what it points to. The sūra's correction is short and sharp: **to the Lord of this House.**

**Fifth: the last verse of the sūra gives a criterion.** "He fed and He secured." Whatever is built in the name of religion in a society can be examined by whether it increases these two outcomes. The Qurʾān itself uses the same criterion in the next sūra: the sign of denying the religion is given not as the absence of belief but as **the poor not being fed**. The measure lies not in the claim but in the outcome.
