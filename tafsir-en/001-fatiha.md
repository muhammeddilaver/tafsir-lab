# 1. Sūrat al-Fātiḥa

## The basmala — بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

*Bismi'llāhi'r-Raḥmāni'r-Raḥīm*
"In the name of God, al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm."

### The words one by one

**بِ (bāʾ)** — one of the smallest meaningful units in Arabic; a single letter, yet it sets the whole direction of the sentence.
The lexicographers count many functions for this letter; three stand out here:

- *Adhesion, being joined* (*ilṣāq*): this is the root sense of the letter. One thing touching another, sticking to it.
- *Seeking help* (*istiʿāna*): the "with" we use when we say "I cut it with this tool".
- *Accompaniment* (*muṣāḥaba*): the sense of "I went in having this with me".

Which of these is meant changes how the basmala is to be understood. Read it as "seeking help" and it says: *I do this drawing strength from the name of God.* Read it as "accompaniment/adhesion" and it says: *the work I do is done stuck fast to His name, never coming loose from Him.* The second is the deeper, because in the first the work is mine and the strength is His; in the second the work itself is attached to Him.

**The missing part of the sentence.** There is no verb in the basmala. "In the name of God" — doing what? Arabic grammar sees this gap and fills it: there is a hidden verb, unspoken. The classical debate is this: is the elided verb something general, like "I begin", or is it whatever you happen to be doing at that moment? The second view (the line al-Zamakhsharī defended) looks the stronger: eating, "I eat"; reading, "I read"; writing, "I write". The basmala is thus not an empty formula; each time, it attaches to the act of that moment and makes that act owned.

One further subtlety: the hidden verb is held to stand **at the end** — "in the name of God I read", not "I read in the name of God". In Arabic a fronted element conveys restriction. The emphasis is therefore: with the name of God *alone*, not with anyone else's name. This is a sentence set against the animals slaughtered and the enterprises opened in the name of idols in the pre-Islamic period (*jāhiliyya*). The basmala is a refusal as much as it is a prayer.

**ٱسْم (ism)** — there are two old views about its root: *s-m-w* (height) or *w-s-m* (mark, trace, brand). In fact both come out at the same place: a name is the sign that separates a thing from the rest and makes it visible.

There is a small oddity in the spelling: where one would normally write *bi-ismi*, the alif has dropped out and it has become بسم. The lexicographers explain this as "wear from heavy use". Words that occur often in a language get shorter — the way *God be with you* has worn down to *goodbye*. This is proof, cut into the writing itself, of how often the basmala was said from the very first day.

And why "in the name of God" rather than "with God"? Because a servant cannot take hold of the Essence directly; what he can take hold of is the name. The name is the face turned towards us.

**ٱللَّه (Allāh)** — whether this word is derived or is directly a proper name is a classical dispute. Those who say it is derived attach it to the root *ʾ-l-h*: to be astonished, for the mind to come to a halt, to worship. Another view attaches it to the root *w-l-h*: to lose oneself out of love. The majority, however, say this: it is the proper name of the Essence, and it has never been given to any other being.

There is a linguistic proof of this, and a very strong one: in Arabic this word cannot be made plural, cannot be made indefinite (one cannot say "a god" with it), and the *al-* prefixed to it cannot be stripped off. The language itself has locked this name into being singular. The Qurʾān states it separately: *"Do you know any that is His namesake?"* (Maryam 19/65).

The sound itself is meaningful: the word ends with an *hāʾ* issuing from the deepest part of the throat, on an outbreath. There is also an observation the Sufi tradition is fond of: go on subtracting letters from the front — *Allāh*, *lillāh* (belonging to Him), *lahū* (His), *hū* (He). Each time the word is clipped, but what it points to does not change. I transmit this not as a ruling but as a nicety; it carries no evidential value, but it shows how the language behaves.

**ٱلرَّحْمَٰن — ٱلرَّحِيم (al-Raḥmān — al-Raḥīm)** — here is the heart of the matter.

Both are from the root *r-ḥ-m*. The original, concrete referent of this root in Arabic is **raḥim**, the womb. The concept of mercy was not born in Arabic as the name of an abstract virtue; it was derived from an organ. This is no accident; it is the very way the language defines mercy:

The womb asks nothing of the one who benefits from it. The foetus has no service, no merit, no request; it is not even aware of its own existence. Despite this, warmth, nourishment, oxygen, protection — all flow without interruption. Without return, and given in advance. This is the measure of what the Qurʾān calls mercy: not what is received by deserving it, but what was given before the possibility of deserving even arose.

A ḥadīth establishes this link explicitly: God says, "I am al-Raḥmān; I created the womb (*raḥim*) and derived for it a name from My own name" (in al-Tirmidhī and other collections, in the chapter on maintaining ties of kinship).

**Why the two names side by side, and what separates them?** The difference lies in the pattern:

*Raḥmān* is on the Arabic **faʿlān** pattern. This pattern conveys fullness and overflow: *ghaḍbān* (filled with anger), *ʿaṭshān* (filled with thirst), *jawʿān* (filled with hunger). Al-Raḥmān, then: brimming over with mercy, His overflow spreading in every direction.

*Raḥīm* is on the **faʿīl** pattern. This pattern conveys a settled, fixed quality that has become a disposition: *karīm*, *ʿalīm*, *ḥakīm*. Al-Raḥīm, then: the one whose mercy is not a passing overflow but an unchanging attribute.

Hence a distinction widespread in the exegetical tradition (mostly ascribed to Ibn ʿAbbās): al-Raḥmān is the general mercy that in this world encompasses everyone — believer, unbeliever, animal, plant, all of them; al-Raḥīm is the particular mercy belonging to the believers in the hereafter. An ordering from the general to the particular, from what encompasses to what selects.

There is a very clean proof of this distinction from within the Qurʾān: **Raḥmān is used of God alone, whereas Raḥīm can be used of a human being as well.** In describing the Prophet the Qurʾān says *"kind (raʾūf) and merciful (raḥīm) towards the believers"* (al-Tawba 9/128) — but nowhere is any human being called "raḥmān". The language has opened one to sharing and closed the other.

### Historical background

The name "al-Raḥmān" was not foreign to the Arabs, but it was contested.

In southern Arabia (the Sabaʾ/Ḥimyar region), inscriptions from the centuries before Islam carry *Rḥmnn* — "al-Raḥmān" — as a name of God; Aramaic and Syriac have the corresponding *Raḥmānā*. The name was thus known in the region. The Meccans, by contrast, resisted using it. The Qurʾān records this resistance: *"When they are told, 'Prostrate to al-Raḥmān,' they say, 'And what is al-Raḥmān?'"* (al-Furqān 25/60), and *"Say: call upon Him as Allāh or call upon Him as al-Raḥmān; by whichever you call, the most beautiful names are His"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/110).

The point where the resistance took concrete form is the Treaty of al-Ḥudaybiya. As the text of the agreement was being written the Prophet had it begin "Bismi'llāhi'r-Raḥmāni'r-Raḥīm"; the representative of Quraysh objected — "We do not recognise al-Raḥmān; write *bismika Allāhumma*, as we used to write" — and it was written that way (al-Bukhārī). That a name could turn into a diplomatic crisis shows that the issue was not a choice of wording: Mecca accepted God — what it refused was His being defined by mercy. Because a God defined by mercy makes intermediaries and idols unnecessary.

### Its place and its extent

The basmala stands at the head of every sūra except Sūrat al-Tawba. It also occurs as a sentence within the Qurʾān: the letter Solomon (Sulaymān) wrote to the queen of Sabaʾ begins with it (al-Naml 27/30), and Noah (Nūḥ) sets the ark afloat "in the name of God" (Hūd 11/41). It is thus the opening formula of a text, of a piece of state correspondence, and of an act of deliverance alike.

That al-Fātiḥa has seven verses is agreed (the Qurʾān calls it "the seven oft-repeated", al-Ḥijr 15/87). The disagreement is over whether the basmala counts as one of those seven: the Shāfiʿīs count it as the first verse and recite it aloud in prayer; the Ḥanafīs and Mālikīs do not count it as part of al-Fātiḥa — the Ḥanafīs hold it to be an independent verse sent down to separate the sūras. This disagreement looks technical, but it has stayed alive because it changes what is actually performed in prayer. Both sides argue from evidence; I state no preference here.

### What this says to the present

What the basmala does is to take an act out of ownership and turn it into something held in trust. The difference between "I am doing this" and "I do this in His name" lies in the ownership of the outcome. In the first, success leads to pride and failure to collapse; in the second, neither belongs to you.

The second point is more critical: the basmala is a boundary. Work that cannot be begun in His name is work that cannot be done. The requirement that the basmala be said over a slaughtered animal establishes exactly this — it binds the touching of a living creature to a permission and a reminder, and takes it out of being an ordinary act of consumption. Carry that measure into the present: the contract you could not make while naming Him, the piece of writing you could not write while naming Him, the account you could not open while naming Him. Each time, the formula calls the act itself to be examined.

Third: the Qurʾān opens itself with mercy. The first sentence of a text is the key to how that text is to be read. Every command, prohibition and warning that follows stands under this heading — not above it.

## 1/1 — ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ

*al-Ḥamdu li'llāhi Rabbi'l-ʿālamīn*
"Praise belongs to God, the Lord of all the worlds."

### How the sentence is built: the unspoken "I"

The most important feature of this verse is invisible at first glance. The verse **does not say "I praise".** In Arabic it could have said *aḥmadu'llāha* (I praise God); it does not. Instead it builds a verbless sentence: *al-ḥamdu li'llāh* — *praise belongs to God.*

In Arabic this distinction is sharp. A verbal sentence conveys occurrence and renewal: that something has taken place, that it needs doing again and again. A nominal sentence conveys fixity and continuity: that something simply is so, of itself and without interruption.

Consider the difference. Had it said "I praise":
- Praise would be tied to my act. When I sleep, when I am heedless, when I die, praise would be cut off.
- The subject of the sentence would be "I". The subject of the Qurʾān's first sentence would be the servant.
- Praise would be a *claim*: "I am doing it."

When it says "praise belongs to God":
- Praise exists and is His even if no one performs it. The servant does not *produce* praise; he *reports* a fact that already holds.
- There is no "I" in the sentence. The self of the servant has been taken out of the Qurʾān's opening sentence.
- Praise becomes a *statement of fact*: this is already how things stand.

This is the measure the Qurʾān sets in its very first sentence: the servant is not the source of praise but the one who notices it.

### الْحَمْد — ḥamd

**The "al-" at its head.** In Arabic this prefix here conveys comprehensiveness: praise of every kind, of every sort, from everyone, what has been in the past and what will be in the future. Not "a quantity of praise" but the whole of praise.

**Ḥamd is not commendation; nor is it thanks.** The distinction drawn by the philologists (al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī in particular) is this:

- **Madḥ (commendation)**: can be offered for anything — even for things not acquired by will. A beautiful face is commended; the person put no effort into it. And commendation can be offered in expectation of return, and can be false.
- **Shukr (thanks)**: is offered only in return for a good that has reached you. Thanks depends on the favour; if the favour has not come to you, no thanks arises.
- **Ḥamd**: is directed at excellences produced by will (narrower than commendation), but does not require that the favour reach you (wider than thanks). Ḥamd is offered for a good no one has seen, and for an excellence that never touched you at all.

Ḥamd therefore unites commendation with gratitude and with love in a single word. Commendation can be cold; ḥamd cannot. There is admiration in it, and indebtedness, and affection. No single English word carries all three at once.

The derivatives of this root (*ḥ-m-d*) are striking: **Muḥammad** (much praised), **Aḥmad** (the one who praises most / the most praised), **Maḥmūd** (praised), **Ḥamīd** (both the one who praises and the one worthy of praise — one of the names of God). The first word of the first sūra of the Qurʾān and the name of the last prophet come from the same root.

### لِلَّه — "belongs to God"

The **lām** here conveys ownership and assignment. Not "praise *for* God" but "praise *belongs to* God". It is like answering the question "whose is the book?" with "Ahmet's" — it states possession.

Here too there is restriction: since the whole of praise is His, no share is left to be set aside for anyone else. This does not forbid thanking a human being; but it does remind you where the person you thank got the capacity to do that good in the first place. The last link of the chain always comes out at the same place.

### رَبّ — Rabb

It is usually rendered "Lord", and taken to mean something like "master, owner". The word carries far more weight than that.

The root (*r-b-b*) carries two senses at once: **to own** and **to nurture** — that is, to bring a thing to ripeness stage by stage, to complete what it lacks and bring it to perfection. Arabic uses this root for "he raised/brought up the child"; a stepdaughter is called *rabība*, because she is the one being raised; the master of a house is *rabb al-dār*.

That the two senses meet in one word is decisive: ownership here is not a right of use but **a responsibility to bring up.** The Rabb is not one who owns his property and consumes it; he is one who makes what he owns grow, feeds it, brings it to completion.

And this is the name that occurs in the first verse of the Qurʾān in the order of revelation: *"Read in the name of your Lord who created"* (al-ʿAlaq 96/1). The first attribute revelation introduces to the human being is that He nurtures.

Note: the word "Rabb" used on its own, without a genitive, is used only of God. Of a human being it can be used only in a construct — in the story of Joseph (Yūsuf) the word occurs when he says to his fellow prisoner "mention me to your master" (Yūsuf 12/42), but always attached to something.

### ٱلْعَٰلَمِين — the worlds

The root is *ʿ-l-m*: to know, or *ʿalāma* (mark, sign). On this reckoning **an *ʿālam* is "that by which something is known"** — a sign, an indication, an indicator. The name of the universe in Arabic is not "a heap of matter" but "a source of knowledge". The name itself says that everything that exists points to something.

**The plural form is what is really interesting.** The plural "*ʿālamīn*" is a plural form normally reserved in Arabic for **beings possessed of reason**. For non-rational multiplicities the expected plural would be *ʿawālim*. The Qurʾān uses the plural of rational beings. The classical exegetes explained this in two ways: either what is meant is the communities possessed of reason (humans, jinn, angels), or all the worlds are being treated under this ruling — because each of them knows and glorifies in its own tongue.

Also, "world" is not singular but plural: not one world but **worlds.** The classical comment is "each kind is a world" — the world of humans, the world of animals, the world of plants. Today we see this multiplicity more concretely: beyond the visible universe, hundreds of billions of galaxies; and at the same time, under the microscope, a separate world made of billions of living things in a single handful of soil. The one thing common across the scales is that all of them are subject to the same laws and the same nurture.

### The logic of the ordering

The sequence of names in the first three verses of al-Fātiḥa is not random; it follows the order of an introduction:

**Allāh** (the name of the Essence — who He is) → **Rabb al-ʿālamīn** (His relation to you — the one who brings you up) → **al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm** (the quality of that relation — mercy) → **Mālik yawm al-dīn** (the outcome of the relation — the reckoning).

First He introduces Himself, then He establishes the bond, then He says that the bond runs on mercy, and only at the end does He recall responsibility. Had the order been reversed — reckoning first, then mercy — an entirely different religion would have emerged. Fear comes after mercy, not before it.

### Its historical and political aspect

The Arab of the pre-Islamic period was no stranger to the notion of a "lord"; but lords were divided up — every tribe, every oasis, every trade route had its own lord. The phrase "Lord of all the worlds" is a claim to unity against this fragmented picture: a single Lord of all the worlds.

The political side of this claim is plainly visible in the Qurʾān. Pharaoh had proclaimed himself *"I am your lord, the most high"* (al-Nāziʿāt 79/24). When Moses (Mūsā) spoke to him of "the Lord of all the worlds", Pharaoh's answer was a question: *"And what is the Lord of all the worlds?"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/23). The question is not a request for information but a refusal — because if the worlds have a Lord, Pharaoh's lordship falls.

To say "Lord of all the worlds" is at the same time to say that **no human being is the lord of another human being**. The sentence has a political side as much as a theological one.

### Its counterpart in the prayer

In a ḥadīth qudsī (Muslim) it is transmitted that God said: "I have divided the prayer into two halves between Myself and My servant." When the servant says *"al-ḥamdu li'llāhi rabbi'l-ʿālamīn"*, God says, "My servant has praised Me"; when he says *"al-raḥmāni'r-raḥīm"*, God says, "My servant has extolled Me"; when he says *"iyyāka naʿbudu"*, God says, "This is between Me and My servant."

Al-Fātiḥa is thus not a text but **a conversation.** Every sentence has an answer. What is recited in the prayer is not a monologue but a dialogue.

### What this says to the present

**First: ḥamd is not conditional.** Thanks depends on the favour — if the favour does not come, neither does the thanks. Ḥamd is not like that. It can therefore be said in a moment of hardship too; because ḥamd does not mean "I am grateful for what has been given to me" but "He is worthy of praise". The difference between the two is whether a person has a point of support independent of his own condition.

**Second: the name Rabb rules out the idea of a God who sets things going and departs.** Nurture is not a job done once and finished; it requires continuity. A seed becoming a tree, elements being forged inside stars, an embryo taking shape stage by stage — all of these are processes, not one-off operations. Rabb is the name of an order that is working; not of a clock wound up and abandoned.

**Third: the Qurʾān opens with this sentence, but it also closes with this sentence.** The same sentence is given as the last word of the people of the Garden: *"And the end of their prayer is, 'Praise belongs to God, the Lord of all the worlds'"* (Yūnus 10/10). The starting point and the point of arrival are the same sentence. Everything in between — creation, trial, reckoning — fits between these two praises.

## 1/2 — ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

*al-Raḥmāni'r-Raḥīm* — "He is al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm."

I gave the root and pattern analysis of these two names in the section on the basmala; I do not repeat it here. The real question here is a different one: **why do the same two names recur two lines later, in the same sūra?**

### Its place in the word order

Grammatically this verse is not a new sentence. It is the continuation of the preceding verse and its qualifier — it attaches to the construct in "to God, the Lord of all the worlds". It gives no separate piece of information; it **qualifies** what came before.

Notice what it qualifies: **the Rabb.**

### Why exactly here

"Lord of all the worlds" means the one who nurtures. And nurture is not always pleasant. Raising involves pruning; education involves compulsion; ripening involves distress. A person's experience of life confirms this: a hand that brings you up can sometimes feel like a blow.

At exactly this point, with no interval at all, comes "al-Raḥmān, al-Raḥīm". What the sentence does is **to fix the quality of the nurture.** The hand that raises you moves not from harshness but from mercy. Hardship is inside the nurture, but its source is not wrath.

Think of it in reverse. Had "Master of the Day of Judgement" come directly after "Lord of all the worlds", the picture would have been of one powerful, all-encompassing and exacting, but of indeterminate tenderness. Mercy has been placed **between** nurture and reckoning. The sūra brings the reckoning along behind mercy.

### The grounds for the praise

Read the first three verses of the sūra as a whole: the first verse lays down a ruling ("praise belongs to God"), and those after it set out the grounds for that ruling.

| | Ground |
|---|---|
| Rabb al-ʿālamīn | Because He brought you and everything else into being from nothing and nurtured it |
| al-Raḥmān | Because His mercy encompasses everything |
| al-Raḥīm | Because that mercy is not passing but lasting |
| Mālik yawm al-dīn | Because He will deliver every right to its owner |

Verse 1 is the assertion, verses 2–3 the evidence. In its very first sūra the Qurʾān does itself what it asks of its reader: it states a ruling and gives its ground.

### The repetition itself

In rhetoric, repetition is not a fault but a means of reinforcement. But there is no mechanical repetition here either: the "al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm" of the basmala is the heading of the whole Qurʾān, whereas this one is a link in al-Fātiḥa's own internal sequence. The same two words stand in two different functions.

## 1/3 — مَٰلِكِ يَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ

*Māliki yawmi'd-dīn* — "Owner/Sovereign of the Day of Judgement."

### Two readings, two meanings

There is a **variant reading transmitted by mass transmission** in this verse, and both are sound:

- **مَالِك (Mālik)** — with a long alif: *owner, possessor.* Thus in the readings of ʿĀṣim and al-Kisāʾī.
- **مَلِك (Malik)** — without the alif: *sovereign, king.* Thus in the readings of Nāfiʿ, Ibn Kathīr and some others.

The difference is not trivial. **Mālik conveys ownership; Malik conveys authority.** A king rules his country but the country is not his property; an owner of property owns it but cannot rule over people. These are two distinct powers, and they are rarely gathered in one hand in this world.

Reading the two together says that on that day these two are gathered in one hand: owner of everything, and sole holder of judgement. Most variant readings work this way — one does not cancel the other; together the two widen the meaning.

### دِين — dīn

The root is *d-y-n*. Under this root lies a cluster of interconnected senses:

- **dayn** — debt
- **dāna** — he obeyed, he submitted
- **dāna li-fulān** — he gave an account to someone, he received his due
- **dīn** — "religion", "judgement", and "requital, penalty" all at once

The key point is this: **religion and debt come from the same root.** The Arabic language's grasp of religion is built on a relation of debt — something has been given, and its return will be paid. "Yawm al-dīn" therefore means "the day of religion", "the day of reckoning" and "the day on which the requital is given"; these are not three separate meanings but three faces of the same thing. English covers the two ends of this root with two unconnected words.

The Arab saying *kamā tadīnu tudān* (as you deal, so you will be dealt with) is also from this root. The idea is simple and universal: what is done comes back.

### Why the owner of "that day"?

God is the owner today as well. Why, then, that day in particular?

Because **today there are claims of ownership.** People hold title deeds, draw boundaries, say "mine". These claims serve a purpose, they found law, life goes on. On that day this layer collapses. The Qurʾān stages the moment directly: *"To whom belongs the kingdom today? To God, the One, the Overpowering"* (al-Muʾmin 40/16) — the one who asks the question and the one who answers it are the same, because no other claimant is left to answer.

The verse does not say that ownership *begins* on that day; it says that on that day it **becomes visible**.

### The end of the ordering

Reckoning comes after mercy. This order closes off the error at both extremes:

- Had only mercy been heard: responsibility would have dissolved, and the laziness of "He will forgive anyway" would have arisen.
- Had only reckoning been heard: despair would have arisen, and the relationship would have been reduced to fear.

Placed one after the other, the two produce a balance. In the classical tradition this is called the balance of fear and hope; it is likened to the two wings of a bird — lacking one, it cannot fly.

### What this says to the present

The transience of ownership is not an abstract sermon but a measurable fact: no one has ever carried anything he owned beyond himself. What we call the law of inheritance is the administrative regulation of that handover. The verse sets it on a moral footing — what you own is held in trust; and the holder of a trust is called to account for it.

Second point: the kinship of the word "dīn" with debt says something about what religiousness is. Religion is not a state of feeling a person lives through in his own inner world; it is **a relation of obligation**. Feeling may arise out of it, but it does not take its place.

## 1/4 — إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ

*Iyyāka naʿbudu wa iyyāka nastaʿīn* — "You alone we serve, and You alone we ask for help."

This is the hinge of the sūra.

### The shift in the direction of address

The first three verses speak of God **in the third person**: "praise is His", "He is al-Raḥmān", "He is the Master of the Day of Judgement." Someone is being described, but He is not in front of us.

In this verse, suddenly, it is **"You"**.

In Arabic rhetoric this is called *iltifāt* — the sudden change of direction in the address. English scarcely uses the device, but its effect is intelligible: it is like speaking about someone and then, in the middle of the sentence, turning to him and beginning to speak to him.

Its function here is very clear. The first three verses are a phase of **coming to know**: you learn who He is, His relation to you, His mercy, His authority. When the knowing is complete, speaking from a distance ends and one enters the presence. Knowledge turns into relationship.

The structure of the sūra breaks here too: the first half belongs to Him (praise, nurture, mercy, ownership), the second half to the servant (request, path, taking refuge). This verse stands in the middle and binds the two together. The place of which the ḥadīth qudsī says "this is between Me and My servant" is exactly here.

### "You alone", twice

The sentence is built unusually in two respects.

**First: "iyyāka" has been brought forward.** In Arabic it could have said *naʿbuduka* (we serve You); it does not, but pulls the object in front of the verb. In Arabic a fronted element conveys restriction — the sense becomes not "we serve You" but "we serve You **alone**". The sentence is not a report but a choice; it is built by closing every other door.

**Second: "iyyāka" is said twice.** "Iyyāka naʿbudu wa nastaʿīn" was grammatically possible and shorter. But the word is repeated. Because the two verbs are meant to be assigned separately:

> In service, You alone. And besides that — in asking for help, You alone.

This distinction is very real in practice. A person can direct his worship to God and expect his help from other doors; he can perform his prayers and get his business done through favouritism. The repetition closes this door: both fields are tied to the same place.

### عبد — service

The root is *ʿ-b-d*. The concrete sense is to submit, to yield. But one usage in Arabic lays the spirit of the word bare:

**"Ṭarīq muʿabbad"** — a road levelled by much walking, its roughness worn away, now easy to pass along.

From the same root. So *ʿibāda*, within the word's own logic, is: **to be levelled by being trodden over and over, to have one's roughness worn away, to become passable.** Service here does not mean humiliation but being worked. To pass over the same place again and again — the prayer's being repeated five times a day with the same movements does exactly this work. A road becomes a road by being walked over.

### "We do" rather than "I do"

Nowhere in the sūra is the singular used. Even someone praying alone says "we do".

This has several aspects. First, it says that worship is not a private matter but belongs to a community — even alone, a person speaks with the mouth of a congregation. Second, it carries a modest side: a person's own worship may not be worth accepting, but it is being offered from within the community. Third, it prevents the damage "I" would do in this sentence — "You alone I serve" would, while stressing the restriction, unwittingly push the person himself into the foreground.

### The order: service first, then request

Worship first, asking for help second. This order is not accidental.

First what is due is delivered, then a request is made. Before the asking begins, it is established that the relationship is not conditional on the asking — that is, not "I will serve You if You give". Service is unconditional; the request comes after it.

Moreover, asking for help is itself a form of service: it is the admission of one's own incapacity. The two sentences are therefore continuous with each other; they are not two separate acts.

## 1/5 — ٱهْدِنَا ٱلصِّرَٰطَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ

*Ihdinā'ṣ-ṣirāṭa'l-mustaqīm* — "Guide us to the straight path."

### The sūra's single request

There is only one request in al-Fātiḥa. Health is not asked for, provision is not asked for, nor victory, offspring, long life, protection. One thing alone is asked for: **the path.**

The meaning of this is: for a person on the right path, the rest either comes or is not needed. For a person on the wrong path, the arrival of the rest does not improve his situation but worsens it — speeding up on the wrong road does not shorten the road.

### هدى — guidance

The root is *h-d-y*. It carries two senses, and the two are akin: **to show the way** and **to give a gift.** *Hadiyya* (gift) comes from the same root.

This kinship is not idle. To show the way is a gift; to describe a direction to someone who cannot find his way is to give him something. The Qurʾān positions guidance not as a right but as a favour.

**The degrees of guidance.** The layers distinguished in the classical commentaries are these: *showing* the way (giving knowledge), *making one walk* on the way (giving will and strength), *holding one steady* on the way (making it continue). The verse asks for all three at once.

From here arises the famous question: **why does someone who is already a Muslim, already on the path, ask for guidance?** The answer is that guidance is not a once-and-for-all acquisition. Being on the path does not guarantee staying on it. A person may go astray at any moment; the request is therefore renewed at every moment. This is why it is repeated at least seventeen times a day in the five daily prayers — the request is not something taken and set aside, but a line kept permanently open.

### صِرَٰط — ṣirāṭ

Arabic has many words for a road, and the Qurʾān uses them with distinction:

- **sabīl** — a particular way of going, a route
- **ṭarīq** — a footpath, a trodden trace
- **ṣirāṭ** — a wide, open, main road; a busy route that anyone can pass along

The origin of the word is disputed; some lexicographers count it as having entered Arabic from outside and connect it with the Latin *strata* (a paved road — the English *street* comes from this). It is not certain, but it is clear that the word carries the associations of a paved, prepared, well-travelled highway.

Why was this word chosen? Because what is asked for is not a hidden footpath reserved for one person, but an open road anyone can walk. Religion is not a secret set apart for an elite.

**Its being singular.** The Qurʾān always uses "ṣirāṭ" in the singular. Against this, *subul*, meaning "ways", is plural. The distinction is established explicitly in one verse: *"This is My straight path, so follow it. Do not follow the other ways; they will separate you from His way"* (al-Anʿām 6/153). One road, many turnings.

### مُسْتَقِيم — mustaqīm

The root is *q-w-m*: to stand, to be erect, to straighten. From the same root come *qiyām* (standing up), *qawm* (a community that stands together), *maqām*, *istiqāma*.

The word says two things at once: **straightness** and **upright bearing.** That is, a path that has no crookedness in it and that also holds a person upright. The English word *upright* still carries this second sense — standing straight and being honest at the same time.

And there is this too: **the shortest distance between two points is a straight line.** The straight path is therefore also the shortest, the most economical path. Crookedness does not only lead you wrong; even when it gets you there, it tires you and lengthens the way. What the verse asks for is both rightness and efficiency.

## 1/6-7 — صِرَٰطَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ غَيْرِ ٱلْمَغْضُوبِ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا ٱلضَّآلِّينَ

*Ṣirāṭa'lladhīna anʿamta ʿalayhim, ghayri'l-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim wa la'ḍ-ḍāllīn*
"The path of those You have favoured; not of those upon whom wrath has fallen, nor of those who go astray."

### The description of the path is not abstract

The "straight path" asked for in the preceding verse is described here. But the description is not a definition; it is **a pointing**: "the path of those You have favoured." The path, that is, is defined by who has walked it.

This is a characteristic method of the Qurʾān: a lived example in place of an abstract principle. The right path is not a theory but a trace opened up within history. And the Qurʾān itself says whose trace it is:

> *"Whoever obeys God and the Messenger, they are with those whom God has favoured: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous."* (al-Nisāʾ 4/69)

The same formula — "those He has favoured" — occurs in two places. The Qurʾān interprets itself: four ranks; the prophets, those who have made truthfulness a disposition, those who have given their lives, those who do their work properly.

### The finest detail in the verse: the vanishing subject

Here is the most striking grammatical point in the whole sūra. Three groups are named, and **in all three the construction of the verb is different:**

| | Arabic construction | The owner of the verb |
|---|---|---|
| Those favoured | **أَنْعَمْتَ** — "You have favoured" | Explicitly God: *"You"* |
| Those upon whom wrath has fallen | **ٱلْمَغْضُوبِ عَلَيْهِمْ** — "those against whom wrath has been shown" | Passive. Who was wrathful is not said |
| Those who go astray | **ٱلضَّآلِّينَ** — "those who go astray" | Active. The verb is their own |

This is no accident. **The favour is attributed directly to God; the wrath is hidden away in a passive construction; the going astray is given as the human being's own act.** The same propriety appears elsewhere in the Qurʾān — Abraham (Ibrāhīm), describing his Lord, says *"and when I am ill, it is He who heals me"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/80); he attributes the illness to himself and the healing to God.

The principle that follows is this: good is directly from Him; evil is what a person falls into by his own choice.

### Two types of deviation

A path can be lost from two directions, and the verse separates them:

**Those upon whom wrath has fallen** — those who have the knowledge but not the practice. They know what is right, but abandon it knowingly. The defect here is not ignorance but will.

**Those who go astray** — those who have the intention but not the knowledge. They are searching, they are striving, but because they do not know the road they arrive at the wrong place. The defect here is not bad intention but lack of direction.

The difference between the two is two distinct kinds of illness: one is knowing the medicine and not taking it, the other is taking the wrong medicine. The right path stands between the two — knowledge and practice both, direction and effort both.

### Who are these two groups? — the disagreement

In most classical commentaries, on the basis of a report (transmitted from ʿAdī b. Ḥātim), "those upon whom wrath has fallen" is explained as the Jews and "those who go astray" as the Christians.

Care is needed here, and I am obliged to say two things at once:

**First:** this interpretation is widespread in the exegetical tradition and cannot be ignored. Historically it points to this: a community that was given a scripture but did not act on its knowledge, and a community that set out with love but lost its way in its creed. The Qurʾān makes both criticisms explicitly within the text.

**Second, and more important:** the verse **names no one; it enumerates attributes.** The text does not say "the Jews" or "the Christians"; it says "those upon whom wrath has fallen" and "those who go astray". A ruling built on an attribute covers everyone who bears that attribute — Muslims included. A Muslim who does not act on what he knows falls under the first description, and a Muslim who strives and turns aside to the wrong place falls under the second. Indeed the person reciting this prayer is a Muslim, and he is asking to be protected from these two states **for himself**; not in order to judge someone else.

Moreover, the Qurʾān itself blocks the making of a sweeping ruling: *"They are not all alike; among the People of the Book there is an upright community who recite God's verses in the watches of the night and prostrate"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/113).

I therefore transmit the classical interpretation as a historical instantiation; not as a permanent, ethnic ruling. A reading that does not make this distinction runs against the verse's own grammar — because the verse deliberately chose to use attributes rather than names.

### The negative description of the path

The right path is described in two ways: positively (whose trace it is) and negatively (which two extremes it keeps clear of). This is the soundest way of establishing boundaries — it is not enough to say what a thing is; one must also say what it is not.

The picture that emerges is the same as the measure the Qurʾān establishes explicitly elsewhere: *"Thus We have made you a middle community"* (al-Baqara 2/143). A position standing between two extremes. Excess, from whichever direction it comes, is a departure from the path.

### The sūra as a whole

In seven verses al-Fātiḥa does the following:

1. It lays down a ruling — praise is His.
2. It gives the ground — the one who nurtures, the merciful, the holder of the reckoning.
3. It stops describing from a distance and enters the presence — You alone.
4. It asks for one thing — the path.
5. It describes the path it asks for — a trodden trace, clear of two extremes.

It begins with praise and arrives at a request; begins with knowledge and moves to relationship, and from relationship to the search for direction. The rest of the Qurʾān is the answer to that last request — and indeed al-Baqara, which comes immediately after, opens thus: *"This is the Book; there is no doubt in it; a guidance for those who are mindful of God"* (al-Baqara 2/2). Al-Fātiḥa says "show us the path"; al-Baqara begins "here is the Book that will show it".

### Āmīn

It is not part of the sūra and is not counted as a verse. It means "accept", "let it be so". Saying it at the end of a prayer is the listener's declaration that he too joins in that prayer — the way of sharing in the imam's prayer in congregation.
