# 111. Sūrat al-Masad

Five verses. One of the shortest and most disquieting sūras in the Qurʾān.

What is out of the ordinary about the sūra lies not in its subject but in its **method**. The Qurʾān generally describes its opponents by their attributes: "the one who denies", "the one who turns away", "the one who grows arrogant". It gives no names. In this sūra, by contrast, a single person is named by his kunya (his by-name), together with his wife, and a definite ruling about his future is issued concerning him. All five verses are given over to this one person.

The disquiet the text leaves in a reader today must be acknowledged from the outset. It would not be honest to try to soften that disquiet; the sūra really is a harsh text. Below we shall take up both the historical ground of this harshness and the inner logic of the text itself as plainly as possible — without mounting a defence, but without skipping the context either.

### The names of the sūra

**Tabbat** — from its first word. **Al-Masad** — from its last word. It is also referred to by the names **al-Lahab** or **Abū Lahab**.

In the copies of the muṣḥaf "Tabbat" or "al-Masad" is generally written. There is a tendency towards avoiding the fixing of a person's name as the title of a sūra; I record this as an observation, not as a rule.

### The time of revelation

Meccan. In the lists of the order of revelation it is placed very early. The report of the occasion of revelation (below) ties the sūra to **the first days of the open call**; that is, to the moment at which the call came out of its secret phase and was proclaimed to Mecca.

This dating is decisive for the most disputed aspect of the sūra, the matter of "a definite ruling about the future"; we shall return to it under a separate heading.

### The report of the occasion of revelation

According to the report found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the event unfolded as follows:

The Prophet climbed the hill of al-Ṣafā and summoned Quraysh; he called out to the tribes one by one by name, and they gathered. He asked them: *"If I told you there was a body of cavalry behind this valley coming upon you, would you believe me?"* — *"Yes, we have known nothing from you but truth"*, they said. Upon this he said: *"I am a warner to you against a severe punishment before you."* Abū Lahab said: *"Was it for this that you gathered us? Ruin take you (tabban laka)!"* The sūra was revealed upon this.

**On the report.** This transmission is found in sound sources and comes by different routes; among the reports of occasions of revelation it is one of those counted relatively sound. There are differences at the level of detail (the expression used, the identity of those who gathered, the manner of the ascent to al-Ṣafā), but the core of the event is stable.

**The side of the report that throws direct light on the sūra** is this: the expression Abū Lahab used, *"tabban laka"*, is **from the same root** as the first word of the sūra. The sūra begins by returning to him the word he said. This is a point worked through in the exegetical tradition, and it is the most concrete evidence of the accord between the report and the text.

And one further detail is worth recording: in the report, the answer of Quraysh is *"we have not heard a lie from you"*. That is, at the first open moment of the call the other side openly concedes the trust it feels for the person; what it rejects is not the man's honesty but the content of what he says.

The basmala was treated in earlier sūras and is not taken up again here; the verses are numbered without counting it.

## Being named in the Qurʾān as a contemporary

This matter must be made clear before entering upon the verses, because the whole sūra rests on it. And here something commonly said must be corrected.

**The contemporaries named in the Qurʾān** — that is, people living in the Prophet's own period — are very few, but they are not one:

- The name **Muḥammad** occurs in four places (Āl ʿImrān 3/144; al-Aḥzāb 33/40; Muḥammad 47/2; al-Fatḥ 48/29). *Aḥmad* occurs in one place, as the glad tidings of Jesus (ʿĪsā) (al-Ṣaff 61/6).
- **Zayd** is named at al-Aḥzāb 33/37 — the only place where the name of a Companion occurs in the Qurʾān.
- **Abū Lahab** is named in this sūra by his kunya.

The correct statement is therefore this: **Abū Lahab is the only opponent named by name in the Qurʾān**; and **the devoting of an entire sūra to a single person is a case with no other example in the Qurʾān.**

To see how exceptional this is, it is enough to look at the Qurʾān's general method:

- *"Leave Me alone with the one I created…"* (al-Muddaththir 74/11 and what follows) — a long and detailed description; the identity is specified in the reports, but **the Qurʾān gives no name.**
- *"A great swearer, contemptible, a constant faultfinder, a carrier of tales…"* (al-Qalam 68/10-11) — description again, and again no name.
- *"He frowned and turned away, because the blind man came"* (ʿAbasa 80/1-2) — the event is recounted, the person is not named.
- The name of no one killed or killing at Badr or at Uḥud occurs. Not one of the harshest opponents among Quraysh is named.

The method the Qurʾān prefers is **to fix not the person but the attribute.** Once the attribute is fixed, the ruling becomes independent of time: whoever bears that attribute, the verse speaks of him. If a name is given, the ruling is locked to history.

The real question, then, is this: **Why was an exception made here?**

The answer lies in who Abū Lahab was. He was **the Prophet's own paternal uncle** — a son of ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, of the Banū Hāshim. And the moment at which the sūra is reported to have been revealed is exactly the moment at which the command *"And warn your nearest kin"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/214) was being carried out.

In tribal society kinship was not a feeling but **a security system.** The sole reason a person could not be touched was the clan standing behind him. The clan was obliged to protect its member; when protection was withdrawn, the person was in effect left outside the law. Abū Ṭālib's protecting his nephew — despite not believing — was this system in operation; the biographical sources attribute the call's ability to survive in the early years largely to this protection.

Abū Lahab reversed this system. The person who stood in the position expected to protect became the most vocal member of the opposing side. It is transmitted in the biographical sources that in the boycott applied to the Banū Hāshim he took the side not of his own clan but of the opposing side, and that at the fairs and in the pilgrimage season he would follow behind the Prophet and give him the lie. The degrees of soundness of these reports vary; the general picture is stable, its details are not.

That is, the sūra's giving a name has to do **not with the degree of the person's wickedness but with the special character of his position.** There were opponents in the same period who did more harm, and none of their names occurs.

This yields the most overlooked consequence of the sūra: **the text proclaims that no privilege is granted to the Prophet's own family.** The first ruling revealed about the uncle of a movement's founder is a ruling that condemns him, not one that protects him. The logic of "one of ours is protected" breaks precisely in the innermost circle.

And the counter-example is in the same family: another of the Prophet's uncles, Ḥamza, believed; yet another (Abū Ṭālib) did not believe but gave protection. Being an uncle neither saves nor condemns. What is decisive is not kinship but stance.

## 111/1 — تَبَّتْ يَدَآ أَبِى لَهَبٍ وَتَبَّ

*Tabbat yadā Abī Lahabin wa tabb*
"May the two hands of Abū Lahab wither; and wither they did."

### تَبَّ — the sense of the root

Root **t-b-b**. The dictionaries give these senses: **to suffer loss, to come to ruin, to be cut off, to diminish, to dry up and go, to perish.**

The most emphasised side of the word is that the loss is **not momentary but continuous**; in the lexicographical tradition *tabāb* is explained as "being in continuous loss". That is, not a one-time damage but a diminishing that flows on and cannot be halted.

No single English word carries it exactly. "May they wither" catches the side of the root that means drying up and becoming useless, and English builds curses in just this shape — "may his hands wither". Renderings such as "let it come to nothing", "let it be in vain", "may nothing come of his hand" are also used.

**Its other uses in the Qurʾān** are within the same frame, and both are in the context of a scheme or a plan:

- *"And the scheming of Pharaoh was nothing but ruin"* (Ghāfir 40/37).
- *"…they added nothing to them but ruin"* (Hūd 11/101) — of what the destroyed peoples worshipped.

In both, the word describes **an effort that comes to nothing.** Labour has been spent, and the result is zero or less. This sets the direction of the sūra's first word: the first thing said about Abū Lahab is not "he is wicked" but **"what he does will come to nothing"**.

### يَدَآ — "his two hands"

Why are the person's hands named rather than the person himself?

**The hand is the organ of action and of gain.** In Arabic *yad* is used figuratively for power, work, capacity, possession, favour. The Qurʾān uses this figure constantly:

- *"For what your hands have earned"* (al-Shūrā 42/30).
- *"That is for what your hands have sent ahead"* (al-Ḥajj 22/10).
- *"Do not cast yourselves into destruction with your own hands"* (al-Baqara 2/195).

In these usages "hand" represents the act itself — naming a part and meaning the whole (*majāz mursal*). "May his two hands wither" therefore means **"may everything he does come to nothing"**. The imprecation is directed not at the person's existence but at his **production**.

**Why the dual?** Not "his hand" but "his two hands". In Arabic the dual conveys completeness and comprehensiveness: not one of his hands but both, that is, in such a way that nothing he might do is left outside. The person's entire capacity for action is covered in a single expression.

Some exegetes say that by "the two hands" the person himself is directly meant; in terms of the resulting sense the difference is not great.

### وَتَبَّ — the second verb and the subtlety in the grammar

The same verb is repeated, this time not for the hands but for **the person himself**: *wa tabb* — "and he too withered / came to ruin".

In the classical grammatical analyses a difference of function is seen between these two verbs:

| Verb | Its function |
|---|---|
| *tabbat* (the first) | **Inshāʾ** — imprecation, wish: "may they wither" |
| *tabba* (the second) | **Ikhbār** — report, statement of fact: "they withered, it is done" |

That is, the sentence first frames a wish and then announces that the wish has come about. Imprecation and ruling in the same sentence, one after the other. This is a structure seen in the other places where the Arabic pattern of imprecation is carried into the Qurʾān: *"May man perish, how ungrateful he is!"* (ʿAbasa 80/17) — the form is imprecation, the content is a ruling.

That the second verb is in the **perfect** also conveys certainty. The Qurʾān tells of things that have not yet come about but are certain in the past tense (this method was seen in Sūrat al-Naṣr as well). Here the perfect says that the ruling is closed to debate.

A note on sound: the sentence begins with *tabbat* and ends with *tabb*. The first verse closes a circle within itself and, striking the same word twice, establishes the rhythm of the sūra.

### أَبِى لَهَبٍ — the kunya

Now we come to the most delicate piece of language-work in the sūra.

**What is a kunya?** In Arabic a *kunya* is a name of address built on the pattern "Abū …" (father of) or "Umm …" (mother of). It is generally built with the name of the person's eldest child (as with Abū Bakr), but it may also derive from a characteristic, a habit or a comparison. The kunya was more widely used than the given name in Arab society, and it was generally an expression of respect.

**Abū Lahab's given name.** In the biographical and biographical-dictionary sources it is given as **ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā b. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib**. Al-ʿUzzā was one of the three great idols around Mecca; it is named in the Qurʾān together with al-Lāt and Manāt (al-Najm 53/19-20). That is, the man's given name means, word for word, **"servant of al-ʿUzzā"**.

**Why did the Qurʾān use the kunya?** The grounds worked through in the classical commentaries:

**1. The given name declared servitude to an idol.** The Qurʾān does not register an idol's name as a human being's identity within a text that establishes the divine unity. The use of the kunya makes it possible to avoid pronouncing that name.

**2. He was better known by his kunya.** For the addressees no ambiguity remains as to who is meant.

**3. And the principal reason: the kunya already overlapped with the subject of the sūra.**

The word **لَهَب**, root **l-h-b**: **to blaze, to catch fire.** *Lahab* is the **smokeless, pure flame** part of a fire — the brightest and most burning place of a fire. From the same root *ilhāb* (setting alight), *lahīb* (flame).

That is, the kunya means, word for word, **"father of the flame"**. It is transmitted in the biographical sources that this kunya came from the redness and brightness of his face; this is a report, and it is not certain.

The upshot is this: a kunya the man bore throughout his life, and which was probably a source of pride — a name suggesting brightness, beauty or fieriness — turns into something else inside the sūra. We shall see in the third verse how this word comes back.

**A note on the variant readings.** A small difference among the imāms of the variant readings is transmitted in the pronunciation of the word in the kunya (*lahab* / *lahb*). It does not change the meaning.

## 111/2 — مَآ أَغْنَىٰ عَنْهُ مَالُهُۥ وَمَا كَسَبَ

*Mā aghnā ʿanhu māluhu wa mā kasab*
"Neither his wealth availed him, nor what he earned."

### Two different "mā"s

*Mā* occurs twice in the verse, and each time it does a different job.

**The first *mā*** — can be read in two ways, and both readings have been defended in the classical commentaries:

| Reading | Sentence |
|---|---|
| **Negation** (*nāfiya*) | "His wealth did not avail him." |
| **Rhetorical question of denial** (*istifhām inkārī*) | "What did his wealth avail him?" |

The second reading carries a stronger emphasis: to ask a question whose answer is known is sharper than to negate directly. The meaning comes out in the same place in the end.

**The second *mā*** — a relative pronoun: "the **thing** he earned".

### أَغْنَىٰ — "to suffice, to be of use"

Root **gh-n-y**: wealth, sufficiency, being in no need. From the same root *ghanī* (rich, in need of nothing — one of the names of God), *ghinā* (wealth), *istighnāʾ* (regarding oneself as self-sufficient).

The pattern *aghnā ʿan* carries a particular sense: **to take someone's place, to meet his need, to ward a danger off from him.** That is, not "to enrich" but "to be of use, to save".

This pattern is almost a standard formula in the Qurʾān, and it always says the same thing:

- *"As for those who disbelieve, neither their wealth nor their children will avail them anything against God"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/10).
- *"His wealth will not avail him when he falls headlong"* (al-Layl 92/11).
- *"His wealth and his children added nothing to him but loss"* (Nūḥ 71/21).

These verses set Sūrat al-Masad in a context: the sūra issues a ruling specific to a person, but the measure it uses is a general measure. **Beyond a certain point, wealth loses its function** — the Qurʾān is not inventing this for Abū Lahab; it already states it as a rule valid for everyone.

**The verb's being in the perfect.** It does not say "will not avail" but "did not avail". Two readings are possible: (a) a future event told in the perfect for the sake of certainty; (b) an actual reference to the past — his wealth has served him for nothing up to now either, except to hold him back from the right road.

### مَالُهُۥ — his wealth

Root **m-w-l**. *Māl* is what is owned. It is noted that in Arabic the word was earlier used especially of **a herd of animals**, and in the Bedouin context of camels; it then generalised and spread to wealth of every kind.

That Abū Lahab was among the men of means of Mecca is stated in the biographical sources. That the sūra names wealth in particular shows that it had a concrete counterpart for the addressees: what is meant is not an abstract idea of riches but a wealth everyone knew.

### وَمَا كَسَبَ — "and what he earned"

Root **k-s-b**: **to obtain by working, to draw to oneself, to gather.** The concrete core of the root is "to draw a thing towards oneself and take it".

The Qurʾān uses this root **both for material gain and for deeds**, and this duality is important:

> *"To its credit is what it has earned, and against it is what it has committed."* (al-Baqara 2/286)

Here *kasb* describes directly what a person does, that is, the record of his deeds. The same root is used both for money that goes into the pocket and for an act that is written into the record — a pairing registered in the language itself.

**What is "what he earned"?** The views of the exegetes:

| View | Ground |
|---|---|
| **The gain he obtained through trade** | *Māl* is wealth that comes by inheritance or accumulation, *kasab* what he earned by his own effort — the two together cover the whole of his wealth |
| **His children** | In Arabic offspring are counted as "a person's earning"; the Qurʾān constantly names wealth and children side by side (Āl ʿImrān 3/10; Nūḥ 71/21; al-Kahf 18/46) |
| **The rank, standing and influence he obtained** | The scope of *kasb* is not only material |
| **The works he did, his deeds** | The sense of "deed" that the root has in the Qurʾān |

These views do not exclude one another. On the contrary, the breadth of the word appears deliberate: *māl* covers what is concrete, and *mā kasaba* covers "whatever he obtained". The sentence thus draws up a closed account — **whatever was in his hand, none of it was of any use.**

And its link with the first verse is this: there the *two hands* had been made to come to nothing. Here the **produce** of those hands is made to come to nothing. The hand is the act, *kasb* is the result of the act; the sūra cancels first the act, then the produce.

## 111/3 — سَيَصْلَىٰ نَارًا ذَاتَ لَهَبٍ

*Sa-yaṣlā nāran dhāta lahab*
"He will enter a fire of flame."

### سَ — the particle of the future

Arabic has two particles that turn the imperfect verb into a future: **سَ (sa-)** and **سَوْفَ (sawfa)**. In the classical books of the language the first is indicated for the nearer future, the second for the more distant.

Here the short one has been chosen. And this single letter establishes the most disputed feature of the sūra: **the text issues a definite ruling regarding the future.** We shall take this up below under a separate heading.

### يَصْلَىٰ — to enter the fire

Root **ṣ-l-y**. Its concrete sense: **to enter a fire, to burn in a fire, to be cast into a fire.** But another derivative of the root goes somewhere entirely different: *iṣṭilāʾ* (اصطلاء) — **to draw near a fire in order to warm oneself.**

The Qurʾān uses this second sense. Moses (Mūsā), seeing a fire from afar in the desert, says to his family: *"…that I may bring you a brand from it, so that you may warm yourselves"* (al-Naml 27/7; similarly al-Qaṣaṣ 28/29). The verb there is *taṣṭalūn* — from the same root as *yaṣlā* in this verse.

The contrast emerges of itself: **the same root describes both drawing near a fire to be warmed and entering into a fire and burning.** Fire is for man both the continuance of life and a cause of destruction; the root carries this duality. What is meant here is not the fire approached but the fire **entered**.

### نَارًا ذَاتَ لَهَبٍ — "a fire possessed of flame"

**نَارًا** — indefinite: "a fire". In Arabic indefiniteness sometimes conveys not indeterminacy but **magnification and terror** (*tahwīl*): a fire that cannot be described, that cannot be compared with the fires that are known.

**ذَاتَ** — "possessor of", "having…". It forms an adjectival construct.

**لَهَبٍ** — and the word from the kunya comes back.

### The return of the kunya

This is the densest piece of language-work in the sūra, and it deserves to be looked at closely.

In the first verse the man was named **Abū Lahab**: *"father of the flame"*. In the third verse the place he will enter is **nāran dhāta lahab**: *"a fire possessed of flame"*.

The same word occurs two verses apart, in two different constructs. But **the direction of the construct has been reversed**:

| | Construct | To whom the flame belongs |
|---|---|---|
| **Verse 1** | *Abū Lahab* — father of the flame | The flame is attributed **to him**; he is in the position of owning the flame |
| **Verse 3** | *nāran dhāta lahab* — a fire possessed of flame | The flame is attributed **to the fire**; he is in the position of being inside that flame |

That is, the kunya presented him as though he were the owner of the flame; the verse places him inside something that has a flame. Ownership changes hands.

Beyond this there is also a simple but effective repetition: a name a man bore throughout his life comes back, word for word, in the description of his punishment. His name becomes the herald of his end.

I do not present an overlap of this kind as a "sign" or an "advance notice" — there is no such claim in the text. What can be said is this: **the sūra uses its addressee's own name as material**, and it does so in a way entirely intelligible within the Arabic culture of the kunya.

## 111/4 — وَٱمْرَأَتُهُۥ حَمَّالَةَ ٱلْحَطَبِ

*Wa'mraʾatuhu ḥammālata'l-ḥaṭab*
"And his wife too — that carrier of wood —"

### ٱمْرَأَتُهُۥ — his wife

*Imraʾa*, woman; with the annexed pronoun *imraʾatuhu*, "his woman/his wife".

In the biographical sources her name is given as **Umm Jamīl**, her given name as **Arwā bint Ḥarb**, and it is transmitted that she was the sister of Abū Sufyān. This information is not in the Qurʾān; it comes from the biographical and biographical-dictionary sources.

Even here the Qurʾān gives no name — the woman is named in relation to her husband. That is, the sūra's exception of "giving a name" is limited to a single person: Abū Lahab.

### The question in the grammar: accusative or nominative

There is a difference of variant reading in this verse that affects the meaning, and it must be recorded.

In the widespread text of the muṣḥaf (the reading of ʿĀṣim, the transmission of Ḥafṣ) *حَمَّالَةَ* is read in the **accusative**. In most of the other readings it is transmitted as being read in the **nominative** (*ḥammālatu*).

| Reading | Grammar | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominative** (*ḥammālatu*) | An adjective or predicate of *imraʾatuhu* | "And his wife, the carrier of wood, (will enter that fire too)" — it attaches to the verb of the previous verse |
| **Accusative** (*ḥammālata*) | **The accusative of dispraise** — a separate construction for censure | "…and his wife too. That carrier of wood!" — the expression is cut off from the sentence and becomes an independent censure |

The classical explanation of the accusative reading is this: in Arabic a word may be taken out of the sentence's normal grammar and put independently in the accusative purely **for the sake of censure or of praise**. In that case the expression ceases to be an adjective and turns into a brand. In English it can be given by punctuation and tone: *"and his wife too — that carrier of wood!"*

The meaning arrives at the same place with both in the end; the difference is in the tone. The accusative reading is more emphatic and sharper.

### حَمَّالَة — "one who carries and goes on carrying"

Root **ḥ-m-l**: to take up a load, to carry. From the same root *ḥaml* (a load, and also pregnancy), *ḥāmil* (one who carries), *ḥiml* (burden).

**The pattern: فَعَّالَة (faʿʿāla)** — the feminine of the intensive form. This pattern in Arabic conveys **continuity and occupation**: not one who does a thing once but **one who does it continuously, whose work this is.** The Turkish loanword *hamal*, a porter whose trade is carrying, comes from this same pattern.

That is, the expression is not "a woman who carried wood" but **"a woman who has made the carrying of wood her occupation"**. The act is given not as an event but as an identity.

### ٱلْحَطَب — wood

*Ḥaṭab*: **dry wood, brushwood** gathered for burning. Not a living tree; something cut, dried, ready to be fuel.

Another place where the word occurs in the Qurʾān throws direct light on this verse:

> *"As for those who have deviated from the right way, they have become **fuel** for Hell."* (al-Jinn 72/15)

And another expression of the same idea:

> *"…the fire whose fuel is people and stones."* (al-Baqara 2/24; similarly al-Taḥrīm 66/6)

Read together with these two verses, the fourth verse establishes a reversal: **the one who carries the wood becomes, in the end, wood herself.** The distance between the one who carries the fuel and the fuel closes.

### What does "carrying wood" mean? — the views

This expression is the most interpreted place in the sūra. The main views:

| View | Ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Carrying thorns/brushwood in the literal sense** | It is transmitted that she threw thorns and thorny brush onto the road the Prophet passed along, carrying and dumping them there | The view most widely transmitted in exegesis; it rests on a report, and the chains are of differing soundness |
| **2. A figure for tale-bearing (carrying words)** | It is noted that in Arabic the image of carrying wood / throwing wood on a fire is used of a person who carries words back and forth to set people against one another | Strong on the side of the language; the link between "making a fire bigger" and "stirring up strife" is established in Arabic |
| **3. That she will carry wood in Hell** | The punishment being of the same kind as the act in this world | It is among the explanations transmitted from the early generations; it has no explicit ground in the text |
| **4. A figure for the burden of sin** | The Qurʾān describes sin as a load that is carried: *"They bear their burdens on their backs"* (al-Anʿām 6/31) | In accord with the Qurʾān's own world of imagery |

**I adopt no preference among these views, nor is one needed.** Two of them — the first and the second — support each other in particular: both describe the act of "making another's road harder", one on the physical and one on the verbal plane.

I should like to add an observation, and I state that this is my own inference: the force of the fourth verse comes from the word's being **readable both concretely and figuratively**. The one who carries thorns carries wood, and so does the one who carries tales. Neither of them lights the fire herself — **someone else will light the fire; she only brings the material.** This is the most widespread form of evil: not the one who burns, but the one who carries.

### Shared responsibility

That the sūra names husband and wife together is also striking. The woman is named here not as an extension of her husband but **by her own act** — *ḥammāla* is an attribute belonging to her.

In the Qurʾān the naming of spouses together is generally in a positive context. Here a partnership in a negative direction is described: two people doing the same work together. This accords with the general frame the Qurʾān establishes regarding the relation of spouses — at al-Taḥrīm 66/10-11 examples are given both of one who was the wife of unbelievers and was saved (Pharaoh's wife) and of those who were the wives of prophets and were not saved. The bond neither saves nor condemns; what is decisive is the person's own stance.

## 111/5 — فِى جِيدِهَا حَبْلٌ مِّن مَّسَدٍۭ

*Fī jīdihā ḥablun min masad*
"On her neck a rope of twisted fibre."

### جِيد — jīd

Arabic has more than one word for the neck, and the difference between them carries the whole verse here:

| Word | Its use |
|---|---|
| **عُنُق (ʿunuq)** | Neck in the general sense; neutral |
| **رَقَبَة (raqaba)** | The nape side; used in the context of slavery (*fakku raqaba* — to free a neck from slavery) |
| **جِيد (jīd)** | **The beautiful, long, adorned neck** — the neck that jewellery is put on |

*Jīd* is the word preferred in Arabic poetry when a woman's beauty is being described; comparisons to the neck of a gazelle are built with this word. The word's field of association is **ornament, elegance, the necklace**.

And **it occurs only here in the Qurʾān.** A word occurring a single time, and in precisely this verse.

### The irony

Now the work the verse does becomes plain.

The sentence could have used a neutral word for *"on her neck"* (*ʿunuqihā*). It does not. It chooses the word used for **the neck on which jewels are hung** — and onto that neck it puts not a necklace but **a rope of twisted fibre**.

The whole force of the expression comes from this substitution. The same place, the same word, an entirely different object. The place that is adorned becomes the place that is bound.

It is transmitted in the biographical sources that Umm Jamīl had a valuable necklace and said she would spend it in the cause of enmity to the Prophet. This report makes the verse more concrete still, but caution is required regarding its soundness; the meaning of the verse does not depend on this report — the association of the word *jīd* is already sufficient of itself.

### حَبْل — rope

Root **ḥ-b-l**: cord, rope, bond.

In the Qurʾān *ḥabl* mostly occurs as **a bond to be held on to, a saving bond**:

- *"And hold fast, all together, to the rope of God"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/103).
- *"…unless with a rope from God and a rope from people"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/112) — here *ḥabl* is in the sense of protection, guarantee.
- *"We are nearer to him than his jugular vein"* (Qāf 50/16) — *ḥabl al-warīd*.

The word's typical sense in the Qurʾān is **the bond of rescue**: what is held on to, what pulls one out, what protects. In this verse the rope occurs with the same word but its function has been reversed: not a rope held in the hand but a rope **put around the neck**.

### مَسَد — masad

Root **m-s-d**: **to twist, to wind tightly, to strengthen fibres by winding them together.**

The explanations transmitted regarding the word:

| Explanation | Note |
|---|---|
| **A rope twisted from palm fibre** | The most widespread explanation |
| **Any strong twisted rope** | The reading most faithful to the original sense of the root; it specifies no material |
| **A chain of iron** | Transmitted from some of the early generations |
| **A rope of fire** | An explanation adapted to the context of Hell |

The core sense of the root is not the material but **the process**: being twisted. Twisting strengthens a rope; twisted fibre is many times stronger than straight fibre. That is, the word describes the rope's unbreakability.

The **palm fibre** explanation adds a social layer as well. Palm fibre was the cheapest, most ordinary material in Arabia, within everyone's reach; it was used for tethering animals and for tying loads. The wife of one of Mecca's wealthy families is described with the least valuable material of the period. An image that is the exact opposite of wealth.

**Indefiniteness.** *Masad* comes indefinite. Here too the indefiniteness can be read in the direction of *tahwīl* (making terrible): a bond whose kind is not fully known, not described.

### The sound texture of the sūra

The closings of the five verses: **tabb — kasab — lahab — ḥaṭab — masad.**

All end on short, closed syllables; four with the same *-ab* sound, the fifth with an *-ad* very close to it. The sounds *b* and *t* recur throughout the sūra. The result is a hard, striking rhythm — read aloud, it moves fast, clipped, and in the same tone all the way to the end.

This accords with the content; but I record it not as a claim of miracle but as an example of the sound character of the short Meccan sūras.

## The most disputed side of the sūra: a definite ruling about the future

This sūra does something rarely seen in the Qurʾān: **it issues a definite ruling about the hereafter concerning a living, named person.** *"Sa-yaṣlā nāran"* — "he will enter a fire". This sentence is the same as saying that this person will die without believing.

The facts are these:

- It is transmitted that the sūra was revealed at the beginning of the open call. This corresponds to around 613 CE.
- It is transmitted in the biographical sources that Abū Lahab died immediately after the battle of Badr (year 2 of the Hijra / 624 CE).
- That is, between the revelation of the sūra and his death there are **about ten years**.
- Throughout these ten years Abū Lahab lived in Mecca, the Qurʾān was recited, the sūra was memorised and spread.
- There is no record in the sources of his having made any attempt to falsify this ruling.

**The interpretation drawn from this** is as follows: the sūra put forward, against the most active member of the opposition, a ruling that **could easily have been falsified**. For Abū Lahab to say "I believe" — even insincerely — would to all appearances have refuted the ruling of the text. And to do this he needed neither to pay a price nor to fight a war; a single sentence would have sufficed. He did not do it.

In transmitting this interpretation three caveats must be entered; otherwise it appears a stronger proof than it is:

**One.** The ruling is a ruling about the hereafter, and a ruling about the hereafter **concerns the heart.** Abū Lahab's saying "I believe" with his tongue would not technically have invalidated the ruling — because what determines entering the fire is not speech but belief. This is therefore, in terms of logic, **not a clean test.** This must be recorded openly.

**Two.** Against this, the following is also true: the other side used far weaker and more indirect arguments against the call; the Qurʾān records them ("he is a poet", "he is a sorcerer", "the tales of the ancients"). That he did not use this direct means that lay to hand is striking. This is a point at the level of **observation**, not of proof.

**Three.** The length of the interval between the revelation of the sūra and his death rests on a dating that rests on a report. The date of revelation is not certain; a reading that argues for a later revelation of the sūra shortens this interval. The "ten years" here is an estimate, not a fixed datum.

**The exceptional character of this sūra within the Qurʾān must also be recorded.** As a rule the Qurʾān does not issue definite rulings about the hereafter concerning individuals. And in Islamic legal method the established maxim is this: **of no one, apart from those specified by name in the Qurʾān or by a sound transmission, is it said that he "is definitely of Paradise" or "definitely of Hell".** The very existence of this maxim shows how rare rulings of this kind are.

The reader must therefore hold two things together: this sūra issues a ruling; and this ruling is not a method to be imitated but a single exception whose source is determinate. For people to frame on their own authority a ruling that the text issues about others is exactly what this sūra **does not** do.

## The moral question: a person's being named and cursed

This is the side of the sūra that leaves the modern reader with the most questions. The question is itself legitimate and should not be brushed aside.

Let us put the matter as plainly as possible: **a scripture takes as its target a named human being and his wife, imprecates against them, and announces their end.** This runs counter to most of today's moral intuitions — both to the principle "criticise the idea, not the person" and to the principle "no ruling can be issued about anyone's end".

I can say the following:

**1. Context does not remove the ruling, but it changes its meaning.**

The sūra was not revealed in a void. In the background are these: the first open day of the call; a relative under an obligation of protection reversing that obligation; and, according to the report, the answer given to a sentence of warning being an imprecation.

In tribal society the position of the paternal uncle was far weightier than today's notion of a "relative". This was not an emotional closeness but a relation of **legal responsibility**. When protection was withdrawn, a person was left legally defenceless. What Abū Lahab did was, within this system, not a "difference of opinion" but **making a target of the person he was obliged to protect**.

**2. The sūra's giving a name does the repaying at the same level.**

Abū Lahab opposed openly, publicly, giving a name — on the hill of al-Ṣafā, before the people. And the sūra answered on the same plane: openly, publicly, giving a name. This is a symmetry. The other opponents whom the Qurʾān does not name had not personalised their opposition to this degree.

**3. The sūra frames no ruling about a lineage, a tribe or a group.**

This is an overlooked and important point. Abū Lahab is **of the Prophet's own clan** — the Banū Hāshim. That is, the sūra's target is not an "other" group but the inside of the family. Had the text framed a ruling on the basis of a belonging, it would have refuted itself.

The consequence of this is that the sūra rejects the principle "one of ours is protected". And it does so in the most costly place, in the founder's own family.

**4. The Qurʾān's own measure is the attribute.**

In the same family there is an uncle who believed (Ḥamza) and an uncle who did not believe yet gave protection (Abū Ṭālib). The sūra speaks of neither of them. The variable is not kinship but stance.

**5. But finally: the harshness of the text is in the text itself.**

To soften this would not be honest. The sūra is a harsh text, and it was intended to leave a harsh effect on its first addressees as well. The source of this harshness is not the style of the text but the situation it answers — but in the end the text is harsh. A reader's feeling disquiet in the face of this harshness does not mean that he has misunderstood the text.

What can be said is this: **the ruling this sūra issues belongs to this sūra.** A ruling that a text issues about a particular person does not give those who read that text the authority to issue rulings about others. On the contrary, how rare rulings of this kind are shows that they have not been left in human hands.

## The sūra as a whole

### A summary of the structure

| Verse | Content | What is cancelled |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | May his two hands wither, and wither they did | **The act** — everything he might do |
| **2** | His wealth and what he earned did not avail | **The produce** — whatever was in his hand |
| **3** | He will enter a fire of flame | **The end** — where he will go |
| **4** | His wife, the carrier of wood | **The partner** — the one beside him |
| **5** | On her neck a twisted rope | **The status** — the place that is adorned |

The sūra takes up in turn every layer surrounding the person: what he did, what he owned, where he will go, the person beside him, his appearance. A closed account in five verses.

### Its neighbours in the muṣḥaf

The sūra stands between al-Naṣr (110) and al-Ikhlāṣ (112).

**Al-Naṣr** tells of people entering in throngs — a participation in a mass.
**Al-Masad** tells of a single person remaining outside.
**Al-Ikhlāṣ** describes what it is that is entered.

This sequence is the order of arrangement, not the order of revelation. Even so, the picture that emerges is a series of an acceptance, a refusal and a description. And what is striking is this: immediately after the sūra that tells of crowds entering, a single person who does not enter is told of. **Number puts no one inside of itself** — and no one is counted as inside because a crowd went in.

### The Qurʾān's image of "effort that comes to nothing"

The sūra's first word is *tabb*, its last word *masad*. The first describes coming to nothing, the last being bound. In between there are wealth, gain, fire, wood.

This is akin to a series of images the Qurʾān often uses: the labour spent remaining without result. *"And We shall turn to the works they did and make them scattered dust"* (al-Furqān 25/23). *"Their deeds are like ashes which the wind scatters violently on a stormy day"* (Ibrāhīm 14/18).

The shared idea is this: **effort is not a value in itself.** A person may work hard, earn much, devote his life to a goal — and all of it, if the direction is wrong, comes in the end to zero. Sūrat al-Masad is the densest and most personalised example of this general principle.

## What this says to the present

**One. Belonging provides no protection.**

This is the most direct ruling of the sūra, and it requires no religious context. Which family, which circle, which institution a person belongs to does not take the place of his own stance. Abū Lahab was the uncle of the movement's founder — the closest position possible. The sūra takes no account of that position at all.

The reverse holds just as much: no one is condemned either because of where he was born, the name he bears or the circle he entered. Within the same family there are opposite ends.

**Two. The question of "what is of use".**

The verb of the second verse — *aghnā ʿan*, "to be of use, to save" — produces a practical question: **have we ever tested in which situations the things in our hands are of use?**

The situations in which wealth, position and standing are of use are known and real; no one denies this. What the sūra says is that **there is also a field in which they are of no use** — and a person mostly passes his life without ever thinking about that field. That the Qurʾān uses this formula again and again (Āl ʿImrān 3/10; al-Layl 92/11; Nūḥ 71/21) is no coincidence; this is the thing most easily forgotten.

**Three. Carrying wood: not the one who lights the fire but the one who brings the material.**

This is the most transferable side of the fourth verse. The *ḥammāla* pattern describes not a one-time act but **a work that has become continuous**.

The interpretation as tale-bearing stands very close to the present at this point. What burns a community is most often not great and visible evils; it is small words carried continuously. The person who carries words generally does not count himself as doing evil — he is only passing something on, only recounting it, and thinks "it was known anyway". The one who carries wood does not light the fire either.

The present-day counterpart of this measure has become more visible as the cost of carrying a word has fallen to almost nothing. Passing on a piece of information now requires neither walking a road nor spending effort. As the cost falls the quantity carried rises; as the quantity carried rises the sense of responsibility disperses — because everyone is only telling one person.

**Four. The two sides of partnership.**

That the sūra names husband and wife together shows that the closest relationship can be both a carrier and a driver. Your closest supporting you does not make you right — it only shows that you are not alone. And not being alone is the strongest illusion of confirmation: when two people think the same thing, each counts himself confirmed.

**Five. The rope on her neck.**

The image of the fifth verse says something plain about the symbols of status: **the place we adorn can be the place we are bound.** The necklace and the rope go around the same neck.

This is not a ruling against ornament or against property. What it says is finer: everything that has become a thing that defines a person — wealth, title, circle, image — is at the same time the thing that binds him. What you cannot leave is not what you own.

**Six. A name turning into a fate.**

Abū Lahab is named by a kunya he probably bore with pride, and that kunya comes back three verses later in the description of his punishment. This is a play on language, but behind it lies a plain observation: **the image a person chooses for himself may at some point become the material of the ruling about him.**

The identities people construct for themselves today — profile, title, brand, "personal story" — have the same structure. An identity does not protect the one who bears it; it only makes him definable. And being definable works in both directions.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

- **Abū Lahab's given name** (ʿAbd al-ʿUzzā), **his wife's name** (Umm Jamīl / Arwā bint Ḥarb), the **details of kinship** and the **time of his death** come from the biographical sources; the Qurʾān gives none of them. This distinction was stated everywhere in the text.
- The report of the occasion of revelation (the hill of al-Ṣafā) is found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim; it was recorded that there are differences of detail.
- Details such as Abū Lahab's taking the opposing side in the boycott and his following the Prophet at the fairs to give him the lie are reports from the biographical sources; their degrees of soundness vary, and they were given with the qualification "it is transmitted".
- The expression **"the only contemporary named in the Qurʾān"** was corrected: Muḥammad, Aḥmad and Zayd are also named. The correct statement is that Abū Lahab is **the only opponent named by name**, and that the devoting of an entire sūra to a single person has no other example.
- The information that the kunya came from the redness of his face is a report; it is not certain.
- The four views regarding the expression *Ḥammālata'l-ḥaṭab* / "carrying wood" were given in a table; no preference is adopted.
- The report about Umm Jamīl's necklace was transmitted, but it was stated that the meaning of the verse does not depend on this report.
- In transmitting **the "open challenge" interpretation** three reservations were separately written out; the most important is that a faith uttered with the tongue would not technically change the ruling about the hereafter — that is, that this cannot be counted as a logically clean test.
- The "about ten years" between the revelation of the sūra and Abū Lahab's death is an estimate; the date of revelation rests on a report and is not certain.
- The differences of variant reading (*lahab/lahb*, *ḥammālata/ḥammālatu*) were given as they are transmitted; they were not attributed to the imāms individually, and only the reading of the widespread text of the muṣḥaf was specified.
