# 49. Sūrat al-Ḥujurāt

Eighteen verses. Medinan. It takes its name from the word **ٱلْحُجُرَٰت** (chambers) in the fourth verse.

There is no other sūra in the Qurʾān that treats the order of conduct within a community as a single subject from beginning to end. Al-Ḥujurāt is a short text — eighteen verses — but in the density of the commands it carries it is one of the most tightly packed passages in the Qurʾān. In eighteen verses there are roughly twelve distinct notes on conduct: not to go ahead, not to raise the voice, not to call from behind, to verify a report, to set matters right between people, to stop the one who attacks, not to mock, not to find fault, not to fix nicknames, to avoid conjecture, not to pry in secret, not to speak behind another's back.

And not one of these notes is a ruling about worship. Not one of them concerns prayer, fasting, pilgrimage or alms. All of them concern **man's relation with man**.

This is the first thing that can be said about the sūra itself, and it shows in the plainest form a fact recorded in many places in this commentary: the Qurʾān sets up religiousness not as a list of acts of worship but as an **order of conduct**. Al-Ḥujurāt is the most compact text of that order.

## The name of the sūra — al-ḥujurāt

**حُجُرَات — the plural of حُجْرَة (*ḥujra*)**: chambers.

**Root: ح-ج-ر.** This root was treated in detail in the section on al-Fajr; the summary recorded there was this: the root idea is not stone but **to hinder, to hold back, to enclose, to draw a boundary**. *Ḥajar* (stone) belongs to this family because a stone is what sets a boundary and blocks a passage. *Ḥijr* — what is forbidden, made inviolable. *Ḥajr* — the restriction of a person's power to dispose of his property. *Ḥijr* — the lap: the area the arms enclose. *Ḥujra* — a chamber: a place separated off by a wall, enclosed.

The Turkish word *hücre* — meaning both a small room and the smallest enclosed unit of a living thing — is a borrowing from this root. English covers the same two senses with the unrelated word *cell*.

**The name of the sūra, then, is by its root a word about boundaries.** And the whole subject of the sūra is boundaries: the boundary of the voice, of speech, of curiosity, of conflict, of a claim.

It is hard to count this link a coincidence; but it must also be remembered that the sūra's name was given afterwards, from the word in the fourth verse. The name, that is, is not a claim of the text but a choice of the tradition. I can say the choice was apt; I record this as **my own reading**.

### The correcting of a confusion of roots

There is a mistake often made in the language of exegesis and preaching, and it needs to be corrected here plainly: **حجر and هجر are distinct roots.**

| Root | First letter | Sense | Derivatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ح-ج-ر** | *ḥāʾ* (ح) | To hinder, to enclose, to draw a boundary | *ḥujra*, *ḥajar* (stone), *ḥijr* (forbidden; the lap), *ḥajr* (restriction), *al-Ḥijr* |
| **ه-ج-ر** | *hāʾ* (ه) | To leave, to depart, to abandon | *hijra*, *muhājir*, *hajr* (abandonment), *mahjūr* (abandoned) |

The word *muhājir* is from the root **ه-ج-ر**, whereas *ḥujra* is from **ح-ج-ر**. To attach the two words to the same root is a common error.

**But a nearness of sense between the two roots can also be seen:** the one encircles a thing and separates it off, the other departs and goes out from a thing. At the centre of both there is an idea of **separation**. I record this not as a claim about derivation but only as an observation about sense; in Arabic it is often the case that roots close in sound carry close senses, but this does not hold in every instance.

## Its being Medinan

There is no disagreement that the sūra was revealed in the Medinan period, and the text itself supports this in every line:

- The Prophet is no longer only one who conveys a message; he is the head of a community to whom delegations come, at whose door people wait, who holds an assembly (1-5).
- There is an administrative structure in which a report about a tribe can arrive and be acted upon (6).
- A community has grown large enough for two groups of believers to come to armed conflict (9).
- There is a mixed population in which different tribes, different pasts, nicknames and habits of mockery all stand together (11-13).
- The period is one in which the surrounding bedouin communities have begun to join the new order (14-18).

This last item suggests that the sūra belongs to the **late Medinan** period: the joining of the surrounding tribes in a body is a consequence of the Medinan order having settled in the region. In the lists of the order of revelation the sūra is generally placed among the last few dozen. I give no definite year; I record this as an estimate of period, not as a dating.

## The structure of the sūra

The sūra consists of five blocks, and the transitions between the blocks are marked by the address *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū* (O you who believe):

| Verses | Subject | The sign that opens the block | Key word |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1-5** | Conduct towards God and the Prophet: not going ahead, not raising the voice, waiting at the door | *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū* (1, 2) | *ṣawt* (voice), *ḥujurāt* (chambers) |
| **6-8** | The report and its verification: whom it came from, what is to be done, its consequence | *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū* (6) | *nabaʾ*, *tabayyun* |
| **9-10** | Conflict and peace: two groups, the attacking side, brotherhood | *wa in* (9) — a conditional sentence | *iṣlāḥ*, *ikhwa* |
| **11-13** | Conduct within the community: mockery, fault-finding, nicknames, conjecture, prying, backbiting; and the proclaiming of the measure | *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū* (11, 12) → *yā ayyuha'n-nās* (13) | *fusūq*, *taqwā* |
| **14-18** | The separating of faith from islām: a claim and its correction | *qālati'l-aʿrāb* (14) — a turn to narrative | *īmān*, *islām*, *ṣidq* |

The boundaries of the five blocks are sharp, but there is an unseen link between them. I take that link up under a heading of its own, because the real idea of the sūra stands there.

## The sūra is a constitution of conduct: a circle narrowing from outside inward

The commands of al-Ḥujurāt are not set out at random. The order is built according to **whom** the one addressed stands in relation with, and in each block the other party of the relation comes one step nearer.

| Ring | Verses | Who stands opposite the one addressed | What is forbidden / commanded | Distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 1-5 | **God and the Prophet** — authority | Going ahead, raising the voice, forcing the door; wait | Farthest: above |
| **2** | 6-8 | **A report** — knowledge coming from outside | Verify; separate the source from the content | Far: outside |
| **3** | 9-10 | **Groups in conflict** — blocs within the community | Step in, set things right, stop the attacker, then set things right again | Near: the community |
| **4** | 11-12 | **The person beside you** — the individual | Do not mock, find fault, fix nicknames, conjecture, pry, speak behind his back | Nearer: the neighbour |
| **5** | 13-18 | **Your own claim to identity** — the person himself | Do not build superiority on your lineage; do not take saying "I believe" for a right | Nearest: inside |

**The circle narrows from outside inward.** The sūra begins with the authority above, continues with the report coming from outside, descends to the blocs of the community, comes to the single person beside you, and finally arrives at the relation the one addressed has **with himself** — with his claim about himself.

This ordering carries an idea of arrangement, and the logic of the arrangement is this: **when the outer rings break, there is visible damage; when the innermost ring breaks, nothing is visible at all.** If you raise your voice, it is heard. If you act on a false report, it comes out. When two groups fight, everyone sees. But your inward contempt for someone is not visible; the conjecture you form about him is not visible; and what lies beneath the sentence "I have believed, after all" is not visible at all.

For this reason the sūra **puts the least visible thing last** and stays there: the whole of the last five verses takes as its subject a difference that cannot be told from outside — the difference between what is said and what is in the heart.

**And there is a counter-movement:** while the circle narrows in subject, it widens in address. The one addressed in the first four rings is *alladhīna āmanū* — a particular community. At the door of the fifth ring the address opens out **to all people**: *yā ayyuha'n-nās* (13). At the very moment the text descends furthest inward, that is, it calls the widest audience.

I record this as my own reading, but the two facts it rests on stand in the text: the change of address in the thirteenth verse, and the steady drawing nearer of the addressees of the blocks.

### The limit of the comparison with a constitution

By "a constitution of conduct" I mean this: the sūra regulates not particular events but **kinds of event**, and it gives its regulations within a hierarchy. That is what a constitution does — it sets up the frame, not the detail.

But the comparison must not be pushed too far. The sūra prescribes no penalty, appoints no authority to apply it, does not define the consequence of a breach in legal terms. The command in the ninth verse to "fight the side that attacks" looks like the single exception to this, and a discussion has been built upon it in classical jurisprudence; I shall return to that in its place.

The sūra, then, is not a text of law but a text of **measure**. And it states the measure openly in its last verse: *wa'llāhu baṣīrun bimā taʿmalūn* — "God sees what you do."

## The basmala

The word-by-word analysis of the basmala was made in the section on al-Fātiḥa; I do not repeat it. The numbering of the verses is counted without the basmala.

One point particular to this sūra can be recorded: the first command of a text that opens with the names al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm is a command **to hold back**. Mercy, in the sense at the root of the word (*raḥim* — the womb), is an act that encloses and protects; and the first thing the sūra does is to draw a boundary for the one addressed. This link between boundary and protection is already present in the root of the sūra's name (ح-ج-ر): what encircles both hinders and protects.

## 49/1 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا تُقَدِّمُوا۟ بَيْنَ يَدَىِ ٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū lā tuqaddimū bayna yadayi'llāhi wa rasūlih, wa'ttaqu'llāh, inna'llāha samīʿun ʿalīm*

"O you who believe! Do not go ahead of God and His Messenger. Guard yourselves against God. God is Hearing, Knowing."

### يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ — the choice of address

The sūra opens with a call, and the one called is **those who believe**.

At first sight this looks ordinary — it is a common address in the Medinan sūras. But considered together with the subject of the sūra it says something: **the conduct corrected in this sūra is the conduct of those who believe.**

The sūra is not describing an opposing side. Those who mock, who find fault, who fix nicknames, who conjecture and pry, who speak behind another's back — all of them are inside the address. In the eleventh verse it will be said *"how evil is the name of transgression after faith"*; the possibility of transgression, that is, is set before those who believe as well.

This settles from the outset how the text is to be read. To take up the text and use it to describe other people is to reverse the direction of the address. The same measure was recorded in the section on al-Humaza, and what was said there holds here all the more: **the text is an instrument of diagnosis, and the diagnosis is made first upon oneself.**

### لَا تُقَدِّمُوا۟ — "do not go ahead"

**Root: ق-د-م.** Its root sense is **foot** (*qadam*) and from there **to go ahead, precedence**. This root was treated at al-Mujādala 58/12 on the occasion of *fa-qaddimū*.

**Form: Form II** — *qaddama*: to put forward, to send ahead.

And here comes the most striking point of the word order.

### The omitted object: "what" are you not to put forward?

*Qaddama* is a transitive verb; it asks for an object. "He put something forward." But in the verse the object is **not said.**

The verse does not say:
- "Do not put your word forward"
- "Do not put your opinion forward"
- "Do not put your decision forward"
- "Do not put yourselves forward"

It says only: **do not put forward.**

In Arabic the deliberate dropping of a verb's object is called *ḥadhf*, and the rule the lexicographers record is this: **the omission of the object states generality.** The verb, that is, comes to cover every object appropriate to it. Because it is not said, it is not limited.

The consequence of this here is that the prohibition is placed not on **a kind of behaviour** but on a **way of positioning oneself**. Do not put your word forward, nor your opinion, nor your decision, nor the thing you did in your haste, nor the ruling you framed before a ruling had come. The text gives no list, because a list always falls short.

The classical exegetes have proposed different supplied objects to fill this gap; all of them are reasonable and none excludes the others:

| The object supplied | Sense |
|---|---|
| *Lā tuqaddimū **qawlan*** | Do not go ahead in speaking |
| *Lā tuqaddimū **amran*** | Do not carry out a matter before the ruling comes |
| *Lā tuqaddimū **anfusakum*** | Do not put yourselves forward |
| No object — the verb is taken as intransitive | Do not go ahead at all, do not stand in front |

**The last line is a separate reading**, and some of the lexicographers take the verb here as intransitive: in the sense of *taqaddama* (he went ahead). Then the sentence becomes "do not go ahead" and no object is looked for.

I impose no preference. **But I record this:** whichever supplied object is taken, the common name of the thing forbidden is **going ahead**; and the leaving of the verb without an object makes it hard to narrow the scope of the prohibition. The text is built so as not to allow itself to be reduced to a single item.

### بَيْنَ يَدَىْ — "between the two hands"

This idiom was treated at al-Mujādala 58/12; what was recorded there was this: word for word, "between the two hands"; as an idiom, **"before, ahead of"**.

Here the **concrete picture** in the idiom needs to be opened out, because the whole idea of the sūra stands in that picture.

When a person stands facing you, his hands are at the two sides of his body. The space that remains between his two hands is exactly **his front**. To enter that space is to come between that person and what he is looking at.

The idiom, then, does not set up an abstract idea of "priority" but **a physical scene**: standing in front of someone. Blocking his body. Speaking where he was going to speak. Stepping first where he was going to walk.

Other uses of this idiom in Arabic carry the same picture:
- *"It is He who sends the winds as bearers of good news **before** His mercy"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/57) — the wind goes ahead of the rain.
- *"Confirming what was **before** it"* (al-Baqara 2/97 and the like) — *muṣaddiqan limā bayna yadayh*: what is ahead in time.

**And that this idiom was chosen says something.** Arabic has shorter ways of saying "before" — *qabla*, *amāma*. The text does not use them; it uses an idiom belonging to the body. Going ahead is pictured not as an abstract breach of order but as **standing in front of someone**.

I record this as my own reading, but the word structure of the idiom is plain to see.

### "Of God and His Messenger" — one verb, two names

There is a subtlety in the word order: the prohibition is built with a single verb (*lā tuqaddimū*) and two names follow it: *God* and *His Messenger*.

In the Qurʾān it is common for God and the Messenger to stand side by side in the same sentence (as in *aṭīʿu'llāha wa aṭīʿu'r-rasūl*). But what is striking here is this: **what is forbidden to go ahead of is in practice the position of the Prophet**, since "standing in front of" God can only be conceived figuratively. The sentence, that is, puts going ahead of the Prophet into the same sentence as **going ahead of God**.

And even so the two are attached to one verb, not counted separately. This is a structure seen elsewhere in this commentary too: the source of obedience is not divided in two.

### وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ — the backbone of the sūra

**Root: و-ق-ي.** Its concrete sense is **to protect, to shield**. This root was treated at al-Baqara 2/2 and al-Shams 91/8; the measure recorded there was this: **taqwā is not fear; there is no fear in the root, there is protection. The one who fears runs; the one who protects himself holds up a shield.**

**And this word is the backbone of the sūra.** It occurs in five places, and each time it is at the joint of a block:

| Place | Expression | The joint it stands at |
|---|---|---|
| **49/1** | *wa'ttaqu'llāh* | The opening of the first block |
| **49/3** | *imtaḥana'llāhu qulūbahum li't-taqwā* | Inside the first block: the quality of those who lower their voices |
| **49/10** | *wa'ttaqu'llāha laʿallakum turḥamūn* | The close of the verse of brotherhood |
| **49/12** | *wa'ttaqu'llāha inna'llāha tawwābun raḥīm* | The close of the verse of backbiting |
| **49/13** | *inna akramakum ʿinda'llāhi **atqākum*** | The measure-verse of the sūra |

Four of the five occurrences are a command (*ittaqū* / *taqwā*), the last is a **measure** (*atqā* — the one most protected). The command repeated throughout the sūra, that is, turns in the thirteenth verse into a unit of value.

I record this as my own reading: **the whole conduct of al-Ḥujurāt begins with taqwā and is measured by taqwā.** All the prohibitions in between — not raising the voice, not mocking, not conjecturing — are forms of the distance a person places between himself and a harm. The idea of "putting something in between" at the root of taqwā is the common structure of all the sūra's commands.

### إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ سَمِيعٌ عَلِيمٌ — the first verse-ending

**سَمِيع (*samīʿ*)** — the Hearing. **عَلِيم (*ʿalīm*)** — the Knowing.

**And the placing of these two names here is a preparation.** In the second verse the matter of the voice will be opened: *lā tarfaʿū aṣwātakum*. Just before it begins to speak about voice, the sūra names **the Hearing**.

The division of work between the two names is also striking:
- *Samīʿ* — meets what comes out: the word spoken, the voice raised.
- *ʿAlīm* — meets what does not come out: the intention, the inward reckoning.

This pairing will be kept throughout the sūra. The eighteenth verse will close with *baṣīr* (the Seeing); the sūra, that is, opens with **hearing** and is sealed with **seeing**. I shall take this link up in the section on the sūra as a whole.

### Context — why is this the sūra's first command?

If we consider that the sūra is a text about conduct, it may be asked why the first command is "do not go ahead". Not mocking, not backbiting, verifying a report — these are more concrete and more common matters.

The answer is in the word order itself: **going ahead is the common structure of all the faults in the sūra.**

- To act on a report without verifying it: to put one's own opinion ahead of the knowledge (6).
- To mock: to put one's own measure ahead of the knowledge of who is better (11).
- To conjecture: to put one's own guess ahead of the fact (12).
- To claim superiority by lineage: to put one's own measure ahead of God's measure (13).
- To say "we have believed": to put one's own declaration ahead of the heart's actual state (14).

**In all five of the five blocks there is the same act: putting a thing ahead of where it belongs.**

I record this as my own reading. But the word order supports it: the leaving of the first command without an object suggests precisely that it is a kind of heading. The object is not said, because the rest of the sūra will count one by one what the object may be.

### Historical background

Several accounts are transmitted in the sources as the occasion of the verse's revelation: that two men disputed in the Prophet's presence over the appointment of a governor to a tribe; that a sacrifice was performed before its time; that certain people gave a decision on a matter before a ruling had come down.

These accounts diverge from one another and do not agree at the level of detail; for that reason I relate none of them in definite terms and I give no names.

**What the reports have in common is this, and it is reasonable:** in Medina the community had grown, decisions had multiplied, and who was to speak and when in an assembly where the Prophet was present had become a practical question. The verse draws a boundary for that question.

**And this note is important:** the Qurʾān does not record the doer of the event by name. The maxim of legal theory recorded in the section on al-Humaza works here too — *al-ʿibratu bi-ʿumūmi'l-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣi'l-sabab*: the ruling follows the generality of the wording, not the particularity of the occasion. The verse forbids not an event but a way of positioning oneself.

### What this says to the present

What this verse says to the present shows itself in the fact that "going ahead" is a question about knowledge.

For a person to decide on a matter he must know that matter. To go ahead is **to decide before the knowledge arrives** — and the ordinary form of this is a person's confusing his own guess with knowledge.

The counterparts of this today are visible: passing a definite verdict on an event in its first hours; speaking about a text without reading it; taking a position in a field of expertise without waiting for the field's own criteria. None of these requires ill will; it is only a breaking of the order.

What the verse lays down is not a command to be silent — further on in the sūra we shall see speaking, setting matters right, even stopping one of two sides, all commanded. What it lays down is an **order**: first the measure, then the verdict.

## 49/2 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا تَرْفَعُوٓا۟ أَصْوَٰتَكُمْ فَوْقَ صَوْتِ ٱلنَّبِىِّ وَلَا تَجْهَرُوا۟ لَهُۥ بِٱلْقَوْلِ كَجَهْرِ بَعْضِكُمْ لِبَعْضٍ أَن تَحْبَطَ أَعْمَٰلُكُمْ وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَشْعُرُونَ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū lā tarfaʿū aṣwātakum fawqa ṣawti'n-nabiyyi wa lā tajharū lahū bi'l-qawli ka-jahri baʿḍikum li-baʿḍin an taḥbaṭa aʿmālukum wa antum lā tashʿurūn*

"O you who believe! Do not raise your voices above the voice of the Prophet. Do not speak loudly to him as you speak loudly to one another, lest your deeds come to nothing while you are not aware of it."

### Two distinct prohibitions

The verse forbids two things separately, and the two are not the same:

| | **The first prohibition** | **The second prohibition** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *lā tarfaʿū aṣwātakum fawqa ṣawti'n-nabiyy* | *lā tajharū lahū bi'l-qawl* |
| What is forbidden | The voice being **higher than the Prophet's voice** | Speaking to him **openly, loudly** |
| Measure | Comparative: his voice | Comparative: as you speak to one another |
| What it regulates | The **level** of the voice | The **manner** of speech |

The first prohibition sets a threshold: do not go above his voice. The second sets a **comparison**: do not speak to him as you speak among yourselves.

The second is the subtler. For a person can speak "as if to one another" without raising his voice at all. What is forbidden is not merely decibels but **a way of treating someone.**

### رَفَعَ and جَهَرَ

**رَفَعَ — root ر-ف-ع.** To raise, to lift. It was named in the section on ʿAbasa on the occasion of *marfūʿa*.

**جَهَرَ — root ج-ه-ر.** Its root sense is **to come out into the open, to become visible or audible**. From the same root:
- *jahr* — to bring the voice out openly (whence "*jahrī* recitation" in the prayer).
- *jahran* — openly, in public. *"Show me openly"* — *arina'llāha **jahratan*** (al-Nisāʾ 4/153).
- *ajhar* — one dazzled in daylight; the "openness/brightness" side of the root.

**At the centre of the root stands the opposite of concealment.** *Jahr* is the opposite of *sirr*. The second prohibition, then, aims less at loudness of voice than at **speaking openly and without restraint**.

Seeing this distinction will also be necessary for understanding the third verse: those praised there are not "those who fall silent" but **those who lower their voices**.

### أَن تَحْبَطَ — the root ح-ب-ط and the swollen animal

This is the heaviest word in the verse, and the concrete sense of the root carries the whole weight of it.

**Root: ح-ب-ط.** The concrete usage the lexicographers record is this: **حَبِطَتِ ٱلدَّابَّةُ** — the animal ate its fill of a harmful plant on the pasture; its belly swelled; and the swelling killed it.

In the dictionaries *al-ḥabaṭ* occurs as the name of this sickness: the swelling of the animal's belly with gas and fodder. The picture is this: the animal is **full**, more than full; its belly has grown; anyone looking from outside sees it sated and fat. But what is happening inside is not nourishment but **poisoning**. And the swelling ends in death.

**So *ḥabṭ* carries this picture at its root: growth on the outside, rotting within.**

From here it is clear how the word passed to its use in the Qurʾān — the coming to nothing of deeds. A person's deeds accumulate, multiply, become visible. Their number grows. But whether they nourish is a separate question.

**And the picture the root chooses is not a picture of "erasure".** The verse does not say *an tamḥuwa* (be wiped out) or *an tadhhaba* (go away). The verb it uses is the verb that relates **a thing's perishing by swelling**. The deeds are not removed; **they swell and burst.** The appearance stands, the content goes.

I take up this root for the first time here in this commentary.

### وَأَنتُمْ لَا تَشْعُرُونَ — "while you are not aware"

The most chilling place in the verse is not the threat itself but this qualification added to the threat.

**شَعَرَ — root ش-ع-ر.** To notice what is fine and hidden. From the same root come *shaʿr* (hair — the fine thing) and *shāʿir* (one who senses the fine thing). The same expression had occurred at al-Baqara 2/9 of the hypocrites: *wa mā yashʿurūn*.

**The function of the qualification is this:** if the coming to nothing of a deed were felt, the person would stop and put it right. That it is not felt makes it impossible for the process to correct itself.

**And this agrees exactly with the concrete picture of the root ح-ب-ط.** The swollen animal does not know it is ill; on the contrary, its belly is full. There is no symptom, because the symptom itself — growth — is taken for a sign of health.

I record this agreement as **my own reading**; I do not ascribe it to any classical exegete. But that the two elements — the concrete sense of the root and the note about "not being aware" — stand side by side in the same verse is a fact that stands in the text.

### The weight and the measure of the prohibition

A balance needs to be struck here, because on a first reading the verse may look disproportionate: the return for a seemingly small act, raising the voice, is set down as the coming to nothing of one's deeds.

**The word order of the verse carries an answer to this.** What is forbidden is not the voice but **the positioning that the voice implies**. The second prohibition says this outright: *ka-jahri baʿḍikum li-baʿḍ* — "as you speak to one another". The matter, that is, is treating the Prophet **as though he were a peer**.

And this is the continuation of the prohibition in the first verse. The first verse regulated position (going ahead), the second regulates **the voice** — because the voice is the most visible sign of position. Who speaks how loudly in an assembly makes the order of that assembly plain at once.

**Some of the classical exegetes enter a note on the severity of the expression "the coming to nothing of deeds" here** and say this: the verse does not state an outcome as certain, it puts forward **a possibility** — the *an* at the head of the clause ("lest…") is a particle of warning, not a statement of a ruling. I relate this explanation; I impose no preference.

### Historical background

One account transmitted in the sources is this: after the verse came down, a Companion with a powerful voice, fearing that his voice naturally came out loud, shut himself up in his house; the matter was reported to the Prophet and he was told that this was not within the scope of the verse.

This account is widespread in the classical sources and a name is given as well. I do not write the name here — in this commentary the practice of not giving names in accounts of the occasions of revelation has been adopted. **But the measure the account carries is worth recording and agrees with the wording of the verse:** the prohibition is placed not on the **physical loudness** of the voice but on the **act** of raising it. To be loud-voiced by birth is not an act.

The wording of the verse supports this too: *lā tarfaʿū* — "do not raise". This is a verb of action, not a statement of a condition.

### What this says to the present

The Prophet's assembly is no longer there. The scene the verse directly addresses is historical. But the verse's measure — that the voice states a position — is not historical.

In any community the level of the voice is never merely a matter of acoustics. Whose speech is cut off, at whose raised voice everyone falls silent, who can be interrupted while speaking — all of these are signs of the ranking in that community, and generally no one speaks about them openly.

What the verse does is to **make these signs visible** and, at one point, to set a boundary to them.

And the picture in the root *ح-ب-ط* works here too: the number of things a person does and the value those things carry are two separate matters. To do a great many things may be swelling. The question the verse asks is: **is what is growing the content, or the volume?**

## 49/3 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يَغُضُّونَ أَصْوَٰتَهُمْ عِندَ رَسُولِ ٱللَّهِ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱمْتَحَنَ ٱللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ لَهُم مَّغْفِرَةٌ وَأَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ

*Inna'lladhīna yaghuḍḍūna aṣwātahum ʿinda rasūli'llāhi ulāʾika'lladhīna imtaḥana'llāhu qulūbahum li't-taqwā, lahum maghfiratun wa ajrun ʿaẓīm*

"Those who lower their voices in the presence of God's Messenger — they are the ones whose hearts God has tested for taqwā. For them there is forgiveness and a great reward."

### The praise that follows the prohibition

The second verse set down a prohibition and added a heavy warning. The third verse builds the **positive side** of the same matter.

This is a structure often seen in the Qurʾān, but here it carries a function: the warning of the second verse could have left the one addressed in a state of fear. The third verse turns it into a door of **gain**. The same act has two faces: raising is loss, lowering is reward.

### يَغُضُّونَ — ghaḍḍ al-ṣawt

**Root: غ-ض-ض.** Its root sense is **to reduce, to lower, to bring down, to lessen.**

The best-known use of the root in the Qurʾān concerns the eye: *"Tell the believing men to **lower** their gaze"* (al-Nūr 24/30-31) — *yaghuḍḍū min abṣārihim*. And in one place it concerns the voice: *"**Lower** your voice"* (Luqmān 31/19) — *waghḍuḍ min ṣawtik*.

**The idea the root carries is not "to shut" but "to reduce".** Used of the eye it does not say "close your eye" but "cast your gaze down, lessen it". Used of the voice it does not say "be silent" but "lower your voice".

This distinction is decisive for the sense of the verse. Those praised are not those who fall silent. Those praised are **those who speak but adjust their voices**. Nowhere in the sūra is speaking forbidden; it is adjusted.

That the same root is used for both eye and voice is also worth recording: both are the organs that most "overstep" within a community, and for both the same verb is used — not to shut, but to lower.

### عِندَ رَسُولِ ٱللَّهِ — "in the presence of"

**عِنْد** is an adverb stating nearness and presence: beside, in the presence of, with.

**And there is a change in the word order:** in the second verse it was *fawqa ṣawti'n-nabiyy* — "above the voice of the Prophet". Here it is *ʿinda rasūli'llāh* — "in the presence of God's Messenger".

Two differences at once:
1. *al-Nabiyy* → *Rasūlu'llāh*. In the second verse the quality of prophethood (*nabī* — one who receives and brings news), in the third the quality of messengership (*rasūl* — one who is sent), and to this the name of God is added: *Rasūlu'llāh*.
2. *Fawqa ṣawtihī* (above his voice) → *ʿindahū* (in his presence). The ground shifts from comparison to place.

The second difference brings this: the prohibition was comparative with the Prophet's voice; the praise concerns **being in his presence**. The measure is no longer "let it not be higher than his voice" but "lower your voice while you are with him". This is a wider note: it holds even if the Prophet is not speaking at all.

### ٱمْتَحَنَ ٱللَّهُ قُلُوبَهُمْ لِلتَّقْوَىٰ

Here is the centre of the verse.

**Root: م-ح-ن.** This root was treated in the section on al-Mumtaḥina; the concrete usage recorded there was this: **مَحَنَ ٱلْأَدِيمَ** — to draw and stretch a hide in tanning it, to make it thin; and **مَحَنَ ٱلْبِئْرَ** — to dig out a well in order to clean it. What the two share: **to work upon a thing so as to bring out what is inside it.**

The dictionaries record one further use of the root, and it is particularly apt for this verse: **the testing of an ore by melting it in fire** — the real metal within the ore becoming visible by being separated from what is mixed with it.

**The common idea is the same:** a test **produces** nothing; it makes visible what is there. Whatever is in the ore put into the fire is what comes out; the fire adds no metal.

**Form: Form VIII (*iftiʿāl*)** — *imtaḥana*. This form here adds to the verb an intensity of "working upon" something.

### لِلتَّقْوَىٰ — "for taqwā" — the work of the letter lām

The most disputed place in the sentence is this single letter. *Li't-taqwā* — "for taqwā", or "towards taqwā", or "because of taqwā"?

The classical explanations go in several directions:

| Explanation | The sense of the lām | The sense of the sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | "for, so that" | God tested their hearts so that taqwā might come out |
| **Result (outcome)** | "with the result of" | He tested them, and the result was taqwā |
| **Cause** | "because of" | Because of their taqwā He found their hearts worth testing |
| **Purifying** | "set apart for, refined for" | He refined and purified their hearts for taqwā |

The fourth explanation is the closest to the root's picture of "refining ore", and some sources note that the verb *imtaḥana* is recorded directly in the sense of **"he made pure, he refined"**. I relate this as a possibility; **I impose no preference and none of them is binding.**

**But there is something all the explanations assume in common, and that is what really deserves recording:** lowering the voice is read as **the outward form** of something in the heart. The verse does not say "one who lowers his voice has done a good deed". It says "one who lowers his voice is one whose heart has been tested".

That is, a small and outwardly visible act (adjusting the voice) is counted as the sign of an unseen condition (the state of the heart). This is the whole logic of the sūra, and in its last block (14-18) it will be repeated in reverse: there too an outward declaration (*āmannā*) will be compared with the condition in the heart.

**The difference between the two blocks is this, and it is worth recording:**

| | **49/3** | **49/14** |
|---|---|---|
| Outward act | Lowering the voice | Saying "we have believed" |
| Inward condition | A heart tested for taqwā | Faith not yet having entered |
| Relation | The outward **shows** the inward | The outward **does not show** the inward |

The sūra, that is, gives two different examples of when an outward act shows an inward condition and when it does not. And the difference lies here: in the first there is a **cost** to the act (to lower one's voice is to draw oneself back), in the second there is none (framing a sentence costs nothing).

I record this as my own reading.

### لَهُم مَّغْفِرَةٌ وَأَجْرٌ عَظِيمٌ

**مَغْفِرَة — root غ-ف-ر.** To cover, to shut over. *Mighfar* (a helmet) is from the same root.

**أَجْر — root أ-ج-ر.** The return for work done, a wage.

**And the order of the two words is striking:** first forgiveness, then reward. This order is common in the Qurʾān and its logic is plain: first what is lacking is covered, then what is over and above is given.

The link with the second verse is worth recording too: there deeds **came to nothing**; here there is a return for deeds (*ajr*). The two ends of the same matter.

## 49/4 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يُنَادُونَكَ مِن وَرَآءِ ٱلْحُجُرَٰتِ أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ

*Inna'lladhīna yunādūnaka min warāʾi'l-ḥujurāti aktharuhum lā yaʿqilūn*

"Most of those who call out to you from behind the chambers do not use their reason."

### The verse that gives the sūra its name

Here the scene changes. The first three verses were inside an assembly — beside the Prophet, in a place where his voice was heard. In the fourth verse the one addressed goes **outside**: behind the chambers.

### ٱلْحُجُرَٰت — chambers

The plural of **حُجْرَة**. The root ح-ج-ر was treated above: to enclose, to hinder, to draw a boundary.

What is meant are the chambers that made up the Prophet's house. According to what is transmitted in the sources these were plainly built, separate compartments adjoining the mosque.

**That the word comes in the plural is worth recording.** Had it been singular ("from behind the chamber") a single place would have been meant. The plural describes a **structure**: adjoining the mosque yet separate from it; right beside the space open to the public, yet not part of it.

### The physical boundary of privacy

This is the side of the verse that most deserves to be dwelt on in this commentary.

Until now the sūra had been speaking about **voice** and **speech** — abstract boundaries. In the fourth verse the boundary turns into **a wall**.

A *ḥujra* is by definition an object of boundary: what encloses an area and separates it from the rest. And the verse puts on record the calling out from behind that boundary.

**The point to notice is this: those who call do not break the wall down, do not force the door, do not go in.** All they do is **call out**. The wall stands where it is; the voice crosses it.

And this is exactly what the sūra records: **a physical boundary can be crossed by voice.** A door may be shut and the person may still disturb the one inside. Privacy is not kept safe by a wall alone; it is kept safe by the wall being respected.

This is directly connected with the prohibition of prying later in the sūra (12), and I shall make that connection there. But the idea in the two verses is the same: **the existence of a boundary is not enough; the boundary has to be acknowledged.**

I record this as my own reading. The fact that stands in the text is this: the act censured is not entering but calling out.

### يُنَادُونَكَ — to call out

**Root: ن-د-ي / ن-د-و.** *Nidāʾ* — to call out to one far off, to summon. *Munādī* — the one who calls. *Al-nādī* — an assembly, a place of gathering (at al-ʿAlaq 96/17, *nādiyah*).

**Form: Form III (*mufāʿala*)** — *nādā*. This form generally states reciprocity; the explanation the lexicographers record here is this: *nidāʾ* includes the sending of the voice to a distance and the expectation of an answer from the other side.

**And the word itself presupposes a distance.** You do not "call out" to the person beside you; you speak. *Nidāʾ* is an attempt to close the distance in between with the voice. Here the distance being closed is a wall.

### مِن وَرَآءِ — "from behind"

**وَرَاء** — behind, beyond. The word is counted among those in Arabic that can mean both "behind" and "before/beyond"; here the sense is plain: the other side of the wall.

The particle *min* indicates the place the voice comes from: the voice comes from there.

### أَكْثَرُهُمْ لَا يَعْقِلُونَ — "most of them do not use their reason"

This is the place in the verse that needs to be read most carefully, and two notes are required.

**The first note: *aktharuhum* — "most of them".** The verse does not say "all of them". This is a qualification the Qurʾān often uses when speaking about communities, and it agrees with the measure repeated in this commentary: **no wholesale ruling is issued against a group.** Not all who do the same act may be doing it for the same reason.

**The second note: *lā yaʿqilūn* — "they do not use their reason".**

**Root: ع-ق-ل.** It was treated in detail in the section on al-Fajr; what was recorded there was this: the concrete sense of the root is **to bind** — the rope that ties a camel's leg is called *ʿiqāl*. **Reason is a bond**, a power of holding; not a capacity but a brake.

**And this changes the ruling of the verse entirely.** *Lā yaʿqilūn* does not mean "their intelligence is poor". In the logic of the root, reason is a person's being able to hold himself back. The verse is saying, that is: **these people cannot hold themselves back.**

The table set up in the section on al-Fajr works here exactly: *ḥijr* (reason) and *ḥujra* (chamber) are from the same root — both are a boundary. And the fourth verse records, of the person who calls out from behind the **ḥujra**, the absence of **ḥijr** (the failure to use reason).

**Two words from the same root, face to face in the same verse:** the person who does not respect the boundary outside is one who is not using the boundary inside — his power of holding himself back.

I record this link as **my own reading**; I do not ascribe it to any classical exegete. But the shared root of the two words is a fact that stands in the language, and it had been recorded independently in the section on al-Fajr.

### Historical background

The sources transmit that a tribal delegation came to Medina and, growing impatient during the Prophet's hour of rest, called out to him from behind the chambers. In the reports the name of the tribe is given as well.

I relate this account with the note "it is transmitted" and do not write the tribe's name. The reason is this commentary's general practice: the accounts of the occasions of revelation diverge from one another at the level of detail, and, more importantly, **attaching the name of a tribe to a verse can be read as a ruling about people who bear that name today or who are traced to that lineage.** The wording of the Qurʾān does not do this: the verse speaks not of a tribe but of "those who call out".

**What the reports have in common is reasonable and historically familiar:** the manners of speech of desert life and those of a settled city — still more of a seat of government — are different. To raise the voice in order to summon someone is normal in a wide and thinly peopled space; in a settlement of chambers with doors it is a breach of a boundary. The verse stands at that transition.

### What this says to the present

The picture in the verse is more than familiar today, because the possibility of "calling out from behind the chamber" has increased as never before.

A person's door is no longer only the door of his house. His telephone, his message box, the end of his working hours, his day off — each of these is a *ḥujra*: an enclosed, separated area. And all of these areas, just like the wall in the verse, can be **crossed by voice**. No one breaks down the door; they only call out.

The verse's measure will come in the fifth verse: **to wait.** But this much can already be recorded: the verse does not charge the one who crosses the boundary with ill will. The word it uses is not moral but mental: *lā yaʿqilūn* — they cannot hold themselves back. Impatience is described not as a wickedness but as **a lack of brakes**.

## 49/5 — وَلَوْ أَنَّهُمْ صَبَرُوا۟ حَتَّىٰ تَخْرُجَ إِلَيْهِمْ لَكَانَ خَيْرًا لَّهُمْ وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Wa law annahum ṣabarū ḥattā takhruja ilayhim la-kāna khayran lahum, wa'llāhu ghafūrun raḥīm*

"Had they been patient until you came out to them, it would have been better for them. God is Forgiving, Merciful."

### The three things the verse does

A short verse, but it does three separate things:

1. **It states the remedy:** to be patient.
2. **It states whose benefit it is:** *khayran **lahum*** — "better for them". Not for the Prophet.
3. **It does not shut the door:** it ends with *ghafūrun raḥīm*.

### صَبَرُوا۟ — patience

**Root: ص-ب-ر.** The root sense the lexicographers record is **to hold, to confine, to keep a thing in one place**. *Ṣabara nafsahū* — he held his soul back. *Ṣabr* is also the name of a bitter plant (aloe); its connection with bitterness is made through the taste of the word.

**And the "holding" sense of the root looks to the same place as *lā yaʿqilūn* in the fourth verse.** Reason is a bond, patience is holding. The fourth verse diagnosed the lack (they cannot hold themselves); the fifth states the remedy (had they held themselves).

This link between the two verses is invisible unless one goes down to the root senses of the words. I record it.

### حَتَّىٰ تَخْرُجَ إِلَيْهِمْ — "until you came out"

The patience has a **term**: until the Prophet comes out.

**This is the most practical side of the verse and is generally passed over quickly.** The verse does not say "never call out", does not say "never come", nor does it give a general piece of advice like "learn to wait". It sets a **threshold**: until he comes out.

The boundary, that is, is set not where the person's patience runs out but where **the other party is ready**. What determines the time of waiting is not the patience of the one waiting but the condition of the one waited for.

**And *ilayhim* — "out to them".** The Prophet will come out. The relation, that is, is not cut off; only the authority to begin it changes hands. It is not the one who knocks at the door who decides but the one who opens it.

### لَوْ … لَكَانَ — an unrealised condition

**لَوْ** in Arabic generally states a condition that **did not happen**: "if only they had…". Its answer comes with *la-kāna*.

The sentence, that is, is judging an event in retrospect: it was not done, but had it been done it would have been better.

**And the word chosen is *khayr*** — "better". The verse does not say "they would have escaped a burden", "they would have kept clear of sin". It uses a positive language: there is a **good** they missed.

### خَيْرًا لَّهُمْ — whose benefit

These two words are the centre of gravity of the verse.

To whose account is the benefit of patience written? Not the Prophet's — **theirs.**

Joined with *lā yaʿqilūn* in the fourth verse, this sets up a logic: impatience harms the one who is impatient. The person who calls out in haste not only disturbs the other's rest; **he diminishes what he himself would have obtained.**

The reason for this is not stated openly in the text. But it becomes visible when read together with the third verse: there, those who lowered their voices were promised *forgiveness and a great reward*. Here, those who do not have patience fail to reach that door.

### وَٱللَّهُ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ — the door left open

The verse began with a censure and ends with a name of forgiveness.

**This verse-ending is no accident and it shows the character of the sūra.** The fourth verse gave a hard ruling: "most of them do not use their reason". The fifth verse turns this into a **lesson** and then leaves the door open.

This feature of the sūra will be seen in the later blocks as well:
- The eleventh verse sets down the prohibition of mockery and nicknames and points to the door of repentance by saying *"whoever does not repent…"*.
- The twelfth verse likens backbiting to eating the flesh of a dead brother and closes with *tawwābun raḥīm*.
- The fourteenth verse rejects a claim and closes with *ghafūrun raḥīm*.

**The sūra, that is, leaves a way back after every heavy prohibition it lays down.** This is something texts of conduct generally do not do: texts that lay down rules are content with describing the one who breaks them. Al-Ḥujurāt says as well what the one who breaks them is to do.

I record this as my own reading; but the sequence of the verse-endings stands in the text and can be counted.

### The whole of the first block (1-5)

Five verses treated a single matter in four stages:

| Verse | Stage | What is regulated |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Position | Not going ahead — the general heading |
| **2** | Voice | Not raising it; not treating him as a peer |
| **3** | Reward | A ruling about the heart of the one who lowers his voice |
| **4** | Place | Not calling out from behind an enclosed space |
| **5** | Time | Waiting for him to come out |

**Four dimensions: position, voice, place, time.** All the physical parameters of a relation with a person. And not one of them is a note about worship; all of them are the manners of being together.

The other party of the relation regulated in this block is the Prophet, and that relation is historical. But the parameters regulated — where you will stand, how loudly you will speak, what you will do before a closed door, how long you will wait — are not historical.

## 49/6 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوٓا۟ إِن جَآءَكُمْ فَاسِقٌۢ بِنَبَإٍ فَتَبَيَّنُوٓا۟ أَن تُصِيبُوا۟ قَوْمًۢا بِجَهَٰلَةٍ فَتُصْبِحُوا۟ عَلَىٰ مَا فَعَلْتُمْ نَٰدِمِينَ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū in jāʾakum fāsiqun bi-nabaʾin fa-tabayyanū an tuṣībū qawman bi-jahālatin fa-tuṣbiḥū ʿalā mā faʿaltum nādimīn*

"O you who believe! If a transgressor brings you a report, investigate it thoroughly; lest you harm a people in ignorance and then become regretful over what you did."

### The verse of the sūra that looks most to the present

This verse is the densest sentence in al-Ḥujurāt — perhaps in the Qurʾān — about the ethics of knowledge. It has five elements, and all five are links of one chain:

| Element | Expression | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **The source** | *fāsiqun* | The condition of the one who brings the report |
| **The content** | *bi-nabaʾin* | The weight of the report |
| **The procedure** | *fa-tabayyanū* | What is to be done |
| **The risk** | *an tuṣībū qawman bi-jahālatin* | What happens if it is not done |
| **The price** | *fa-tuṣbiḥū … nādimīn* | The irreversibility of the outcome |

The verse lays down no prohibition. It lays down a **procedure**. In this respect it stands alone in the sūra.

### فَاسِق — what comes out of its husk

**Root: ف-س-ق.** This root was treated in the sections on al-Ḥashr, al-Ḥadīd and al-Munāfiqūn. The concrete sense recorded there was this: **the date's coming out of its skin** — *fasaqati'r-ruṭabatu ʿan qishrihā*. The same verb is used when a mouse comes out of its hole.

The summary recorded: *fisq* is not the name of a "sin" but the name of a **position** — the thing that ought to be inside being outside. And the place gone out of is a **husk**: a protective covering. Fruit that comes out of its skin is both left unprotected and spoiled.

**The work of the word in this verse is a distinct one and asks for attention.**

The verse does not say that the bringer of the report is **a liar**. It does not say *kādhib*. It says *fāsiq* — one who stands outside the boundary. And this distinction determines the logic of the procedure the verse lays down:

- If the bringer of the report were a liar, the right course would be to **reject** the report.
- If the bringer of the report is a *fāsiq*, the right course is to **investigate** the report.

**The verse, that is, does not identify the condition of the source with the truth of the report.** What a *fāsiq* brings may well be true. The fault of the source does not make the report void; it only prevents its **being accepted directly**.

This is a distinction that looks fine at first sight but has large consequences, and it is often lost in the modern setting of information. To look at who said a thing and then pass a verdict on its content — in either direction — is to depart from both halves of the procedure the verse sets up. What the verse says is: the source determines **how much the report is to be investigated**; not **whether it is true**.

### بِنَبَإٍ — not an ordinary report

**Root: ن-ب-أ.** This root was treated in detail in the section on al-Nabaʾ, and this verse was specially dwelt on there. I do not repeat it; the summary is this:

- Beneath the root lies the idea of **coming from one place to another, being carried**. A report is what is **carried**.
- According to the definition recorded in al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī's *al-Mufradāt* and widely transmitted in the exegetical tradition, a *nabaʾ* is a report **that carries great benefit, and by which knowledge or a strong conviction comes about**.
- **And in the section on al-Nabaʾ a note was entered on this definition, precisely on account of this verse:** the Qurʾān uses the word *nabaʾ* here for a report that is commanded to be verified — that is, one that may be false. The condition of the definition is therefore not that the report **be true** but that it be **of a kind to carry weight** if it is true.
- The sentence drawn out in the section on al-Nabaʾ: ***"A nabaʾ is a report at which something has to change once it is heard."***

**And this determines the scope of the verse.** The verse does not take every rumour as its subject. What it takes as its subject is a report of the kind **that will be acted upon once it is heard**. As was recorded in the section on al-Nabaʾ: the report of this verse is heavy enough to lead to an expedition against a people. For a trivial piece of gossip the verse would not have said "investigate it thoroughly".

This makes the verse more serious still: the procedure is laid down for reports **whose consequences are heavy**.

### فَتَبَيَّنُوا۟ — and a difference of variant reading

This is the command of the verse, and here there is a difference of variant reading. **Because the difference changes the meaning, it must be taken up.**

Two readings are recorded in the sources:

| Reading | Root | Form | Concrete sense | The content of the command |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **فَتَبَيَّنُوا۟** (*fa-tabayyanū*) | ب-ي-ن | Form V (*tafaʿʿul*) | *Bayān*: to be clear, to be distinguished, to have a space opened between | **Bring it into the open, make it clear, investigate** — do something |
| **فَتَثَبَّتُوا۟** (*fa-tathabbatū*) | ث-ب-ت | Form V (*tafaʿʿul*) | *Thubūt*: to be fixed, not to be moved from one's place | **Stay where you are, do not hurry, remain fixed** — do not do something |

Both readings are transmitted among the variant readings of mass transmission. **Since I cannot state with certainty which imam read which, I write no names**; this is the practice adopted in this commentary.

**How the two readings change the meaning is this:**

*Tabayyun* is an **active** command: you will do something in order to bring the truth of the report to light. You will ask, you will check, you will go to the source. The concrete sense of the root — *bayn*: the space between two things, separation — implies an act of **separating**: separating the true from the false.

*Tathabbut* is a **passive** command: when the report comes, do not move from your place. The concrete sense of the root — *thabata*: a thing's standing fixed and in place — implies an **immobility**: do not hurry, delay your reaction.

| | *Tabayyun* | *Tathabbut* |
|---|---|---|
| The direction of the command | **Do** something | **Do not do** something |
| Time | After the report comes | The moment the report comes |
| What it aims at | The truth of the report | The person's reaction |
| If it is left undone | Acting on false information | Haste even with true information |

**And the two readings complete one another.** When a report comes, two distinct errors are possible: acting on false information, and acting too early even on true information. *Tabayyun* closes off the first, *tathabbut* the second.

**I record this complementarity as my own reading.** It is also transmitted in the classical sources that the two readings were held to be close in meaning. But the concrete senses of the two roots are different, and there is no need to erase that difference: one is a command to investigate, the other a command to halt.

**And in practice the two work together:** investigation takes time; to gain time one must halt. *Tathabbut*, that is, is the precondition of *tabayyun*.

### أَن تُصِيبُوا۟ قَوْمًۢا — "that you strike a people"

**أَصَابَ — root ص-و-ب.** Its root sense is **to hit, to reach the target**. *Muṣība* is from the same root: what strikes a person. *Ṣawāb* — right, on target.

**The interesting side of the root is that it carries both "to be right" and "to strike".** An arrow strikes its target; a calamity strikes a person; and an opinion is "on target". The common idea: **a thing's reaching a mark.**

The sense used here is the negative one: to make a harm **strike** a people.

**And *qawman* comes indefinite:** "a people". Which people is not said. This generalises the ruling — whichever tribe may figure in the account of the occasion of revelation, the verse is not closed upon that tribe.

**It is also worth recording that the one harmed is a *qawm* — a people.** The harm of acting on a false report is written to the account not of one person but of a group. This agrees with the weight of the report (*nabaʾ*), and its counterpart today is the same: the price of an unverified report is generally paid by the community that is its subject, not by those who spread it.

### بِجَهَٰلَةٍ — ignorance, or anger?

Here there is a distinction the classical exegesis records, and it widens the sense of the verse.

**Root: ج-ه-ل.** Its common sense is **not to know**; *jahl* is the opposite of *ʿilm*.

**But a second sense of the root is also recorded in Arabic: to act in anger, without thinking, with levity.** *Jahl* is also the opposite of *ḥilm* (gravity, self-restraint). The connection of the word *jāhiliyya* with this sense is debated in the classical sources: on one view *jāhiliyya* is not "the age of ignorance" but "the age of roughness, of failure to hold oneself back".

The two senses:

| Sense | What *bi-jahālatin* means | What the verse says |
|---|---|---|
| **Ignorance** | Without knowing the truth | To do harm on false information |
| **Roughness / anger** | In anger, without thinking, without measure | To act without measure even on true information |

**The second sense widens the scope of the verse** and coincides directly with the *tathabbut* reading: that a report is true does not show that the reaction given to it is measured.

I relate this second sense as a view recorded in the classical sources; **I impose no preference and it is not binding.** But this must be said: the two senses do not exclude one another. A person who acts without investigating the truth of a report is generally both ignorant and hasty.

### فَتُصْبِحُوا۟ … نَٰدِمِينَ — regret

**أَصْبَحَ — root ص-ب-ح.** Its root sense is **to come into the morning**. *Ṣubḥ* — morning. In Arabic this verb came in time to be used also in the sense of "to come to be, to become" (not in the sense of passing the night through until morning, but in the sense of "he came to be…").

**But the root sense leaves a picture here:** a thing done at night being seen in the morning. A step taken in the dark, a step whose nature is understood in the light.

I record this not as a claim but as a point where the origin of the word intersects with the context; a similar note was entered in the section on al-Ḥashr.

**نَادِم — root ن-د-م.** Regret. The sense the lexicographers record: the grief and self-reproach felt after something done.

**And that the word comes in the form of an *active participle* is worth recording:** *nādimīn* — "those who regret". Not a condition but a **quality**. Regret is described as a state that clings to a person.

**That the verse ends with this word and says nothing of a punishment is worth recording.** The verse sets up no threat of chastisement. What it sets up is a consequence that comes in this world and immediately after the act: **irreversibility.**

For this is the definition of regret. If a person can take back what he has done, he does not regret it, he corrects it. Regret is the feeling that appears when the possibility of correction is gone.

**The deterrent element of the verse, that is, is this: what is done on a false report is not taken back.** A word can be taken back; the harm done to a people cannot.

### The place of this verse in legal theory

This verse has been counted in the Islamic tradition of learning as one of the grounds of a method: **examining the condition of the one who brings a report.**

The field called *jarḥ wa taʿdīl* in the science of hadith — the examination and recording, one by one, of the reliability of the transmitters — is largely grounded on the principle this verse sets up: **a report cannot be assessed independently of its source.**

I record this as a note of history. What is worth recording is this: the idea of examining first **the chain that carries** a report in order to assess it is an early and systematic instance in the history of knowledge. The structure of what is done today under the name of "source verification" is similar: to look at the carrier before looking at the content.

**And there is one further debate** — it occupies a wide place in the books of legal theory:

The verse says "if a transgressor brings a report, investigate". Does the **contrary** also carry a ruling — that is, does it follow that "if a just man brings a report, do not investigate"?

| View | Ground | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **The contrary implication is a proof** | A ruling tied to a condition falls away when the condition is absent | The report of a just man is accepted without investigation |
| **The contrary implication is not a proof** | A condition may not be the sole cause of the ruling; the verse regulates only the report of a transgressor | The verse says nothing about the report of a just man |

This is a question of legal theory on which the schools divide, and here **I make no choice and issue no legal ruling.** I record both views; neither is binding.

**But a practical note can be entered:** both views agree that the source of a report matters. Where they part is over whether a report from a trustworthy source is also to be investigated.

### Historical background

The following account is widely transmitted in the sources as the occasion of the verse's revelation: an officer was sent to collect the alms from a tribe; on the way he turned back for some reason and reported that the tribe had refused to pay the alms and had risen against him. Upon this, moving against the tribe came under consideration; a delegation of the tribe arrived and said that this was not so, that on the contrary they had come out to meet the officer, and the verse came down.

In the reports the names of both the officer and the tribe are given. **I do not write the names here.** For two reasons: there are differences among the details of the reports and their soundness has been debated by the classical scholars; and, more importantly, to record a particular person in definite terms as the one addressed by the Qurʾān's word *fāsiq* is a heavier ruling than the transmissions in my hands can carry.

**That is the uncertain side of the account. The certain side is the text itself:** the verse gives neither a personal name nor a tribal name. *Fāsiqun* — indefinite. *Qawman* — indefinite. **Both sides have been left unnamed.**

And this shows what the verse does: it is not recording an event, it is setting up a **procedure**.

### What this says to the present

This verse is the place in al-Ḥujurāt that looks most directly to the present, and it requires no additional interpretation.

**One. The obligation is placed on the receiver.**

This is the most striking side of the verse. It does not say "let the transgressor not carry reports". It does not say "punish the one who spreads the report". It addresses **the one who receives the report**: *fa-tabayyanū*.

The reason for this can be understood: the behaviour of the one who brings a report cannot be controlled, but the behaviour of the one who receives it is in his own hands. A report spreads only so far as those who receive it pass it on. The chain is broken at the receiver.

**Two. The asymmetry between speed and truth.**

The time it takes a report to spread and the time it takes to verify it are not the same. Verification is an operation and takes time; spreading is not an operation and takes none. This asymmetry existed in the period the verse came down as well — a report would go round a city in a matter of days, while verifying it required sending a messenger. Today the asymmetry has grown: spreading has become almost costless, while verification still requires the same work.

The *tathabbut* reading looks exactly to this point: **if you cannot verify, at least wait.** Waiting does not take the place of verification, but it limits the harm.

**Three. The correction cannot catch up with the report.**

The last word of the verse is *nādimīn* — regretful. And the cause of regret is that what has been done cannot be taken back.

When a false report has spread, its correction generally reaches only a part of the places the report reached. What spreads and what corrects do not travel at the same speed. This is today's form of the "irreversibility" the verse records.

**Four. A limit: this verse is not a command to be silent.**

To read the verse as "do not receive reports, do not speak, do not get involved" is contrary to the text. The command is *tabayyun* — investigate. To investigate requires taking an interest. In the ninth verse of the sūra, stepping in when two groups fight will be commanded; the verse, that is, does not teach indifference to what is going on.

What it teaches is an **order**: verify first, then act. And this is the same as the command in the sūra's first verse — do not put a thing ahead of where it belongs.

**Five. And this commentary's own note.**

In writing what this verse says to the present, I have avoided using the verse about any particular side, any particular event, or any current controversy. The reason is the rule of the method (`USLUP.md`), and it agrees with the verse itself: **the verse named neither of the two sides.** To place it as a weapon in the hand of one side would be to undo the first thing the verse does.

## 49/7 — وَٱعْلَمُوٓا۟ أَنَّ فِيكُمْ رَسُولَ ٱللَّهِ لَوْ يُطِيعُكُمْ فِى كَثِيرٍ مِّنَ ٱلْأَمْرِ لَعَنِتُّمْ وَلَٰكِنَّ ٱللَّهَ حَبَّبَ إِلَيْكُمُ ٱلْإِيمَٰنَ وَزَيَّنَهُۥ فِى قُلُوبِكُمْ وَكَرَّهَ إِلَيْكُمُ ٱلْكُفْرَ وَٱلْفُسُوقَ وَٱلْعِصْيَانَ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلرَّٰشِدُونَ

*Wa'ʿlamū anna fīkum rasūla'llāh, law yuṭīʿukum fī kathīrin mina'l-amri la-ʿanittum, wa lākinna'llāha ḥabbaba ilaykumu'l-īmāna wa zayyanahū fī qulūbikum wa karraha ilaykumu'l-kufra wa'l-fusūqa wa'l-ʿiṣyān, ulāʾika humu'r-rāshidūn*

"Know that God's Messenger is among you. If he were to obey you in much of the matter, you would fall into hardship. But God has made faith beloved to you and adorned it in your hearts, and has made denial, transgression and disobedience repellent to you. These are the ones who are rightly directed."

### The link with the sixth verse

The seventh verse is the direct continuation of the sixth, and the link is made here:

The sixth verse took as its subject a report brought by a **transgressor**. The seventh verse says: "God's Messenger is among you."

**The contrast, then, is this:** on one side, information coming from outside whose reliability is in question; on the other, a verified source present among you. The verse reminds the one addressed that a means of verification is already at hand.

This makes the command of *tabayyun* in the sixth verse concrete: investigation is not an abstract undertaking; there is a place to ask.

### وَٱعْلَمُوٓا۟ أَنَّ فِيكُمْ رَسُولَ ٱللَّهِ — the emphasis in the word order

In Arabic the normal order of the sentence would have been: *anna rasūla'llāhi fīkum* — "God's Messenger is among you."

The verse has reversed this: *anna **fīkum** rasūla'llāh* — "among you is God's Messenger."

**The bringing forward of the adverbial phrase (*fīkum*) states emphasis.** What is emphasised is not who the messenger is — but **where he is.** "Among you." That is, at a distance where he can be reached, asked, consulted.

And this is directly connected with the first block (1-5): there the one addressed was warned **not to go ahead** of the Prophet and **to wait at his door**. Here he is reminded that the same person is *among you*. A boundary was set, but no distance was set.

### لَوْ يُطِيعُكُمْ — "were he to obey you"

This is the most unusual sentence in the verse and is generally passed over quickly.

Many times in the Qurʾān it is said "obey God and His Messenger". Here the relation is **reversed**: what if the Messenger obeyed you?

**And the answer is this: *la-ʿanittum* — you would fall into hardship.**

**عَنِتَ — root ع-ن-ت.** The concrete sense the lexicographers record: **difficulty, hardship, distress**; and as one use of the root, the re-breaking of a bone that had knit — that is, **the breaking again of something that had been mended** — is recorded. I relate this second use as it occurs in the dictionaries, not in definite terms.

The word occurs in several places in the Qurʾān and always states a **being strained**: *"They desire what will cause you hardship"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/118) — *waddū mā ʿanittum*.

**What the sentence says is this:** for a community to be governed according to its own momentary wishes is not to that community's benefit.

At first sight this may look like a sentence in defence of authority. But the word order says something else: the sentence does not say **"he does not obey you"**, it says **"if he did, you would suffer for it"**. What is recorded, that is, is not the direction of obedience but its **consequence**.

And read together with the sixth verse, a table emerges:

| Verse | Warning | Its source |
|---|---|---|
| **6** | Do not act without verifying the report | Information coming from outside may be false |
| **7** | Do not demand that your own wish be carried out | A demand coming from inside may be wrong too |

**Two verses close off two different sources of error:** the one information coming from outside, the other a demand coming from inside. And the structure common to both is the same as in the sūra's first verse — putting a thing ahead of where it belongs.

I record this as my own reading; that the two verses come one after the other stands in the text.

### فِى كَثِيرٍ مِّنَ ٱلْأَمْرِ — "in much of the matter"

The qualification is important: **not "in every matter", but "in much of the matter".**

The verse, that is, does not say that the view of the one addressed is always wrong. The Qurʾān itself commands consultation elsewhere: *"Consult them in affairs"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/159). And, as was recorded earlier in this commentary, Sūrat al-Mujādala records a woman's disputing with the Prophet without censuring it.

**The verse, then, takes as its subject not consulting but imposing.** The difference lies between giving an opinion and demanding that the opinion be carried out.

### حَبَّبَ / زَيَّنَ / كَرَّهَ — three verbs, one subject

The second half of the verse is a sentence of thanksgiving, and its word order is striking. Three verbs, all three in **Form II**, and the subject of all three is God:

| Verb | Root | Sense | Its object |
|---|---|---|---|
| **حَبَّبَ** | ح-ب-ب | Made beloved | *al-īmān* |
| **زَيَّنَ** | ز-ي-ن | Adorned, made to appear fair | (the same) *fī qulūbikum* |
| **كَرَّهَ** | ك-ر-ه | Made repellent, made loathsome | *al-kufr, al-fusūq, al-ʿiṣyān* |

**Form II here states a causing:** not loving but making beloved, not finding repellent but making repellent.

**And the subject of the verbs is not conduct but *inclination*.** The verse does not say "He commanded you faith"; it says "He made it beloved". A command and a making-beloved are different: a command is an outward note, a making-beloved an inward turning.

**This is an important point for the whole of the sūra, and I record it as my own reading:** al-Ḥujurāt is a sūra made of commands from beginning to end. And right in its middle, alongside the commands, a language of **inclination** enters. The text records, that is, that conduct is not built by obeying rules alone, that an order of liking and loathing is needed. That a person avoids backbiting and that he is repelled by backbiting are two separate conditions; the twelfth verse will try to produce exactly the second.

### ٱلْكُفْر — ٱلْفُسُوق — ٱلْعِصْيَان — the triad

Three things are counted as made repellent, and in the classical commentaries it is recorded that these state a **gradation**:

| Word | Root | Concrete sense | What it describes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **كُفْر** | ك-ف-ر | To cover (a farmer too is called *kāfir*: he covers the seed with soil) | Not accepting, covering over — the innermost refusal |
| **فُسُوق** | ف-س-ق | To come out of the husk | Going outside the boundary |
| **عِصْيَان** | ع-ص-ي | *ʿAṣā*: a staff, stiffness; resisting being bent | Not obeying a particular command |

The order runs from inside outward: first the refusal of belief, then going out beyond the boundary, then failing to obey individual commands. I relate this gradation as an explanation recorded in the classical sources; **I impose no preference.**

**And there is a balance in the word order:** on the other side stands a single positive term — *al-īmān*. One thing made beloved, three made repellent. One of the classical explanations is this: faith is one, the ways of departing from it are many.

**It must be recorded that this is the third occurrence of the root ف-س-ق in the sūra.** In the sixth verse the word was the epithet of the one who brings the report (*fāsiqun*); here it is one of the things made repellent (*al-fusūq*); and in the eleventh verse it will come back as a name: *biʾsa'l-ismu'l-fusūqu baʿda'l-īmān*. I shall gather the three occurrences in the section on the sūra as a whole.

### أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلرَّٰشِدُونَ

**رَشَدَ — root ر-ش-د.** *Rushd*: finding the right way, maturity, hitting the mark. Its opposites are *ghayy* (going astray) and *safah* (lightness of mind).

The word has a life in jurisprudence: *rushd* is a person's reaching the maturity to manage his own property (al-Nisāʾ 4/6). *Rushd*, that is, is not an abstract rightness but the competence **to see to one's own affairs**.

**And this stands face to face with *lā yaʿqilūn* in the fourth verse:**

| 49/4 | 49/7 |
|---|---|
| *lā yaʿqilūn* — they do not use their reason | *al-rāshidūn* — those who have reached *rushd* |
| Root: ع-ق-ل — to bind, to hold | Root: ر-ش-د — to find the right way, to come to maturity |
| Those who do not acknowledge the boundary | Those who find their direction |

The first seven verses of the sūra are built between these two poles.

**And the *ḍamīr al-faṣl* (the pronoun of separation) is used:** *ulāʾika **hum** al-rāshidūn* — "these, precisely, are the ones". This structure makes the ruling definite; it will be used several more times in the sūra (11, 15).

## 49/8 — فَضْلًا مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ وَنِعْمَةً وَٱللَّهُ عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

*Faḍlan mina'llāhi wa niʿmah, wa'llāhu ʿalīmun ḥakīm*

"As a bounty from God and a favour. God is Knowing, Wise."

### The shortest verse of the sūra

The eighth verse completes the sentence of the seventh. It is not a sentence on its own; it is a note added to the end of the preceding verse.

### The debate in grammar

*Faḍlan* comes in the accusative, and the grammarians differ as to what it is:

| View | Its case function | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **Object of purpose (*mafʿūl lahu*)** | An object stating cause | "…**so that** it might be a bounty" |
| **Cognate accusative (*mafʿūl muṭlaq*)** | The verbal noun of an omitted verb | "He **did this as** a bounty" |
| **Circumstantial (*ḥāl*)** | An element stating condition | "This came about **in the manner** of a bounty" |

All three explanations are recorded in the sources and none of them changes the sense in any essential way. **I impose no preference.**

**The common conclusion is this:** what was counted in the preceding verse — faith being made beloved, denial being made repellent — is not recorded as the achievement of the one addressed.

### The function of the verse

The place of this verse in the sūra is more important than it looks.

The seventh verse ended with a praise: *ulāʾika humu'r-rāshidūn* — "these are the ones who are rightly directed." That praise could have produced in the one addressed a feeling of **entitlement**. The eighth verse cuts this off at once: *faḍlan mina'llāh* — this is a bounty.

**And this movement will be repeated in the sūra's last block (17):**

> *"They count it a favour to you that they have submitted. Say: Do not count your submission a favour to me; **rather it is God who has bestowed a favour on you in guiding you to faith**."* (49/17)

**The parallel between the two places is exact:** in both, coming to faith is taken out of the sphere of a claim of right and recorded as a bounty. The eighth verse says this to those who believe; the seventeenth to those who say they believe.

I record this parallel as my own reading; the shared vocabulary of the two verses (*faḍl* / *yamunnu*, both stating a giving) and the similarity of position stand in the text.

### عَلِيمٌ حَكِيمٌ

**عَلِيم** — the Knowing. **حَكِيم — root ح-ك-م.** The concrete sense of this root is **to hinder, to rein in**: the bit put in a horse's mouth is called *ḥakama*. *Ḥukm*, *ḥikma* and *ḥākim* come from the same root, and all of them state a work of **holding and putting in place**.

**And the root is here a kin of the sūra's name:** ح-ج-ر hinders by enclosing, ح-ك-م hinders by reining in. Both roots carry an idea of boundary.

I record this as an observation about the language, not as a claim.

**The work of the name here:** what the seventh verse relates is an ordering — faith made beloved, denial made repellent. *Ḥakīm* states that this ordering is not arbitrary.

### The whole of the second block (6-8)

Three verses regulated the distance between knowledge and action:

| Verse | Its note |
|---|---|
| **6** | A report coming from outside: verify it, do not hurry |
| **7** | A demand coming from inside: do not impose the carrying out of your own view |
| **8** | And the right direction you hold is not your own achievement |

**The block closes off three different errors about knowledge:** trusting false information, putting one's own wish in the place of knowledge, and claiming what is right as one's own.

## 49/9 — وَإِن طَآئِفَتَانِ مِنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ ٱقْتَتَلُوا۟ فَأَصْلِحُوا۟ بَيْنَهُمَا فَإِنۢ بَغَتْ إِحْدَىٰهُمَا عَلَى ٱلْأُخْرَىٰ فَقَٰتِلُوا۟ ٱلَّتِى تَبْغِى حَتَّىٰ تَفِىٓءَ إِلَىٰٓ أَمْرِ ٱللَّهِ فَإِن فَآءَتْ فَأَصْلِحُوا۟ بَيْنَهُمَا بِٱلْعَدْلِ وَأَقْسِطُوٓا۟ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُقْسِطِينَ

*Wa in ṭāʾifatāni mina'l-muʾminīna'qtatalū fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā, fa-in baghat iḥdāhumā ʿala'l-ukhrā fa-qātilu'llatī tabghī ḥattā tafīʾa ilā amri'llāh, fa-in fāʾat fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā bi'l-ʿadli wa aqsiṭū, inna'llāha yuḥibbu'l-muqsiṭīn*

"If two groups of the believers fight one another, set things right between them. If one of them attacks the other, then fight the one that attacks until it returns to God's command. If it returns, set things right between them with justice, and act equitably. God loves those who act equitably."

### The single most important detail of the word order: "of the believers"

In the first clause of the verse stands the single most striking expression in the sūra: ***ṭāʾifatāni mina'l-muʾminīn*** — "two groups of the believers."

**Two sides that have come to armed conflict are still called "believers".**

To see how unusual this is, one must look at what the verse does not do. The verse does not say:

- "One of the two groups is not to be counted as having believed."
- "Those who come to conflict cease to be believers."
- "Of the two, one is a believer and the other is not."

And, more importantly: **the attacking side is not named either.** The verse does not call it *kāfir*, does not call it *munāfiq*, does not even call it *fāsiq*. The only word it uses is a verb: *allatī **tabghī*** — "the one that attacks". Not an epithet but a **verb**. What is described, that is, is not the identity of the persons but what they are doing at that moment.

And the next verse will carry this further still: *innama'l-muʾminūna **ikhwa*** — "the believers are but brothers." The two sides in conflict are not merely counted as believers; they are counted as **brothers**.

**The consequence of this detail of word order is heavy and must be written plainly:** the text does not put the sides of a conflict outside the religion. What the Qurʾān sets up here is not a ruling about the identity of the sides but a ruling about their **conduct**. This is the sharpest instance of the measure recorded again and again in this commentary: what the verse describes are **qualities**, not identities.

What the neglect of this measure has produced in Islamic history is well known: in internal conflicts, the sides declaring one another outside the religion. The wording of the verse is closed to this, and the great majority of the classical scholars have recorded, on the basis of this verse, that the sides of an internal conflict cannot be declared unbelievers.

**I write this as a note of history and of legal theory; no side is taken in this text about any current conflict.**

### طَآئِفَة — a group

**Root: ط-و-ف.** This root was treated in the sections on al-Raḥmān and al-Wāqiʿa; its root sense is **to turn, to go round about**. *Ṭawāf*, *ṭāʾif* and *ṭāʾifa* are from the same root.

*Ṭāʾifa*, according to what the lexicographers record, comes from the idea of "a company that turns about a thing"; in usage it means **a group of people**, and its limit in number is indefinite — definitions ranging from "at least one person" to "a company" are transmitted in the sources.

The indefiniteness serves a purpose here: the verse does not fix the scale of the conflict. It may be two people, or it may be two tribes.

### ٱقْتَتَلُوا۟ — a dual subject, a plural verb

This is the most discussed grammatical point of the verse.

The subject is **dual**: *ṭāʾifatāni* — "two groups". But the verb comes in the **plural**: *iqtatalū* — "they fought" (for three and more).

The expected form would have been *iqtatalā* (dual). Why the plural?

The explanation the grammarians record is this: ***ṭāʾifa* is singular in wording but a company in sense.** When "two groups" is said, those actually fighting are not two persons but two crowds. The verb has followed not the wording but the **sense**. In Arabic this is called *ḥaml ʿala'l-maʿnā* (construction according to sense).

**And the real beauty of the word order is here:** after the verb has come in the plural, the command that follows immediately returns to the **dual**: *fa-aṣliḥū **baynahumā*** — "set things right between **the two of them**."

| Element | Number | What it relates |
|---|---|---|
| *ṭāʾifatāni* | Dual (2) | There are two sides |
| *iqtatalū* | Plural (3+) | The crowds that fight |
| *baynahumā* | Dual (2) | The two sides to be reconciled |

**The sequence is: two → many → two.**

I record this as an observation about the word order, and I add the following reading as **my own**: when a conflict begins the sides scatter, grow into crowds, and who is with whom becomes unclear — the verb goes into the plural. The work of reconciliation, on the other hand, again presupposes **two sides**; for an agreement can be made only between two sides with definite parties. The verse makes a scattered conflict solvable by reducing it once more to two sides.

**Form: Form VIII (*iftiʿāl*)** — *iqtatala*. This form here states reciprocity: they fought one another. The verse, that is, describes not a one-sided attack but a mutual conflict — the determining of the attacking side will come at a second stage.

### فَأَصْلِحُوا۟ — iṣlāḥ

**Root: ص-ل-ح.** This root was treated in the sections on al-Qalam and al-Munāfiqūn; the sense recorded: **a thing's being in a serviceable, sound, fit condition.** *Ṣalāḥ* — soundness. *Ṣulḥ* — peace: the mending of a relation that has broken down. Its opposite is *fasād*.

**Form: Form IV (*ifʿāl*)** — *aṣlaḥa*: to set right, to repair. The verb, that is, is transitive: someone else sets a thing right.

**And the picture the root carries is important: *iṣlāḥ* is not making from nothing but repairing what has broken.** When a vessel is broken, the work done is *iṣlāḥ*; not the making of a new vessel. The word presupposes that what is repaired **was sound before**.

This carries the whole logic of the verse: between the two sides in conflict there **is** a relation to be repaired. The tenth verse will give it its name: brotherhood.

**Who is the one addressed by the command?** The verb is plural: *aṣliḥū* — "set right". And the one addressed is neither the first nor the second side; it is **the third party**. The verse, that is, lays an obligation on those who stand outside the conflict.

**This is the most practical side of the verse and is worth recording:** to stand outside a conflict, on the verse's terms, produces not a right to neutrality but an **obligation to intervene**. Standing aside is not counted among the options.

### بَغَتْ — the root ب-غ-ي

**Root: ب-غ-ي.** Its root sense is **to want, to seek, to demand.** *Ibtighāʾ* — seeking; *bughya* — the thing sought. In the Qurʾān: *"**Seek** of God's bounty"* (al-Jumuʿa 62/10) — *wa'btaghū min faḍli'llāh*.

**How, then, does one pass from "to want" to "to attack"?**

The transition the lexicographers record is this: the demand's **overstepping the bound.** A person wants what is his; when he begins to want what is not his, the act turns into *baghy*. *Baghy*, that is, is wanting in its boundary-overstepping form.

The derivatives of the root show this line:
- **بَغْي (*baghy*)** — wrongdoing, injustice, attack.
- **بَاغٍ (*bāghī*)** — one who oversteps, who attacks. Its plural is *bughāt*.
- **بَغِيّ (*baghiyy*)** — used in one place in the Qurʾān of unchastity: *"Nor have I been unchaste"* (Maryam 19/20).

**The common idea: a wanting that oversteps a boundary.**

**And this agrees with the whole of the sūra.** *Fisq* was coming out of the husk; *baghy* is a demand that oversteps a boundary; *ḥujra* is the boundary itself. The sūra is woven with words of boundary.

**The striking side of the usage in the verse:** the verse begins with *baghat* (perfect: it attacked) and continues with *allatī **tabghī*** (imperfect: the one that is attacking). The object, that is, is the side **whose attack continues**. When the attack ends the description ends too — and the close of the verse will say this openly.

### حَتَّىٰ تَفِىٓءَ — the root ف-ي-ء and the afternoon shadow

This is the most beautiful word in the verse.

**Root: ف-ي-ء.** The root sense the lexicographers record: **to turn, to come back.**

And the concrete picture of the root is this: **الفَيْء (*fayʾ*)** is the shadow that **comes back** after the sun has passed its zenith.

Arabic distinguishes two words for shadow, and the difference lies exactly here:

| Word | Root | Which shadow |
|---|---|---|
| **ظِلّ (*ẓill*)** | ظ-ل-ل | Shadow in general; and especially the shadow that **shortens** from morning to noon |
| **فَيْء (*fayʾ*)** | ف-ي-ء | The shadow that **comes back and lengthens** after noon |

The picture is this: in the morning the shadow is long; as the sun rises it shortens; at noon it comes to its shortest — it almost disappears; then it **comes back** and lengthens until evening.

**That is *fayʾ*: the return of the shadow.** The coming back of what was thought to be lost.

**And that this word was chosen in the verse is worth noticing.** The verse could have said "*ḥattā tarjiʿa* (until it returns)" — that is the common verb for returning in Arabic. It chooses the verb *tafīʾa*.

What the difference brings I record as **my own reading**: *rujūʿ* is a neutral returning — in any direction. *Fayʾ*, however, is a returning **to one's own former place**, and it is the name of a natural event: the shadow turns back not under compulsion but of itself, by the course of the sun. The word pictures the return not as a surrender but as **a coming back to a natural state**.

The attacking side, that is, is called not to "be defeated and surrender" but **to return to its place**.

**The same root occurs in the Qurʾān as a legal term too:** *fayʾ* is property obtained without fighting and "returning" to the community (al-Ḥashr 59/6-7). In the section on al-Ḥashr this verse (49/9) had already been named. What the two uses share is the same: a thing's returning to where it belongs.

### حَتَّىٰ — the limit of the fighting

The most critical word in the command is this: **"until."**

*Fa-qātilu'llatī tabghī* — fight the side that attacks. But the sentence does not end there: *ḥattā tafīʾa ilā amri'llāh* — until it returns to God's command.

**The fighting, that is, has a purpose and an end point, and neither of them is the destruction of the attacking side.** The purpose: return. The end point: the return's taking place.

**And the verse says as well what is to be done after the return:** *fa-in fāʾat fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā bi'l-ʿadl* — "if it returns, set things right between them **with justice**."

Note: after the return, no punishment of the attacking side is commanded. What is commanded is a **repetition** of the command given at the head of the verse: set things right between them. But this time with a qualification: *bi'l-ʿadl.*

**There is a ring in the word order:**

> *fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā* (beginning) → fighting → *fa-aṣliḥū baynahumā bi'l-ʿadl* (end)

The same command, twice. The fighting in the middle does not take the place of the command — it is a parenthesis opened so that the command can be carried out. **Iṣlāḥ stands both before and after the conflict.**

I record this as my own reading, but the ring stands in the text and the words are repeated exactly.

### بِٱلْعَدْلِ and أَقْسِطُوا۟ — two words for justice

At the end of the verse two different roots come one after the other, and both are translated "justice":

**عَدْل — root ع-د-ل.** Its root sense is **to make equal, to level, to make straight.** The two pans of a balance coming even. *Taʿdīl* — setting straight, evening out.

**قِسْط — root ق-س-ط.** Its root sense is **a share, a portion**; and from this **to give a share as it is due.** *Qisṭ* — a portion. *Muqsiṭ* — one who gives the portion rightly.

**The interesting side of the root:** ق-س-ط, when it goes into **Form IV** (*aqsaṭa*), states acting justly, whereas in its **bare triliteral** form (*qasaṭa*) it states **acting wrongfully**. The same root takes opposite senses according to the form — it is among the instances the lexicographers name under the heading *aḍdād*. In the Qurʾān: *"As for those who act wrongfully, they have become fuel for Hell"* (al-Jinn 72/15) — *wa ammā'l-**qāsiṭūna***.

This distinction was treated in the section on al-Jinn.

**The difference between the two words:**

| | **عدل** | **قسط** |
|---|---|---|
| Picture | The balance — evening out | The share — giving what is due |
| Vantage | On the whole: are the two sides even | On the party: has each received his share |

In the classical sources this distinction is recorded in different ways and there is no complete agreement. **I impose no preference.**

**But that the two come together in the verse says something:** the work of reconciliation must have regard both to the balance of the whole (*ʿadl*) and to the right of each side (*qisṭ*). A conflict can be ended "by silencing both sides" — that gives balance but does not give what is due. Or it can be ended by giving one side everything it claims — that gives what is due but does not establish balance. The verse asks for both at once.

**إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُقْسِطِينَ** — This expression occurs in several places in the Qurʾān: al-Māʾida 5/42, this verse, and al-Mumtaḥina 60/8. It was named in the sections on al-Jinn and al-Mumtaḥina.

### The debates drawn from this verse in classical jurisprudence

This verse is the principal ground of a discussion known in classical jurisprudence as *aḥkām al-bughāt* (the law concerning the attacking group). The subject is what is to be done when armed division arises within a Muslim community.

**I issue no legal ruling here.** I relate the headings debated in the classical sources together with their disagreements. Not one of the lines below is a preference of this text and **none of them is binding.**

| The question debated | The views recorded |
|---|---|
| **Is the *bāghī* declared an unbeliever?** | In the mainstream schools of law, **no** — the verse calls them "believers". Wide agreement is recorded on this point |
| **Is their property counted as spoils?** | The majority say **no**; the rules of the law of war concerning an external enemy do not operate here |
| **What becomes of their captives?** | They cannot be enslaved; the widespread view is that they must be released once the conflict ends |
| **Is the fleeing man pursued, is the wounded man killed?** | The majority say **no**; the ground is the qualification *ḥattā tafīʾa* in the verse — the purpose is return, not destruction |
| **What is done first?** | Fighting is not entered upon directly; it is recorded that first the cause must be asked, the doubt removed, the call made. The verse's own first command is *iṣlāḥ* as well |
| **Who carries out the command?** | Some see this as within the authority of the legitimate power; some count it a general obligation of the community. The divergence is plain at this point |

**The last line is particularly important and requires a note.** The command of the verse is plural (*fa-qātilū*) and its addressee is indefinite. Some of the classical scholars have not read this as the authority to stop a conflict being handed out to everyone at large; on the contrary, they have tied it to the office that bears responsibility for order. The ground is practical: reading the command as open to all could produce the very thing the verse means to prevent — the spreading of the conflict.

**This text makes no choice.** But this must be recorded: the order the verse itself sets up is plain and beyond dispute — **first *iṣlāḥ*, then (if necessary) stopping, then *iṣlāḥ* again.** To read the verse beginning from the middle item is to break the text's word order.

### Historical background

According to what is transmitted in the sources, the verse came down over a quarrel that broke out between two groups in Medina and went on with slaps or light blows. Some reports state that the sides struck one another with palm branches and sandals — that is, the scale is small.

I relate this account with the note "it is transmitted"; there are differences in the details and I give no names.

**But the scale of the account is worth recording:** if the report is sound, the verse came down not over a great civil war but over **a small quarrel**. And even so its language is extremely heavy: *iqtatalū* (they fought), *fa-qātilū* (fight).

One explanation of this is the following, and it is pointed to in the classical sources as well: **the way to prevent small conflicts from growing is not to treat them as small.** By describing in the language of war an incident still at the level of a quarrel, the verse records the weight of the matter from the start.

I relate this as an explanation, not as a settled ruling.

### What this says to the present

In writing what this verse says to the present I enter into no current conflict; this is the plain rule of the method (`USLUP.md`). What can be recorded is the **structure** of the verse, and the structure is general:

**One. The responsibility laid on the third party.** The verse addresses not the two sides in conflict but those outside. To stand aside in a dispute does not figure as an option in the verse's sequence.

**Two. The fixing of the purpose.** The qualification *ḥattā* ("until") placed at the end of the command limits the purpose of the intervention. The purpose is not to finish off one side but to stop the conflict. Intervention without a purpose produces nothing but the spreading of the conflict to the third party.

**Three. The preservation of identity.** This is the verse's firmest note: the sides do not lose their identity even while they are fighting. To count the other side in a dispute as "no longer one of us" in effect destroys the ground of any agreement. In order to preserve that ground the verse goes on calling both sides in conflict by the same name.

**Four. That the ending has two stages.** The stopping of the conflict and the repairing of the relation are separate works, and the verse commands each separately. A quarrel that has merely been silenced has not ended.

## 49/10 — إِنَّمَا ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ إِخْوَةٌ فَأَصْلِحُوا۟ بَيْنَ أَخَوَيْكُمْ وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ

*Innama'l-muʾminūna ikhwatun fa-aṣliḥū bayna akhawaykum, wa'ttaqu'llāha laʿallakum turḥamūn*

"The believers are but brothers. So set things right between your two brothers. And guard yourselves against God, that you may be shown mercy."

### إِنَّمَا — the particle of restriction

**Root: *inna* + *mā*.** As was recorded in the section on al-Mursalāt, *innamā* is the particle of **restriction (*ḥaṣr*)**, and this very verse was given as the example: *"Only, solely — innama'l-muʾminūna ikhwa."*

The work of restriction is this: it locks a ruling onto a subject and cuts off what is outside. The sentence "the believers are brothers" gives a piece of information; the sentence "the believers are **but** brothers" leaves room for no other form of relation.

**And the place where the particle stands is important:** the ninth verse related an armed conflict. The tenth verse, immediately after that conflict, gives the **single** definition of the relation. The sentence, that is, is placed not as a wish but as a finding: even if there has been a conflict, the name of the relation has not changed.

The same pattern will come again in the fifteenth verse: *innama'l-muʾminūna'lladhīna āmanū bi'llāhi wa rasūlih*. **The two *innamā* sentences say two different things about the believers:** the one their relation with one another (brotherhood), the other their definition (the conditions of faith). The two stand in two different blocks of the sūra and complete one another.

### إِخْوَة — brotherhood

**Root: أ-خ-و.** *Akh* — brother. Its plural comes in two forms, and **there is a difference of usage between them:**

| Plural | Generally | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **إِخْوَة (*ikhwa*)** | Brotherhood of descent (lineage) | Yūsuf 12/7: *In Joseph and his **brothers** there are signs* — *wa ikhwatihī* |
| **إِخْوَان (*ikhwān*)** | Brotherhood of friendship, companionship | *"As brothers"* (al-Ḥijr 15/47) |

**This distinction is a tendency the lexicographers record, not an absolute rule** — in the Qurʾān both plurals are found in different contexts. I do not present it as a settled ruling.

**But that the form *ikhwa* was chosen in the verse is worth recording**, and the classical commentaries dwell on this: if the distinction holds, then the verse names the bond among believers with the pattern of **lineage** brotherhood and not of **friendship**.

And the difference has a practical consequence: friendship is chosen and can be given up; brotherhood is not chosen and cannot be given up. A person may be angry with his brother, may fall out with him, may not speak to him for years — but he cannot make him cease to be his brother.

**Read together with the ninth verse, this explains why the verse stands where it does:** the relation of the two sides in conflict is defined as being of a kind that does not come to an end with the conflict.

I record this as a reading conditional on the distinction's holding.

### بَيْنَ أَخَوَيْكُمْ — "between your two brothers"

The most striking detail of the word order is here.

The head of the sentence is plural: *al-muʾminūna ikhwatun* — the believers are brothers. The command is plural too: *aṣliḥū* — set right.

But the party to be set right is in the **dual**: *bayna **akhawaykum*** — "between your **two** brothers".

The expected form would have been *bayna ikhwatikum* (between your brothers). The verse uses the dual.

**Two explanations of this are recorded:**

| Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|
| **A link back to the ninth verse** | There were two sides there (*ṭāʾifatān*); the dual here refers back to that |
| **Making it concrete** | However large the conflict, the work of setting right is done each time between **two persons** |

**The second explanation is worth recording, and I stress it as my own reading:** a rift within a community is not repaired in the mass. There is no such operation as "let everyone make peace". Reconciliation is always made between two sides, one pair at a time. The verse's use of the dual shows the scale of the operation: two people, then two more.

**A note on the variant readings:** the sources state that a plural reading *بَيْنَ إِخْوَتِكُمْ* (*ikhwatikum* — your brothers) is also transmitted. **Since I cannot state with certainty which imam it belongs to, I write no name.** This reading removes the emphasis of the second explanation above and generalises the command; it does not change the substance of the sense.

### وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمْ تُرْحَمُونَ

The third occurrence of **taqwā** in the sūra (see 49/1). In the first verse it was placed at the end of the relation with the Prophet; here it is placed at the end of the relation with brothers.

**تُرْحَمُونَ — root ر-ح-م.** *Raḥim* — the womb. It was treated in the section on al-Fātiḥa.

**And that the verb is passive is worth recording:** *turḥamūn* — "that you may be shown mercy". The agent is not named.

**Moreover the choice of the root is meaningful and cannot be counted a coincidence in this verse:** the root ر-ح-م comes from *raḥim* (the womb), and *raḥim* is at the same time **the name of the bond of kinship** — in Arabic relatives are called *dhawu'l-arḥām* (those of the wombs).

The verse, that is, ends a sentence that speaks of brotherhood with a word whose root is kinship: *ikhwa* (brothers) → *turḥamūn* (you will be shown mercy).

**The link is this:** those who treat one another as brothers are met with a mercy whose source is kinship. I record this as my own reading; the semantic field of the two words is plain, but I make no claim that the verse sets up such a link.

### The whole of the third block (9-10)

Two verses regulated a conflict from beginning to end:

| Stage | 49/9 | 49/10 |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Two groups are fighting | — |
| First operation | Set things right between them | — |
| Deviation | One of them attacks | — |
| Response | Stop the attacker | — |
| Limit | Until it returns | — |
| Final operation | Set things right with justice | — |
| **Ground** | *mina'l-muʾminīn* | ***ikhwa*** — the name of the relation |

The tenth verse is the **ground** of the ninth. The ninth verse said what is to be done; the tenth says why it is to be done.

And the ground is not an advantage but a **definition**: they are brothers. The verse does not say "peace is more useful" or "conflict does harm". It says what the relation is and derives the command from there.

## 49/11 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ لَا يَسْخَرْ قَوْمٌ مِّن قَوْمٍ عَسَىٰٓ أَن يَكُونُوا۟ خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ وَلَا نِسَآءٌ مِّن نِّسَآءٍ عَسَىٰٓ أَن يَكُنَّ خَيْرًا مِّنْهُنَّ وَلَا تَلْمِزُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَكُمْ وَلَا تَنَابَزُوا۟ بِٱلْأَلْقَٰبِ بِئْسَ ٱلِٱسْمُ ٱلْفُسُوقُ بَعْدَ ٱلْإِيمَٰنِ وَمَن لَّمْ يَتُبْ فَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلظَّٰلِمُونَ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū lā yaskhar qawmun min qawmin ʿasā an yakūnū khayran minhum wa lā nisāʾun min nisāʾin ʿasā an yakunna khayran minhunn, wa lā talmizū anfusakum wa lā tanābazū bi'l-alqāb, biʾsa'l-ismu'l-fusūqu baʿda'l-īmān, wa man lam yatub fa-ulāʾika humu'ẓ-ẓālimūn*

"O you who believe! Let no people mock another people; it may be that they are better than themselves. Nor let women mock other women; it may be that they are better than themselves. Do not find fault with yourselves, and do not fix nicknames on one another. How evil is the name of transgression after faith! And whoever does not repent — these are the wrongdoers."

### Three prohibitions

The verse forbids three separate acts, and all three are things done with the tongue:

| Prohibition | Verb | Root | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | *lā yaskhar* | س-خ-ر | To mock |
| **2** | *lā talmizū* | ل-م-ز | To find fault, to pick at a flaw |
| **3** | *lā tanābazū* | ن-ب-ز | To fix a nickname |

And there is an ordering among the three: mockery is an **attitude**, fault-finding a **gesture**, a nickname a **lasting record**. The order runs from the passing to the permanent.

### سَخِرَ — mockery

**Root: س-خ-ر.** This verse was named in the section on al-Ḥāqqa. The root sense the lexicographers record is **to put someone under a yoke, to use him for one's service, to make him subject to oneself**.

And this is visible in the root's second life: **تَسْخِير (*taskhīr*)** — it occurs many times in the Qurʾān and means **a thing's being placed at man's service**: *"He made the sun and the moon **subservient** to you"* (Ibrāhīm 14/33).

**The same root, that is, carries both "to mock" and "to place in service".**

This common ground shows what mockery is, and it is pointed to in the classical sources: **the one who mocks makes the person he mocks the instrument of his own amusement.** The other is no longer a subject but an object with which something is done.

**And this joins with the conclusion recorded in the section on al-Humaza for *hamz* and *lamz*.** It was written there: *"Both treat a human being as an object: for the one an object to be pushed down, for the other an object to be picked over for faults."* Mockery is the third form of the same operation: **an object to be used.**

This is the idea common to the three roots: the removal of the other person from the position of subject.

### قَوْمٌ مِّن قَوْمٍ — two indefinite peoples

Two indefinite nouns stand side by side in the word order: *qawmun min qawmin* — "a people at another people".

The indefinite usage generalises the ruling: which people is not said, because it makes no difference.

**And more importantly: since the sides are not named, who is the mocker and who the mocked is not fixed.** Today's mocker is tomorrow's mocked. The verse records this by naming both sides with the same indefinite word.

**The separate mention of women** is debated in the classical exegesis:

| View | Ground |
|---|---|
| The word *qawm* is in origin used of **men** (its root ق-و-م — one who stands, who carries the affair on); for this reason women are mentioned separately | Some of the lexicographers record this usage |
| *Qawm* already includes women; the separate mention is for **emphasis** | The general use of the word *qawm* in the Qurʾān |
| Because in the account of the occasion of revelation the incident took place among women, they are mentioned separately | An explanation resting on transmitted report |

**I impose no preference; all three explanations are recorded in the sources.**

**But there is something that can be recorded in the word order:** the prohibition is repeated twice, with the same structure, and in the second repetition the feminine plural form *ʿasā an yakunna* is used. The sentence, that is, does not merely say "women are included"; it **builds the prohibition afresh** for women. To be covered by a ruling and to be addressed separately are different things.

### عَسَىٰٓ أَن يَكُونُوا۟ خَيْرًا مِّنْهُمْ — the suspending of judgment

This is the strongest place in the verse, and the form of the reason given is worth noticing.

The verse does not forbid mockery with these grounds: "because they too are human", "because it is coarse", "because they will be hurt".

The ground it gives has to do with **knowledge**: **it may be that they are better than you.**

**عَسَىٰ** — in Arabic this is a word stating *tarajjī* (hope) and possibility: "it may be that, perhaps". It states no certainty.

**And that the ground is built with this indefinite word is the whole logic of the verse.**

The verse does not say "they are better than you" — that too would be a judgment, only in the opposite direction. It says: **you do not know.**

Mockery is in essence an act of ranking: the one who mocks places himself above the one he mocks. That placing is a claim to knowledge — "I am superior to him". The verse removes the **ground** of that claim.

**And removing the ground is different from refuting the claim.** The verse does not set up a counter-ranking; it says that **ranking cannot be done**.

**This is directly connected with the thirteenth verse of the sūra, and the link is worth recording:**

> **49/11:** *ʿasā an yakūnū khayran minhum* — it may be that they are better (it cannot be known).
> **49/13:** *inna akramakum ʿinda'llāhi atqākum* — the most honoured of you with God is the one who guards himself most.

The eleventh verse suspends human ranking; the thirteenth says where the real ranking is done. And the measure of that ranking — taqwā — is something a person cannot see.

**Together the two verses set up this:** there is a ranking of worth among people, but that ranking cannot be made by people. The impossibility of mockery comes from here: mockery is the announcing of the result of a measurement that cannot be made.

I record this as my own reading; but that the two verses stand in the same sūra, two verses apart, is the text's own word order.

### وَلَا تَلْمِزُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَكُمْ — "do not find fault with yourselves"

**Root: ل-م-ز.** This root **was treated in detail in the section on al-Humaza** and this verse was named there as well. I do not repeat it; the summary is this:

- *Lamz*: to find fault, to pick at a flaw, to belittle with a movement of the brow or eye, to point at someone in a way that lowers him.
- On the difference between *hamz* and *lamz* there are **mutually contradictory definitions** in the classical sources; they were given in a table in the section on al-Humaza and none was preferred.
- The conclusion drawn there: the common ground of the two words is the act itself — to diminish someone — and the difference lies in the instruments of that act.

**What is to be taken up here is the single thing not present in al-Humaza: the object.**

### The work of the object: *anfusakum*

The verse does not say **"do not find fault with one another"**. What it says literally is: ***anfusakum*** — **"do not find fault with yourselves."**

This point was indicated in the section on al-Humaza and recorded thus: *"Note: not 'one another' but literally 'yourselves' — anfusakum. That is, whoever finds fault with another has found fault with himself."*

Now it is necessary to open out how this point works.

**In Arabic the form *anfusakum* has two readings:**

| Reading | Sense | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Reciprocity** | "One another" | The word *nafs* sometimes states reciprocity in the Qurʾān: *"do not kill yourselves"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/29) is widely explained in the sense of "do not kill one another" |
| **Unity** | "Yourselves" — because the believers are like a single soul | The definition of brotherhood in the tenth verse |

**Both explanations are in the classical sources and they do not exclude one another.** I impose no preference.

**But what the second explanation carries deserves to be opened out here, and I record this as my own reading.**

The verse looks as if it deliberately leaves the word indefinite, because on either reading something true is being said:

**One.** To find fault with a person is to attribute a flaw to him. And for the attributing of a flaw to work, a shared measure is needed — a ground on which it is agreed that "this is a flaw". Whoever accepts that ground has placed himself on the same ground. The fault-finder, that is, has laid down a measure for himself as well, and he is subject to it.

**Two.** Within a community, the finding of fault with one person does not stay confined to the one it is aimed at. Once it is accepted that a given flaw is something one may be laughed at for, everyone who carries that flaw — today or tomorrow — becomes the object of the same operation. Fault-finding is done not to a person but to **a category**.

**Three.** And by the definition of the tenth verse: the believers are brothers. Your brother's flaw is written to your own account.

**The real beauty of the word order is this:** the verse does not say "do not find fault with another, because he too is a human being like you" — that would be a piece of advice. **It changes the object grammatically.** It takes the target of the verb away from the other and gives it back to the agent. It builds the prohibition, that is, not with a moral note but with a **grammatical move**.

### وَلَا تَنَابَزُوا۟ بِٱلْأَلْقَٰبِ — nicknames

**Root: ن-ب-ز.** *Nabz* — a nickname, the fixing of a name; especially a **disparaging** name. The lexicographers record *al-nabz* as "to call a person by a name he does not like".

**Form: Form VI (*tafāʿul*)** — *tanābaza*. This form states **reciprocity**: do not fix nicknames on one another.

**And the choice of the form says something:** the fixing of nicknames does not remain one-sided. Within a community nicknames are produced reciprocally; one man fixes a name, the other answers. The form records this cycle.

**A note on a root close in sound:** the root ن-ب-ذ (*nabadha* — to throw, to cast, to abandon) is close to it in sound and occurs in the Qurʾān (*"they cast the Book behind their backs"* — Āl ʿImrān 3/187, *nabadhūhu*). **I make no claim** that there is a link of derivation between the two roots; their last letters differ (ز / ذ) and the lexicographers treat them under separate roots. But the nearness of their semantic fields — throwing a thing and throwing a name at a person — is an association that can be recorded, and I build no interpretation upon it.

**أَلْقَاب — the plural of لَقَب.** Root **ل-ق-ب**. A *laqab* is a name given to a person besides his real name.

**And a note is needed here:** the verse does not forbid all nicknames. In Arabic and in Islamic history complimentary by-names are common and have not been censured. What is forbidden is what the rest of the verse says: **the name of transgression.** That is, the name that diminishes a person, that proclaims his flaw, that he does not like.

The classical exegetes record this distinction, and the verse's own sentence supports it.

### بِئْسَ ٱلِٱسْمُ ٱلْفُسُوقُ بَعْدَ ٱلْإِيمَٰنِ

**بِئْسَ** — in Arabic a *verb of dispraise*: a frozen verb stating blame. Its counterpart is *niʿma* (how excellent).

There are two directions to the sense of the sentence in the classical sources:

| View | What *al-ism* answers to | The sense of the sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **A nickname** | The name fixed | "How evil it is, after faith, to fix on someone the name of 'transgression'" |
| **Repute, being spoken of** | The quality a person is known by (*dhikr*, *ṣīt*) | "How evil a name it is to be known for transgression after faith" |

**The first view agrees with the verse's coming immediately after the prohibition of nicknames.** The second agrees with the note about repentance at the end of the verse — one who repents has removed the name from himself.

**I impose no preference; both are recorded in the sources and neither is binding.**

**This is the third and last occurrence of the root ف-س-ق in the sūra.** The three occurrences form a chain:

| Occurrence | Form | What |
|---|---|---|
| **49/6** | *fāsiqun* — an active participle | The **person** who brings the report |
| **49/7** | *al-fusūq* — a verbal noun | The **act** made repellent |
| **49/11** | *al-fusūq* — a verbal noun, but as a *name* | The **name** that is fixed |

**The word moves from a person to an act, and from an act to a name.** And in the last occurrence the verse qualifies the word as a "name": *biʾsa'l-ismu'l-fusūq*. The sūra, that is, comes to count calling someone a "transgressor" as itself transgression.

I record this as my own reading; but the forms of the three occurrences stand in the text and can be counted.

### وَمَن لَّمْ يَتُبْ فَأُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلظَّٰلِمُونَ

The verse qualifies the one who does not repent as a *wrongdoer*.

**The root ظ-ل-م** was treated in the section on al-Jumuʿa: **to put a thing somewhere other than its place.** Wrongdoing, at the root of the word, is an error of **displacement**.

**And this coincides exactly with the whole subject of the verse.** Mockery, fault-finding and nicknames — all three are operations of taking a person from the place he is in and putting him somewhere else: lower. The word *ẓulm* is the exact name of that operation.

I record this as an observation about the language; the sense of the root was recorded independently in the section on al-Jumuʿa.

**And the structure of the conditional clause is important:** *wa man **lam yatub*** — "whoever **does not repent**". The ruling, that is, is attached not to the one who commits the act but to **the one who does not repent**.

This is a repetition of the feature recorded at the fifth verse: the sūra leaves a way back after every heavy prohibition it lays down. Here the way back is placed inside the wording of the verse itself: the ruling is given after the door has closed.

### Historical background

Several accounts are transmitted in the sources: that a woman mocked another woman on account of her height or her appearance; that people who came to Medina were called by nicknames left over from the age of ignorance; that a group made fun of newcomers.

I relate these accounts with the note "it is transmitted", give no names, and tie none of them to the verse in definite terms.

**What the reports have in common is historically reasonable:** Medina was a place where people from different tribes, different pasts and different economic positions had begun to live together. In such a place a nickname is the easiest way of marking an identity quickly — and it generally works through the most humiliating feature.

### What this says to the present

I do not repeat what was written in the section on al-Humaza about the present-day form of mockery; the measure recorded there was: *"The returns of belittling have risen and its cost has fallen."*

What is to be added here are two things particular to this verse:

**One. The form of the ground.**

The verse does not forbid mockery as a rule of politeness. It forbids it on the ground of a **deficiency of knowledge**: *it may be that they are better than you.*

The practical consequence of this is: to refrain from mockery does not require thinking well of the other. The verse does not say "love them", it says "you cannot pass judgment on them". This is a workable measure — because it demands no feeling from anyone; it asks a person **to give up a claim**.

**Two. The permanence of a nickname.**

Mockery is passing, fault-finding is a moment; a nickname sticks to the person. A name fixed on a person comes before everything that is thought about him and shapes even the knowledge held about him.

Today this operation works differently from before, but more lastingly: namings that identify a person with one sentence, one image or one incident become searchable together with his name and are not erased. The *tanābuz* form the verse uses — reciprocal and ongoing — describes this cycle.

**And the verse's remedy is interesting:** the verse does not command the removing of a nickname (which is most often not possible); it forbids the **fixing** of one. The chain is broken at the first link.

## 49/12 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ ٱجْتَنِبُوا۟ كَثِيرًا مِّنَ ٱلظَّنِّ إِنَّ بَعْضَ ٱلظَّنِّ إِثْمٌ وَلَا تَجَسَّسُوا۟ وَلَا يَغْتَب بَّعْضُكُم بَعْضًا أَيُحِبُّ أَحَدُكُمْ أَن يَأْكُلَ لَحْمَ أَخِيهِ مَيْتًا فَكَرِهْتُمُوهُ وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ تَوَّابٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanu'jtanibū kathīran mina'ẓ-ẓann, inna baʿḍa'ẓ-ẓanni ithm, wa lā tajassasū wa lā yaghtab baʿḍukum baʿḍā, a-yuḥibbu aḥadukum an yaʾkula laḥma akhīhi maytan fa-karihtumūh, wa'ttaqu'llāh, inna'llāha tawwābun raḥīm*

"O you who believe! Keep clear of much conjecture; for some conjecture is sin. Do not pry into one another's hidden affairs, and do not speak behind one another's backs. Would any of you like to eat the flesh of his dead brother? You would loathe it. And guard yourselves against God. God is the Accepter of repentance, the Merciful."

### Three steps: a chain that grows from inside outward

This verse contains one of the most tightly built chains of conduct in the sūra — and probably in the Qurʾān. The three prohibitions are not set out at random.

| Step | Verb | Where it takes place | How many people | Can it be taken back |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Conjecture** | *al-ẓann* | **In the mind** | One person | Yes — no one has heard |
| **2. Prying** | *lā tajassasū* | **In the hand or foot** — searching | One person + his object | Partly — the knowledge now exists |
| **3. Backbiting** | *lā yaghtab* | **On the tongue** — to a third person | Three people | No — the word has entered circulation |

**The chain grows from inside outward, and each step is the product of the one before:**

A conjecture is formed about a person. The conjecture wants to be confirmed — because the human mind tends to close an open file. The search for confirmation is **prying**. And when the search finds something — or fails to find it — that knowledge is shared: **backbiting.**

**The word order of the verse records this growth and cuts the chain at the very beginning.** The prohibition does not begin with backbiting. It begins with conjecture — that is, in the place no one can see, where no harm has yet come about, which no law can reach.

I record this as my own reading; but the order of the three verbs is in the text and has not been reversed.

### ٱجْتَنِبُوا۟ — "do not go near it"

**Root: ج-ن-ب.** Its root sense is **side, flank.** *Janb* — the side of a thing. *Jānib* — a side. *Ajnabī* — a stranger: one who remains at the side, who comes from outside.

**Form: Form VIII (*iftiʿāl*)** — *ijtanaba*: to leave a thing to one side, to draw oneself to the side of it, to keep away.

**And the choice of the verb is meaningful:** the verse does not say *lā taẓunnū* (do not conjecture). It says *ijtanibū* — "do not go near it, stay away from it."

The difference is this: "do not do" forbids an act; "keep away" asks for a **distance**. Conjecture is not a voluntary act — the human mind produces guesses of itself, and it is not possible to stop this by command. The verse does not force this. What it asks is **not to approach** conjecture: not to feed it, not to press on with it, not to enlarge the guess that has been formed.

This determines the verse's practicability. To tell a person "never conjecture" is an empty command. To say "do not go near most conjecture" is a workable command.

### كَثِيرًا مِّنَ ٱلظَّنِّ — "much of" and إِنَّ بَعْضَ ٱلظَّنِّ إِثْمٌ — "some"

The finest logic of the verse lies between these two words, and it is generally skipped.

The verse uses two different quantities:
- **What is to be kept clear of:** *kathīran* — **much.**
- **What is sin:** *baʿḍa* — **some.**

**The area of avoidance, that is, is kept wider than the forbidden area.**

The logic is this: **some** conjecture is sin. But which part it is cannot be known while the conjecture is being formed — if it could be known it would not be conjecture. A person cannot tell in advance whether the guess he is forming is of the sinful kind.

Faced with this uncertainty the verse widens the area of avoidance: **because a small part of it is sin, keep away from much of it.**

**This is the logic of a margin of safety**, and a similar principle is recognised in classical legal theory: in cases that are not certain, the side of caution is preferred. The verse sets up this logic with two words.

**And that neither of the two words is "all" is worth recording.** The verse does not forbid conjecture altogether.

**Root: ظ-ن-ن.** This root was treated at **al-Baqara 2/46** and its two poles were compared in a table at **al-Inshiqāq 84/14** and in **al-Muṭaffifīn 83**. I do not repeat it; the summary is this:

- In Arabic the root can answer both to **a doubtful guess** and to **a firm conviction**; the lexicographers call this *aḍdād* (contrariety of sense).
- The measure drawn out in the section on al-Inshiqāq: *"The Qurʾān does not censure conjecture in itself. What it censures is the putting of an unproven supposition in the place of certain knowledge."*

**And this measure explains exactly the distinction between "much" and "some" here.** Conjecture cannot be forbidden entirely, because human life does not run without guessing: in entering upon a work, in trusting a person, in reckoning a risk, conjecture is used. What is forbidden is conjecture's **taking the place of a judgment** — especially about a person.

**إِثْم — root أ-ث-م.** The sense the lexicographers record: **to leave behind, to delay, to slow down.** *Ithm* is what holds a person back from what is good. Starting from this origin of the word, some lexicographers describe sin as an "obstacle".

### وَلَا تَجَسَّسُوا۟ — ج-س-س and feeling with the hand

**Root: ج-س-س.** The concrete sense the lexicographers record: **to feel by touching with the hand, to search with the hand.**

*Jassa'l-khabara* — he searched out the report. *Jassa'n-nabḍa* — he felt the pulse: the doctor putting his finger to the wrist and trying to learn what is inside.

**And from the same root: جَاسُوس (*jāsūs*) — a spy.** In Turkish the word was taken over in exactly this form, as *casus*. A spy is by definition one who tries to bring into the open what is not visible.

**The concrete picture of the root carries the whole force of the verse:** *tajassus* is not looking with the eye — it is **feeling with the hand.** Trying to understand what is inside a thing by touching its outside. Squeezing a fruit to see whether it is ripe, feeling a bundle to guess what is in it.

The verb, then, describes an attempt to reach the inside of something closed, in spite of its being closed. **And this is the same picture as in the fourth verse:** there were people there calling from behind a wall — the wall stood where it was but was crossed by voice. Here too the covering stands where it is, but an attempt is made to cross it by feeling with the hand.

**The link between the sūra's name and this verse is made here.** A *ḥujra* (ح-ج-ر) is a boundary: what encircles, separates, makes invisible. *Tajassus* is the attempt to learn what is inside in spite of that boundary.

The sūra takes up privacy twice: in the fourth verse as a **physical** boundary, in the twelfth as a boundary of **knowledge**. The two are two states of the same idea, and this shows why the sūra's name is apt.

I record this as my own reading; the shared root stands in the language.

**Form: Form V (*tafaʿʿul*)** — *tajassasa*. This form here states **effort and repetition**: to search with labour, insistently.

**A kindred root: ح-س-س.** *Taḥassus* — to search out by the senses, by hearing. It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"Go and **search out** Joseph and his brother"* (Yūsuf 12/87) — *fa-taḥassasū min Yūsufa wa akhīh*.

In the classical sources the following distinction is transmitted between the two words: *tajassus* is searching with a bad purpose, *taḥassus* with a good one. **I relate this distinction as a transmitted view; I do not claim that all the lexicographers agree on it**, and it is also recorded that the two words are sometimes used for one another.

**But the usage in the verse of Joseph shows the reasonable side of the distinction:** there the one searched for is a lost brother, and the search is in order to find him. Here the person searched is present, and what is searched for is what he **conceals**.

**The difference lies in whose the knowledge is:** the whereabouts of a lost brother is no one's private matter; a person's concealed condition belongs to him.

### وَلَا يَغْتَب بَّعْضُكُم بَعْضًا — backbiting

**Root: غ-ي-ب.** This root was treated in the sections on al-Ḥadīd, al-Ḥashr, al-Ṭūr, al-Jumuʿa and elsewhere. Its root sense is **to disappear from sight, not to be present, to remain covered.** *Ghayb* — what cannot be reached by the senses; *ghāʾib* — one not present; *ghaybūba* — setting, disappearing.

**Form: Form VIII (*iftiʿāl*)** — *ightāba*. The word states speaking in a person's **absence**.

**And the whole definition of the word stands in that single word: absence.** The distinguishing feature of backbiting is not whether what is said is true or false; it is that the person spoken of **is not there**.

**The definition of backbiting.** In a report found in the hadith sources and widely transmitted in the explanation of this verse, it is stated that backbiting is "your mentioning your brother with something he would not like"; and when it was asked "what if what I say is in him?", the answer given was "if it is in him you have backbitten him, and if it is not you have slandered him".

**The measure this definition carries is worth recording and agrees with the wording of the verse:** backbiting is not lying — lying has a name of its own (*buhtān*). Backbiting is saying **what is true** behind a person's back. The act, that is, is not cleared by a test of truth.

**And there is a detail in the word order:** the verse does not say *lā taghtābū* (do not backbite — direct address). It says *lā **yaghtab** baʿḍukum baʿḍā* — "let not some of you backbite others." A third-person form and the *baʿḍ… baʿḍ* (some… some) construction.

This construction pictures the act as a cycle within a community: backbiting is reciprocal. The one who speaks today is spoken of tomorrow. The same structure was present in the *tanābazū* form of the eleventh verse (Form VI, reciprocity).

### The flesh of a dead brother: the four elements of the comparison

This is the strongest place in the sūra, and the comparison is built out of four distinct elements. Each element does a separate work; take one away and the comparison collapses.

The sentence is: *a-yuḥibbu aḥadukum **an yaʾkula** **laḥma** **akhīhi** **maytan***

> "Would any of you like **to eat** the **flesh** of his **dead** **brother**?"

Let us take the four elements one by one.

#### One. أَن يَأْكُلَ — to eat

**Root: أ-ك-ل.** To eat, to consume.

**And the work of the verb is this: to describe backbiting as an act of *consumption*.**

Speaking is a producing; eating is a consuming. By naming backbiting not speaking but **eating**, the verse reverses the direction of the act: the one who backbites is not saying something, he is **taking** something.

And what he takes is something subtracted from the other. When a person is spoken about, something goes from him — his standing, his trustworthiness, what is thought about him. The act of eating pictures exactly this: what is eaten grows less.

**Second, and more importantly: eating is a satisfying and pleasurable act.**

A person eats not because he is compelled but because he wants to. The verse's choice of verb covers this side too, and it will be said openly at the next point: the sentence does not ask *a-yafʿalu* (would he do it) but ***a-yuḥibbu*** (would he like it).

**This is the sharpest side of the comparison.** The question is directed not at the act but at the **appetite**.

And this matches the real psychology of backbiting: backbiting is not hard work, no one is forced into it. Backbiting is **attractive**. One of the most easily formed kinds of closeness within a community is speaking together about a shared third person. The verse puts the prohibition not on the act but on that attraction: do you **like** this?

#### Two. لَحْم — flesh

**Root: ل-ح-م.** *Laḥm* — flesh. From the same root come *luḥma* (the weft thread in weaving — the thread that binds the fabric crosswise) and *iltiḥām* (knitting together, the closing of the lips of a wound). The common idea is **sticking together, knitting**: flesh is what has knitted to the bone and to its own fibres.

**And the work of the word is this: to set out not the person but a part of him.**

The verse does not say "to eat his brother". It says "his brother's **flesh**". This is not a diminution but an **intensification**.

The reason: flesh is the covered side of a human being. One who looks at a person does not see his flesh; he sees skin, clothing, bearing. The flesh is beneath the covering.

**What is said in backbiting, that is, is exactly the covered side of the person.** No one gathers to talk about a feature of a person that everyone knows and that is out in the open. What is talked about is what is beneath the covering — what is not known, what he conceals, what he does not show openly.

**And the link with the step of prying is made here:** at the second step what was beneath the covering **was being felt with the hand**; at the third step what is beneath the covering **is eaten.** The chain is really a chain: what prying finds is what backbiting eats.

I record this as my own reading; the order of the three verbs is the verse's own word order.

#### Three. أَخِيهِ — his brother

**Root: أ-خ-و.** This root was treated **in the tenth verse**: *innama'l-muʾminūna **ikhwa*** — the believers are but brothers.

**And the work of this element is to bind the comparison to the rest of the sūra.**

The verse does not say "a human being's flesh". It says "the flesh of his **brother**". And that brotherhood had been defined two verses earlier.

**What this adds to the comparison is: what is eaten is not a stranger's body but a body of one's own kin.**

To eat human flesh is repellent in itself. To eat one's own brother's flesh adds another layer to the repulsion — because there is not merely a taboo there but a **betrayal**. You are consuming what you are bound to protect.

And the pronoun is striking: *akhī**hi*** — "**his** brother". The brother, that is, of the person who is the subject of the sentence. The verse leaves the bond upon the eater: what you eat is your brother.

**This element is the continuation of the *anfusakum* point in the eleventh verse.** There the object of fault-finding was made "yourselves". Here the owner of the flesh eaten is made "your brother". **Both verses do the same thing: they bring the target of the act closer to the agent himself.**

#### Four. مَيْتًا — dead

This is the last and subtlest element of the comparison.

**Root: م-و-ت.** To die. *Mayt* / *mayyit* — dead. *Mayta* — an animal that has died of itself; in the Qurʾān it is counted among the things forbidden to eat (al-Baqara 2/173, al-Māʾida 5/3).

**Its place in the grammar: *maytan* is in the accusative and is a *ḥāl*** — an element stating condition. That is, "his brother's flesh, **it being dead**".

To what the *ḥāl* belongs has been debated: to *laḥm* (the flesh being dead) or to *akh* (the brother being dead)? The grammarians record both possibilities. In terms of sense the difference is not great.

**Now the real question: what work does the word *maytan* do?**

The answer has more than one layer, and all of them come out at the same place.

**First, and most importantly: the dead cannot answer back.**

Backbiting is by definition done in a person's **absence** — this is the very root غ-ي-ب. And the one who is absent cannot reply to what is said about him. He does not hear, does not object, does not correct, does not defend.

**The one backbitten, that is, is in the position of a dead man so far as defence goes.** The verse's word *maytan* pictures this structural feature of backbiting in a single word.

**And this separates the act definitively from a word said face to face.** To tell a person a fault of his to his face may be coarse, may be hurtful — but the other can answer. In backbiting this possibility does not exist. There is no other party.

**Second: the dead cannot consent.**

A living person may allow himself to be spoken about. A dead one cannot. In backbiting no permission is taken from the person either — had it been taken, it would not have been backbiting.

**Third: the dead is defencelessness in its final form.**

A person's control over his own body ends entirely when he dies. The verse places the one backbitten in this position: everything can be done about him, and he himself can do nothing.

**Fourth: dead flesh is already forbidden.**

*Mayta* is among the things the Qurʾān has plainly declared forbidden. The comparison, that is, piles three prohibitions one on another:

| Layer | Prohibition |
|---|---|
| Eating human flesh | The most basic of taboos |
| Eating one's brother's flesh | A breach of kinship is added to it |
| Eating him in his dead state | The prohibition of *mayta* is added to that |

**Three layers in one sentence.** The verse draws its revulsion not from a single source but from three at once.

#### The work the four elements do together

| Element | What it secures | What happens if it is taken away |
|---|---|---|
| **Eating** | Describes the act as consumption and **pleasure** | Backbiting looks like harmless talk |
| **Flesh** | Shows that what is spoken of is the **covered** side | The act cannot be told apart from saying what is out in the open |
| **Brother** | Records that the target is **not a stranger** | The act is taken for something done to someone far off |
| **Dead** | Shows that the target **cannot answer back** | The act is confused with saying something to a person's face |

**The four elements close off, one by one, four separate defences of backbiting:**

- "We are only talking" → no, you are **eating**.
- "Everyone knows anyway" → no, you are eating his **flesh**: what is beneath the covering.
- "What is it to me, I do not even know him" → no, he is **your brother**.
- "I am telling the truth, I would say it to his face too" → no, **he is not there**; he is like a dead man.

I record this table as my own reading. But that each of the four elements is present in the verse and cannot be removed is the text's own structure: the sentence is built out of these four words.

### فَكَرِهْتُمُوهُ — "you have loathed it"

There is a grammatical subtlety in the close of the sentence, and it is generally lost in translation.

The question is in the present/imperfect: *a-**yuḥibbu** aḥadukum* — "would he like it?"

The answer is in the **perfect**: *fa-**karihtumūhu*** — "you have loathed it."

**The expected form would have been "you would loathe it".** The verse says "you have loathed it".

The grammarians explain this thus: **the perfect form states the certainty of the thing's coming about.** In Arabic the past tense is used to say that a thing will certainly happen — because the past is what has happened and cannot be changed.

**The answer, that is, is not asked of the one addressed; it is given on his behalf and as certain.**

And this has two consequences:

**One.** The verse places the loathing not as a **demand** but as a **finding**. It does not say "you must loathe it"; it says "you have loathed it". The loathing, that is, is already in the person; the verse does not produce it, it **reminds** him of it.

**Two.** And this explains the whole rhetorical force of the sentence. The verse is not trying to prove that backbiting is bad. It takes a reaction already present in the one addressed and attaches it to another act.

**The link with the seventh verse is made here:** there it was said that God had made *denial, transgression and disobedience repellent* (*karraha*) — the same root, ك-ر-ه. Here the one addressed himself loathes something: *karihtumūh*.

**The operation of "making repellent" in the seventh verse, that is, is seen at work in the twelfth.** The verse uses the loathing already present in the one addressed as a hook.

I record this link as my own reading; the shared root stands in the text.

### وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ تَوَّابٌ رَّحِيمٌ

The fourth occurrence of **taqwā** in the sūra.

**تَوَّاب — root ت-و-ب.** *Tawba* — to turn back. The form is on the **faʿʿāl** pattern: intensive. "One who accepts repentance again and again."

**And the placing of this verse-ending here shows the character of the sūra once more.** The verse reached its harshest point with the comparison of eating human flesh; and immediately afterwards it opens the door of return.

**A note is needed, and it is dwelt on in the classical sources:** the repentance for backbiting carries a side different from other sins — because the act has an aspect of **a debt to another person**. Two tendencies are recorded on this in classical jurisprudence:

| View | What is required | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| Pardon must be sought from the one backbitten | Repentance + pardon from the person | A debt to another person falls away only by its owner's forgiveness |
| If seeking pardon produces a new harm, asking forgiveness and praying for that person suffice | Repentance + prayer | Informing a person who does not know of it produces a fresh hurt |

**This is a juristic disagreement; I issue no ruling here and neither view is binding.** I record both views.

### What this says to the present

**One. The first link of the chain.**

The most practical side of the verse is that it places the prohibition at the very beginning. Conjecture is in a place no one can supervise — and precisely for that reason it is the only place where the chain can be broken. At the later links the act has come out into the open and cannot be taken back.

**Two. Prying has become costless.**

*Tajassus*, in the period the verse came down, was work that took effort: to learn what a person concealed, one had to ask people, to watch, to come near a door. That effort was a natural obstacle.

Today that obstacle has largely fallen away. To reach information going back into a person's past, to see who is connected with whom, to find a sentence said years ago — all of this can be done in minutes and without leaving a trace.

**And where the verse's prohibition stands becomes important here:** the prohibition is placed not on the information found but on the **act of searching**. *Lā tajassasū* — do not pry. The boundary, that is, is drawn before anything is learned. Once a piece of information has been obtained, "not using it" is far harder; the verse does not enter into that difficulty at all and stops the operation at the outset.

**Three. Backbiting entering the record.**

The element *maytan* in the verse's comparison — the inability to answer back — has taken on a new form today. What is said about a person no longer stays within one gathering; it is written, recorded, made searchable, and lives a life of its own independently of the person.

This increases the defencelessness: the person is not merely absent at that moment; he is **permanently** absent from most of the places through which the word travels.

**Four. And a distinction.**

The use of this verse to silence a person is widespread and is contrary to the text. The verse does not forbid the voicing of an injustice, the reporting of a harm, or the warning against a danger; these have been kept outside the definition of backbiting in classical jurisprudence, and wide agreement is recorded on this.

**The measure of the distinction stands in the verse's own comparison:** eating is an act performed for the **pleasure** of the eater. A word said in order to prevent a harm is not that act — because no pleasure is taken from it; a need is met.

**I issue no legal ruling;** in the classical sources the exceptions to backbiting are treated under a separate heading and there is disagreement in the details.

## 49/13 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَٰكُم مِّن ذَكَرٍ وَأُنثَىٰ وَجَعَلْنَٰكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَآئِلَ لِتَعَارَفُوٓا۟ إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ أَتْقَىٰكُمْ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ خَبِيرٌ

*Yā ayyuha'n-nāsu innā khalaqnākum min dhakarin wa unthā wa jaʿalnākum shuʿūban wa qabāʾila li-taʿārafū, inna akramakum ʿinda'llāhi atqākum, inna'llāha ʿalīmun khabīr*

"O people! We created you from a male and a female, and made you peoples and tribes so that you might come to know one another. The most honoured of you with God is the one among you who guards himself most. God is Knowing, Aware."

### The change of address

This is the sūra's most marked break, and it must be seen.

Throughout the sūra the address has been to **alladhīna āmanū** (those who believe). Five times:

> **49/1** *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū*
> **49/2** *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū*
> **49/6** *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū*
> **49/11** *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū*
> **49/12** *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū*

And in the thirteenth verse the address changes:

> **49/13** ***yā ayyuha'n-nās*** — **"O people!"**

**Once in the sūra, and exactly here.**

**To see what this change does, one must look at when it is made.**

The eleventh and twelfth verses regulated the narrowest matters inside a community: mockery, nicknames, conjecture, backbiting. The one addressed was a particular community, and the subject was that community's own inner relations.

The thirteenth verse, standing in the same place, addresses **all mankind**.

**And the subject widens too:** what is now spoken of is no longer conduct within a community but **the separateness of people from one another** — peoples, tribes, lineage.

**The meaning of this transition, which I record as my own reading, is this:** the narrowing circle of the sūra has here reached its floor, and what is met at the floor is man's narrowest claim to identity: **lineage.** And at exactly that point the text opens the address to its widest form.

The reason can be understood: a claim of superiority by lineage is not an internal matter of a community. It is impossible to resolve it from within a group, because the claim itself rests on the group's boundary. The verse resolves it by carrying the measure **outside** the group — and in order to do so it takes the address outside the group as well.

**And one further observation:** after the thirteenth verse the address will change once more. The fourteenth verse no longer addresses anyone; it begins with a sentence of **narrative**: *qālati'l-aʿrāb* — "The bedouin said". The sūra's sequence of address, that is, is this:

| Blocks | Address | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| 1-12 | *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū* | A particular community |
| 13 | *yā ayyuha'n-nās* | **All people** |
| 14-18 | *qālati'l-aʿrāb* (narrative) + *qul* (a command to the Prophet) | **Speaking about** a particular group |

**The scope first widens, then narrows.** And the thirteenth verse stands exactly at the summit.

### إِنَّا خَلَقْنَٰكُم مِّن ذَكَرٍ وَأُنثَىٰ

**What this sentence means has been debated in the classical exegesis:**

| View | Who the *dhakar* and *unthā* are | The sense of the sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **Adam and Eve** | The first human pair | All people come from the same father and mother; a claim by lineage has no ground |
| **Every person's mother and father** | General | Every human being is born in the same way, from a man and a woman; the manner of birth is the same for all |
| **The *nuṭfa*** | The share of the man and of the woman | The matter of creation is the same in everyone |

**All three views are recorded in the sources and they do not exclude one another.** I impose no preference.

**But all three arrive at the same place, and that is the logic of the verse:** what a claim of superiority rests on — lineage — leads back to a common source. Between people there is no difference in respect of birth.

**And a detail in the word order: *dhakarin wa unthā* comes indefinite.** "A male and a female" — not a particular male and female. This supports the second and third views but does not exclude the first.

### شُعُوبًا وَقَبَآئِلَ — peoples and tribes

**شُعُوب — the plural of شَعْب.** **Root: ش-ع-ب.**

This root was treated **in the section on al-Mursalāt** and this verse was named there as well. What was recorded there was this:

- The root is among those the lexicographers count as **aḍdād** (a word stating its own opposite as well): **شَعَبَ** is recorded both as **it separated, it divided** and as **it joined, it gathered**.
- **شُعْبَة** — a branch, an arm, a parting end.
- **شِعْب** — a mountain pass, the cleft road between two slopes.
- That the root is counted among the *aḍdād* is a view the lexicographers record; it was not claimed that all the lexicographers agree on it.
- And the idea common to the two senses was recorded thus: ***branches that part from one trunk have both parted and are attached to the same trunk. In branching, parting and joining are present at the same time.***

**And this common idea is exactly what this verse says.**

The verse divides people into *shuʿūb* — and the word itself carries the fact that the parting is at the same time an attachment. Peoples are separate; but the trunk from which they are separate is one. The preceding clause had named that trunk: *min dhakarin wa unthā*.

**I record this intersection as my own reading.** The root's contrariety of sense was recorded independently in the section on al-Mursalāt, without any connection being made with this verse; the fitness here was seen afterwards.

**قَبَائِل — the plural of قَبِيلَة.** **Root: ق-ب-ل.** Its root sense is **facing, front, turning towards.** *Qibla* — the side one turns to. *Qabūl* — to receive, to meet. *Muqābala* — to come face to face.

*Qabīla*, according to what the lexicographers record, comes from the idea of "a group turned towards one another, standing face to face".

**And the order of the two words runs from larger to smaller:** *shaʿb* is wide, *qabīla* narrow. A more detailed sequence is also transmitted in the classical sources (*shaʿb* → *qabīla* → *ʿimāra* → *baṭn* → *fakhdh* → *faṣīla* and the like), but there is disagreement over the details of the sequence; **I give no definite series.**

**What is worth recording is this: the verse counts group names at two different scales.** The division of people into groups, that is, is not of a single layer but of layers set inside one another. And the verse does **not** negate this.

### What the verse does to the groups

This is the most misread side of the verse and it must be written plainly.

**The verse does not abolish groups. It establishes them.**

The verb is this: ***wa jaʿalnākum*** shuʿūban wa qabāʾila — "We **made** you peoples and tribes". The subject of the verb is God. The distinction of groups, that is, is not presented as a fault, a malfunction or an obstacle to be overcome; it is recorded as **an act of creation**.

**What the verse does is not to abolish groups but to change their *function*.**

- The **existence** of the distinction: accepted (*jaʿalnākum*).
- The **purpose** of the distinction: changed (*li-taʿārafū*).
- The distinction's **producing value**: refused (*inna akramakum… atqākum*).

**Seeing this threefold distinction is important**, because the verse says neither "we are all the same, there are no differences" nor "differences produce a ranking". It says a third thing: **differences exist, but they do not produce a ranking; they exist for another work.**

### لِتَعَارَفُوٓا۟ — the purpose: to come to know one another

**Root: ع-ر-ف.** *Maʿrifa* — to know, to recognise. *ʿUrf* — a known, recognised custom. *ʿĀrif* — one who knows.

**Form: Form VI (*tafāʿul*)** — *taʿārafa*. This form states **reciprocity**. This pattern was named in the section on al-ʿAṣr: *ʿārafa* (he came to know) → *taʿārafū* (they came to know one another).

**And the work of the form is decisive here.**

Form VI states that the two sides **do the same thing to one another**. In *taʿāruf*, that is, there is no situation in which one side is the knower and the other the known. Both sides both know and are known.

**This is a pattern in which a relation of superiority is grammatically impossible.**

The comparison is this:

| Form | Relation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The bare triliteral** (*ʿarafa*) | One-directional: the subject knows, the object is known | One side holds the knowledge, the other is its subject-matter |
| **Form VI** (*taʿārafa*) | Reciprocal: both sides in the same position | Both sides are at once subject and object |

**The verse, that is, makes the purpose not the groups' "examining", "studying" or "getting to know" one another — but their coming to know one another *reciprocally*.** One group's making the other an object of knowledge falls outside the form.

**And what the purpose is not is worth recording too.** The verse does not say:

- …*li-tafāḍalū* (that you might compete in superiority)
- …*li-taghālabū* (that you might overcome one another)
- …*li-tattaḥidū* (that you might become one)

It says: **that you might come to know one another.**

**Coming to know one another requires the preservation of difference.** Two things that are the same do not come to know one another; coming to know presupposes that the two sides are distinct. The verse, that is, makes difference the **condition** of the purpose: had the differences been removed, the purpose would have been removed with them.

I record this as my own reading; but that the form states reciprocity and that the purpose is set down as *taʿāruf* is the text's own wording.

### إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ أَتْقَىٰكُمْ

This is the sūra's measure-sentence and one of the most often quoted sentences of the Qurʾān. It has been named as a measure earlier in this commentary in the sections on al-Baqara, al-Infiṭār and elsewhere.

**أَكْرَم — root ك-ر-م.** This root was treated in the sections on ʿAbasa and al-Fajr. The sense recorded: *karam* — generosity, worth, nobility, hospitality. *Karīm* is both generous and of worth. *Ikrām* is conduct showing that someone is held in worth.

**And the choice of the word is worth noticing.**

The verse does not say *afḍalakum* (the most excellent of you), does not say *aʿlākum* (the highest of you). It says ***akramakum***.

**The reason is the life of the word in the Arabic of the age of ignorance.** *Karam* was the most central word of value in that period and was directly connected with **lineage**: *karam* meant nobility; *karīm* meant nobly born. A tribe's boast was the *karam* of its ancestors.

**The verse, that is, fights the argument with the other side's own word.** It does not change the word of value — it changes its **measure**. "What you call *karam* is a true thing; but its measure is not lineage, it is taqwā."

I record this as an observation about language and history; the central place of the word in the poetry of the age of ignorance is a known fact.

**عِندَ ٱللَّهِ — "with God"**

These two words are the most critical qualification in the sentence, and they are generally passed over quickly.

The verse does not say "the most honoured of you is the one who guards himself most". It says **"the most honoured of you *with God*"**.

**The work of the qualification is this:** the verse does not claim that people do not rank one another. Societies always rank — by profession, wealth, achievement, standing, lineage. The verse does not dispute this fact.

What it does is to say **where the binding ranking is made**. *ʿInda'llāh* — there. And there is where man cannot reach.

**أَتْقَىٰكُم — root و-ق-ي.** The fifth and last occurrence of taqwā in the sūra; and its only occurrence in the form of an *elative* (a form of superiority): *atqā* — the one who guards himself most.

**And now the whole logic of the sentence becomes visible.**

What is laid down as the measure — taqwā — is something **invisible**. How far a person guards himself cannot be measured from outside. It can neither be counted, nor compared, nor ranked.

**The verse, that is, does two things at once:**

1. It cancels a visible measure (lineage).
2. In its place it puts a measure **no human being can use**.

**The second move is more important than the first.** For if the verse had put another visible measure in the place of lineage — the quantity of one's worship, say, or knowledge — the operation of ranking would have gone on, only its measure changed. And people would have begun to rank one another by the new measure.

The verse does not do this. The measure it lays down is a measure **taken out of man's hands**.

**And this closes the ring with the eleventh verse:**

> **49/11:** *ʿasā an yakūnū khayran minhum* — "it may be that they are better" (you cannot know).
> **49/13:** *inna akramakum ʿinda'llāhi atqākum* — there is a measure, but it is with God and it is taqwā.

**The eleventh verse suspended the ranking; the thirteenth says why it was suspended.** Ranking cannot be done, because the measure is invisible.

I record this link as my own reading; that the two verses stand in the same sūra, two verses apart, is the text's word order.

### إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلِيمٌ خَبِيرٌ

**عَلِيم** — the Knowing. **خَبِير — root خ-ب-ر.** The concrete sense the lexicographers record is interesting: **الخَبَار (*khabār*)** is soft, loose soil; *khabara'l-arḍa* — he ploughed the land and brought up what was under it. From this *khubr* — to know the inner side of a thing; *khabīr* — one who is aware of the inner side.

**The difference between the two names:** *ʿalīm* states knowledge; *khabīr* states knowledge **of the inner side**. Not the surface of the soil but what is beneath it.

**And the placing of this verse-ending here seals the logic of the verse.** The measure is taqwā; taqwā is invisible; and the verse ends with the name of the one who knows the invisible.

**A note between ن-ب-أ and خ-ب-ر:** in the section on al-Nabaʾ the difference between *nabaʾ* and *khabar* was treated. Here the word occurs as a divine name and means the knowing of the inner side.

### What this says to the present

In writing what this verse says to the present a note is needed, and I write it at the outset: **no wholesale ruling is issued in this text about any ethnic, national or religious group, and no side is taken in any current political controversy.** This is the plain rule of the method (`USLUP.md`), and it coincides with the verse's own logic — because the verse lays down precisely a measure that makes it impossible to pass judgment on the whole of a group.

What can be recorded is the **structure** of the verse:

**One. Keeping clear of both extremes.**

In the matter of lineage and origin there are two widespread attitudes: the first, to maintain that one lineage is superior to another; the second, to present the differences as though they did not exist.

The verse does neither. It records the difference (*jaʿalnākum*), gives it a function (*li-taʿārafū*), and cuts off its producing value (*ʿinda'llāh… atqākum*).

**This threefold structure is the verse's most practical side.** For denying differences does not work — people know where they come from and care about it. The verse does not suppress this; it changes its **direction**.

**Two. The measure's being unmeasurable.**

Within a community the antidote to a claim of superiority is not a counter-claim of superiority — that is the continuation of the same operation. The antidote the verse lays down is **the taking of the authority to measure out of human hands**.

The concrete counterpart of this is: in order to be able to say that one person is "more worthy" than another, we should have had to measure a side of that person we cannot see. The verse says this cannot be done. And this holds for everyone — including those who pass a favourable judgment on their own group.

**Three. Coming to know one another as a purpose.**

The form *li-taʿārafū* says that difference exists **for encounter**. A person's coming to know someone from outside his own group is, in the verse's sequence, not an act of tolerance; it is the reason difference exists.

And this has a consequence: a life that never meets anything outside its own group is not realising the purpose the verse points to. This is a judgment about no one; it is a measure that follows from the verse's sentence, and **I record it as my own reading.**

**Four. And the verse's own address.**

*Yā ayyuha'n-nās.* Everyone who reads this verse must know that he himself is the one it addresses. The verse does not defend one group against another; it addresses **all of them together**. To turn it into a proof in the hand of one side would be to narrow the scope of the address.

### The whole of the fourth block (11-13)

| Verse | Subject | Measure |
|---|---|---|
| **11** | Mockery, fault-finding, nicknames — done with the tongue | *ʿAsā an yakūnū khayran minhum* — you cannot know |
| **12** | Conjecture, prying, backbiting — from the mind to the tongue | *A-yuḥibbu… maytan* — loathing |
| **13** | Lineage and origin — concerning identity | *Inna akramakum ʿinda'llāhi atqākum* — the measure is with God |

**The block closes off three ways of diminishing a person:** by speech (11), in secret (12), and by birth (13). And each time the manner of closing is the same: **to show that the authority to pass judgment is not in your hands.**

## 49/14 — قَالَتِ ٱلْأَعْرَابُ ءَامَنَّا قُل لَّمْ تُؤْمِنُوا۟ وَلَٰكِن قُولُوٓا۟ أَسْلَمْنَا وَلَمَّا يَدْخُلِ ٱلْإِيمَٰنُ فِى قُلُوبِكُمْ وَإِن تُطِيعُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥ لَا يَلِتْكُم مِّنْ أَعْمَٰلِكُمْ شَيْـًٔا إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ

*Qālati'l-aʿrābu āmannā, qul lam tuʾminū wa lākin qūlū aslamnā wa lammā yadkhuli'l-īmānu fī qulūbikum, wa in tuṭīʿu'llāha wa rasūlahū lā yalitkum min aʿmālikum shayʾā, inna'llāha ghafūrun raḥīm*

"The bedouin said, 'We have believed.' Say: You have not believed; but say 'we have submitted'. For faith has not yet entered your hearts. If you obey God and His Messenger, He will not diminish anything of your deeds. God is Forgiving, Merciful."

### First a note: who are the ٱلْأَعْرَاب?

This verse is one of those on which the most mistaken rulings have been built. For that reason one must begin from the first word.

The word **ٱلْأَعْرَاب (*al-aʿrāb*)** does **not mean "the Arabs".**

| Word | Its meaning |
|---|---|
| **ٱلْعَرَب (*al-ʿarab*)** | The Arab people — the name of a people |
| **ٱلْأَعْرَاب (*al-aʿrāb*)** | **The bedouin** — communities living the unsettled life of the desert |

*Aʿrāb* is not the plural of *ʿarab*; it is a separate word and states **a way of living**: nomadic, dwelling in the desert, outside the settled order. A city-dwelling Arab is, in respect of language, *ʿarab*, but he is not *aʿrābī*.

**The verse, that is, is not speaking of an ethnic group; it is speaking of a form of life and of a community living within that form.**

### And the Qurʾān itself issues no wholesale ruling about this group

This is a point that deserves to be dwelt on separately, and its proof is the Qurʾān itself.

In Sūrat al-Tawba, **two verses apart**, there are two different rulings about the same word:

> *"**The bedouin** are more severe in denial and hypocrisy…"* (al-Tawba 9/97)
>
> *"And among **the bedouin** there are those who believe in God and the Last Day…"* (al-Tawba 9/99)

**The same word, two verses apart, two opposite rulings.**

And between them stands 9/98, which also describes a part of the bedouin. The Qurʾān, that is, relates this community by **dividing** it: some are thus, some are otherwise.

**The consequence of this is plain and coincides exactly with the general measure of this commentary:** what the verse describes is a **quality**, not an identity. Whoever carries that quality is who the verse is speaking of; whoever does not carry it, it is not speaking of. That the word is the name of a community does not change this, because the Qurʾān itself has divided that community in two.

**And in this verse too there is a qualification:** the verse does not say *"all the bedouin"*; it says *"qālati'l-aʿrāb"* — it records a particular incident and the group that spoke in it. The account of the occasion of revelation speaks of a particular delegation as well.

### قَالَتِ ٱلْأَعْرَابُ ءَامَنَّا — the claim

For fourteen verses the sūra has proceeded by direct address. Here it turns to a **sentence of narrative**: "They said."

And what they said is: *āmannā* — "we have believed."

**The word order of the sentence is worth recording:** *āmannā* is in the perfect and states a completed act. They are speaking, that is, not of a process but of **a closed condition**: the thing is done, belief has been given.

### قُل لَّمْ تُؤْمِنُوا۟ — the form of the refusal

The answer comes directly: *lam tuʾminū* — "you have not believed."

**And the hardness of the refusal must be seen.** The verse does not soften the sentence, does not say "not entirely", does not say "incompletely". It negates directly.

**But immediately afterwards it points to a place:** *wa lākin qūlū **aslamnā*** — "but say 'we have submitted'."

**The refusal, that is, is not an exclusion but a *correction of place*.** The verse does not take them out of one category and put them nowhere; it places them **in another category**. And that category is not a negative one — *islām* is a positive word of the Qurʾān.

This agrees with the general character of the sūra: after every heavy sentence a place is left.

### إِيمَان and إِسْلَام — the roots of two words

This verse is the central text of the whole classical debate on the difference between the two words. **This verse was named with that description in the section on al-Dhāriyāt**, and the table given there was this:

| | **مُؤْمِن** | **مُسْلِم** |
|---|---|---|
| Root | أ-م-ن — security | س-ل-م — submission, soundness |
| What it brings forward | **The inward** — assent, trusting | **The outward** — submitting, the showing of allegiance |

Now let us open out the concrete senses of the roots.

**أ-م-ن.** Its root sense is **fearlessness, being in safety, tranquillity.** *Amn* — safety. *Amān* — a guarantee of safety. *Amāna* — what is left in trust. *Muʾmin* — one who both trusts and gives trust. And among God's names *al-Muʾmin* is from this root too.

**At the centre of the root there is an *inward state*: the going of fear, the settling of the heart.** Faith, by its root, is not an "accepting" but a **trusting**. And trusting is something that cannot be produced by command.

**س-ل-م.** Its root sense is **to be sound, to be without defect, to be complete.** *Salāma* — soundness. *Salām* — peace. *Silm* — peace. *Taslīm* — to hand a thing over complete.

**At the centre of the root there is an *operation*: handing a thing over to the other party in sound condition.** *Islām* is in Form IV: to hand oneself over.

**The difference between the two roots follows from this:**

| | **إيمان** | **إسلام** |
|---|---|---|
| The picture in the root | The going of fear, trusting | Handing a thing over complete |
| Where it takes place | In the heart | Outwardly — in word and conduct |
| Can it be seen | No | **Yes** |
| Its verb in the verse | *yadkhulu* — **it enters** | *aslamnā* — **we did it** |

**The last line is the finest detail of the word order and is generally skipped.**

Islām is related with a **verb**: *aslamnā* — "we have submitted". The subject is the human being; a thing has been done.

Faith, on the other hand, is related with an **entering**: *yadkhuli'l-īmānu fī qulūbikum* — "let faith enter your hearts". And here **faith is the subject**, not the human being. The person does not do faith; faith enters the person.

**The two words, that is, stand in two different grammatical positions:** the one a work the person does, the other something that comes to the person. This establishes the difference between them by word order before any definition.

I record this as my own reading; the subjects of the verbs are in the wording of the verse.

### The disagreement over the relation of the two words

**In the classical sources the relation of these two words has been debated at length and no single conclusion has been reached.** As was recorded in the section on al-Dhāriyāt too, the two words are found used for one another in some places.

The main tendencies recorded:

| View | The relation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Islām is wider** | Every believer is a *muslim*, not every *muslim* is a believer | This verse: "you have not believed, say we have submitted" |
| **The two are the same** | The words are synonyms; they state the same thing from different sides | The use of the two words for the same community at al-Dhāriyāt 51/35-36 |
| **It varies with the context** | When mentioned separately they differ; when mentioned singly each covers the other | The variety of the usages in the Qurʾān |

**I impose no preference, and this is a question debated among the schools as well; I issue no juristic or theological ruling.** The sentence recorded in the section on al-Dhāriyāt holds here too: *"The relation between the two words cannot be settled without taking the whole of the Qurʾān into account."*

**But the distinction this verse makes within itself is clear and independent of the debate:** here *islām* is placed as a more outward category than *īmān*.

### وَلَمَّا يَدْخُلِ — the subtlety of the particle *lammā*

**This is the finest and most important word in the verse.**

In Arabic there are two particles that negate the past, and there is a difference between them:

| Particle | Negation | Ending | Expectation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **لَمْ (*lam*)** | It did not happen in the past | The negation may have ceased | None |
| **لَمَّا (*lammā*)** | It has not happened **up to now** | The negation **continues to the present** | **There is one** — it is expected to happen |

The two differences the grammarians record are these:

**One. Continuity.** *Lammā* states that the negation has continued unbroken up to the moment of speaking. In *lam* this is not a condition.

**Two — and the more important: expectation.** *Lammā* implies that what is negated **is expected to happen**. Its exact counterpart in English is **"not yet"**.

This is the difference between "he did not come" and "he has not come yet": the second says that his coming is expected.

**Now let us look at the sentence of the verse.**

The verse uses two negations, and **the two with different particles**:

> *قُل **لَمْ** تُؤْمِنُوا۟* — "you have not believed" (*lam*)
> *و**لَمَّا** يَدْخُلِ ٱلْإِيمَٰنُ فِى قُلُوبِكُمْ* — "faith has **not yet** entered your hearts" (*lammā*)

**Two different particles have been used in the same sentence. This cannot be a coincidence.**

The first negation refuses the claim: *lam tuʾminū* — what you say is not true.

The second negation describes the condition and **leaves the door open**: *lammā yadkhul* — it has not entered yet.

**The distance between "it has not entered yet" and "it will not enter" is the whole mercy of this verse.**

The verse does not close a door. It describes a **process**: faith is something expected to enter the heart; at this moment it has not entered; but it is expected.

And the word order supports this: the sentence that follows immediately is a **conditional** sentence — *wa in tuṭīʿu'llāha wa rasūlah* — "if you obey God and His Messenger…" A way, that is, is being shown.

**Seeing this difference of particle determines how the verse is to be read.** Had *lammā* not been used, the sentence would have been an exclusion. With *lammā* it becomes **a statement of a condition** and an **invitation**.

I record this as a reading resting on the difference of particles the grammarians record; that *lammā* implies expectation is a settled note in Arabic grammar.

### يَدْخُلَ فِى قُلُوبِكُمْ — faith's entering the heart

**دَخَلَ — root د-خ-ل.** To enter. **And the verb sets up a conception of place:** the heart is somewhere that is entered.

This is consistent with the Qurʾān's general language about the heart: the heart is sealed (al-Baqara 2/7), locked (Muḥammad 47/24), it holds a sickness within it (al-Baqara 2/10), there is rust upon it (al-Muṭaffifīn 83/14). All of these are conceptions of a **closed volume**.

**And here too there is an intersection with the sūra's name.** A *ḥujra* is an enclosed volume; so is the heart. The sūra began by preserving the boundary of the chambers outside; at the very end it stands at the door of the chamber within.

I record this intersection as **my own reading**; the text does not say that it sets up such a link.

### وَإِن تُطِيعُوا۟ … لَا يَلِتْكُم مِّنْ أَعْمَٰلِكُمْ شَيْـًٔا

**لَا يَلِتْكُم — root أ-ل-ت.** This root was treated **in the section on al-Ṭūr** and this verse was named there. The sense recorded: **to diminish, to lessen, to cut short what is due.**

The same root occurred at al-Ṭūr 52/21: *wa mā alatnāhum min ʿamalihim min shayʾ* — "We diminished nothing of their deeds."

**The pattern: *mā/lā … min shayʾin*** — an absolute negation. The *min* is redundant (*zāʾida*) and gives the sense of "nothing at all".

**A note on the variant readings:** it is transmitted that the word is also read in the form **لَا يَأْلِتْكُمْ** (with hamza, *yaʾlitkum*). Both readings go back to the same root and the sense does not change. **I give no imam's name.**

**The work of the sentence is this:** the verse has placed those it addresses in a lower category. This could have produced the anxiety that what they had done would be counted worthless. The sentence cuts this off: **if you obey, nothing of your deeds will be diminished.**

The difference of category, that is, does not mean a deduction **from the return for a deed**.

**And this is the most just side of the verse, and I stress it as my own reading:** the verse on the one hand refuses the claim, and on the other gives what has been done its due. It does not confuse the two operations. A person's claim may be excessive; that does not require that what he has done should go unrequited.

**إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ غَفُورٌ رَّحِيمٌ** — And the verse closes, as in the fifth verse of the sūra, with a verse-ending of forgiveness.

### What this says to the present

**One. The difference between joining and believing.**

The distinction the verse sets up holds for anyone who joins any structure: to join an institution, a movement, a community is one thing, and to believe in what it carries is another. The first is an operation and is completed; the second is a process and takes time.

In the section on al-Naṣr this verse was already named with that description, and the sentence recorded there was: *"The number of those who enter does not tell the quality of those who enter."*

**Two. And that this verse cannot be used about someone else.**

This is the most important note and it must be written plainly.

In the verse the side that gives the ruling is clear: the answer was given to the Prophet with the command *qul* (say), and the source of the knowledge is revelation. For a person to look at another person and say "you are only a *muslim*, you are not a believer" is not an authority this verse gives.

**Because:**

- The measure of the verse is the inside of the heart, and the thirteenth verse had said that the measure is *ʿinda'llāh*.
- As was recorded in the section on al-Mumtaḥina: a ruling about a person is built upon outward signs and declaration; **the authority to know the heart has been given to no one.**
- And to use this verse about someone else would be to do the very thing the eleventh and twelfth verses of the sūra forbid: to place a person lower on the basis of something you cannot see.

**The sūra's own inner logic, that is, closes off the use of this verse as a weapon against another.** The verse stands so that the one who reads it may measure his own claim.

## 49/15 — إِنَّمَا ٱلْمُؤْمِنُونَ ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ بِٱللَّهِ وَرَسُولِهِۦ ثُمَّ لَمْ يَرْتَابُوا۟ وَجَٰهَدُوا۟ بِأَمْوَٰلِهِمْ وَأَنفُسِهِمْ فِى سَبِيلِ ٱللَّهِ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلصَّٰدِقُونَ

*Innama'l-muʾminūna'lladhīna āmanū bi'llāhi wa rasūlihī thumma lam yartābū wa jāhadū bi-amwālihim wa anfusihim fī sabīli'llāh, ulāʾika humu'ṣ-ṣādiqūn*

"The believers are only those who have believed in God and His Messenger, then have not fallen into doubt, and have striven with their wealth and their lives in the way of God. It is they who are the truthful."

### The second إِنَّمَا sentence

The fourteenth verse refused a claim. The fifteenth describes what the claim really answers to.

**And the particle *innamā* (restriction) is used for the second time in the sūra:**

> **49/10:** *innama'l-muʾminūna **ikhwa*** — the believers are but brothers.
> **49/15:** *innama'l-muʾminūna **'lladhīna āmanū…*** — the believers are only these.

**Two sentences, with the same pattern, saying two different things:** the one gives the **relation** of the believers with one another, the other their **definition**. And the two stand in two different blocks of the sūra: one in the block of conflict, the other in the block of identity.

I record this parallel as an observation about the word order.

### Three conditions

The verse counts three things, and the order of the three is meaningful:

| Order | Condition | Of what kind | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | *āmanū bi'llāhi wa rasūlih* | An event — a beginning | In the heart |
| **2** | *thumma lam yartābū* | A **continuity** — going on | In the heart |
| **3** | *jāhadū bi-amwālihim wa anfusihim* | An **act** — the return given | Outwardly |

**The order runs from inside outward** — the reverse of the sūra's general direction. From beginning to end the sūra narrowed from outside inward; in the last block the direction reverses: it begins in the heart and comes out to wealth and life.

I record this as an observation about the word order.

### ثُمَّ لَمْ يَرْتَابُوا۟ — "then they did not fall into doubt"

**ثُمَّ** in Arabic generally states **sequence with an interval of time**: then, after a while has passed.

But the grammarians record that *thumma* sometimes states **sequence of rank** (*tartīb fi'l-rutba*) as well: coming afterwards not in time but in **importance**.

**Both readings are meaningful here:**

- **The reading by time:** they believed, then time passed and they did not fall into doubt. The test of faith, that is, is duration.
- **The reading by rank:** to believe is one thing; not to fall into doubt is a weightier thing than that.

**I impose no preference.**

**Root: ر-ي-ب.** *Rayb* — doubt; and, according to what the lexicographers record, not merely doubt but **doubt that disquiets**: a misgiving that troubles one inwardly. *Rība* — a state that arouses suspicion.

**And this root was treated in the second verse of Sūrat al-Baqara:** *dhālika'l-kitābu **lā rayba** fīh* — "in that Book there is no doubt."

**The link between the two places is worth recording and I record it as my own reading:**

> **al-Baqara 2/2:** There is no *rayb* in the Book.
> **al-Ḥujurāt 49/15:** The believers are those who do not fall into *rayb*.

The same word, once as the quality of the text and once as the quality of the reader. The doubtlessness of the Book and the doubtlessness of the person are stated with the same root.

**And that the verb *lam yartābū* is in a negative form is striking.** The verse does not say "they attained certainty" (*ayqanū*); it says "they did not fall into doubt". The condition, that is, is not to **gain** something but for something **not to happen**.

This is a workable measure: no ever-increasing certainty is demanded; what is asked is not to surrender to doubt.

### وَجَٰهَدُوا۟ بِأَمْوَٰلِهِمْ وَأَنفُسِهِمْ

**Root: ج-ه-د.** Its root sense is **strength, capacity; and the straining of one's strength to its limit.** *Jahd* — effort, exertion. *Juhd* — capacity, strength. *Ijtihād* — to use one's strength to the utmost on a question.

**Form: Form III (*mufāʿala*)** — *jāhada*. It states reciprocity or intensity.

**And the order is worth recording: first *wealth*, then *life*.**

This pair occurs in many places in the Qurʾān and almost every time wealth comes first. Several things are recorded in the classical commentaries as explanations of this:

| Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|
| Wealth is given before life — the order is practical | A person does not give his life before giving his wealth |
| Wealth is more general: everyone can contribute with his wealth, not everyone may be in a position to put his life at stake | Generality |
| Wealth is an extension of a person's life; to give it is a preparation for giving life | Gradation |

**I impose no preference.**

**And the place of this verse in the last block is important:** in the fourteenth verse the bedouin said "we have believed", and in the seventeenth they will **count their submission a favour**. There is a **claim** on the table, that is, and the claim has no cost.

The fifteenth verse says what is given in return for faith: **wealth and life.** The difference between a claim and a cost is established here.

### أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلصَّٰدِقُونَ

**Root: ص-د-ق.** To speak truly. *Ṣidq* — the conformity of a word to reality. From the same root come *ṣadaqa* (property given — it is recorded that it takes this name because it shows the truth of one's faith) and *ṣadīq* (a friend — one who keeps his word).

**And the work of the word here is to complete the answer given to the fourteenth verse.**

There a word had been spoken: *āmannā* — "we have believed". Here it is stated who those are who speak that word **truly**: *ulāʾika humu'ṣ-ṣādiqūn* — "it is they who are the truthful."

**The matter, that is, is not set up as a question of lying; it is set up as a question of *truthfulness*.** The word is the same word; what makes the difference is what stands behind it.

**The root ص-د-ق will come once more in the seventeenth verse:** *in kuntum **ṣādiqīn*** — "if you are truthful". The two occurrences frame the last block.

**And the *ḍamīr al-faṣl* (the pronoun of separation) is used again:** *ulāʾika **hum** al-ṣādiqūn*. Its third use in the sūra (7, 11, 15).

## 49/16 — قُلْ أَتُعَلِّمُونَ ٱللَّهَ بِدِينِكُمْ وَٱللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ مَا فِى ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَمَا فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَٱللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

*Qul a-tuʿallimūna'llāha bi-dīnikum wa'llāhu yaʿlamu mā fi's-samāwāti wa mā fi'l-arḍ, wa'llāhu bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm*

"Say: Are you teaching God your religion? God knows what is in the heavens and what is on the earth. God knows all things."

### The form of the question

The sentence is a question, but not a question that expects an answer. In Arabic this is called **istifhām inkārī** (a question of denial): a refusal in the form of a question.

**And the picture the question produces is a hard one.**

*A-tuʿallimūna'llāha bi-dīnikum* — "are you teaching God your religion?"

**عَلَّمَ — root ع-ل-م.** In Form II: to teach. That is, to convey knowledge to one who does not know.

**The scene the verb sets up is this:** on one side there is a teacher, on the other a learner. The teacher knows more than the learner. The verse places the sentence of those it addresses into this scene.

When a person says "I have believed", he is giving a piece of information about his own heart. And that information is one the other side is assumed not to know — otherwise there would be no need to say it. The verse brings this assumption into the open: **you are giving information to a side that does not know.**

**And the second half of the sentence destroys the assumption:** *wa'llāhu yaʿlamu mā fi's-samāwāti wa mā fi'l-arḍ.*

### بِدِينِكُمْ — "your religion"

**Root: د-ي-ن.** In Arabic this root has a wide field of meaning, and the lexicographers record these derivatives:

- **دَيْن (*dayn*)** — a debt.
- **دِين (*dīn*)** — both "religion" and **judgment, requital, recompense**. *Yawmu'd-dīn* — the day of reckoning.
- **دَانَ (*dāna*)** — he submitted; and he requited.
- **مَدِين** — indebted, submitting.

**The common idea: a relation of allegiance and requital.** A debtor is bound to his creditor; a servant is bound to his Lord; and in both there is a "giving of what is due".

**And the particle *bi-* is worth recording:** *tuʿallimūna'llāha **bi**-dīnikum* — "to teach God **about** your religion". What they are trying to teach, that is, is their own religiousness.

### The place of the verse in the sūra

This verse joins the sūra's two ends on the subject of knowledge:

> **49/6:** When a report comes, **you** must investigate — because your knowledge may be incomplete.
> **49/16:** Do not try to give **Him** information about your own condition — because His knowledge is not incomplete.

**The two verses record the two directions of the asymmetry of knowledge.** Man's knowledge is incomplete, and for that reason verification is needed; God's knowledge is complete, and for that reason a declaration is unnecessary.

I record this as my own reading; the shared root of the two verses (ع-ل-م / ب-ي-ن) and their shared subject stand in the text.

### وَٱللَّهُ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍ عَلِيمٌ

**The occurrences of the root ع-ل-م in the sūra deserve to be gathered:**

| Verse | Expression | Who knows |
|---|---|---|
| **49/1** | *samīʿun **ʿalīm*** | God |
| **49/7** | *waʿ**lamū*** — "know that" | The one addressed (a command) |
| **49/8** | ***ʿalīmun** ḥakīm* | God |
| **49/13** | ***ʿalīmun** khabīr* | God |
| **49/16** | *a-tu**ʿallim**ūna'llāha* / *ya**ʿlam**u* / *bi-kulli shayʾin **ʿalīm*** | A contrast: what is claimed ↔ what is so |
| **49/18** | *inna'llāha **yaʿlamu** ghayba's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* | God |

**The root occurs in six verses and in five of them its subject is God.** The single exception is the seventh verse, and there too the verb is a command: "know that".

**This shows why a sūra of conduct is woven with words of knowledge.** All the sūra's commands — lower your voice, verify the report, do not mock, do not conjecture, do not pry, do not boast of your lineage — all of them are built upon a **limit of knowledge**: do not pass judgment on something you do not know.

And where knowledge is complete is repeated in six places.

## 49/17 — يَمُنُّونَ عَلَيْكَ أَنْ أَسْلَمُوا۟ قُل لَّا تَمُنُّوا۟ عَلَىَّ إِسْلَٰمَكُم بَلِ ٱللَّهُ يَمُنُّ عَلَيْكُمْ أَنْ هَدَىٰكُمْ لِلْإِيمَٰنِ إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰدِقِينَ

*Yamunnūna ʿalayka an aslamū, qul lā tamunnū ʿalayya islāmakum, bali'llāhu yamunnu ʿalaykum an hadākum li'l-īmāni in kuntum ṣādiqīn*

"They count it a favour to you that they have submitted. Say: Do not count your submission a favour to me. Rather it is God who has bestowed a favour on you in guiding you to faith — if you are truthful."

### مَنَّ — two senses in one root

**Root: م-ن-ن.** And this root carries two different senses — indeed senses that work in opposite directions:

| Sense | Usage | Its value |
|---|---|---|
| **1. To bestow, to give a favour** | *manna ʿalayhi* — he bestowed a favour on him | Positive |
| **2. To throw one's giving in another's face** | *manna ʿalayhi* — he cast what he had given in his face | Negative |

**The same verb, the same form, the same preposition (*ʿalā*).** What determines the sense is the context.

**And the concrete life of the root is interesting:** *al-mann*, in the Qurʾān, is the name of the food sent down from the sky to the Children of Israel — *al-mannu wa's-salwā* (al-Baqara 2/57). The word, that is, is the name of **a provision given without return**.

*Al-mann* is also recorded by the lexicographers in the sense of **to cut, to diminish**; the expression *ajrun ghayru mamnūn* (Fuṣṣilat 41/8 and elsewhere) — "a reward without cease" — comes from this sense.

**How the two senses meet in the same root can be seen:** giving is a bond. The giver comes to hold a claim upon the receiver. When that claim stays silent it is a gift; when it is voiced it becomes a reproach. **The root carries both these consequences of the act of giving at once.**

**And the Qurʾān separates the two plainly:** *"Do not void your alms with reproach (**mann**) and injury"* (al-Baqara 2/264). Reproach, that is, is an operation that cancels the very thing given.

### يَمُنُّونَ عَلَيْكَ أَنْ أَسْلَمُوا۟ — the final form of the claim

There is an escalation in the last block, and this verse is its summit:

| Verse | What they do |
|---|---|
| **14** | They say "we have believed" — a **claim** |
| **16** | They present it as information — a **declaration** |
| **17** | They count it a favour — a **debt owed to them** |

**Three steps: claim → declaration → debt.**

At the last step, having become a *muslim* is presented as a good turn: "we joined you, this is something we did for you."

**And this forms a ring with the sūra's first verse.** In the first verse it was said *lā tuqaddimū* — do not go ahead. To count a thing a favour is exactly a form of going ahead: to place oneself as creditor and the other as debtor.

**The sūra closes with the plainest instance of the positioning it forbade in its first verse.** I record this as my own reading.

### قُل لَّا تَمُنُّوا۟ عَلَىَّ إِسْلَٰمَكُم — the form of the refusal

The answer comes in two moves.

**The first move: refusal.** *Lā tamunnū ʿalayya islāmakum* — "do not count your submission a favour to me."

**And there is a subtlety in the word order:** the sentence does not say *lā tamunnū ʿalayya **bi**-islāmikum* (with a preposition), but directly *islāmakum* (as a direct object). That is, not "do not reproach me with your islām" but **"do not make your islām a reproach to me."**

The difference is this: in the first, islām is an instrument; in the second, islām itself becomes the thing thrown in another's face. The grammarians explain this by saying that the verb is used transitively here in the sense of "to make a thing the subject of an obligation".

**The second move: reversing the direction.** *Bali'llāhu yamunnu ʿalaykum* — "rather it is God who has bestowed a favour on you."

**بَلْ** — the particle of *iḍrāb*: it invalidates the preceding sentence and puts a new one in its place. "No, it is not so; it is thus."

**And the same verb is used in the reverse direction:** *yamunnūna ʿalayka* → *yamunnu ʿalaykum*. Subject and object change places.

**A question must be asked here:** if counting something a favour is blameworthy, how can God's *mann* be?

The answer lies in the two senses of the root: **God's act is in the first sense — to bestow.** And why it does not fall into the second sense can be understood:

A person's throwing his giving in another's face is a claim to create a **debt** in the other, and that claim is not true — for no human being is an absolute creditor over another. God's recalling of a favour, on the other hand, creates no debt; **it states a situation that already exists.**

**And further:** when a person casts his giving in another's face, he puts himself in the place of the giver. The verse corrects this: you are not the giver.

I record this as my own reading; the two senses of the root are a fact the lexicographers record.

### أَنْ هَدَىٰكُمْ لِلْإِيمَٰنِ — what the gift is

Here is the finest place in the sentence.

**What did they say?** *An aslamū* — "we have submitted". They put forward the act they had done: **islām.**

**What does the verse count as the favour?** *An hadākum li'l-**īmān*** — "in guiding you to **faith**".

**The word has changed.** They said *islām*; the verse says *īmān*.

And in the fourteenth verse it had been said that faith had not yet entered their hearts.

**What this change means needs to be dwelt on, and one must be careful here.**

The qualification at the end of the sentence is decisive: ***in kuntum ṣādiqīn*** — "if you are truthful."

The sentence, that is, is conditional. The favour holds if the condition is fulfilled.

**Two readings are possible and both find support in the sources:**

| Reading | What *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* attaches to | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **The condition attaches to the guidance** | "In guiding you to faith — if you have truly believed" | The realising of the favour depends on the truth of the claim |
| **The condition attaches to the claim** | "If you are sincere in saying 'we have believed', then the real obligation is this" | If sincerity is assumed, the ruling is this |

**I impose no preference.**

**But what is common to both readings is this, and I record it as my own reading:** the verse shows the rank they are said not to have reached (faith) as **the subject of the favour**. What it says, that is, is this: if anything is to be counted a favour, what is to be counted is not the work you did (islām); it is **what has been given to you** (faith) — and that is not your doing in any case.

And read together with the *lammā* of the fourteenth verse, one sees that the sentence still holds the door open: faith has not entered yet, but when it does it will be a favour.

### The ring with the eighth verse

The twin of this verse in the sūra is the eighth:

> **49/8:** *faḍlan mina'llāhi wa niʿmah* — "as a bounty from God and a favour."
> **49/17:** *bali'llāhu yamunnu ʿalaykum* — "rather it is God who has bestowed a favour on you."

**The two verses say the same thing:** to reach faith is not a person's own achievement.

**But their addressees differ:**

| | **49/8** | **49/17** |
|---|---|---|
| Addressee | Those who believe | Those who say "we have believed" |
| Context | After praise | After a claim |
| Its function | To prevent praise turning into entitlement | To prevent a claim turning into a debt owed |

**The sūra makes the same correction in two different places:** to the one praised and to the one who claims. In both, what is done is to change the owner of the act.

I record this parallel as my own reading; the words and the positions of the two verses stand in the text.

### What this says to the present

**One. Participation turning into a debt owed.**

When a person joins a community, a cause or a work, the participation itself may in time come to be felt as a debt owed to him: "I am here", "I joined", "it is thanks to me". This feeling requires no ill will; it is a natural by-product of the act of giving — the two senses of the root م-ن-ن already record this.

What the verse does is to question the **direction** of the debt: did the one who joined give something, or was the ability to join given to him?

**Two. And a note.**

It is possible for this verse to be used to belittle someone's contribution, and that is the reverse of what the verse does. The verse does not **deny** what a person has done — the fourteenth verse had refused this openly: *lā yalitkum min aʿmālikum shayʾā*, "He will not diminish anything of your deeds."

**The sūra does two things separately, that is:** it gives what has been done its due (14), and it prevents what has been done from being turned into a debt owed (17). To confuse the two is to break the verse's word order.

## 49/18 — إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَعْلَمُ غَيْبَ ٱلسَّمَٰوَٰتِ وَٱلْأَرْضِ وَٱللَّهُ بَصِيرٌۢ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ

*Inna'llāha yaʿlamu ghayba's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ, wa'llāhu baṣīrun bimā taʿmalūn*

"God knows the unseen of the heavens and the earth. And God sees what you do."

### The seal of the sūra

For eighteen verses the sūra has recorded conduct: lower your voice, wait at the door, verify the report, set matters right, do not mock, do not find fault, do not fix nicknames, do not conjecture, do not pry, do not speak behind another's back, do not boast of your lineage, do not throw your claim in another's face.

**And the ground of all these notes is gathered into a single sentence: being seen.**

### غَيْب — and the ring with the twelfth verse

**Root: غ-ي-ب.** It was treated in the twelfth verse on the occasion of *ghība*: to disappear from sight, not to be present, to remain covered.

**And now the word comes back with the root's original sense: *ghayb* — what is unknown, what is covered.**

**The link between the two occurrences is the sūra's tightest weave:**

> **49/12:** *wa lā **tajassasū** wa lā **yaghtab** baʿḍukum baʿḍā* — do not pry into what is hidden, do not speak in another's absence.
> **49/18:** *inna'llāha yaʿlamu **ghayba**'s-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — God knows the **unseen** of the heavens and the earth.

**The sūra, that is, after forbidding the searching out of what is covered, closes by saying who it is that knows what is covered.**

And this completes the ground of the prohibition. The prohibition of prying is not a prohibition of knowledge — the knowledge exists already and is known. The prohibition is about **whose that knowledge is**.

**The consequence of this is the following, and I record it as my own reading:** a person's searching out another person's hidden things is not idle curiosity; **it is an attempt to take upon oneself a work that belongs to another.** The verse makes the attempt meaningless by showing whose work it is.

The same principle was recorded in the section on al-Mumtaḥina: *"the authority given to you is not the authority to know the heart."*

### بَصِيرٌۢ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ — and the ring with the first verse

**بَصِير — root ب-ص-ر.** To see; and not the eye only, but **baṣīra** — the sight that grasps the inner side of a thing.

**And a ring is formed with the sūra's first verse:**

> **49/1:** *inna'llāha **samīʿun ʿalīm*** — God is **Hearing**, Knowing.
> **49/18:** *wa'llāhu **baṣīrun** bimā taʿmalūn* — God **sees** what you do.

**The sūra opens with hearing and closes with seeing.**

**And this agrees exactly with the sūra's own subject:**

- The sūra's **first block** was about voice: do not raise your voices, those who lower their voices, those who call from behind the chambers. And at the door of that block the name **the Hearing** was placed.
- The sūra's **last block** is about claim and deed: what you say and what you do. And at the close of that block the name **the Seeing** is placed.

**Hearing looks to the word, seeing looks to the act.** The sūra began with commands about the word and ends with a note about the act.

I record this ring as my own reading; but the words and the positions of the two verse-endings are the text's own structure.

### تَعْمَلُونَ — and the choice of the last word

**Root: ع-م-ل.** To do, to work. *ʿAmal* — the work done.

**And that the sūra's last word is a *verb* is worth recording.**

Throughout the sūra a great deal has been spoken of: voice, report, conjecture, identity, claim, lineage, faith. Most of these are things that are inward and invisible.

**The last word comes out into the open: *taʿmalūn* — you do.**

**And a link is formed with the second verse:** there it was said *an taḥbaṭa **aʿmālukum*** — "lest your deeds come to nothing". The same root at the beginning and at the end of the sūra: at the start the coming to nothing of deeds, at the close the seeing of deeds.

I record this as an observation about the wording.

### The whole of the fifth block (14-18)

| Verse | What happens |
|---|---|
| **14** | A claim comes; it is refused; the right word is given in its place; the door is left open (*lammā*) |
| **15** | The real counterpart of the claim is described: faith + not falling into doubt + striving with wealth and life |
| **16** | The presenting of the claim as information is refused |
| **17** | The presenting of the claim as a debt owed is refused; the direction is reversed |
| **18** | And the seal: it is He who knows and sees |

**The block is the taking up of a single sentence — "we have believed" — in four different ways:** as a thing done, as a thing measured, as information, as a debt owed. And at the end it is said where the measure is.

## On the sūra as a whole

### The closing of the circle

At the beginning of the sūra I recorded that the commands trace a circle narrowing from outside inward. Now that all eighteen verses have been read, it can be seen how the circle closes.

| Ring | Verses | Who stands opposite the one addressed | The common name of what is forbidden | Visibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 1-5 | Authority | Going ahead, raising the voice, forcing the door | Everyone hears |
| **2** | 6-8 | A report | Acting without verifying | It comes out in the outcome |
| **3** | 9-10 | Groups in conflict | Standing aside; overstepping the bound | Everyone sees |
| **4** | 11-12 | The person beside you | Mockery, fault-finding, nicknames, conjecture, prying, backbiting | Partly hidden |
| **5** | 13-18 | Your own claim to identity | Superiority by lineage; taking a claim for a debt owed | **Wholly hidden** |

**The right-hand column gives the logic of the sequence.** The sūra descends from the most visible to the least visible. And in the last ring — in a person's claim about himself — no outward check remains.

**And the sūra's last verse stands at exactly this point:** *wa'llāhu baṣīrun bimā taʿmalūn.*

**The sūra, that is, names the one who watches at the place where watching ends.** At the centre of the circle, where a person is left alone with himself, the one thing that remains is being seen.

I record this reading as my own reading; the word order and the placing of the verse-endings are the text itself.

### The web of words: the roots that hold the sūra together

The reason al-Ḥujurāt holds together despite its short compass is that the same roots come back in different blocks.

| Root | Verses in which it occurs | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **و-ق-ي** (taqwā) | 1, 3, 10, 12, 13 | At the joints of the five blocks; at the end it becomes the **measure** |
| **ع-ل-م** (to know) | 1, 7, 8, 13, 16, 18 | The ground of the limit of knowledge; in five the subject is God |
| **ف-س-ق** (to come out of the husk) | 6, 7, 11 | Person → act → **name** |
| **أ-م-ن** (faith) | 1, 2, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 17 | Both the address and the subject |
| **ص-ل-ح** (to set right) | 9 (twice), 10 | Both before and after the conflict |
| **أ-خ-و** (brother) | 10, 12 | Definition → comparison: a brother's flesh |
| **ص-د-ق** (truthfulness) | 15, 17 | It frames the last block |
| **ك-ر-ه** (to find repellent) | 7, 12 | God made it repellent → you loathed it |
| **س-ل-م / ي-س-ل-م** | 14, 17 | The word of the claim |
| **ع-م-ل** (deed) | 2, 14, 18 | Coming to nothing → not being diminished → **being seen** |
| **غ-ي-ب** (covered) | 12, 18 | The prohibition → the ground of the prohibition |
| **ق-و-م** (a people) | 6, 11 (twice) | The people harmed / the mocker and the mocked |

**The most striking row in the table is the second from the end:** the root غ-ي-ب occurs only twice in the sūra, and these two occurrences are a prohibition and its ground, standing six verses apart.

**And the three occurrences of the root ع-م-ل make up one sentence:**
- **49/2:** your deeds may come to nothing.
- **49/14:** nothing of your deeds will be diminished.
- **49/18:** and what you do is being seen.

### The verse-endings

The endings of the sūra's verses show its character:

| Verse | Verse-ending | Its kind |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *samīʿun ʿalīm* | A divine name |
| 2 | *wa antum lā tashʿurūn* | A warning |
| 3 | *maghfiratun wa ajrun ʿaẓīm* | A promise |
| 4 | *aktharuhum lā yaʿqilūn* | A ruling |
| 5 | *ghafūrun raḥīm* | **Forgiveness** |
| 6 | *ʿalā mā faʿaltum nādimīn* | A consequence |
| 7 | *ulāʾika humu'r-rāshidūn* | A promise |
| 8 | *ʿalīmun ḥakīm* | A divine name |
| 9 | *inna'llāha yuḥibbu'l-muqsiṭīn* | A statement of love |
| 10 | *laʿallakum turḥamūn* | **Mercy** |
| 11 | *fa-ulāʾika humu'ẓ-ẓālimūn* | A ruling |
| 12 | *inna'llāha tawwābun raḥīm* | **Repentance** |
| 13 | *ʿalīmun khabīr* | A divine name |
| 14 | *ghafūrun raḥīm* | **Forgiveness** |
| 15 | *ulāʾika humu'ṣ-ṣādiqūn* | A promise |
| 16 | *bi-kulli shayʾin ʿalīm* | A divine name |
| 17 | *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* | A condition |
| 18 | *baṣīrun bimā taʿmalūn* | A divine name |

**The count is this:** of eighteen verse-endings, five end with a divine name; four carry a note of forgiveness, mercy or repentance; three a promise; two a ruling.

**Despite the sūra's heavy prohibitions, that is, most of the verse-endings state a door that opens or a name.** Only two verse-endings state a ruling (4 and 11), and the second of them comes after the qualification *"whoever does not repent"*.

I record this count as my own observation; the verse-endings can be counted one by one.

### Why the sūra gives its rules of conduct in this order

A question can be asked about the order of the sūra's blocks: why does the report (6-8) come before the conflict (9-10)? And why does the conduct of the individual (11-12) come after the conflict?

**The text's own word order carries an answer, and I record it as my own reading:**

**One. A report is a cause of conflict.** The sixth verse took as its subject an unverified report's harming a people. The ninth verse takes the fighting of two groups. The order runs from cause to effect: a false report produces conflict.

**Two. Mockery and backbiting are causes of conflict too — but causes that work more slowly.** The acts in the eleventh and twelfth verses do not split a community in a day; they wear it down over time. The sūra gives the fast cause (the report) first and the slow cause (the tongue) after.

**Three. And the claim of lineage (13) is the slowest and deepest cause.** Mockery is a piece of conduct; superiority by lineage is a view of the world and lays the ground for all the other conduct.

**Four. And at the very end stands the root beneath all of these (14-18):** a person's claim about himself. The assumption common to the one who mocks, who finds fault, who conjectures, who boasts of his lineage is this: **I stand somewhere.**

**The sūra, that is, sets out the causes of conflict from the fast to the slow, from outside inward**, and at the end arrives at the source of them all.

### Al-Ḥujurāt and al-Mujādala: two sūras of conduct

There are two sūras in the Qurʾān that directly regulate the manners of assembly and speech, and both are Medinan. The difference between them is worth recording:

| | **58. al-Mujādala** | **49. al-Ḥujurāt** |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Private conversation (*najwā*), the order of the assembly, private audience | Voice, report, conflict, tongue, identity |
| The relation regulated | The **private** relation with the Prophet | The relation with the Prophet and **with one another** |
| Its main word | *najwā* (drawing aside) | *ḥujurāt* (an enclosed space) |
| The direction of the prohibition | **Withdrawing** from the crowd | **Entering** a boundary |
| Shared idiom | *bayna yaday* (58/12, 13) | *bayna yaday* (49/1) |

**The two sūras use the same idiom in opposite directions**, and this was recorded in the section on al-Mujādala:

> **58/12:** *fa-qaddimū **bayna yaday** najwākum ṣadaqa* — put an alms **before** your conversation.
> **49/1:** *lā tuqaddimū **bayna yaday**i'llāhi wa rasūlih* — do not go **before** God and His Messenger.

The sentence drawn out in the section on al-Mujādala was: *"Do not put yourself forward, but put your wealth forward."*

**And what the two sūras do in common is this:** when a community grows, who stands where produces a hierarchy of itself — the seat in the assembly, the frequency of private audience, the loudness of the voice, nearness to the door. Both sūras break up this spontaneously produced ranking.

### What the sūra says to the present — as a whole

What each verse says to the present has been written verse by verse; here I record only what can be said about the **whole** of the sūra.

**One. Where the measure of religiousness is placed.**

In al-Ḥujurāt there is not a single command about worship. All eighteen verses regulate man's relation with man.

This is not written in order to repeat a general sentence such as that religiousness does not consist of worship. What is recorded is more concrete: **the Qurʾān has given a whole sūra, without speaking of worship at all, to the conduct of behaviour.** And the word repeated throughout the sūra — taqwā — is the word most often identified with worship. In this sūra taqwā is named together with lowering the voice, verifying a report, not mocking, and setting matters right.

**Two. The limiting of the authority to pass judgment.**

This is the sūra's most repeated move, and it is seen in five separate places:

| Verse | The judgment suspended |
|---|---|
| **6** | The judgment about a report — verify it |
| **9** | The judgment about the identity of a side in conflict — still a believer |
| **11** | The judgment about who is better — *ʿasā* |
| **12** | The conjecture about a person — *ijtanibū* |
| **13** | The judgment about who is more worthy — *ʿinda'llāh* |

**In all five the same operation is performed: the instrument of measurement is taken out of human hands.**

And the reason for this stands in the sūra's last verse: measuring belongs to the one who knows.

**Three. And a note: how this text should not be read.**

Al-Ḥujurāt is an extremely convenient text for describing other people. Within it there are mockers, fault-finders, fixers of nicknames, gossips, spreaders of unverified reports, boasters of lineage and makers of empty claims. A reader can easily distribute this whole list among the people he knows.

**And this is the very thing the sūra forbids.**

The measure recorded in the section on al-Humaza holds here all the more: *"To use a text in order to place people lower is precisely lamz."*

The sūra's address had already settled this: **five times *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū*.** The text takes as its addressee not those outside but the one who reads it. And even when it opens the address to all people in the thirteenth verse, it puts no one outside the address.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

At several points in the text definite language was deliberately not used; the reader should know these:

- **That the roots ح-ج-ر and ه-ج-ر are distinct was recorded plainly.** That the word *muhājir* comes from the same root as *ḥujra* is a common error and was corrected. The nearness of sense drawn between the two roots was presented **not as a claim about derivation** but only as an observation.

- **The link the sūra's name makes with its subject (boundary) was recorded as my own reading.** That the name was given afterwards, from the word in the fourth verse, was also stated.

- **In the accounts of the occasions of revelation no personal or tribal name was given.** The reason has two layers and was explained in the text: the details of the reports diverge; and to record in definite terms a particular person as a *fāsiq*, or a tribe as the object of the ruling *lā yaʿqilūn*, is a heavier judgment than the transmissions in my hands can carry.

- **The supplied objects proposed for the omitted object in 49/1 were given in a table; no preference was made.** That the omission of the object states generality was related as a rule the lexicographers record.

- **The picture of the "swollen animal" for the root ح-ب-ط** was given as a concrete usage the lexicographers record. The link drawn between this picture and the note *"while you are not aware"* was presented as **my own reading** and was not ascribed to any exegete.

- **Whether the expression "the coming to nothing of deeds" in 49/2 states a certain outcome or a warning:** the note in the classical sources was related; no preference was imposed.

- **For the function of the letter lām in the expression *li't-taqwā* in 49/3** four explanations were given in a table; **none was preferred and it was recorded that none is binding.**

- **The difference of variant reading in 49/6** (*fa-tabayyanū* / *fa-tathabbatū*) was treated in a table, and it was shown how the two readings change the meaning. **Which imam read which was not written**, because I cannot state it with certainty. The interpretation that the two readings complete one another was recorded as **my own reading**.

- **The sense "anger, roughness" for the word *bi-jahālatin*** was related as a view recorded in the classical sources; no preference was imposed.

- **The debate over the contrary implication (*mafhūm al-mukhālafa*)** (whether a just person's report is to be investigated) was given as two views; **no legal ruling was issued and no preference was made.**

- **The juristic debates drawn from 49/9** (*aḥkām al-bughāt*) were related in a table together with their disagreements. **None of them is this text's preference and none is binding.** Only the order in the verse's own word order — first *iṣlāḥ*, then stopping, then *iṣlāḥ* again — was stated as a finding resting on the wording of the text.

- **The distinction of the plurals *ikhwa* / *ikhwān* in 49/10** was given as a tendency the lexicographers record; **it was stated plainly that it is not an absolute rule**, and the reading drawn from it was made conditional on this.

- **The reading *bayna ikhwatikum* in 49/10** was recorded; **no imam's name was given.**

- **The three explanations of the separate mention of women in 49/11** were given in a table; no preference was made.

- **The two views on what the word *ism* in the expression *biʾsa'l-ismu'l-fusūq* is** (a nickname / repute) were given; **neither was preferred.**

- **The two readings of the word *anfusakum*** (reciprocity / unity) were given in a table; that both say something true was presented as **my own reading**.

- **It was not claimed that there is a link of derivation between the roots ن-ب-ز and ن-ب-ذ;** it was written plainly that the two roots are distinct, and the nearness of sense was recorded only as **an association that is not certain**. No interpretation was built upon it.

- **The distinction between *tajassus* and *taḥassus*** (bad purpose / good purpose) was given as a transmitted view; it was not claimed that all the lexicographers agree on it.

- **The definition of backbiting** was related as a report found in the hadith sources and widely transmitted in the explanation of this verse. **No ascription to a particular source (al-Bukhārī / Muslim and the like) was given**, because I cannot state it with certainty.

- **The analysis of the four elements of the comparison of a dead brother's flesh is my own reading.** That the four elements are present in the verse is the wording of the text; the interpretation that each does a separate work is mine and has not been ascribed to any exegete.

- **The two views about repentance for backbiting** (seeking pardon / asking forgiveness and praying) were given in a table; **no legal ruling was issued.** It was also stated that on the exceptions to backbiting there is disagreement in the details.

- **That the root ش-ع-ب is counted among the *aḍdād*** was related, as in the section on al-Mursalāt, as a view the lexicographers record; it was not claimed that all the lexicographers agree on it.

- **For the sequence of tribal layers** (*shaʿb* → *qabīla* → …) no definite series was given, because there is disagreement in the sources.

- **The three views on whom the expression *min dhakarin wa unthā* in 49/13 means** were given in a table; no preference was made.

- **The connection of the word *karam* with lineage in the Arabic of the age of ignorance** was recorded as an observation about language and history, and no cultural ruling was drawn from it.

- **That the word *al-aʿrāb* in 49/14 means not "the Arabs" but "the bedouin" was recorded plainly.** And that the Qurʾān itself issues no wholesale ruling about this community was shown by setting al-Tawba 9/97 and 9/99 side by side. **No wholesale ruling is issued in this text about any ethnic, national or religious group.**

- **The three main tendencies on the relation of faith and islām** were given in a table; **no preference was imposed and no theological ruling was issued.** The note recorded in the section on al-Dhāriyāt — that "this relation cannot be settled without taking the whole of the Qurʾān into account" — was repeated.

- **That the particle *lammā* implies expectation** was related as a settled note in Arabic grammar; the reading drawn from it, that "the door is left open", was grounded on that note.

- **That 49/14 cannot be used about someone else** was stated on the ground of the sūra's own inner logic (verses 11-12 and the *ʿinda'llāh* qualification of verse 13) and of the measure recorded in the section on al-Mumtaḥina.

- **The two readings of whether the particle *thumma* in 49/15 states time or rank** were given; no preference was made.

- **The three explanations of the order of wealth and life** were given in a table; no preference was made.

- **The reading *lā yaʾlitkum* in 49/14** was recorded; it was stated that it does not change the meaning, and **no imam's name was given.**

- **The two readings of what the condition *in kuntum ṣādiqīn* in 49/17 attaches to** were given in a table; no preference was made.

- **The ring by which the sūra opens with hearing and closes with seeing, the narrowing of the circle, the table of the web of words, the count of the verse-endings and the logic of the order of the blocks — all of these were recorded as my own reading and observation.** The data they rest on (the places of the words, the text of the verse-endings, the repetitions of the roots) stand in the text and can be counted; the interpretations drawn from them are mine.

- **No definite year was given for the period of the sūra's revelation;** the estimate "late Medinan" was recorded as an estimate of period, not as a dating.

- **No claim resting on the reckoning of numbers and letters was used.** The count of the verse-endings and the repetitions of the roots were given not as a claim to a hidden meaning but as observations about style.

- **No side was taken in any current political controversy.** In writing what 49/6, 49/9 and 49/13 say to the present this rule was stated separately as well.
