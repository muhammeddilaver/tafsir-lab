# 94. Sūrat al-Inshirāḥ

Eight verses. There is a judgement close to unanimity that it is Meccan. In the lists of the order of revelation it is shown immediately after al-Ḍuḥā; with the note that these lists are the product of individual judgement and are not established by mass transmission.

The sūra has several names, and all of them derive from the first sentence of the text: **al-Inshirāḥ**, **al-Sharḥ**, **Alam nashraḥ**, **Alam nashraḥ laka**. The word *inshirāḥ* does not occur in the text of the sūra in that form; the verb occurs (*nashraḥ*), and the name was produced from it.

## The continuation of al-Ḍuḥā

This sūra does not bear a similarity of subject with the one before it; it stands rather like **the continuation of the same speech**. The link between the two texts will be set out in detail in a separate section at the end of the sūra, but there are a few things that need to be known from the outset:

- Both speak to the same addressee, about the same situation.
- Both turn to the past with the same question pattern: *alam…*
- Both draw evidence from the past and close with a command.
- **Their last verses are of almost the same skeleton** — this is the most concrete datum showing the link between the two sūras at the level of language.

Some scholars have said that these two sūras ought to be counted as a single sūra. The majority have not accepted this; the ground is plain: in the order of the muṣḥaf there is a basmala between them, and the arrangement is established by mass transmission. But even that the view arose at all shows how strongly the link was felt in the first generations. The same thing happened between al-Fīl and Quraysh as well (treated in the section on Quraysh).

## The skeleton of the sūra

| Section | Verses | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **The reminder** | 1-4 | Three operations: We opened, We set down, We raised |
| **The rule** | 5-6 | With hardship, ease — twice |
| **The command** | 7-8 | When you finish, labour; turn to your Lord |

## The structure of the sound

As in al-Ḍuḥā, here too the verse-endings correspond one to one with the sections of the sūra — and here in three layers:

- **1-4:** *ṣadrak, wizrak, ẓahrak, dhikrak* — four verses, the same *-raka* sound.
- **5-6:** *yusran, yusran* — two verses, the same word.
- **7-8:** *fa'nṣab, fa'rghab* — two verses, the closed *-b* sound.

That is, 4 + 2 + 2. And the changes of sound fall exactly where the changes of meaning are: where the reminder ends and the rule begins, where the rule ends and the command begins.

The same thing had happened in al-Ḍuḥā: the open *-ā* sound running through eight verses was cut off at the place where the commands begin. **Both sūras build the sound according to the sections of the meaning.** I record this as an observation about the sound.

Since the basmala was treated in earlier sūras it is not taken up again here; the verse numbers have been counted without the basmala.

## 94/1 — أَلَمْ نَشْرَحْ لَكَ صَدْرَكَ

*Alam nashraḥ laka ṣadrak*
"Did We not open your breast for you?"

### أَلَمْ — *taqrīr* again

The same pattern as at al-Ḍuḥā 6: hamza (interrogative) + *lam* (negation). Its function is not to ask for information but to make the addressee say "yes" out of his own life.

That the two sūras make the same move, opening with the same particle, is one of the most visible signs of the link. Al-Ḍuḥā had asked *"Did He not find you an orphan and shelter you?"*; al-Sharḥ asks *"Did We not open your breast?"*

### But the subject has changed

And here there is a difference worth dwelling on.

In al-Ḍuḥā the verbs were in the **third person**: *yajidka*, *āwā*, *hadā*, *aghnā* — "he found", "he sheltered". And the subject was given by a noun explicitly mentioned: *Rabbuka* — "your Lord".

In al-Sharḥ the verbs are **first person plural**: *nashraḥ*, *waḍaʿnā*, *rafaʿnā* — "did We not open", "We set down", "We raised". The *nūn* of majesty (the plural of exaltation).

This is **iltifāt** — a shift in the direction of address or of the person of the verb. And its direction is clear: **from far to near.** The sentence "your Lord found you" and the sentence "we opened your breast" can give the same information, but they do not give it from the same distance.

When the two sūras are read together, a movement emerges: what is first recounted in the third person is then said in the first. At the end of the sūra it will return to the third person by saying *"to your Lord"* once more (verse 8) — that is, the distance closes and opens again. This reading is my own arrangement.

### The root شرح

**Root: ش-ر-ح.** The concrete sense given by the dictionaries is this: **to spread out and open flesh, to widen it by cutting it into thin slices.** *Sharaḥtu'l-laḥm* — I sliced the meat and spread it out.

From here it widens in two directions:

- **To open a thing, to widen it.** To spread out what is closed.
- **To open up a piece of speech.** The Arabic *sharḥ* — the exposition of a text — is exactly this. To open out a text that stands closed and spread it, to make its layers visible.

This second sense names the very text now being read. Exegesis is a kind of *sharḥ*; and the first word of the sūra is the name of that work. I note this as a coincidence, not as a claim — but that the word should name its own work is striking.

### صَدْر — the breast

**Root: ص-د-ر.** What comes to the front, what comes first, the place of issuing. From the same root:

- **مَصْدَر (*maṣdar*)** — place of issuing, source. In grammar, the root noun from which the verb derives.
- **صَدَرَ (*ṣadara*)** — he came out, he issued forth. Al-Zilzāl 99/6: *"On that day people will come out in groups."*
- **صَدْر (*ṣadr*)** — the front part, the head, of a company or of an affair.

The breast is the part of the body that comes to the front. But in the Qurʾān the *ṣadr* is more than an anatomical part: it is **the seat of what is hidden, of intention and of feeling**.

*"He knows what is within the breasts"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/154; also al-Mulk 67/13, Hūd 11/5). And the plainest description: *"It is not the eyes that go blind; what goes blind is the hearts within the breasts"* (al-Ḥajj 22/46).

That is, "the opening of the breast" is not the widening of an organ; it is **the widening of a person's inner capacity**.

### The Qurʾān's own description: al-Anʿām 6/125

What this concept means is described by the Qurʾān itself, and the description is extraordinarily clear:

*"Whomever God wishes to guide, He opens his breast to islām; and whomever He wishes to lead astray, He makes his breast narrow and constricted — as though he were climbing up into the sky."*

The verse sets **narrowing** over against opening; and it recounts the narrowing by a comparison: like one climbing into the sky.

The comparison rests on a physical observation: as one rises, the breath grows short. I do not present this as a claim of a "scientific miracle" — the shortening of the breath at altitude is something known to everyone who lives in mountainous country or who climbs, and no special knowledge is required for it to be known in the Arabian Peninsula. What I record is simpler: the verse recounts inner distress by **a bodily description**, and the description it chooses has to do with the breath.

### *Sharḥ al-ṣadr* in the Qurʾān

The concept occurs in several places in the Qurʾān, and all of them are inward:

- *"Is the one whose breast God has opened to islām, so that he is upon a light from his Lord, [like one whose heart is hardened]?"* (al-Zumar 39/22).
- *"Whoever opens his breast to disbelief, upon them is a wrath from God"* (al-Naḥl 16/106).

The second verse establishes an important balance: **"opening" is a neutral act.** A breast may be opened; what is decisive is what it is opened to. Widening is not in itself a virtue.

### The prayer of Moses

And the strongest link is here:

*"My Lord, open my breast for me; make my affair easy for me; and loose the knot from my tongue, that they may understand my speech"* (Ṭāhā 20/25-28).

Moses (Mūsā), when he is sent to Pharaoh, **asks** for these. Muhammad is **reminded** that they have been given.

And note the second item of the prayer: *"wa yassir lī amrī"* — "make my affair easy for me". The root is **ي-س-ر**, that is, **yusr**. The key word of the fifth and sixth verses of Sūrat al-Sharḥ.

That is, the first two items of Moses's prayer and the first and fifth verses of al-Sharḥ carry the same two concepts: **sharḥ** and **yusr**. This overlap is beyond dispute in that the words are one and the same at the level of the root. What conclusion is to be drawn from it is interpretation — what I record here is that two texts have used the same two concepts in the same order.

(There is one further observation about the continuation of the prayer; it will be returned to at the word *wizr* in verse 2.)

### لَكَ — "for you"

*Alam nashraḥ ṣadraka* could have been said and the sense would have been complete. **Laka** has come in between.

This is an addition that separately registers that the act was done **for the benefit of** the addressee. And in the fourth verse the same addition will be repeated: *wa rafaʿnā **laka** dhikrak.* In the second verse, however, a different preposition will come: *wa waḍaʿnā **ʿanka** wizrak* — "from you".

Three verses, three prepositions, three directions:

| Verse | Preposition | Direction | Operation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | لَكَ | for you / to you | opening |
| 2 | عَنكَ | away from you | taking |
| 4 | لَكَ | for you | raising |

This shows that the first four verses of the sūra are built on a scheme of place: the inside was widened, what lay on top was taken away, the name was carried upward.

### The relation of the verse to the report of "the splitting of the breast"

In the sources of biography and hadith, reports are transmitted that the Prophet's breast was actually split open — some tied to the period of his childhood, some to the night of the ascension. A portion of these reports is found in the sound collections.

Some exegetes have tied this verse to that event. Here one must be careful, and I am obliged to separate two things:

**The existence of the reports is one matter, the wording of the verse another.** In the verse there is no word that indicates a physical splitting. And the expression *sharḥ al-ṣadr* is inward in all the Qurʾān's other usages — al-Anʿām 6/125, al-Zumar 39/22, al-Naḥl 16/106, Ṭāhā 20/25. In all four what is meant is an inner state in the sense of the breast's being opened and widened.

Therefore to tie the verse to that report is an exegetical interpretation, not a requirement of the text. I do not reject the report; nor do I say that the verse recounts it.

## 94/2-3 — وَوَضَعْنَا عَنكَ وِزْرَكَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنقَضَ ظَهْرَكَ

*Wa waḍaʿnā ʿanka wizrak / alladhī anqaḍa ẓahrak*
"And We set down from you your burden — that burden which had made your back crack."

The two verses must be taken together; the second is the qualifier of the first and has been counted as an independent verse.

### وَضَعَ — to set down

**Root: و-ض-ع.** To put down, to lay aside, to set down.

The Turkish words *vaziyet* (situation) and *mevzu* (subject — that which has been set down) are borrowings from this root.

**An interesting usage:** the same verb is used in Arabic also for **giving birth**. *"I have given birth to her, a female"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/36); *"Every pregnant one lays down what she carries"* (al-Ḥajj 22/2). The reason is direct: to give birth is to set down a carried load. The Arabic word *ḥaml* means both burden and pregnancy.

That is, the single idea lying beneath the root is this: **the laying down of something carried.**

**And in the Qurʾān almost exactly the same structure occurs once more:**

*"…that Prophet who commands them what is right and forbids them what is wrong, who makes the good things lawful for them and the foul things unlawful, and **who sets down from them their heavy loads and the chains that were upon them**"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/157).

The verb is the same (*yaḍaʿu*), the preposition is the same (*ʿanhum* — "from them"), the act is the same. The difference is this: in al-Sharḥ this operation is performed **upon the Prophet**, in al-Aʿrāf it is performed **by the Prophet**.

When the two verses are set side by side an order emerges: first the one whose load is set down, then the one who sets loads down. What is given becomes what is passed on. This is the same logic as the symmetry al-Ḍuḥā established — there too the favours received turned into the commands given.

### وِزْر — burden

**Root: و-ز-ر.** A heavy load.

Derivatives:

- **وَزِير (*wazīr*)** — one who shares the load, who takes on the ruler's burden. The word's present-day political sense comes from here.
- **وَزَر (*wazar*)** — refuge, a shelter in the mountains. Al-Qiyāma 75/11: *"No! There is no refuge!"*
- **وِزْر (*wizr*)** — both burden and **sin**.

The last item is settled in the Qurʾān. There is a pattern repeated in five separate places: *"No bearer of a burden bears the burden of another"* (al-Anʿām 6/164, al-Isrāʾ 17/15, Fāṭir 35/18, al-Zumar 39/7, al-Najm 53/38). The "burden" here is the responsibility for the act committed.

That is, Arabic conceives sin as **a weight that is carried**. English carries the same intuition in "to shoulder the blame", "a weight on the conscience", "to be weighed down by guilt".

### A return to the prayer of Moses

The prayer at Ṭāhā 20/25-28 was mentioned above. The continuation of the prayer runs thus:

*"And give me a **helper (wazīr)** from my family: my brother Aaron (Hārūn)"* (Ṭāhā 20/29-30).

The root is the same: **و-ز-ر**. Moses asks for a *wazīr* — a partner in the load. In al-Sharḥ, on the other hand, the *wizr* — the load itself — is set down.

That the two texts should use the same root in two different derivatives is striking. But I draw no ruling from this: Moses asked for someone to share the load, Muhammad's load was set down — these are not the same thing. What I record is the common root; the link to be built is left to the reader's judgement.

### What is the *wizr* here? — the disagreement

The text does not say what the burden was. Several explanations are given in the classical commentaries:

| View | What it means | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shortcomings before prophethood** | A forgiven past | A link is made with the verse *"that God may forgive you what went before of your sin and what came after"* (al-Fatḥ 48/2) | It enters into the debate about the impeccability of the prophets; moreover "wizr" is described here as a weight that is set down, not as an offence that is pardoned |
| **The weight of the message** | The load of the task of conveying | *"We shall cast upon you a weighty word"* (al-Muzzammil 73/5). Revelation itself is characterised as "heavy" in the Qurʾān | The verse says "We set down"; the load of the message was not set down, it continued |
| **The distress caused by his people's not believing** | The inner weight arising from the resistance of those addressed | The Qurʾān records this weight repeatedly: *"You will almost destroy yourself because they do not believe"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/3; also al-Kahf 18/6, Fāṭir 35/8) | The text does not state it; it is inferred from the context |
| **The weight of the uncertainty before revelation** | The weight of the state of "not knowing the way" at al-Ḍuḥā 7 | The link between the two sūras; the state of *ḍāll* was a deprivation, and deprivation weighs | The connection is indirect |

**What can be drawn from the text itself.** Three things can be said:

1. The verse says *"We set down from you"* — that is, the burden was a weight **standing upon** him. This describes pressure rather than guilt.
2. The verse comes immediately after the first verse, and the first verse recounts an **inner widening**. The two together describe a psychological lightening.
3. The third verse describes the burden by the **effect** it left on the body of the one carrying it — as will be seen below. This too shows that the speech is not in the language of guilt but in the language of carrying.

These three observations support the second, third and fourth readings. But I state no definite preference; the text does not name it.

### أَنقَضَ — it made it crack

The most concrete word of the sūra.

**Root: ن-ق-ض.** Its original sense is **to undo, to unpick, to unravel what has been woven.**

Its settled usage in the Qurʾān has to do with the covenant: *"Those who break the covenant of God after its confirmation"* (al-Baqara 2/27). And an extraordinary comparison: *"Do not be like her who unravelled her yarn after she had spun it firmly"* (al-Naḥl 16/92).

**In Form IV (*anqaḍa*)** the word takes on a special sense: **a thing's cracking, its joints giving out a sound**, under a weight. The lexicographers explain this usage by the sound the spine gives out under a load; its link with the root's sense of "coming undone, unravelling" is direct — what cracks is what has begun to come apart.

**The sound of the word.** *Anqaḍa* — the *nūn* comes out through the nose, the *qāf* bursts from deep in the throat, the *ḍād* is a heavy, thick sound. The pronunciation of the word seems to imitate the sound it recounts.

That certain roots in Arabic have the character of sound-imitation (onomatopoeia) is recorded by the lexicographers. I do not present this as a definite ruling; but it is something observable for one who reads the word aloud.

### The verse's real point: where the measure lies

This is one of the finest places in the sūra.

The verse does not describe the **weight** of the burden. How heavy it was is not said, what it resembled is not said, it is not weighed.

What is described is the **effect the burden left on the body of the one carrying it**: it had made his back crack.

This shifts the measure from the objective to the subjective. The question is not how many kilos the load was; it is how the one under it stood.

And this has a consequence: **the weight of a burden cannot be weighed from outside.** The same load gives out two different sounds in two people. The measure the verse chooses is closed to comparison.

### ظَهْر — the back

**Root: ظ-ه-ر.** The back; but from the same root:

- **ظَاهِر (*ẓāhir*)** — what appears, what is in the open. The opposite of *bāṭin*.
- **ظُهُور (*ẓuhūr*)** — coming into view.
- **ظَهِير (*ẓahīr*)** — supporter, one who backs another. In the Qurʾān, the word of Moses: *"I shall never again be a supporter of the guilty"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/17).
- **ظُهْر (*ẓuhr*)** — noon; the clearest moment of the day, when the shadow is shortest.

The point is here: the back is both **what carries** and **what is leaned on**. English carries the same double sense: to *back* someone, to have *backing*, to lean one's back against something.

That is, when the back cracks two things go at once: the power to carry and the support. The choice of word intimates that being crushed under a load is not only a matter of strength.

## 94/4 — وَرَفَعْنَا لَكَ ذِكْرَكَ

*Wa rafaʿnā laka dhikrak*
"And We raised for you your remembrance."

### وَضَعْنَا ↔ رَفَعْنَا — an exact opposition

This is the clearest point of word order in the sūra, and it is liable to be missed.

*Waḍaʿa* (to put down, to set down) and *rafaʿa* (to lift, to raise) are in Arabic the classic opposites of one another. The two are used together in order to establish a contrast.

The second verse says *waḍaʿnā*, the fourth says *rafaʿnā*. The same form, the same mood, the same first person plural — and opposite verbs:

- **وَضَعْنَا عَنكَ** — We set down from you
- **وَرَفَعْنَا لَكَ** — We raised for you

Down and up. Taking and giving. And between them stands the third verse — the description of the burden — so that the opposition does not come immediately side by side, but it stands.

Two things that can be done for a person: **to take away what is on top of him** and **to lift him up.** The sūra names both.

### ذِكْر — remembrance

**Root: ذ-ك-ر.** It carries two fields of meaning, and the two are bound to one another:

- **To remember, to keep in mind.** *Tadhakkur*, *dhikrā* (a reminding), *tadhkira*.
- **To mention, to give utterance to.** *Dhikr*, *dhākir*.

The link between the two is direct: to mention a thing is the way of keeping it in memory.

The Qurʾān gives itself this name: *"It is We who sent down the reminder, and it is We who will guard it"* (al-Ḥijr 15/9). And the Prophet's task is described with this word: *"So remind; you are only one who reminds"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/21).

Here *dhikraka* — "your remembrance" — is in a third sense: **repute, standing, the fact that your name is spoken.**

### What does "We raised your remembrance" mean?

Several explanations are given in the classical commentaries:

**1. That his name is mentioned together with the name of God.** In the call to prayer, in the testimony of faith, in the *tashahhud*. This explanation is the most widespread in the commentaries and is also grounded on a report. In the report it is transmitted that God said *"When I am mentioned, you too are mentioned with Me"*; this account occurs frequently in the books of exegesis, but **its chain is disputed** and one must approach its being transmitted in the language of certainty with caution.

**2. The proclamation of the prophethood and his name's occurring in the scriptures.** *"…that Prophet whom they find written down with them in the Torah and the Gospel…"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/157); and at al-Ṣaff 61/6 the good tidings given by Jesus (ʿĪsā) are related.

**3. Standing and honour in general.**

These do not exclude one another. The text does not narrow it.

### The point of context: when it was said

Here is the real matter.

This sentence is said in the period when the addressee's name was **most heavily blackened**.

The Qurʾān itself records the epithets fastened on him in that period: **majnūn**, possessed (al-Ḥijr 15/6, al-Qalam 68/2, al-Takwīr 81/22), **shāʿir**, poet (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/5, al-Ṣāffāt 37/36), **sāḥir**, sorcerer, and **kāhin**, soothsayer (al-Ṭūr 52/29, al-Dhāriyāt 51/52). These are not random insults; they are the standard ways in that culture of voiding a man's speech — classifying the speaker instead of debating what is said.

It is in just this setting that "We raised your remembrance" is said.

**And the verb is in the past tense.** *Rafaʿnā* — "We raised". Not "We shall raise".

This is the same as the point about the past tense in Sūrat al-Kawthar (*innā aʿṭaynāka* — "We have given you", when no visible abundance was there). As was treated there: the sentence speaks not with the situation the addressee can observe but with what has been brought about.

The difference from al-Ḍuḥā also becomes visible here: **al-Ḍuḥā had used the future** (*la-sawfa yuʿṭīka* — "he will give"), **al-Sharḥ uses the past** (*rafaʿnā* — "We raised"). The two together hold the two ends of a promise: what will be and what has been.

## On verses 1-4

### Three operations

| Verse | Verb | Sentence type | Where | What |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | نَشْرَح — We opened | Question (*taqrīr*) | Inward | Breast — capacity |
| 2 | وَضَعْنَا — We set down | Statement | Down from on top | Burden — what is carried |
| 4 | رَفَعْنَا — We raised | Statement | Outward, upward | Remembrance — standing |

Three separate fronts: **the person's inside**, **what the person carries**, **the person's visible name.** Nothing further is needed in order to describe the state of a human being.

And the sequence itself carries a logic: unless the inside widens the load cannot be carried; unless the load comes down one cannot get to one's feet; the name of one who does not get to his feet is not raised. This reading of the sequence is my own inference; the text offers no such justification.

### A question, then statements

The first verse is a question, the second and fourth statements.

This is exactly what al-Ḍuḥā 6-8 does: there too it began with an *alam*, and the ones after it were joined only with *wa*. The force of the interrogative particle spreads to those that follow but is not repeated.

The two sūras share the same rhetorical move: **first get the addressee to acknowledge, then give the information.** Once the addressee has said "yes", no ground is left for objecting to what follows.

## 94/5-6 — فَإِنَّ مَعَ ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا / إِنَّ مَعَ ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا

*Fa-inna maʿa'l-ʿusri yusrā / Inna maʿa'l-ʿusri yusrā*
"Surely with hardship there is ease. Surely with hardship there is ease."

The heart of the sūra and one of the most repeated sentences of the Qurʾān. There are four matters that must be dwelt on one by one: the letter *fāʾ*, the particle *maʿa*, the difference of definiteness, and the repetition itself.

### فَ — from a particular case to a general law

The **فَ** at the head of the fifth verse establishes a link of cause and effect: since these things happened, **therefore** the following is true.

This single letter changes the status of the sentence.

The four verses before it concerned **one person**: your breast, your burden, your back, your remembrance. Across four verses the second person singular pronoun is repeated (*-ka*).

In the fifth verse the pronoun disappears. There is no addressee in the sentence. *"With hardship there is ease"* — whose hardship, whose ease, is not said.

That is, the sūra passes from a single experience to **a general rule**. And the letter *fāʾ* establishes the ground of that passage: the rule is drawn from the experience.

This explains why the verse can be read so widely by everyone. The sentence has been taken out of being a particular consolation — and this is not a generalisation made afterwards but the text's own move.

### The roots عسر and يسر

**عُسْر (*ʿusr*)** — from the root **ع-س-ر**: hardship, difficulty, a matter's coming hard.

**يُسْر (*yusr*)** — from the root **ي-س-ر**: ease, a matter's flowing, favourableness.

The two words are regularly set against one another in the Qurʾān:

- *"God desires ease for you, He does not desire hardship for you"* (al-Baqara 2/185). Within the verses on fasting, as the ground of the dispensations.
- *"If anyone is in straitened circumstances, then wait until there is ease"* (al-Baqara 2/280). Here *ʿusra* and *maysara* stand side by side — in the relation of debt.
- *"Whoever guards against God, God will make for him an ease in his affair"* (al-Ṭalāq 65/4).

And the root *yusr* occurs in the Qurʾān as the name of a path as well: *"We shall make it easy for you to follow the easiest way"* (al-Aʿlā 87/8); *"We shall ease him towards the easiest"* / *"We shall ease him towards the hardest"* (al-Layl 92/7 and 92/10). In Sūrat al-Layl the two words become the names of two separate paths.

### (a) "Maʿa" — with, not after

The most treated place in the verse, and rightly so.

**مَعَ (*maʿa*)** is an adverb reporting accompaniment. "Together with", "beside", "along with".

The particle that reports succession in time is **بَعْدَ (*baʿda*)**: "after".

**And there is decisive evidence that this is a choice: the Qurʾān says "baʿda" in another place.**

*"God will bring about ease **after** hardship"* (al-Ṭalāq 65/7).

The same two words (*ʿusr* and *yusr*), with a different particle. That is, the Qurʾān knows how to say "after" and says it when it is required. In al-Sharḥ it does not.

This is the soundest datum showing that the choice of particle is deliberate — because the comparison is made from within the Qurʾān itself, not by an assumption brought from outside.

**Then what does "with" mean?** Several explanations are given in the classical commentaries:

**1. Accompaniment in respect of time.** The ease exists at the same moment as the hardship; but it is not yet visible. It stands within the hardship, unopened.

**2. Nearness.** *Maʿa* here reports the absence of distance: the ease is immediately adjacent to the hardship; no gap comes between them.

**3. The certainty of the promise.** "After" leaves an indeterminate interval — how long after? "With" removes that interval.

All three are reasonable and they do not exclude one another. The plainest thing the text says with certainty is this: **there is no separation between hardship and ease.**

**And the practical consequence of this changes the form of the consolation.**

To say "it will come afterwards" leaves the present moment empty. The person's work is to wait; the present moment consists in being what precedes what is to come.

To say "it is together with" says that there is something in the present moment itself. The person's work is to look.

The difference emerges in what a person does at the moment of hardship. The one who waits stands still; the one who knows that it is together with him looks.

**An inference — as my own reading.** It is also possible that the hardship produces the ease: a matter's being hard requires the forming of the capacity able to do it; and capacity develops within the hardship. I do not present this reading as a transmitted report; I record it only as a possibility opened by the particle *maʿa*.

### (b) A definite hardship, an indefinite ease

The word order in the verse is this:

- **ٱلْعُسْر** — definite (with the article *al-*). The same in both verses.
- **يُسْرًا** — indefinite (with tanwīn, *nakira*). The same in both verses.

This asymmetry is real and observable.

**The observation widely treated in the classical commentaries** is this: in Arabic, when a **definite** noun is repeated the second is generally the same as the first; when an **indefinite** noun is repeated the second is generally other than the first.

By this rule: the two *al-ʿusr*s are the same hardship, while the two *yusran*s are two separate eases. The conclusion is the sentence so often repeated in the tradition: **"one hardship cannot overcome two eases."**

One must be honest about the standing of this observation. I am obliged to enter four qualifications:

**First — attribution.** This inference is widely treated in the classical commentaries and is also transmitted as a saying. But the chain of the accounts that come attributed to the Prophet is disputed; it is mostly recorded as *mursal* or as the word of a Companion. **I do not attribute it with certainty either to an exegete or to the Prophet.** What can be said is this: the observation is widespread in the exegetical tradition.

**Second — the rule itself is not absolute.** Sameness in the repetition of the definite and difference in the repetition of the indefinite is **a general tendency in Arabic, not a binding rule.** The lexicographers give it with the qualification of "in the majority of cases", and counter-examples are found. To draw a numerical conclusion from a tendency is to load onto the tendency more than it can carry.

**Third — there is a repetition here.** The sixth verse is a **word-for-word repetition** of the fifth (apart from the difference of the *fāʾ* discussed below). If this is a *taʾkīd* (a repetition for the sake of emphasis), the operation of counting is invalid from the start: to say the same sentence twice is not to promise two separate things. One who says "I'm coming, I'm coming" has not promised to come twice.

**Fourth — the kind of the article "al-" is uncertain.** The article in *al-ʿusr* may be one of previous reference (*ʿahd*, pointing to a known, particular hardship); but it may also be one of genus (*jins*, the whole kind of hardship). If it is taken as generic the sense becomes: "with every hardship there is an ease" — which is also what the common English renderings give. On this reading the operation of counting does not come into play at all.

**Conclusion.** The observation is a fine one, it is widespread in the tradition, and it is consoling. But from the side of language it is not a certain inference, and the ruling of the verse does not depend on it.

What the verse says with certainty is plainer and — in my view — stronger: **hardship and ease are inseparable.** There is no need to make a count; the particle itself already says quite enough.

One further note: that indefiniteness (tanwīn) can report **magnification** in Arabic was treated in the sections on al-Ikhlāṣ and al-ʿAṣr. The indefinite form of *yusran* can be read in this light as well: an ease whose extent is not stated, which is not described. This reading is independent of the counting reading and does not conflict with it.

### (c) The function of the repetition

Why twice?

**1. *Taʾkīd*.** In Arabic the repetition of the same sentence is one of the strongest forms of emphasis. And the **place** of the repetition is meaningful: a sentence is repeated at the point where it is hardest to believe.

It is assumed that a person in the midst of hardship cannot hold on to what he has heard once. This is a finding about the state of the addressee — and it is realistic. A known feature of the state of distress is that consoling knowledge does not hold; the person hears it, accepts it, and a few minutes later returns to the same place.

**2. Rhythm.** That the two verses are the same turns the repetition itself into a breath. The text does what it says by its form as well: within the sentence neither *ʿusr* nor *yusr* is left alone.

**3. And the one concrete difference: the *fāʾ*.**

This is worth dwelling on.

- **Verse 5:** *فَإِنَّ مَعَ ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا* — **fa**-inna.
- **Verse 6:** *إِنَّ مَعَ ٱلْعُسْرِ يُسْرًا* — inna.

The fifth verse is **tied** by the *fāʾ* to the preceding four verses. In the sixth verse there is no *fāʾ* — the sentence stands **independent**.

That is, the two are not word for word the same. And the difference lies exactly here: **the first is a conclusion, the second is a ruling.**

The first sentence is tied to the addressee's story: these things were done for you, therefore with hardship there is ease. The second sentence is tied to nothing: with hardship there is ease.

This reading rests on a firmer foundation than the count of "two eases" — because it is built on a visible difference in the text, not on a grammatical tendency. The second sentence's being unattached takes it out of the context and makes it a general law.

I present this as my own reading; the existence of the difference of the *fāʾ* is beyond dispute, the conclusion drawn from it is interpretation.

## 94/7 — فَإِذَا فَرَغْتَ فَٱنصَبْ

*Fa-idhā faraghta fa'nṣab*
"When you are emptied, stand up / labour."

### إِذَا — not "if" but "when"

In Arabic there are two particles that report a condition, and the difference between them matters:

- **إِنْ (*in*)** — reports possibility. "If it happens." Its occurrence is uncertain.
- **إِذَا (*idhā*)** — reports certainty. "When it happens." Its occurrence is assumed.

The verse uses *idhā*. That is, not "if you finish" but **"when you finish".**

Behind this there is an assumption, and the assumption is correct: **every task ends.** The possibility of its not ending is not taken into account.

### The root فرغ

**Root: ف-ر-غ.** Its original sense is **to become empty**: a vessel's emptying, a place's being left empty, the hand's being freed of work.

Derivatives: *farāgh* (emptiness, free time), *fārigh* (empty).

Two of its usages in the Qurʾān illuminate the root:

- *"The heart of the mother of Moses became empty"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/10). Her state after setting her child upon the water. Here *fārighan* means emptied within.
- *"O you two weighty ones! We shall attend to you"* (al-Raḥmān 55/31). The *sa-nafrughu* here comes from the verb "to become empty", but its sense is "we shall be free of other business and turn wholly to this".

The second usage shows the word's second face: to become empty is at the same time **to become able to turn wholly to something**. Emptiness is not a state in itself; it is the precondition of the next turning.

### The root نصب — two senses

**Root: ن-ص-ب.** The word has two main senses in Arabic, and in the classical commentaries both are applied to this verse.

**1. To grow weary, to undergo toil.** *Naṣab* = weariness.

- *"We have indeed grown weary on this journey of ours"* (al-Kahf 18/62). The word of Moses and his young companion.
- *"No weariness touches us there, nor any exhaustion"* (Fāṭir 35/35). In the description of the Garden.
- The word of Job (Ayyūb): *"Satan has touched me with weariness and torment"* (Ṣād 38/41).

**2. To set up, to stand upright, to raise on its feet.** *Naṣb* = setting up.

- *"Do they not look at the mountains, how they were set up?"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/19).
- **نُصُب (*nuṣub*)** — a stone set up; the stones at which sacrifice was made in idolatrous worship (al-Māʾida 5/3, 5/90).
- The **accusative case** in grammar is from this root too: the letter's standing upright.

**Both senses work in the verse and they join together.** The command becomes: when you are emptied, **stand up** and give yourself to **a new weariness.**

In the classical commentaries no preference is imposed; the two are given together. And at the point where they join the sense of the verse is completed: to stand upright is to accept weariness.

### The opposition

*فَرَغْتَ* (you were emptied) ↔ *فَٱنصَبْ* (stand up / labour).

The two verbs of the sentence are opposites of one another — just like *waḍaʿnā / rafaʿnā* in verses 2 and 4. The sūra uses this technique twice: in one case spreading it across two verses, here compressing it within a single sentence.

### From what and to what?

The verse leaves both verbs **absolute**. What one is emptied of, and what one is to weary oneself with, is not said.

The explanations enumerated in the classical commentaries:

| Explanation | From what | To what |
|---|---|---|
| When free of the prayer, to supplication | Worship | Worship |
| When free of the obligatory, to the voluntary | Worship | Worship |
| When free of conveying the message, to worship | Duty | Worship |
| When free of worldly business, to the business of the hereafter | Livelihood | Worship |
| When free of fighting, to worship | Duty | Worship |

All of these occur in the classical sources. **But the text says none of them** — and that it does not say them is meaningful.

When both verbs are left without an object, the principle becomes applicable to every task. The verse does not set a rule of transition between two particular occupations; it sets **a rule about emptiness**.

### The moment at which the command is given

The most striking side of this verse is **at which moment** the command is placed.

It does not say "when you are weary, rest." It says **"when you are emptied, labour."**

The difference matters and is often missed:

- The opposite of **weariness** is rest.
- The opposite of **emptiness** is not rest but a new turning.

The verse does not forbid rest. Rest is the transition between two tasks and is itself precisely the "emptying". What is forbidden is that emptiness **becoming permanent**.

A person rests when he is weary; when he is emptied he either turns to something new or falls apart. The verse closes the second possibility.

### Its place within the sūra: its link with 5-6

This is the finest link in the sūra, and without it being made the seventh verse hangs in the air.

The fifth and sixth verses had said "with hardship there is ease". When this sentence is read on its own it can produce a passive conclusion: **waiting.** The hardship will pass, the ease will come, and then one will be at rest.

The seventh verse closes that door. What is to be done when the ease comes? **One is to enter a new hardship.**

That is, the verse builds ease not as a **point of arrival** but as a **transition**. The relation of hardship and ease is not a goal but a rhythm.

When this link is made, verses 5-6 are read differently too: the sentence does not say "hold on, it will pass"; it says "this is how the thing works".

## 94/8 — وَإِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ فَٱرْغَب

*Wa ilā Rabbika fa'rghab*
"And to your Lord alone turn."

### The root رغب

**Root: ر-غ-ب.** The concrete sense recorded by the lexicographers is **width**: *raghīb*, that which is wide; *raghuba'l-wādī*, the valley widened.

From here the figurative sense emerges: **to open oneself to something, to widen towards it, to turn to it with appetite.**

**And the word has an interesting property: when the particle changes, the sense turns around.**

- **رَغِبَ فِي / إِلَى** — to want, to desire, to turn towards it.
- **رَغِبَ عَنْ** — to turn away, not to want, to withdraw from it.

The Qurʾān contains both usages:

- *"Who turns away from the religion of Abraham (Ibrāhīm) but one who makes a fool of himself?"* (al-Baqara 2/130). Here *yarghabu **ʿan***.
- The word of Āzar to Abraham: *"Are you turning away from my gods, O Abraham?"* (Maryam 19/46). Again *ʿan*.
- And in the positive direction: *"We are of those who turn to God"* (al-Tawba 9/59). Here *ilā'llāhi rāghibūn* — **exactly the same structure as al-Sharḥ 8**: *ilā* + desire.
- *"They used to call upon Us in hope and in fear"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/90). *Raghaban wa rahaban* — the pair of hope and fear.

That is, the same verb reports turning towards or turning away according to its particle. A person's opening himself to something and closing himself off from it are, in the language, two directions of a single word.

### Bringing forward — *ḥaṣr*

*Fa'rghab ilā Rabbika* could have been said and the sense would have been complete. It was not said.

The prepositional phrase **إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ** has been drawn **before** the verb. In Arabic an element brought forward from its normal position gains emphasis and **ḥaṣr** (restriction) — the same technique was treated in the last verse of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ.

The sense becomes: *to your Lord alone turn.* It does not command turning in general; it makes the address of the turning single.

### The balance of the two commands

The seventh and eighth verses must be read together, because each balances the other.

- **فَٱنصَبْ** — horizontal. From task to task. When one ends, to the next.
- **فَٱرْغَب** — vertical. Upward. Turning.

What does the seventh verse give when read on its own? A command of unceasing work. And where that leads is a known thing: the work itself becoming the end, exhaustion, and finally falling apart.

The eighth verse closes that door. Unceasing occupation is commanded, but the place to which the occupation is directed is separately stated. Work is not left as a cycle that justifies itself.

**And the order between the two is meaningful as well:** first the work, then the turning. The turning does not take the place of the work; it determines the direction of the work.

### The circle of the sūra

An observation — as my own reading:

The sūra began with **شرح**: opening, widening.
The sūra ends with **رغب**: a word that likewise has width at its root.

The first is the work God does for the servant (We opened your breast). The second is the work the servant does towards God (turn, open yourself).

That is, the sūra begins with an opening and closes with an opening; and the two openings are in opposite directions. In the first the addressee is the object, in the second the subject.

This is a reading resting on the overlap of the concrete senses of the two roots. The sense of "width" for the root ر-غ-ب is recorded in the dictionaries, but I claim no kinship between the two roots — the roots are separate, the link established is conceptual.

## The link between the two sūras

That al-Ḍuḥā and al-Sharḥ require to be read together was said at the outset. Now it can be established with concrete data.

### The proof at the level of language: the final verses

This is the soundest datum, because it is not open to interpretation.

**al-Ḍuḥā 93/11:** وَأَمَّا بِنِعْمَةِ رَبِّكَ فَحَدِّثْ
**al-Sharḥ 94/8:** وَإِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ فَٱرْغَب

The skeleton is one and the same:

| Element | al-Ḍuḥā 11 | al-Sharḥ 8 |
|---|---|---|
| Conjunction | وَ | وَ |
| Prepositional phrase brought forward | بِنِعْمَةِ رَبِّكَ | إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ |
| The noun within it | رَبِّكَ | رَبِّكَ |
| *Fāʾ* | فَ | فَ |
| Imperative verb | حَدِّثْ | ٱرْغَب |

Both sūras close with the same move of word order, by drawing forward a prepositional phrase containing the same word (*rabbika*), joined by the same *fāʾ* to a command.

This is not a resemblance; it is the same pattern used twice.

### A structural comparison

| | **al-Ḍuḥā (93)** | **al-Sharḥ (94)** |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Two oaths — from the outer world | A question — from the addressee himself |
| Method of consolation | **A question:** "did He not find you…?" | **A statement:** "We opened, We set down, We raised" |
| How God is mentioned | Third person: *your Lord* (3, 5, 11) | First person plural: *We* (1, 2, 4) — in the last verse *your Lord* |
| Time | Past evidence + a **future** promise (*la-sawfa*) | Past evidence + a **past** finding (*rafaʿnā*) |
| Generalisation | None — a single addressee from beginning to end | Present — in verses 5-6 the addressee disappears |
| What is enumerated | Three **deprivations** removed | Three **weights** lifted |
| Number of commands | Three (two prohibitions, one command) | Two (both commands) |
| Direction of the commands | Outward — to people (the orphan, the beggar) | Inward and upward — to work and to the Lord |
| Verse-endings | 8 open (*-ā*) + 3 closed | 4 + 2 + 2 |

### Two types of consolation

The most meaningful row in the table is the second.

**Al-Ḍuḥā asks.** It forces the addressee into participation: he is the one who will give the answer out of his own life.
**Al-Sharḥ tells.** It gives the information directly.

When the two come one after the other an order emerges: **first you remember, then let me tell you.**

And this order is the right order in respect of how consolation works. Information given from outside does not hold unless the person has established a ground with his own memory. Al-Ḍuḥā establishes the ground, al-Sharḥ builds upon it.

### Giving and taking

Another complementarity:

**Al-Ḍuḥā enumerates the lacks:** an orphan, not knowing the way, in need. And their counterparts: He sheltered, He guided, He freed from need. That is, **what you did not have was given.**

**Al-Sharḥ enumerates the weights:** a narrow breast, a burden that made the back crack. And their counterparts: We opened, We set down. That is, **what you had was taken away.**

Two kindnesses that can be done for a person: to give him what he lacks and to take away what is too much. The two sūras have divided them between them.

### The movement of distance

A reading — I record it as my own inference.

In al-Ḍuḥā God is spoken of throughout in the third person: *Rabbuka*. Three times.

Al-Sharḥ moves to the first person plural: *nashraḥ*, *waḍaʿnā*, *rafaʿnā*. The distance closes.

Then in the last verse it returns to *Rabbika*.

That is, across the two sūras there is a movement: far — near — far. And the last return is not a withdrawal; it is **the naming of the address to which one is to turn.** The subject who says "We" becomes "your Lord" again just as the addressee is about to turn to Him.

I do not present this as a deliberate move of the text; I record it as an observable order.

### The shared method: evidence from the past

This is the most basic thing the two sūras have in common.

Both are texts of consolation. Neither builds its consolation upon feeling. Both make the same move: **using what has come about in the past as evidence for the future.**

Al-Ḍuḥā enumerates three favours and supports from them the conclusion "it will be given to you".
Al-Sharḥ enumerates three operations and draws from them the rule "with hardship there is ease".

In both the flow is the same: **it happened → it happens.**

And both end with a command. That is, the consolation is not left in a relief; it is tied to a work.

## What this says to the present

**First: the language of constriction.**

The sūra recounts inner distress by a bodily description: the narrowing of the breast. Al-Anʿām 6/125 extends the same description: being pressed like one climbing into the sky. And al-Sharḥ 3 does the same work with another bodily description: the cracking of the back.

This is a common property of language — inner states are recounted through the body in nearly every language. English too has "to feel constricted", "a tightness in the chest", "to be crushed under a burden", "it broke his back", and these have less to do with metaphor than with the thing described actually being felt that way.

What is known today about the physiology of anxiety does not make these descriptions idle: the shallowing of the breath and the sensation of pressure in the chest during distress are among the clinically defined symptoms. I write this not as a "miracle" but as **the record of an ancient observation**. The verse does not give a piece of physiological information; it uses the way a person describes his own state.

**Second: the difference between "with" and "after" is a practical difference.**

It was treated above at the level of the particle; the side of it that faces the present is this:

To know that a hardship "will pass" teaches a person **to wait**. Time becomes an interval to be got through; the present moment loses its value, because what is valuable is in the future.

To know that there is "something within" a hardship teaches a person **to look**. The present moment is not what precedes what is to come; it is a state that harbours something within it.

The difference is visible in what a person does during the hardship. And this is not a matter of morale; it is a matter of where the attention is directed.

**Third: the immeasurability of the burden.**

The third verse does not weigh the burden; it records the sound on the back of the one carrying it.

The side of this facing the present is direct: **the weight of a burden cannot be measured from outside.** The same work, the same loss, the same responsibility gives out two different sounds in two people. The sentence "is that any reason to react so much?" shows that the measure is being sought in the wrong place.

The measure the verse chooses is closed to comparison. And its being closed prevents both the belittling of another's burden and the treating of one's own burden as void by comparing it with someone else's.

**Fourth: the seventh verse is not a prescription for burnout.**

The verse *"when you are emptied, labour"*, taken on its own, resembles the kind of sentence modern work culture likes best: work without stopping, leave no gap, always the next target.

Three things break this resemblance:

**First**, the verse forbids **emptiness**, not **rest**. Rest is the transition between one task and another; the verse does not remove that transition, it prevents that transition from becoming permanent.

**Second**, the second sense of the root *naṣb* — to stand upright — takes the command out of being a command to exhaustion. To stand upright is not to collapse.

**Third and most important**, there is the eighth verse. The command of unceasing occupation has been limited by the command of turning. Work itself is not made into the end.

There is a finding often voiced in modern discussions of burnout: what wears a person out is most often not the quantity of the work but the blurring of what the work serves. The two commands of the sūra make exactly this distinction — one regulates the quantity, the other the direction.

**Fifth: the emptiness after things end.**

The seventh verse has an observable counterpart: the collapse experienced at the end of great undertakings.

The completion of a long project, the sitting of an examination, the handing over of a responsibility, retirement — the emptiness that follows these is a common and often unexpected difficulty. Because a person awaits the ending as a relief; and when it ends, instead of being relieved, he falls apart.

The assumption of the verse appears to be this: **a person does not stand well when he is not turned towards something.** Emptiness is not a neutral state. This is why the command has been placed at the moment when the emptiness forms — not afterwards.

**Sixth: in whose hands standing lies.**

The fourth verse says "We raised your remembrance" in the period when the addressee's name was most heavily blackened. And it uses the past tense.

The side of this facing the present has to do with where standing is established. How a person's name is spoken is now determined largely by mechanisms outside his control — and these mechanisms are fast, wide and largely irreversible.

What the verse does is to tie standing to another authority. This does not make the blackening unimportant; **it changes the final authority over standing.** The same move was made in Sūrat al-Kawthar: the one who decides who is "cut off" is not the person who issues that verdict.

**Seventh: *sharḥ*, for a text and for a person alike.**

A closing observation.

The same word names two works at once: the opening of a text and the opening of a breast. In both the work done is the same — spreading out and widening something that stands folded and compressed.

And a link can be made between the two: a text that stands closed says nothing until it is opened. So too a person who stands closed. Understanding requires widening.

I record this not as a ruling but as a possibility offered by the word itself.

- **The relation between the reports of "the splitting of the breast" and 94/1:** the existence of the reports was accepted, but it was stated that there is no word in the wording of the verse indicating a physical splitting and that the expression *sharḥ al-ṣadr* is inward in all the Qurʾān's other usages. The report was not rejected; nor was it said that the verse recounts it.
- **The views on what the word *wizr* at 94/2 is were given in a table; none was presented as certainly correct.** Three observations that can be drawn from the text itself were stated separately, and it was recorded that these are inferences.
- The common root between *wazīr* at Ṭāhā 20/29 and *wizr* at al-Sharḥ 94/2 was recorded as an observation; no ruling was drawn from it and it was explicitly stated that the two cases are not the same.
- The character of the word أنقض as sound-imitation (onomatopoeia) was presented as a character recorded by some of the lexicographers; not as a definite ruling.
- None of the explanations given for "We raised your remembrance" at 94/4 was counted as certainly correct. It was stated that the chain of the report *"When I am mentioned, you too are mentioned with Me"* is disputed, and it was not transmitted in the language of certainty.
