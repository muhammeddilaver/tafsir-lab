# 67. Sūrat al-Mulk

Thirty verses. Meccan. It takes its name from the word **ٱلْمُلْك** in the first verse; on account of its first word it is also called **Tabāraka**.

The sūra opens with a sentence of exaltation and closes with a cup of water. What passes in between is this: a claim is made about who holds the dominion, then proofs are set out for that claim — the sky, death, the earth, the birds, provision — and every proof is tied to a question. Through the sūra twelve questions are put to the one addressed, and these questions form its backbone.

And the questions settle into a single pattern: **"And if He had not — then who?"** Who holds the birds up? Who protects you? Who gives the provision? Who brings the water?

**Let me say at the outset what the sūra really does, as my own reading: al-Mulk takes the claim of ownership out of being an abstract theological debate and turns it into an inventory of dependence.** The sentence "God is the owner of everything" can remain an article of belief. The sūra does not let it remain one; it runs it through the air you breathe, the ground you tread, the water you drink. That is why the last verse is a cup of water.

## The skeleton of the sūra

| Section | Verse | Its function |
|---|---|---|
| The claim | 1-2 | In whose hand the dominion is; why death and life were created |
| The first looking | 3-4 | Look at the sky, search for a flaw — and come back without finding one |
| The lamps | 5 | The adornment of the nearest sky |
| The scene of Hell | 6-11 | A boiling fire, the question at the gate, the confession within |
| The other side, and knowledge | 12-14 | Those who fear unseen; hidden speech; "shall the one who created not know?" |
| The earth | 15 | A tamed mount |
| Two questions about safety | 16-18 | What if the earth caves in, what if stones rain down |
| The birds | 19 | The thing held up in the air |
| Two "who is there?" questions | 20-21 | An army and provision |
| Two walks | 22 | On the face / upright |
| The inventory of instruments | 23-24 | Hearing, eyes, hearts; scattering and gathering |
| "When?" | 25-27 | The date of the promise is asked, no answer is given |
| Two "say" commands | 28-29 | What changes if I die; we have relied on Him |
| The close | 30 | If your water were to sink away |

## An observation about sound

All of the first twenty-one verses of the sūra end with the same sound: a long vowel followed by **rāʾ** — *qadīr, ghafūr, fuṭūr, ḥasīr, saʿīr, maṣīr, tafūr, nadhīr, kabīr, saʿīr, saʿīr, kabīr, ṣudūr, khabīr, nushūr, tamūr, nadhīr, nakīr, baṣīr, ghurūr, nufūr.* A rhyme unbroken across twenty-one verses.

And it breaks in exactly the twenty-second verse: **mustaqīm.**

I record this as an observation, not as a claim of miracle. But where the observation stands is striking: the verse in which the sound changes is the verse of the man "walking upright on a straight path". The ear, which has gone along the same road for twenty-one verses, enters another road there.

## Meccan

On the sūra's being Meccan there is something close to unanimity, and the content supports it: proof drawn from nature, a construction centred on the hereafter, the absence of the language of ruling and sanction, and the one addressed standing in the position of saying "what is it you are threatening us with?"

**I relate no particular report of an occasion of revelation.** I have no information I am sure of that a definite incident is attached to this sūra.

In the tradition, reports about the recitation of this sūra — particularly at night and in the context of protection against the punishment of the grave — are very widespread, and the sūra's being called by names such as *al-Wāqiya* (the one that protects) and *al-Munjiya* (the one that delivers) comes from this. **Because I cannot give the attribution of these reports to a source with certainty, I give no name of a hadith collection and no chain of transmission;** I content myself with recording that the practice and the naming exist.

The basmala was treated in earlier sections; the verses are counted without the basmala.

## 67/1 — تَبَٰرَكَ ٱلَّذِى بِيَدِهِ ٱلْمُلْكُ وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

*Tabāraka'lladhī bi-yadihi'l-mulku wa huwa ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr*
"Blessed is He in whose hand is the dominion; and He is powerful over everything."

### تَبَارَكَ — the place where the camel kneels

**Root: ب-ر-ك.** And the concrete sense of this root is the place that carries all the abstract uses of the word.

*Baraka'l-baʿīr* — the camel knelt. The camel's setting its breast to the ground and settling, not hurrying to rise. The derivatives of the root confirm this picture:

- **بَرْك (*birk*)** — the camel's breast; the part that touches the ground when it kneels.
- **مَبْرَك (*mabrak*)** — the place where the camel kneels, a halting-place.
- **بِرْكَة (*birka*)** — a pool. That is, **water that does not flow, that stands in one place.** In Turkish, the resemblance to the words *birikinti* ("a pool of standing water") and *birikmek* ("to accumulate") is a coincidence, but it conveys the sense well: a *birka* is water that has gone and settled.
- **بَرَكَة (*baraka*)** — the good that settles in a thing and stays and increases.

**Now the real definition of "blessing" comes out, and it differs from the way the English word is used:** blessing does not mean "abundance". Blessing is **permanence and increase**. A thing that is much may not be blessed — it runs out quickly. A thing that is little may be blessed — it stays and multiplies.

This distinction stands in the root of the word: the camel, when it kneels, stops; the pool, because it does not flow, remains. The picture of blessing is **a good that settles somewhere and does not get up from there**.

In the places where the Qurʾān uses this word, this sense can be seen:

- *"And He has made me **blessed** wherever I may be"* (Maryam 19/31) — the words of Jesus (ʿĪsā) in the cradle. Blessing here is not a quantity but a continuous quality attached to a person.
- *"And We sent down **blessed** water from the sky"* (Qāf 50/9) — the blessing of rain is its staying where it falls and producing.
- *"We sent it down on a **blessed** night"* (al-Dukhān 44/3).
- *"From a **blessed** tree, an olive"* (al-Nūr 24/35).

### The pattern تَبَارَكَ — a verb the Qurʾān reserves for God

The word is in **Form VI** (*tabāraka*). In Arabic this form generally conveys reciprocity (*taqātala* — they killed one another) or gradual becoming (*tanāwama* — he acted as though falling asleep). But here it carries neither function; the lexicographers say that this form here conveys **abundance and greatness**: *tabāraka* — His blessing became exceedingly great and high, He is exalted.

**And what is really worth recording is this: in the Qurʾān this verb is used only of God.**

*Tabāraka* is not said of any human being, angel, prophet or object. The conjugation of the word is defective as well: its imperfect, its verbal noun and its active participle are not used. It comes only in this one form, with one person, with one subject.

Of the places where it occurs, those I am sure of:

- *"Behold, His is the creating and the commanding. Blessed is God, the Lord of all the worlds"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/54)
- *"Blessed is God, the best of creators"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/14)
- *"Blessed is He who sent down the Furqān upon His servant"* (al-Furqān 25/1)
- *"Blessed is He to whom belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth"* (al-Zukhruf 43/85)
- *"Blessed is the name of your Lord, possessor of majesty and honour"* (al-Raḥmān 55/78)

Looking at this list an order appears: *tabāraka* comes when an ownership or an act is to be mentioned immediately afterwards. That is, the word is not an exclamation of praise but a **heading**: one must look at what comes after it.

In Sūrat al-Mulk what comes after it is: **the dominion.**

### بِيَدِهِ — "in His hand"

The word order of the sentence calls for attention. The normal order would be *"al-mulku bi-yadihi"* — "the dominion is in His hand". The verse has brought **bi-yadihi** forward.

**In Arabic the element brought forward conveys restriction.** This rule was treated in the discussion of the basmala: when *"in the name of God"* is brought forward, the sense "in His name only" arises. The same rule works here: the dominion is in His hand **only**. Not in another hand, and not partly in another hand either.

**And the matter of the "hand".** Expressions ascribed to God in the Qurʾān such as *yad* (hand), *wajh* (face), *ʿayn* (eye) are the most debated subject in the history of *kalām*. The method held to in this commentary is the following, and it will be applied once more later in the sūra (68/42, *sāq*):

| Position | What it does | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Tafwīḍ** (consignment) | Accepts the wording as it stands and consigns its modality to God: "He has a hand, we do not know how" | The position ascribed to the majority of the early generations; the principle is not to get ahead of the revealed text |
| **Taʾwīl** (interpretation) | Understands the wording according to its figurative use in Arabic: *yad* here means **power and disposal** | If Arabic says "in so-and-so's hand" and means ownership or authority, then the verse too is using this language |

**I take no side between the two positions.** Both have a ground and both agree on the principle that God does not resemble created things — where they differ is in how the wording is to be handled.

This much should be recorded as a linguistic datum: in Arabic the idiom *"bi-yadi fulān"* is used in everyday speech for **authority and disposal**. It has exactly the same structure as the English "the matter is in his hands", and in English nobody understands this as an organ. This is the linguistic ground of the *taʾwīl* side; the *tafwīḍ* side says that this ground is not enough to settle the revealed wording.

### ٱلْمُلْك — dominion

**Root: م-ل-ك.** To own, to hold in hand, to have disposal over. Beneath the root lies an idea of **holding tightly**; the lexicographers record the closeness between *malakahu* (he owned it) and *malakahu* (he held it fast).

Its derivatives show the field of meaning:

- **مِلْك (*milk*)** — ownership, having something as one's property.
- **مُلْك (*mulk*)** — sovereignty, rule; not only ownership but **the authority to dispose.**
- **مَلِك (*malik*)** — king.
- **مَالِك (*mālik*)** — owner.
- **مَلَكُوت (*malakūt*)** — the highest, unseen layer of the dominion.

In the discussion of al-Fātiḥa the readings *mālik / malik* and the difference between them were treated: *mālik* conveys ownership, *malik* sovereignty; one brings the relation of property to the fore, the other the relation of authority. The word **mulk** used here is the common subject of those two words — the thing itself that is owned and disposed of.

**And the point to note: the verse does not say *of what* the dominion is.** It does not say "the dominion of the heavens and the earth"; it says only **"al-mulk"**. At al-Zukhruf 43/85 the construct was made; here it has not been.

This is the same as the rule recorded for *al-ghāshiya* in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya: **a word whose object is not stated becomes unlimited.** Had it said "the dominion of such-and-such", a place outside that dominion would have remained. Since it is not said, none remains.

### وَهُوَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ قَدِيرٌ — power and measure

**Root: ق-د-ر.** And the proper sense of this root is not "power" but **measure**.

*Qadara* — he measured, he determined its quantity. *Qadar* — what has been measured. *Miqdār* — quantity. *Taqdīr* — measuring out.

In the discussion of al-Fajr (89/16) this root was dwelt on and it was debated whether the expression *"fa-qadara ʿalayhi rizqahu"* means "to straiten" or "to measure". The finding there is useful here: at the centre of the root stands **measuring**, not straitening; straitening is a consequence of the measure.

**From this the exact equivalent of the word *qadīr* emerges:** not the possessor of brute force, but **the one who can do a thing to its measure.** Power, in this Arabic root, is force joined with quantity. To be able is not enough; one must do it knowing how much is to be done.

And this will take on meaning when joined with the third verse: *"You will see no disharmony in the creation of the Merciful."* The absence of disharmony is the measure's having been hit exactly. That is, the last word of the first verse is the ground of the third verse's claim. **I record this as my own reading.**

### What this says to the present

The sentence "the dominion is His" can easily turn into a ceremonial sentence — it is said, and one passes on. The whole of the sūra is built to prevent this: after the sentence, an **inventory** is drawn up across thirty verses.

And the logic of the inventory is simple: a claim of ownership is tested by the possibility of losing what is owned. Can the person who says he owns a thing go on holding it? Can he hold the water, the air, the heartbeat, the fixity of the ground?

The sūra does not once say "you are not the owner". It only asks questions. And the answer to the questions appears as soon as they are asked.

## 67/2 — ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ ٱلْمَوْتَ وَٱلْحَيَوٰةَ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ أَيُّكُمْ أَحْسَنُ عَمَلًا ۚ وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْغَفُورُ

*Alladhī khalaqa'l-mawta wa'l-ḥayāta li-yabluwakum ayyukum aḥsanu ʿamalā, wa huwa'l-ʿazīzu'l-ghafūr*
"He who created death and life, to test which of you is best in deed. He is the mighty, the much-forgiving."

The centre of gravity of the sūra is here. And in two separate places it steps outside the usual: in **death's being mentioned first**, and in the measure's being not "the most" but "the best".

### خَلَقَ ٱلْمَوْتَ — death is a created thing

If the sentence is passed over without dwelling on it, this is not noticed: **death is said to have been "created".**

The most widespread thought about death is that it is a **nothingness**. There is life, and death is the ending of life; there is light, and darkness is the absence of light. On this understanding death is not a thing — it is the coming to an end of a thing.

The verse does not accept this understanding. **What is the object of the act of creating is a thing that exists.** Nothingness is not created.

This has two consequences:

**One. Death is an event, not a void.** The Qurʾān sets it up this way elsewhere too: death is "tasted" (*"Every soul shall taste death"* — Āl ʿImrān 3/185). Tasting is contact with something; nothingness is not tasted.

**Two. Death is not a loss but part of the plan.** To say "it was created" is to say "it was there in the design". Death is not the breaking down of the machine of life; it is the way the machine works.

The practical counterpart of this is weighty and works directly in modern life: **a mind that counts death a malfunction lives its life expecting a malfunction.** A mind that counts death part of the design sees the same event not as an end but as a limit. The difference between the two changes the direction of fear.

### Why is death mentioned first?

The expected order would be "life and death" — because that is the order of time: first you live, then you die.

The verse does the reverse.

In classical exegesis several explanations are given for this; I record them all and then add my own reading:

| Explanation | Its ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **The order of existence** | Before life there is lifelessness; the human being was first in the state of the dead, then was brought to life | The Qurʾān sets up this order openly elsewhere: *"You were dead and He gave you life"* (treated in connection with al-Baqara 2/28). A strong explanation |
| **The order of effect** | Mentioning death first shakes the one addressed; the warning comes first | Reasonable as rhetoric, but the subject of the verse is not warning, it is testing |
| **The order of the subject** | The subject of the verse is **the test**; the moment the test ends is death. If the ending is stated first, the interval between takes on meaning | The best suited to the context |

**I add my own reading:** by bringing death forward the verse makes life **bounded**. If in a sentence a limit is set first and then the field is described, the field is read as limited from the outset. Had it said "life and death", death would have been an appendix coming at the end of life. When it says "death and life", life settles inside death.

And it agrees with the whole of the sūra: the first verse had said in whose hand the dominion is. The real owner of a dominion is **the one who can take it back.** The second verse puts the name of taking back right at the beginning.

### لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ — to test

**Root: ب-ل-و.** This root was treated in the discussion of al-Baqara (2/49); I do not repeat it here. The summary of the finding there is this: the borrowed word has narrowed in Turkish to mean only calamity, whereas in Arabic the root means **to test, to try by wearing down**, and it covers testing by good as well — *"We test you with evil and with good, as a trial"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/35).

A layer must be added here, because the concrete side of the root is also at work in Sūrat al-Mulk.

**The proper sense of the root is "to wear out, to wear down".** *Baliya'l-thawb* — the garment wore out. *Bālin* — worn, old. That testing is named with this word is no accident: the Arabs tested a thing's durability **by using it**. Whether a sword is good is understood not by looking but by striking. Testing is wearing down.

**And now the two halves of the verse come together:** to test by wearing down, a period of wearing down is needed first. Death and life are the two ends of that period. The word sits exactly on its subject.

### Is the test God's learning something He did not know?

This question is unavoidable and must be handled honestly, because the verse says "in order to test", and testing is normally a method of acquiring information.

The classical answer is this, and it is reasonable: **a test is not for producing knowledge but for producing an act.** God's knowledge encompasses what will be; but the **realisation** of a thing that is known is a separate matter. A teacher's knowing that a student will fail does not require that the student not sit the examination — because the function of the examination is not to inform the teacher but to bring the outcome about **in fact**.

A second answer is given by way of the language: in the Qurʾān verbs of this kind (*li-yaʿlama*, *li-yabluwa*) are often used in the sense of "**so that it may come out into the open**" — what is meant is not the forming of knowledge but the matter's becoming public.

**I relate these two explanations; I do not present either as a transmitted report.** Both are widespread in the tradition and they do not exclude one another.

And this should be added: the verse was not framed in order to answer this question. The one addressed by the verse is not a theologian curious about God's knowledge but a human being who does not know what his own life was given for.

### أَيُّكُمْ أَحْسَنُ عَمَلًا — the finest place in the sūra

Here is the distinction.

The verse does not say **"which of you will do the most deeds"**. The word **أَكْثَر (*akthar* — more)** exists in Arabic and is used in the Qurʾān. It was not chosen.

The word chosen is **أَحْسَن (*aḥsan* — better, finer)**.

**Root: ح-س-ن.** To be beautiful, to be good, to be fitting. Beneath the root lies the idea of **fittingness and aptness**: *ḥasan* is a thing's being as it ought to be. In Arabic the beauty of a face, the soundness of a piece of work and the rightness of a character are all named with the same root. *Iḥsān* comes from the same root and means "to do a work giving it its due".

**When the measure changes everything changes. The difference needs making concrete:**

| | **The measure of quantity** (*akthar*) | **The measure of quality** (*aḥsan*) |
|---|---|---|
| Its question | How many? | How? |
| Its measurability | Countable from outside | Not fully measurable from outside |
| Its form of competition | By comparison with another | By comparison with one's own means |
| A person of few means | Structurally at a disadvantage | Not at a disadvantage |
| Saturation point | None — more is always possible | There is one — this work was given its due |

**The fifth row is especially important.** With the measure of quantity nobody can be satisfied, because "more" is an infinite direction. This is exactly what was treated in Sūrat al-Takāthur: the race to multiply is itself a sickness and its stopping point is death.

Sūrat al-Mulk cancels this race from the outset. If the measure is **not quantity**, there is no such thing as getting ahead by multiplying.

**And there is a sixth consequence, which I write as my own reading:** the measure of *aḥsan* **equalises** the test. The one with great means and the one with little means answer the same question: did you do what was in your hand giving it its due? This question can be put to a billionaire and to a labourer with the same weight. The question "how many?" cannot.

### The Qurʾān explained by the Qurʾān: the same pattern in two more places

This expression does not stand alone in the Qurʾān:

- *"He who created the heavens and the earth in six days… **to test which of you is best in deed**"* (Hūd 11/7 — *li-yabluwakum ayyukum aḥsanu ʿamalā*, exactly the same expression as al-Mulk 67/2).
- *"We have made what is on the earth an adornment for it, **so that We may test which of them is best in deed**"* (al-Kahf 18/7 — *li-nabluwahum ayyuhum aḥsanu ʿamalā*).

**In all three places the measure is the same: *aḥsan*. In none of them does *akthar* occur.**

And the contexts of the three complete one another:

| Place | The ground of the testing |
|---|---|
| **Hūd 11/7** | The creation of the heavens and the earth |
| **al-Kahf 18/7** | The adornments on the earth — that is, property, beauty, means |
| **al-Mulk 67/2** | Death and life |

That is, the material of the testing is given at three separate scales: the universe, one's means, and one's lifetime. The measure stays the same every time.

### وَهُوَ ٱلْعَزِيزُ ٱلْغَفُورُ — an unexpected pair

The end of the verse calls for attention. After a sentence that speaks of a test, the expected pair of names would have been *al-ʿazīzu'l-ḥakīm* (mighty and wise) or *al-samīʿu'l-ʿalīm*. The pair that comes is: **al-ʿAzīz — al-Ghafūr.**

**عَزِيز — root ع-ز-ز.** The concrete sense of the root is **hardness, being strong, unconquerability**; and a second branch of meaning is **rarity and preciousness** (*ʿazīz*: hard to come by, precious). Joined together: the one not overcome, not reached, whose worth is not measured against another's.

**غَفُور — root غ-ف-ر.** And the concrete sense of this root is **to cover**. *Mighfar* — the helmet drawn over the head, covering the skull. From the same root *ghifāra*, a cover drawn over a thing.

That is, forgiveness is not the sin's being **counted as nothing** but its being **covered**. There is something there; it has been closed over.

**Now the meaning of the pair appears.** The two names placed at the end of a verse announcing a test describe the two sides of the test:

- **ʿAzīz:** there is no escaping the examination, no bargaining with the one who set it, no power that can have the result annulled.
- **Ghafūr:** but when the result comes out badly it can be covered over.

**Their standing side by side sets the tone of the sūra.** Had there been only *ʿAzīz*, the test would have been an execution. Had there been only *Ghafūr*, the test would have had no meaning. The two together: an examination to be taken seriously but not to be despaired of.

### What this says to the present

**The matter of the measure works especially well today.**

Modern life is built almost entirely on the measure of quantity: how many hours you worked, how many books you read, how many steps you took, how many people follow you, how many tasks you finished. What is measurable is manageable, and what is manageable is preferred. This is not a mistake; it is a convenience.

But the convenience has a price: **what cannot be measured becomes invisible.** Because whether a work was done giving it its due does not turn into a number, it does not appear on the chart. What does not appear is in time counted unimportant.

The measure the verse lays down is exactly the measure that does not go on the chart.

Its counterpart in the life of worship is direct and uncomfortable: how many cycles of prayer, how many pages, how many days — all of these can be counted, and counting them gives peace. The measure of *aḥsan* disturbs that peace, because it cannot be counted. The question "have I done enough?" can be answered; the question "did I give it its due?" cannot.

And the sūra lays down as its measure the one that cannot be answered.

**And one must look at it from the other side too.** The measure of *aḥsan* does not only make things harder; it also **rescues**. Someone who can do little is hopeless on the measure of quantity — he can never catch up. On the measure of quality he is not hopeless. What is in his hand is little, but the little also has its due, and that due can be given.

## 67/3 — ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ سَبْعَ سَمَٰوَٰتٍ طِبَاقًا ۖ مَّا تَرَىٰ فِى خَلْقِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ مِن تَفَٰوُتٍ ۖ فَٱرْجِعِ ٱلْبَصَرَ هَلْ تَرَىٰ مِن فُطُورٍ

*Alladhī khalaqa sabʿa samāwātin ṭibāqā, mā tarā fī khalqi'l-raḥmāni min tafāwut, fa'rjiʿi'l-baṣara hal tarā min fuṭūr*
"He who created seven heavens in layers. You will see no disharmony in the creation of the Merciful. Turn your gaze back and look: do you see any crack?"

### طِبَاقًا — layer upon layer

**Root: ط-ب-ق.** A thing's fitting exactly onto another thing, a lid's fitting its vessel. *Ṭabaq* — lid, cover; *muṭābaqa* — correspondence, exact matching; *ṭabaqa* — each of a set of layers laid one on another.

**Both senses of the word are at work here at the same time:** the seven heavens are both **one upon another** (layers) and **in accord with one another** (correspondence). Rendering it into English as "layer upon layer" gives the first sense and misses the second.

And the second sense is the ground of what follows in the verse: disharmony is going to be searched for. The name of the place where disharmony is searched for has been given from the root of "harmony".

**A note on the number "seven".** The Qurʾān gives the number of the heavens as seven. Matching this number with some layering in modern astronomy (atmospheric layers, orbital belts and the like) is a widespread practice; **it is not done in this commentary.** The ground is written in the method: the verse is not an observation report, and every one of these matchings is a candidate for falling apart at the next scientific revision. Besides, in Arabic the number "seven" also has a use conveying **multitude**, and some of the classical exegetes record this possibility. I record both possibilities and pass on.

### مَّا تَرَىٰ فِى خَلْقِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ — why "the creation of the Merciful"?

The sentence does not say *"in the creation of God"*, nor does it say *"in His creation"*. It uses the name **ٱلرَّحْمَٰن**.

In the discussion of al-Fātiḥa two properties of this name were recorded: its pattern (*faʿlān*) conveys fullness and overflowing, and **it is used only of God** — no human being is called *raḥmān*. It was also mentioned there that the name was disputed in Mecca, that a group did not recognise it; the Qurʾān records this openly: *"When they are told 'prostrate to the Merciful', they say 'and what is the Merciful?'"* (al-Furqān 25/60).

**Now look at the distribution in Sūrat al-Mulk.** This name occurs four times in the sūra and all four are at a disputed point:

| Verse | Use | Context |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | *khalqi'l-raḥmān* | The owner of flawlessness |
| 19 | *illa'l-raḥmān* | The one who holds the birds up in the air |
| 20 | *min dūni'l-raḥmān* | The absence of anyone else to help |
| 29 | *huwa'l-raḥmān* | "That is the Merciful; we have believed in Him" |

**The sūra insistently uses the name the one addressed does not recognise, and every time uses it together with an act of power.** And in the twenty-ninth verse it closes the debate: *"Say: That is the Merciful."*

I record this as an observation. But the meaning the observation produces is plain: the sūra answers a dispute about a name with the acts of the owner of that name. The being whose name is disputed is the being who set up the seven heavens.

**And the coming together of the name of mercy and the act of creating is meaningful in its own right.** Flawlessness is presented here not as an engineering compliment but as **a proof of mercy**: an order's being coherent is a good for those who live inside it. A ceiling without cracks is a mercy for the one sitting beneath it.

### تَفَاوُت — disharmony

**Root: ف-و-ت.** And the proper sense of the root is **to miss, to slip out of one's hand, to get away through a gap**. *Fātahu'l-amr* — the matter escaped him. *Fawt* — missing, losing; the Turkish *vefat* ("decease") is from a different root and must not be confused with it.

**In Form VI (*tafāwut*)** the word takes on reciprocity: two things' **not matching each other**, one escaping the other, an opening remaining between them.

It is rendered into English as "disharmony" or "discrepancy", but the picture in the word is more concrete: **two parts not sitting on each other, a gap remaining between them.** The light that seeps in between two boards a carpenter has joined. The place left open in a tailor's seam.

**And now see the arrangement of the verse:**

- *ṭibāqā* — fitting one another, matching like a lid.
- *tafāwut* — not fitting one another, escaping from one another.

**In the same sentence, two words that are exact opposites.** First the quality of the structure is stated (harmonious), then what will not be found is stated (disharmony). The sentence carries its own answer within itself.

**A note on the variant readings:** for this word two readings are recorded, **تَفَاوُت** and **تَفَوُّت**. The difference of meaning is next to nothing — both give "disharmony, discrepancy"; it is a difference of pattern. **Because I cannot give with certainty which reading belongs to which imam, I write no name.**

### فَٱرْجِعِ ٱلْبَصَرَ — turn the gaze back

The choice of word in this command is one of the most striking places in the sūra.

The verse does not say *"unẓur"* (look). It says **ٱرْجِعْ** — **root ر-ج-ع: to turn back, to send back, to return.**

That is, looking is described as a work of **sending out and taking back**. The eye is sent somewhere, then called back, then sent again.

**This says that looking is not a one-off act.** In the discussion of al-Ghāshiya the verb *"do they not look?"* (*a-fa-lā yanẓurūna*) was dwelt on and this finding was made: the flaw the sūra diagnoses is not failure to see but failure to look; habit is attention's greatest enemy.

Al-Mulk asks for one step beyond that: looking is not enough, **looking again** is asked for. And the ground of the repetition will be stated in the next verse.

**ٱلْبَصَر — root ب-ص-ر.** Sight, the sense of sight, and **insight** (*baṣīra*). Beneath the root lie both the act of the eye and the act of the mind; Arabic does not separate the two. *Baṣīr* — one who both sees and understands.

**And this root is the backbone of the sūra.** The places where it occurs in the sūra:

| Verse | Word | Who or what |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | *tarā… fa'rjiʿi'l-baṣar* | The human being's gaze: search for a flaw |
| 4 | *yanqalib ilayka'l-baṣar* | The human being's gaze: it comes back |
| 19 | *innahu bi-kulli shayʾin **baṣīr*** | God's seeing |
| 23 | *wa'l-**abṣār*** | The eyes given to the human being |

**Note the sequence:** the human being's gaze is first sent out (3), comes back exhausted (4), then who the real seer is is stated (19), and at the very end it is recalled who gave that eye (23). **I record this as my own reading:** after showing the limit of the ability to look, the sūra states the source of that ability.

### فُطُور — crack

**Root: ف-ط-ر.** This root was treated at length in the discussion of al-Infiṭār; I do not repeat it here. The summary of the finding there: beneath the root lies the idea of **what is closed being opened**, and for this reason the same root carries both the sense "to split" and the sense "to bring into being for the first time" — *fāṭir* is the one who splits out of nothing, *fiṭra* is the state at the moment of splitting, *infiṭār* is the splitting of the sky.

**In Sūrat al-Mulk the root comes with a third face: the crack of a flaw.**

*Fuṭūr* is a plural — cracks, fissures. In al-Infiṭār the splitting was an **event** (at the resurrection the sky will split); here the fissure is a **deficiency** (is there a crack in the structure?).

**And the picture the three uses of the root give together is this:**

| Use | Where | Its meaning | Its value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **فاطر** | al-Anʿām 6/14, Fāṭir 35/1 | The one who splits out of nothing | Creation itself |
| **فطرة** | al-Rūm 30/30 | The state of first creation | What is unspoiled |
| **انفطار** | al-Infiṭār 82/1 | The splitting of the sky | The ending of the order |
| **فطور** | al-Mulk 67/3 | Crack, flaw | **Not to be found** |

**What comes out of this, as my own reading:** the same root gives both the name of the creator and the name of the flaw sought in the created. The one who is *Fāṭir* has split; but in what He has split there is no *fuṭūr*. The act of splitting belongs to the creator, and the fissure is absent in the created.

This is a fine point that comes out of the language itself and it may be a coincidence. But this much can be said: Arabic is a language that uses the same root for "to create" and "to crack", and this verse brings the two ends of that root face to face in a single sentence.

### هَلْ تَرَىٰ — "do you see?"

The verse uses two different negations and the difference between them is important:

- **مَّا تَرَىٰ** — "you will not see" (a declarative sentence, negative).
- **هَلْ تَرَىٰ** — "do you see?" (an interrogative sentence).

The first **states** the result, the second **makes the one addressed find it**.

That is, the verse first gives the answer and then sends the one addressed to verify it with his own eye. The order could have been the reverse — search first, and I will tell you the result afterwards. It has not been so.

**I think this is an expression of confidence, as my own reading:** to state the result at the outset and then say "go and look" is what only someone certain of the result would do. To give the answer to the examination and then send someone to work it out is the act of a teacher who knows the answer is right.

## 67/4 — ثُمَّ ٱرْجِعِ ٱلْبَصَرَ كَرَّتَيْنِ يَنقَلِبْ إِلَيْكَ ٱلْبَصَرُ خَاسِئًا وَهُوَ حَسِيرٌ

*Thumma'rjiʿi'l-baṣara karratayni yanqalib ilayka'l-baṣaru khāsiʾan wa huwa ḥasīr*
"Then turn the gaze back twice over; the gaze will come back to you dulled and worn out."

The most cinematic verse of the sūra. And a sentence in the theory of knowledge.

### ثُمَّ — the time set in between

**ثُمَّ** in Arabic is the conjunction that conveys **delay** between two things; *fāʾ* conveys coming immediately after, *thumma* the passing of time in between.

That is, between the looking in the third verse and the looking in the fourth verse there is an interval. The command is not "look again at once" but **"look again after a while."**

This detail is important, because it describes a method of observation: repeated observation must be spaced. To look one after another is the same look; to look with an interval is a new look.

### كَرَّتَيْنِ — twice

**Root: ك-ر-ر.** And the proper place of the root is the battlefield. *Karra* — to attack again after charging and falling back. In Arab cavalry tactics *karr wa farr* (attack and withdraw) is a known method: the unit attacks, withdraws, attacks again. *Karra* — each of these moves; from here the sense "time, occasion" arose.

**The word's origin in war changes the picture of the verse.** Looking is described here not as a passive viewing but as **a unit sent out and drawn back**. And at the end of the fourth verse that unit comes back defeated.

**The matter of the number — a disagreement:**

| View | Explanation | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Exactly two** | In the third verse one look was taken, here two more; three in all | The apparent sense of the wording |
| **"Again and again"** | *Karratayn* here is not a definite number but conveys multitude | In Arabic the dual is sometimes a figure for multitude — in patterns like *labbayka* (at your service, over and over) and *saʿdayk* the dual does not convey number |
| **Reinforcement** | The dual shows the insistence of the command | Close to the above |

**My preference is the second view**, and its ground is the outcome in the verse: the gaze comes back "worn out". Two looks do not produce exhaustion; exhaustion comes from the repetition being prolonged. **It is not binding;** the first view is closer to the wording and is defensible.

### يَنقَلِبْ — to come back transformed

**Root: ق-ل-ب.** And this root is one of the most loaded in Arabic: **to turn a thing over, to overturn it, to change its face.**

From the same root comes **قَلْب (*qalb*)** — and the heart is given this name because it is the thing that **turns, changes, passes from state to state**. Arabic has named the inner centre of the human being not "the fixed one" but "the one that is turned".

**In the verse it comes in Form VII (*yanqalib*): reflexive or passive.** That is, the eye does not exactly turn itself back; **it is turned.** The verb gives the return of the gaze not as a choice but as a result.

And the second sense of the word works here: the eye does not merely **come back**, it comes back **changed**. Going out it was one thing, coming back another. This is in the word.

### خَاسِئًا — driven off

**Root: خ-س-أ.** This word was treated in the discussion of al-Baqara (2/65); the record there was: *khāsiʾ*, **driven off, made to withdraw**; from the root used for driving an animal away with a shout.

**A layer must be added here.** The concrete use of the word is driving off a dog: *khasaʾtu'l-kalba* — I drove the dog off, I shouted it away. And *khasaʾa'l-kalbu* — the dog was driven off and cowered, drew back.

That is, the word describes both sides: the side that drives off and the side that is driven off and comes back **cowering**. What is used in the verse is the second.

**Now the scene becomes clear.** The eye is sent somewhere. There it looks for something: a crack, a flaw, a gap. It does not find one. And on the way back — like a driven-off animal — it cowers.

**The difference from the use in al-Baqara is worth recording.** There *khāsiʾīn* was an attribute of punishment: humiliated, driven off. Here it is an attribute of defeat: not having found what was sought. The same word describes in one place a moral fall and in the other a cognitive limit. **The common ground of the root is the same in both: having been repulsed.**

### حَسِير — worn out

**Root: ح-س-ر.** And the idea beneath this root is **uncovering, stripping, the removal of what is on top**.

- *Ḥasara'l-kumma* — he rolled up his sleeve, he opened the cuff.
- *Ḥāsir* — bare-headed, without a helmet; a warrior without armour.
- *Ḥasra* — regret, heartache. **And how the word comes to this sense is instructive:** regret is the **taking away** of what one had — an inside whose protection has been stripped off, left bare.
- *Ḥasīr* — a tired animal, one whose strength is spent. Especially a camel that has collapsed on a long road and can walk no further.

**That tiredness comes from the root of "stripping" gives the picture of the word:** the one who is tired is the one whose strength has been taken off him. In the discussion of al-Ghāshiya a similar finding was made for the word *naṣab* (weariness): there weariness derived from "standing up" — the weary one is the one who cannot sit. Here it derives from somewhere else: the weary one is **the one left bare**.

**The use of the two words together gives two separate defeats of the gaze:**

| Word | What it says | The kind of defeat |
|---|---|---|
| **خاسئ** | It did not find what it sought, it was repulsed | A defeat of **outcome** |
| **حسير** | Its strength was spent, it collapsed | A defeat of **resource** |

That is, the eye both failed and was used up. To come back empty is one thing; to come back empty and exhausted is another.

### What is this verse saying? — a place that must be read carefully

There is a possibility of misunderstanding here and it must be closed off from the start.

**The verse does not say "do not look, it is pointless."** It does the opposite: it **commands** looking, and repeatedly at that. The imperative comes twice in two verses (*fa'rjiʿ*, *thumma'rjiʿ*).

What is defeated is **not the searching but the claim to find a flaw**. The eye is sent and does not find; its not finding shows not the worthlessness of looking but **the soundness of what is looked at**.

**As my own reading I add this:** the verse describes a method of observation and the method has three elements:

1. **Repetition** — one look is not enough (*karratayn*).
2. **Interval** — not one after another, but spaced (*thumma*).
3. **Clarity of aim** — what is to be sought is specified: *fuṭūr*, a crack.

These are the elements of a method. And the verse does not shrink from announcing the result of the method in advance.

**But this limit must be drawn:** I do not draw from this any claim such as "the Qurʾān is describing the scientific method". The verse is not a laboratory protocol; it is a sentence of invitation and its subject is the flawlessness of the universe. The resemblance of method is an observation, not a claim of discovery.

### The Qurʾān explained by the Qurʾān

A counterpart of this looking occurs in Āl ʿImrān: *"Our Lord! You did not create this in vain"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/191 — *rabbanā mā khalaqta hādhā bāṭilā*). There too there is a human being looking at the heavens, and the conclusion reached is the same: there is no gap in the structure.

The difference is this: the sentence in Āl ʿImrān is a **prayer**, coming from the mouth of the one who looks. The sentence in al-Mulk is a **statement**, made before the looking. There is no tension between the two; one announces the result, the other shows someone who has arrived at the result.

### What this says to the present

**Observation's running into its own limit** is something particularly understandable today.

This is the most repeated lesson in the history of modern knowledge: an instrument of measurement cannot see what lies below its own sensitivity. The telescope takes you a certain distance and stops there. The microscope goes down a certain distance and stops there. And when it reaches the limit the instrument is not faulty — it is simply at the limit.

The weariness the verse describes is this weariness: not the breaking down of the instrument but **the filling up of its capacity**.

This has a practical consequence and it works in everyday life: **the difference between "I did not find it" and "there is none".** The eye did not find a crack; does that show there is no crack? Here the verse sets up a claim — *mā tarā… min tafāwut* — and supports the claim by inviting one to look. But the tiring of the instrument also says that the instrument does not encompass everything.

**And from here a thought comes out that connects to the whole of the sūra, as my own reading:** the tiring of the gaze is a proof of the greatness of what is looked at. An eye that looks at a small thing does not tire; tiring is the sign that the scales do not match.

A second aspect: **looking in search is different from looking as a spectator.** The verse commands not spectating but searching — and searching is done with a target. What a person looking at the sky today mostly does is spectate; what the verse asks for is to look with a question. To look with a question turns the same view into something else.

## 67/5 — وَلَقَدْ زَيَّنَّا ٱلسَّمَآءَ ٱلدُّنْيَا بِمَصَٰبِيحَ وَجَعَلْنَٰهَا رُجُومًا لِّلشَّيَٰطِينِ ۖ وَأَعْتَدْنَا لَهُمْ عَذَابَ ٱلسَّعِيرِ

*Wa laqad zayyanna'l-samāʾa'l-dunyā bi-maṣābīḥa wa jaʿalnāhā rujūman li'l-shayāṭīn, wa aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāba'l-saʿīr*
"We have adorned the nearest sky with lamps and made them things to be hurled at the satans. And We have prepared for them the punishment of the blaze."

### ٱلسَّمَآءَ ٱلدُّنْيَا — the nearest sky

**Root: د-ن-و.** This root was treated in the discussion of al-Baqara: **to be near** and **to be low**. And as was recorded there, *dunyā* (this life) is also from this root — the name of this life is **"the near one"**, and *adnā* from the same root means "nearer / lower".

In the discussion of al-Baqara this reservation was also entered: the badness of this world is not in its quality but **in its price** — the near has been exchanged for the lasting.

**In the use here the word comes with its full lexical sense and this is instructive.** *Al-samāʾu'l-dunyā* — "the near sky", that is, the one seen by the eye, the lowest. The same word, carrying no moral load at all, conveys only **distance**.

**This shows how the word "world" is to be understood:** the word is not the name of a bad thing but the name of **what is near**. The badness is not in nearness but in preferring the near to the far.

**And the construct says one thing more:** to say "the nearest sky" requires that there be further skies. Two verses earlier "seven heavens" was said; here the lowest of them is spoken of. The two verses complete each other.

### مَصَابِيح — lamps

**Root: ص-ب-ح.** Morning, brightness, shining. *Ṣubḥ* — morning; *aṣbaḥa* — he entered upon the morning, he came into a state; *miṣbāḥ* — **a lamp with a wick and oil.**

**The choice of word calls for attention.** The Qurʾān uses the words *nujūm* and *kawākib* for the stars too. Here neither of the two has been used; **lamp** has been said.

The difference is this: *najm* and *kawkab* name the body, *miṣbāḥ* names **the function**. A lamp is a thing that gives light; a lamp that gives no light is not a lamp.

That is, when the verse speaks of the bodies in the sky it says not what they are but **what they are for**. This is on the same line as the finding recorded in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/22 for the sky's being called a "building": the word chosen conveys function.

**Other occurrences of the word in the Qurʾān:**

- *"We adorned the nearest sky with lamps and guarded it"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/12) — almost the same sentence as al-Mulk 67/5.
- *"That light is like a niche within which is a lamp"* (al-Nūr 24/35) — here the lamp is inside a likeness; a representation of light.

**The link with al-Nūr 24/35 is worth recording:** the same word is used in one place for the lights in the sky and in another for the representation of divine light. What both have in common is the function of **showing the way in darkness**.

### زَيَّنَّا — We adorned

**Root: ز-ي-ن.** To adorn, to beautify. *Zīna* — adornment.

And there is a fine point here: **adornment is what is not necessary.** A lamp is hung in order to give light; its being an adornment is a separate thing. The verse names the lights of the sky first as **adornment**.

This is akin to the expression at al-Kahf 18/7: *"We have made what is on the earth an adornment for it"* — there too adornment was mentioned as the material of the test.

**As my own reading I add this:** that there is in the universe a beauty beyond function connects quietly with the measure of *aḥsan* in the second verse. If the measure is "the best deed", then the work of the one who set the measure also contains beauty. The difference between what has to be done and what has been done beautifully is looked for both in the universe and in the human being's own deeds.

### رُجُومًا لِّلشَّيَاطِينِ — a difficult verse

This is the place in the sūra that produces the most questions, and one must behave honestly.

**Root: ر-ج-م.** To stone, to throw stones. *Rajm* — stoning; *rajīm* — stoned, driven off (an attribute of the satan). *Rujūm* is a plural: things hurled, missiles.

**What does the verse say?** The lamps have been made "things to be hurled" at the satans.

The Qurʾān mentions this subject elsewhere too:

- *"We adorned the nearest sky with stars and guarded it from every rebellious satan. They cannot listen to the highest assembly and are pelted from every side"* (around al-Ṣāffāt 37/6-8; the expressions are within this frame).
- *"We touched the sky and found it filled with strong guards and flames. We used to sit there in places to listen; but now whoever wishes to listen finds a flame watching for him"* (al-Jinn 72/8-9).

**I record the views on how these verses are to be understood, and I issue no ruling among them:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **On the apparent sense** | There is a guarding in the sky; the satans' attempt to reach knowledge is blocked; this blocking takes place with flames that are hurled |
| **The lamps and what is hurled are separate** | The lamps that adorn and what is hurled at the satans are not the same bodies; the verse mentions two functions together and does not reduce them to one object |
| **A representation** | The expression is a representation conveying that knowledge of the unseen is protected; it is framed so as to block claims of soothsaying and of announcing the unseen |

**What I shall not do is clear: I shall not match this verse with meteors and construct a scientific miracle out of it.** This matching is very widespread; but what the stones falling from the sky are is not the subject of the verse, and the link that is set up says more than the verse says. The rule written in the method is applied here.

The function of the verse within its context, by contrast, is beyond dispute and it is this that should be recorded: **the sūra says that every channel claiming to bring news from the sky is closed.** In Mecca there were soothsayers, and it was being put forward that revelation too came from such a source. In Sūrat al-Qalam we shall meet another form of this claim (the accusation of *majnūn*). Here the source is cut off at the outset.

### وَأَعْتَدْنَا لَهُمْ — to whom does the pronoun refer?

At the end of the verse it says *"and We have prepared for them the punishment of the blaze"*. To whom does the pronoun **لَهُمْ** refer?

There are two possibilities: **the satans** (those mentioned most recently) or **the deniers** (those who will be mentioned in the next verse). Most of the classical exegetes refer it to the satans; since the next verse will frame a separate sentence for the deniers, this seems reasonable. **I record it as a preference; it is not binding.**

**أَعْتَدْنَا — root ع-ت-د.** To prepare, to keep ready. *ʿAtīd* — the one that is ready. The verb is in the perfect: the punishment **has been** prepared, not is to be prepared. This form is often used in the Qurʾān for scenes of the hereafter and its effect is this: what is to come is mentioned, at the level of language, as **already done and finished**.

**ٱلسَّعِير — root س-ع-ر.** The flame's catching and rising, the fire's blazing up. *Saʿara'l-nār* — he made the fire blaze. From the same root comes *siʿr* (price) — the mentioning of price by way of its "blazing" is an old usage in Arabic. *Saʿīr* is fire that does not go out, that has been made to blaze.

This word occurs three times in the sūra (5, 10, 11) and in all three it names the end of a group.

## 67/6-7 — وَلِلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ بِرَبِّهِمْ عَذَابُ جَهَنَّمَ ۖ وَبِئْسَ ٱلْمَصِيرُ ۝ إِذَآ أُلْقُوا۟ فِيهَا سَمِعُوا۟ لَهَا شَهِيقًا وَهِىَ تَفُورُ

*Wa lilladhīna kafarū bi-rabbihim ʿadhābu jahannam, wa biʾsa'l-maṣīr — idhā ulqū fīhā samiʿū lahā shahīqan wa hiya tafūr*
"And for those who denied their Lord there is the punishment of Hell; what an evil destination! When they are cast into it they hear it drawing a terrible breath as it boils."

### وَبِئْسَ ٱلْمَصِيرُ — the destination

**مَصِير — root ص-ي-ر.** And the proper sense of this root is **to turn from one state into another**. *Ṣāra* — became, turned into. In Arabic grammar the verb *ṣāra*, one of *kāna* and its sisters, conveys exactly this: a thing's coming to be another thing.

**Maṣīr** is the noun of place of this verb: **the place turned into, the final state arrived at.**

It is rendered into English as "destination" and this is right; but the idea of **transformation** inside the word is lost. *Maṣīr* is not only the place gone to; it is the place where **what a person has become** is settled.

In the discussion of al-Ghāshiya the difference between the words *iyāb* and *rujūʿ* was recorded. *Maṣīr* differs from these: *rujūʿ* is to return, *maṣīr* is to be transformed. One conveys a place, the other a state.

### شَهِيق — the donkey's drawing in of breath

**Root: ش-ه-ق.** And the concrete equivalent of the word is needed in order to understand the verse fully.

In Arabic breath is named with two verbs:

- **زَفِير (*zafīr*)** — the **letting out** of breath; the deep, rasping sound that comes from the chest.
- **شَهِيق (*shahīq*)** — the **drawing in** of breath; the long, muffled sound made while pulling air into the lungs.

And the most concrete example the lexicographers record is this: **the braying of a donkey.** Braying is in two parts — first the drawing in (*shahīq*), then the letting out (*zafīr*). Arabic has named these two movements separately.

The Qurʾān uses both:

- *"There they will have *zafīr* and *shahīq*"* (Hūd 11/106) — **here both sounds are the sounds of the people of the Fire.**

**And now see the reversal in Sūrat al-Mulk:** here *shahīq* is not the sound of the people but the sound of **the fire**. *Samiʿū **lahā** shahīqan* — "they hear **its** drawing of breath."

**The change of roles is a literary move worth recording.** In Sūrat Hūd it is the people who breathe and the fire is a silent place. In Sūrat al-Mulk the people are listeners and the fire is what breathes.

What does this do? **It takes the fire out of being a place and sets it up like a living thing.** The next verse will take this further still: the fire will be enraged.

**And note the form of the verb:** *samiʿū* — they heard. The first contact is not seeing but **hearing**. The one who is cast in does not first see, he first hears. This is the real experience of someone thrown into a dark place: sound comes before sight.

### تَفُورُ — to boil

**Root: ف-و-ر.** To boil, to bubble, to rise by overflowing. *Fawwāra* — a fountain, gushing water. *Fāra'l-māʾ* — the water boiled, bubbled up.

Its best-known use in the Qurʾān is in the story of Noah: *"Until, when Our command came and the oven boiled over…"* (Hūd 11/40 — *wa fāra'l-tannūr*).

**The fine point of the word is this:** *fawr* is heat's becoming **visible**. While a vessel is heating nothing is seen; when it boils the heat turns into movement. The word *fevrî* that has passed from the same root into Turkish still carries this sudden and overflowing movement.

**And the picture the two words give together:** *shahīq* is sound, *tafūr* is movement. One addresses the ear, the other the eye. The verse completes the scene by laying two senses one on the other.

**And the forms of the verbs differ too:** *samiʿū* is perfect (they heard), *tafūr* imperfect (it boils). That is, the hearing is a moment, the boiling a continuity. The boiling was there before they came and goes on after they come.

## 67/8-9 — تَكَادُ تَمَيَّزُ مِنَ ٱلْغَيْظِ ۖ كُلَّمَآ أُلْقِىَ فِيهَا فَوْجٌ سَأَلَهُمْ خَزَنَتُهَآ أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ نَذِيرٌ ۝ قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ قَدْ جَآءَنَا نَذِيرٌ فَكَذَّبْنَا وَقُلْنَا مَا نَزَّلَ ٱللَّهُ مِن شَىْءٍ إِنْ أَنتُمْ إِلَّا فِى ضَلَٰلٍ كَبِيرٍ

*Takādu tamayyazu mina'l-ghayẓ, kullamā ulqiya fīhā fawjun saʾalahum khazanatuhā a-lam yaʾtikum nadhīr — qālū balā qad jāʾanā nadhīrun fa-kadhdhabnā wa qulnā mā nazzala'llāhu min shayʾin in antum illā fī ḍalālin kabīr*
"It almost bursts apart from fury. Every time a company is cast into it, its keepers ask them: 'Did no warner come to you?' They say: 'Yes, a warner came to us; but we cried lies and said: God has sent down nothing, you are in nothing but great error.'"

### تَكَادُ تَمَيَّزُ — the threshold of bursting

The verb **كَادَ** in Arabic conveys "to draw near, to be on the point of". And it has an important property: **kāda speaks of what does not happen.** Someone who says "he nearly fell" has not fallen.

That is, the fire is not bursting apart; **it stands at the threshold of bursting** and stays there.

**This turns the scene from a moment into a continuity.** A thing that explodes is finished; a thing that stands at the threshold of exploding is not finished. Because the tension is not released, it goes on.

**تَمَيَّزُ — root م-ي-ز.** To separate, to sort out from one another. *Mayyaza* — he separated, he distinguished; *tamyīz* — discrimination (still used in Turkish: *temyiz kudreti*, the power of discernment).

*Tamayyaza* is in the reflexive form: **to separate from oneself, to come apart into pieces.**

**The fine point of the word is here:** the verse does not say "it will explode" or "it will split". It says **"it will come apart into its own pieces"**. This is the picture of rage: not an attack outwards but **a disintegration from within**.

**ٱلْغَيْظ — root غ-ي-ظ.** And this word's place in Arabic is particular.

In Arabic there are two basic words for anger:

| | **غَضَب (*ghaḍab*)** | **غَيْظ (*ghayẓ*)** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Anger that strikes outward, that is seen | Anger that stays inside, that is swallowed |
| Its outcome | It turns into an act | It gathers in the breast |
| In the Qurʾān | It is ascribed to God too (*al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim*) | It is used of the human being |

*Ghayẓ* is **swallowed anger**. The Qurʾān records this openly: one of the qualities of those who guard themselves is *al-kāẓimīna'l-ghayẓ* — "those who swallow their anger" (Āl ʿImrān 3/134). And the verb "to swallow" (*kaẓm*) will meet us again in Sūrat al-Qalam: Jonah's attribute is *makẓūm*.

**Now the picture of the verse becomes clear.** The fire is not angry; it is in a state of **having swallowed its anger**. Because it cannot let it out, it comes apart from within. *Takādu tamayyazu* — it has come to the point of coming apart but cannot come apart.

**What is the function of this description?** I record it as an observation: Hell is described here not like an instrument of execution but like **something that reacts to injustice**. Not an object but a party. And this is a method repeated in the Qurʾān's descriptions of the resurrection — the earth speaks (al-Zalzala), the skin bears witness, the sky splits and "submits" (al-Inshiqāq). The universe is not set up as a stage indifferent to what happens.

### خَزَنَتُهَا — guards, or storekeepers?

**Root: خ-ز-ن.** And the concrete sense of the word is **to store, to keep in a store**. *Khazīna* — the place where stored things are kept; the Turkish *hazine* (treasury) and *hazne* (reservoir) are the same word. *Khāzin* — the one in charge of the store, the cellarer.

**That is, the name given to those in charge of the fire is not "sentry" but "storekeeper".** This is a strange and striking choice.

The meaning the word produces is this: a sentry protects a place, a storekeeper **records and takes delivery of** a thing. And the work they do in the verse fits this: they receive each arriving group and **ask a question**. This is an intake procedure.

**I record it as my own reading:** the word suggests that Hell is set up not like a dungeon but like a **store** — a record is kept of what is put into it. It may be thought that this reading is forced; but the root of the word is plain, and although Arabic has other words for "guard" (*ḥāris*, *raqīb*), this one was chosen.

### كُلَّمَا — every time

**كُلَّمَا** is an adverb conveying repetition: "whenever… every time."

And the whole weight of the verse is here: **the question is not asked once, it is asked to every group that comes.**

The scene is this: a group is cast in, the question is asked, the answer is received. Then another group. The same question. The same answer. Then another group.

**The effect of the repetition is heavier than the question itself.** A single interrogation is a reckoning; an interrogation repeated endlessly turns into a **procedure of record**. And the answer's never changing — all of them say the same thing — shows that the question is rhetorical.

**فَوْج — root ف-و-ج.** A body moving together, a group, a wave. In the word there is the idea of **coming together**. In Sūrat al-Naṣr the same word is used in reverse: *"you will see people entering God's religion **in throngs**"* (al-Naṣr 110/2). The same word, one at an entrance and one at a fall.

### أَلَمْ يَأْتِكُمْ نَذِيرٌ — the exact text of the question

The question is carefully framed. What is asked is not **"did you believe?"**, nor is it **"did you do good works?"**

What is asked is: **"did a warner come to you?"**

That is, the interrogation begins not from the person's act but from **the information that reached him**. The precondition of responsibility is what is asked about.

**This agrees with a principle of the Qurʾān:** *"We do not punish until We have sent a messenger"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/15). Without the warning arriving there is no reckoning.

**نَذِير — root ن-ذ-ر.** To warn, to give notice of danger. And the distinguishing side of the root is this: *indhār* is a warning given **while there is still time**. Giving notice after the danger has come is not called *indhār*.

From the same root comes *nadhr* (a vow) — to place an obligation on oneself. What joins the two senses is the idea of **binding oneself in advance**.

**The word occurs four times in the sūra** (8, 9, 17, 26) and its distribution is meaningful:

| Verse | Who says it | What is said |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | The keepers of Hell | "Did no warner come to you?" |
| 9 | Those inside | "Yes, one came." |
| 17 | God | "You shall know how My warning is." |
| 26 | The Prophet | "I am only a clear warner." |

**Read in reverse order a chain emerges:** the messenger introduces himself as a warner (26), the seriousness of the warning is announced (17), and finally it is confessed that the warning arrived (9). The sūra has placed the whole life-cycle of the warning into four verses. **I record this as my own reading.**

### قَالُوا۟ بَلَىٰ — the text of the confession

The particle **بَلَىٰ** has a special function in Arabic: it gives **an affirmative answer to a negative question**. Had *naʿam* been said to the question "did none come?", it would have meant "yes, none came"; when *balā* is said it becomes "no, one came".

That is, the denial is refuted with a single word.

**And the continuation of the confession contains the confessors' own sentences:**

*"…we cried lies and **said: God has sent down nothing, you are in nothing but great error.**"*

**This is the harshest place in the verse.** The one confessing does not merely say "we cried lies"; he **repeats word for word** the sentence he uttered on that day.

And look at the content of the sentence he repeats:

1. *"God has sent down nothing"* — **absolute denial.** Not "this message is not true"; "there is no message at all".
2. *"You are in great error"* — **a counter-accusation.** They did not merely refuse; they declared what they refused to be error.

**The second sentence is especially important, because it shows the roles.** The party that accuses the warner of error ends by repeating its own words with its own mouth. The twenty-ninth verse of the sūra will answer this sentence directly: *"You shall soon know who is in clear error."*

**This correspondence set up between the two verses is one of the plainest internal links of the sūra:** *ḍalālin kabīr* (9) and *ḍalālin mubīn* (29). The same word, from two different mouths, with two different adjectives.

### What this says to the present

**The timing of the confession** is worth dwelling on.

The people in the verse are not ignorant. They possess the knowledge — they know the warner came, they know what he said, they even remember their own answers. What is lacking is not knowledge.

**What is lacking is the moment at which the knowledge is accepted.** The same knowledge was there in the world too; it was not accepted. Now it is accepted and it is of no use at all.

The everyday counterpart of this is very familiar: people generally admit they were wrong **afterwards**, and admitting it afterwards is mostly free of charge. Admitting it in time is costly — it loses one's position, requires stepping back, angers one's own side. Admitting it afterwards is only a sentence.

The sūra ties the value of a confession to **its timing**. The same sentence saves in one place and does nothing at all in another.

## 67/10-11 — وَقَالُوا۟ لَوْ كُنَّا نَسْمَعُ أَوْ نَعْقِلُ مَا كُنَّا فِىٓ أَصْحَٰبِ ٱلسَّعِيرِ ۝ فَٱعْتَرَفُوا۟ بِذَنۢبِهِمْ فَسُحْقًا لِّأَصْحَٰبِ ٱلسَّعِيرِ

*Wa qālū law kunnā nasmaʿu aw naʿqilu mā kunnā fī aṣḥābi'l-saʿīr — fa'ʿtarafū bi-dhanbihim fa-suḥqan li-aṣḥābi'l-saʿīr*
"And they say: 'Had we listened or used our reason, we would not be among the people of the blaze.' So they confess their sin. Away with the people of the blaze!"

The most quoted verse of the sūra, and rightly. It counts in a single sentence the two instruments a human being has for deliverance.

### نَسْمَعُ أَوْ نَعْقِلُ — two channels

First note the forms of the verbs: **imperfect** (*nasmaʿu*, *naʿqilu*), used together with *law kunnā*.

*Law kunnā nasmaʿu* — "had we been listening". That is, not "had we listened once" but **"had we been people who listen."** The form describes not a single event but a **state**. What was lacking is not an act but a capacity.

**سَمِعَ — root س-م-ع.** To hear. And the Qurʾān uses this word on two levels: the act of the ear, and **to accept**. *Samiʿnā wa aṭaʿnā* — "we heard and we obeyed" (al-Baqara 2/285). There hearing is the first step of acceptance.

English has this too: the sentence "you're not listening to me" has nothing to do with the ears.

**The *samʿ* in the verse is in this second sense.** The people in Hell were not deaf; they had heard the voice of the warner — in the preceding verse they say so themselves: *"a warner came to us."* They heard. **They did not listen.**

**عَقَلَ — root ع-ق-ل.** This root was treated in the discussion of al-Baqara (2/44); I do not repeat it here. The summary of the finding there: the meaning of the root is **to bind** — *ʿiqāl* is the rope tied round a camel's knee so that it does not run off. And Arabic has given the intellect this name: the intellect is not a store of knowledge but a **bond**; the thing that holds a person, that keeps him from being swept away.

The finding in al-Baqara was this: *you have knowledge, you read, you even teach — but if the knowledge does not bind you, you have not used your reason.*

**Al-Mulk 67/10 is the counterpart of that finding in the hereafter.** There was a human being there who knew but was not bound; here that human being sees the outcome and names what was lacking.

### أَوْ — "or": the finest letter in the verse

The verse does not say **وَ (and)**. It says **أَوْ (or)**.

The difference is great and is generally lost in translation:

- *"Had we listened **and** used our reason"* — **both together** would have been needed for deliverance.
- *"Had we listened **or** used our reason"* — **one** would have been enough for deliverance.

**The verse says the second. And this multiplies the weight of the claim.**

Because it means this: there were **two separate roads** to deliverance and these people used **neither**. Had one road been closed there would have been an excuse; that neither of two independent roads was used leaves no excuse.

**What the two roads are needs separating out, because the whole idea of the verse is here:**

| | **سمع — to hear** | **عقل — to use reason** |
|---|---|---|
| The source of knowledge | **From outside**: the report that comes, the messenger, revelation | **From inside**: one's own reasoning |
| What it requires | Receptivity, humility, listening | Effort, thinking, consistency |
| Its starting point | Someone else's saying it | One's own noticing it |
| Its name in the Qurʾān | Transmission, report | Contemplation, reflection |
| To whom it is open | Open even to one who has no learning | Open even to one whom no report has reached |

**The last row is especially important.** These two roads are each other's reserve: a person whom no report has reached has an intellect; a person who cannot manage to reason can be reached by a report. That both channels should be closed at once is possible only if **both are closed off**.

**And the sūra says who closed them:** they closed them themselves. A warner came (the channel of hearing was open); seven heavens were shown and they were asked to look (the channel of reason was open). Neither was used.

### An internal link: with 67/23

This verse connects directly with the twenty-third verse, and the link stands in the text:

> *"Say: It is He who created you and gave you **hearing**, **eyes** and **hearts**. How little you are grateful!"* (67/23)

**The two things whose lack is stated in the tenth verse are counted in the twenty-third verse as things that were given.**

| 67/10 (in Hell) | 67/23 (in the world) |
|---|---|
| *law kunnā **nasmaʿu*** | *wa jaʿala lakumu'l-**samʿ*** |
| *aw **naʿqilu*** | *wa'l-abṣāra wa'l-**afʾida*** |

That is, the instrument had been given. The sentence spoken in Hell is refuted by the inventory in the world: to the person who says "had we listened", it is said "hearing was given to you".

**I record this as my own reading,** but that the two verses stand in the same sūra with the same words is a datum of the text.

### فَٱعْتَرَفُوا۟ — confession

**Root: ع-ر-ف.** And this root is surprising: **to know, to recognise.** *Maʿrifa* — recognition; *maʿrūf* — the known, the good that custom recognises; *ʿurf* — what a society recognises.

*Iʿtirāf* is in Form VIII and adds reflexivity: **to recognise something against oneself.**

**That is, confession is not the giving of new information; it is the recognising of what is known.** The root of the word says this. The one who confesses is not teaching anything; he is **accepting** something already known.

And this explains the futility of the scene: the confession adds nothing. The keepers already knew; those confessing already knew. The only new thing that appears is **the acceptance itself**.

### فَسُحْقًا — away with them

**Root: س-ح-ق.** And both senses of the root are at work here:

1. **To crush, to grind to powder.** *Saḥaqa* — he pounded it in a mortar and reduced it to dust. The Turkish *sahk* (pounding) is from this root.
2. **To be far.** *Saḥīq* — very distant. The Qurʾān uses this: *"…like one whom the wind has flung to a far place"* (al-Ḥajj 22/31 — *makānin saḥīq*).

**That the two senses are found in the same root is no accident:** to crush a thing is to scatter it and put it far off. What has become dust does not hold together.

The *suḥqan* in the verse is a verbal noun in the accusative; in Arabic it is a pattern of imprecation — like *buʿdan* (be far), *tuḥqan*. The closest English is not "damn them" but **"away with them"**.

**The choice of the word is meaningful and it connects with the whole of the sūra.** Throughout the sūra the matter of **nearness** is treated: the nearest sky (5), the punishment seen brought near (27, *zulfa*). Here, by contrast, distance is stated as a punishment.

**And one more observation:** the expression is not *"let there be punishment for them"* but *"let them be far"*. The punishment is named not as a torture but as a **distance**.

### Why is the confession not accepted? — an honest question

The verse is heavy: people accept their mistakes and the answer is "away with them". At first sight this may seem merciless, and the question needs asking.

The answer has to do with what repentance is.

In the discussion of al-Baqara (2/37) repentance was treated. The record there is this: repentance is, by its root, **to turn** — that is, to pass from one direction to another. Turning is possible only while there is a road.

The confession in Hell is not a turning; it is a **surrender**. The person is not changing direction, because there is no direction left to change. To say the same sentence in the world would have been a choice; to say it here is a statement of fact.

**And there is one thing more, as my own reading:** the confession in the verse still contains an excuse. *"Had we listened or used our reason"* — note the grammar of this sentence. It is in the conditional, and the condition's not having been fulfilled is presented as an **impossibility**. The person does not say "I did not listen"; he says "had I listened" — as though listening had not depended on him.

The twenty-third verse cuts exactly this off: hearing **was given** to you.

### What this says to the present

**The pair "to hear or to use reason" names the two ways of acquiring knowledge: to receive from someone, and to think for oneself.**

And today both are corrupted in their own particular way.

**The corruption of hearing:** knowledge has never been so abundant and has never been so little listened to. A person meets hundreds of utterances in a day and passes most of them without hearing. The problem is not access; it is **receptivity**. To hear a thing is to allow it to change you; and when that permission is not given, hearing does not get beyond registering.

**The corruption of reasoning:** the outsourcing of thinking. To learn what one is to think about a subject without thinking about the subject is extremely easy today — ready-made opinions are plentiful and free. The difference between holding an opinion and using one's reason is exactly the difference recorded at al-Baqara 2/44: in one, knowledge is acquired; in the other, one is bound.

**The danger the verse points to is the closing of both at once** — and these two closings feed each other. The person who does not listen is left at the mercy of his own thought; the person who does not think surrenders to the last voice he heard.

The measure the sūra gives is plain: **for deliverance both together are not required; but one is required.**

## 67/12 — إِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ يَخْشَوْنَ رَبَّهُم بِٱلْغَيْبِ لَهُم مَّغْفِرَةٌ وَأَجْرٌ كَبِيرٌ

*Inna'lladhīna yakhshawna rabbahum bi'l-ghaybi lahum maghfiratun wa ajrun kabīr*
"As for those who fear their Lord unseen: for them there is forgiveness and a great reward."

The sūra's first and only counter-picture. After eleven verses of a scene of punishment, a single verse of good news.

### خَشِيَ — which kind of fear

In Arabic there are several words for fear and the Qurʾān uses them with distinctions:

| Word | What it describes |
|---|---|
| **خَوْف (*khawf*)** | General fear; the expectation of a harm. Its object is concrete |
| **خَشْيَة (*khashya*)** | **Fear joined with knowledge**; the drawing back felt because one has grasped the greatness |
| **رَهْبَة (*rahba*)** | Alarm, dread; fear that makes one flee |
| **وَجَل (*wajal*)** | The trembling of the heart |

The distinguishing side of *khashya* is that **it rests on knowledge.** The Qurʾān says this openly: *"Of His servants, only **those who know** feel khashya towards God"* (Fāṭir 35/28).

**This connects directly with the tenth verse of the sūra.** There what was lacking was hearing and reasoning — that is, the two channels of knowledge. Here what is praised is the fear that knowledge produces.

**That is, two verses apart the sūra has set this up, as my own reading:** the person without knowledge does not fear; the person with knowledge fears. Fear here is not the result of ignorance but the result of knowledge.

This turns round a widespread opinion about the notion of fear. A claim often used in the criticism of religion is this: fear is born of ignorance, and as knowledge increases fear decreases. The verse says the reverse — and the kind of fear it speaks of is different: not the fear that makes one flee, but the fear that **gives measure**.

### بِٱلْغَيْبِ — "with the unseen"

How this expression is to be understood is disputed and both readings are strong:

| Reading | Its meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Without seeing** | They fear Him although they do not see Him | *Ghayb* means "what cannot be reached by the senses"; at al-Baqara 2/3 *yuʾminūna bi'l-ghayb* is in the same sense |
| **In secret** | They fear even when nobody is watching | *Ghayb* here looks at the person's own condition: in the moments when he is out of sight |

**The two readings do not exclude each other and both come out at the same principle: to act without a supervising eye.**

On the first reading the supervisor is not seen; on the second the supervisor is supposed absent. In both the test is the same: **conduct towards what is unseen.**

**And this connects directly with the next verse of the sūra (13):** *"Whether you keep your speech secret or make it public, He knows the very substance of the breasts."* That is, the twelfth verse praises the one who fears in secret, and the thirteenth verse says that there is no such thing as secret.

**The link with al-Baqara 2/3.** There the first quality of those who guard themselves was *"faith in the unseen"*. Here the same expression is framed with *khashya*. Read together, the two verses bring out the two faces of the relation with the unseen: **to believe** and **to draw back**. One is acceptance, the other conduct.

### مَّغْفِرَةٌ وَأَجْرٌ كَبِيرٌ — the matter of the order

First **forgiveness**, then **reward**. The order could have been reversed.

**The logic of the order is this:** forgiveness looks at the past (there was something that needed covering), reward looks at the future (there is a return to be given). That is, first the account is cleared, then the prize is given.

This order agrees with the pair of names in the second verse: there *al-ʿAzīzu'l-Ghafūr* was said — power and covering. Here the same covering is mentioned by way of its outcome.

**And the adjective *kabīr* is striking:** in the ninth verse the words the deniers said to the messenger were *"ḍalālin **kabīr**"* — "great error". The same adjective is used here for the reward: *ajrun **kabīr***.

That the same word is used three verses apart, once for an accusation and once for a recompense, stands in the text. The meaning I draw from it is my own reading: the sūra is comparing who applies the adjective "great" to what.

## 67/13-14 — وَأَسِرُّوا۟ قَوْلَكُمْ أَوِ ٱجْهَرُوا۟ بِهِۦٓ ۖ إِنَّهُۥ عَلِيمٌۢ بِذَاتِ ٱلصُّدُورِ ۝ أَلَا يَعْلَمُ مَنْ خَلَقَ وَهُوَ ٱللَّطِيفُ ٱلْخَبِيرُ

*Wa asirrū qawlakum awi'jharū bih, innahu ʿalīmun bi-dhāti'l-ṣudūr — a-lā yaʿlamu man khalaqa wa huwa'l-laṭīfu'l-khabīr*
"Keep your speech secret or make it public; He knows the very substance of the breasts. Shall the one who created not know? He is the Subtle, the Aware."

### أَسِرُّوا۟… أَوِ ٱجْهَرُوا۟ — an indifference in the imperative

Two imperatives side by side: "keep secret" and "make public". But this is not a command.

**In Arabic, when the imperative offers two contrary things as options it ceases to be a command and gives the sense "it makes no difference".** English has the same structure: "do it or don't" is not a command but a declaration of indifference.

That is, the verse says: **whichever of the two you do, the outcome does not change.**

**And the particle *aw* (or) is at work here too — as it was three verses earlier.** In the tenth verse *aw* separated two roads of deliverance; here two ways of speaking. The sūra uses this particle twice, in two different functions.

**سَرَّ — root س-ر-ر.** To keep secret. From the same root come *sirr* (what is hidden), *sarīra* (what is kept within), and — according to an explanation the lexicographers transmit — *surūr* (joy): because joy is born within. The reservation entered in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya for the root-link between *sarīr* (throne) and *surūr* holds here too: these explanations are accounts transmitted by the lexicographers and carry no claim of certainty.

**جَهَرَ — root ج-ه-ر.** To bring out into the open, to raise the voice. *Jahr* — reciting aloud in prayer; *jahran* — openly. In the concrete sense of the root there is **making visible or audible**.

### بِذَاتِ ٱلصُّدُورِ — "the substance of the breasts"

This construct is a repeated pattern in the Qurʾān and is hard to translate exactly.

The word **ذَات** means "the possessor of, the very thing"; it is feminine. *Dhātu'l-ṣudūr* — "that which the breasts possess", that is, **what is inside the breasts**.

But the word does not say "what is inside" (*mā fi'l-ṣudūr*). **ذات** is deeper: not what is found inside a thing but what **is one with it**. The Turkish word *zat* still carries this sense.

**That is, the verse says that He knows not the thoughts that are in the breast but the breast's own state.** Not an item inside, but the thing that makes the inside what it is.

**A point of grammar:** the verse says *"keep your speech secret"*, but its answer is *"He knows the substance of the breasts"*. That is, the subject starts from speech and shifts to **what is not speech**. Even the furthest degree of concealment — never saying anything at all — does not fall outside the scope.

### أَلَا يَعْلَمُ مَنْ خَلَقَ — a sentence with two readings

This sentence carries an ambiguity fit for the textbooks of Arabic grammar, and the ambiguity is meaningful.

The expression **مَنْ خَلَقَ** can be read in two ways:

| Reading | The role of *man* | Its meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **First** | Subject | "Shall **the one who created** not know?" — shall the creator not know what he created? |
| **Second** | Object | "Shall He not know **what He created**?" — shall He not know the thing He created? |

**Both readings are mentioned in the classical sources and both come out at the same conclusion.** The difference is where the stress falls: the first makes **being the creator** the proof, the second makes **being created** the proof.

**I adopt no preference;** the sentence's being framed so as to carry both readings at once may also be assessed not as a deficiency but as a density.

**The logic of the proof is the same on both readings and is extremely simple:** the maker knows what he has made. The person who made a device knows what is inside it; the user may not. The verse takes this everyday principle and applies it to the breasts.

**And this has a side-consequence, as my own reading:** a human being's not being able to know his own inside fully is, in the logic of the verse, not a defect but something to be expected. The human being did not make himself; that he should not fully know the contents of what he did not make is natural. That the effort of self-knowledge is never completed is, looked at from here, not a failure but a matter of position.

### ٱللَّطِيف — a name carrying two senses at once

**Root: ل-ط-ف.** And beneath this root lie two clusters of meaning; no single English word carries both.

**The first cluster — fineness, smallness, penetration:**
*Laṭīf* is what is materially **fine, rare, too small to be seen by the eye**. Air's being *laṭīf* is the opposite of water's being *kathīf* (dense). And fineness makes penetration possible: what is fine passes through a narrow place.

**The second cluster — gentleness, kindness, favour:**
*Luṭf* — courtesy, delicacy, treating someone gently. The Turkish *lütuf* (favour) and *latif* (gracious) are in this sense.

**That the two clusters are found in the same root is no accident, and there is a widespread explanation among the lexicographers:** a favour is **a kindness done without being noticed.** A coarse kindness is visible and puts the recipient under obligation; a fine kindness is invisible. That is, the second sense is the first carried over into the field of ethics.

**And the use in the verse requires both senses at once:**

- **The one who penetrates:** knowledge that reaches the substance of the breasts has reached a place only the finest could reach.
- **The gracious one:** and this reaching is presented not as a threat but as a nearness.

**This duality sets the tone of the verse.** Had the same sentence been read only with the first sense it would have been a notice of surveillance: "He knows everything about you." When the second sense is added it becomes something else: "the one who knows everything about you is gentle towards you."

Its occurrences in the Qurʾān confirm this duality:

- *"Eyes do not grasp Him, but He grasps the eyes. **He is the Subtle, the Aware**"* (al-Anʿām 6/103) — **exactly the same pair of names as al-Mulk 67/14**, and there too the context is the limit of seeing and grasping.
- *"Truly my Lord is **subtle** in what He wills"* (Yūsuf 12/100) — the words of Joseph at the end of a plan completed after forty years. Here *luṭf* plainly means "a design working unnoticed".

**The link with al-Anʿām 6/103 is especially important for Sūrat al-Mulk.** There it is said that eyes cannot grasp Him; here, four verses earlier, the eyes had come back exhausted. **The same pair of names stands, in both places, right beside the limit of seeing.** I record this as an observation.

### ٱلْخَبِير — the one who knows by trying

**Root: خ-ب-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root is instructive: *khabara'l-arḍa* — **he ploughed the land, he turned it over.** *Khabr* — ploughed soil; *khibra* — knowledge acquired by trying a thing.

That is, *khabīr* is the one who knows not by looking from a distance but **by entering into**. The Turkish word *haberdar* ("informed") comes from the root's second sense (report); but the proper sense is deeper.

**What the two names give together:**

| Name | The quality of the knowledge |
|---|---|
| **اللطيف** | Knowledge that **reaches** the finest place |
| **الخبير** | Knowledge that **knows by trying** the place it reaches |

One states the access, the other the depth. To reach a place and to know that place are separate things; the verse sets the two side by side.

### What this says to the present

**The matter of hidden speech is the most everyday side of this verse, and today it has taken on a particular character.**

A significant part of human life passes on two levels: what is said and what is not said. What is spoken in a meeting and what is spoken after it; what is said to a person and what is said about him; what is written and what is deleted.

The verse makes these two levels **one level**. And it does so not with a threat but with a notice of fact: concealment is technically not possible.

**Its moral consequence is this, as my own reading:** consistency is presented here not as a virtue but as **an accord with reality**. The person who acts with two faces is mistaken not because he is bad but because he is relying on something that does not exist (secrecy).

And the pair of names *al-Laṭīfu'l-Khabīr* takes this knowledge out of being a fear of surveillance. The end of the verse is not "He sees everything, be careful" but "He is subtle and knows from within". The difference between them is two different ways of living the same reality.

## 67/15 — هُوَ ٱلَّذِى جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلْأَرْضَ ذَلُولًا فَٱمْشُوا۟ فِى مَنَاكِبِهَا وَكُلُوا۟ مِن رِّزْقِهِۦ ۖ وَإِلَيْهِ ٱلنُّشُورُ

*Huwa'lladhī jaʿala lakumu'l-arḍa dhalūlan fa'mshū fī manākibihā wa kulū min rizqih, wa ilayhi'l-nushūr*
"It is He who made the earth submissive for you. So walk upon its shoulders and eat of His provision. And to Him is the rising."

### ذَلُولًا — a mount broken in

**Root: ذ-ل-ل.** And beneath this root lie two neighbouring senses; they need separating.

- **ذُلّ (*dhull*)** — humiliation, being despised. Negative.
- **ذِلّ (*dhill*)** — softness, tractability, docility. Not negative.

**And *dhalūl* comes from the second.** The concrete equivalent of the word is **an animal broken in to be ridden**: a camel or a horse that comes to the halter, that can be loaded, that does not turn skittish. The wild one is *ṣaʿb*; *dhalūl* is the one that has been tamed.

**The Qurʾān uses this word very clearly in one other place, and that is Sūrat al-Baqara:**

> *"…a cow not broken in (*lā **dhalūlun** tuthīru'l-arḍa*), that does not plough the soil or water the field…"* (al-Baqara 2/71)

In the discussion of al-Baqara, when the story of the cow was treated, this description came up: the animal sought was **not** *dhalūl* — that is, it had never been worked, had never known the yoke.

**Now, setting the two verses side by side, the meaning of the word becomes entirely clear:**

| | **al-Baqara 2/71** | **al-Mulk 67/15** |
|---|---|---|
| Of what | A cow | The earth |
| How | *lā dhalūl* — not broken in to the yoke | *dhalūl* — made submissive |
| Its outcome | Unused, untouched | Ready for use |

**That is, the earth is described, in the Qurʾān's own word, like "an animal put to work".**

### The three words by which the earth is qualified

In the discussion of al-Baqara 2/22, *firāsh* (bedding) was treated and this reservation was entered: this expression does not say that the earth is **flat**; what the word says is not shape but function — the earth has been made a place that can be stood on and lived on. And it was noted there that the Qurʾān mentions the same function with other words too: *mihād* (a cradle, al-Nabaʾ 78/6) and *dhalūl* (al-Mulk 67/15).

**Now, resting on that record, we can compare the three:**

| Word | Place | What it is likened to | What comes to the fore |
|---|---|---|---|
| **فراش** | al-Baqara 2/22 | Bedding, a spread | Being able to stand on it, comfort |
| **مهاد** | al-Nabaʾ 78/6 | A cradle | Protection, being carried, being raised |
| **ذلول** | al-Mulk 67/15 | A tamed mount | **Being able to be steered, submission** |

**The third says something different from the first two, and the difference is important.** Bedding and a cradle are **passive**: they are spread out, one lies in them. A mount is **active**: it walks, it carries, but it has to be driven.

The word *dhalūl* sets the earth up as something in motion — not a floor to stand on but an animal to be mounted.

**And here the warning from the discussion of al-Baqara 2/22 must be repeated:** no conclusion about the shape of the earth is to be drawn from these words. Neither "it is flat" nor "it is round" has been said; **"it is tamed"** has been said. The question the verse answers is not a question of shape but a question of relation.

**And I do not go into a forced reading in the other direction either:** to draw from the word *dhalūl* a proof of the earth's motion (its rotation) is the other face of the same mistake. The word is a mount; a mount's moving is part of the likeness, not an announcement about astronomy.

### فَٱمْشُوا۟ — walk

An imperative. And this is the third command in the sūra — after the "look" commands in the third and fourth verses. The first two were given to the eye; this one to the foot.

**مَشَى — root م-ش-ي.** To walk. Beneath the word lies **going on foot, step by step**; not running (*saʿā*, *jarā*) or travelling (*sāra*). Everyday, ordinary, slow movement.

**And this verb will come once more in the sūra — in the twenty-second verse, twice:** *"Is one who **walks** fallen on his face better guided, or one who **walks** upright on a straight path?"*

**The link between the two verses stands in the text and is worth recording:** the fifteenth verse commands walking, the twenty-second verse asks how one is to walk. The command and the measure, seven verses apart.

### مَنَاكِب — shoulders

**Root: ن-ك-ب.** And the root is interesting: **to swerve to one side, to incline sideways.** *Nakaba ʿani'l-ṭarīq* — he swerved from the road; *nakbāʾ* — the wind that blows from between two cardinal directions. The Turkish *nekbet* (misfortune) is from this root too: the swerving of the road.

**مَنْكِب (*mankib*)** — shoulder. The shoulder is given this name because it is **the side of the trunk**: not the centre but the edge.

*Manākib* is a plural, and in the verse the "shoulders" of the earth are spoken of. What does this mean?

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Mountains, high places** | The shoulder is the high and hard part of the body; the shoulders of the earth are likewise its high and rugged parts |
| **Its roads, its passes** | From the root's sense of "side": the earth's sides, edges, shores |
| **Every part of it** | The expression means "in every part of it"; shoulder is here a comprehensive figure |

**All three views are found in the classical sources and they do not exclude one another.** I state my preference: within the wholeness of the likeness, **the first and the second** seem to work together — the shoulders of a riding animal are the highest and firmest place one stands on. But this is not binding.

**And the likeness as a whole is this, as my own reading:** the earth is a tamed animal and the human being walks not on its back but on its **shoulders**. That is, on it but not at its centre; carried but not steering.

**One more observation:** the proper sense of the root is "to swerve", and in the verse the human being is sent to walk in exactly the places where one may swerve. This tension inside the word — shoulders both carry and open out to the sides — seems to prepare the ground for the question of the "straight path" in the twenty-second verse. I write this as an inference, not as a classical transmitted report.

### وَكُلُوا۟ مِن رِّزْقِهِۦ — "of His provision"

The second command: eat. And the construct calls for attention: **"of His provision"** — the pronoun refers not to the earth but to God.

**The logic of the sentence is this:** it is you who walk, the earth is beneath you, it is you who bring out the food — but the provision is **His.**

**رِزْق — root ر-ز-ق.** The share given, one's portion. The distinguishing side of the root is this: *rizq* is **not what is earned but what is given**. Arabic uses the word *kasb* for earning; *rizq* is something else.

The difference between the two will turn into a question in the twenty-first verse of the sūra: *"If He were to withhold His provision, who is there to provide for you?"* That is, the question of where provision comes from is here a statement and there a challenge.

### وَإِلَيْهِ ٱلنُّشُورُ — the rising

**Root: ن-ش-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root is **to spread out, to lay out, to open**. *Nashara'l-thawb* — he spread out the cloth. *Nashara'l-kitāb* — he opened the book or the page. The Turkish *neşretmek* (to publish) is from the same root: to open out a text and spread it.

**Nushūr**, from this root, means "the resurrection". And the likeness is plain: death is a **folding up**, resurrection an **opening out**. The spreading again of a folded cloth.

**Now set the three parts of the verse side by side:**

1. **The earth was made submissive for you** — the ground was made ready.
2. **Walk and eat** — movement and provision.
3. **To Him is the rising** — and the return.

**The structure of the sentence is the structure of a day, indeed of a lifetime:** get up, walk, eat, return.

**And there is a fine point of vocabulary, which I record as my own reading:** *nashr* (spreading) and *mashy* (walking) stand in the same scene. The human being is **spread** over the earth (scattering by walking), then **spread** again (opened out by rising). And in between there is a gathering: in the twenty-fourth verse *tuḥsharūn* — you will be gathered. Scatter, gather, open.

### What this says to the present

**The command "walk" is worth dwelling on,** because it is the kind of command the Qurʾān uses in very few places: turned towards the world, turned towards production, conveying movement.

That this command is not a command of worship is striking. The verse does not say "the earth has been given to you, worship"; it says **"walk and eat"**. To work on the earth, to move about, to seek a living — these are mentioned not as a field outside religious life but inside the same sentence.

**And the end of the sentence bounds this field:** *wa ilayhi'l-nushūr.* Walking and eating are framed by the sentence of return.

This threefold structure — means, use, reckoning — is a summary of the relation the Qurʾān sets up with the world. The means are not refused, the use is not blocked; only a frame is placed.

**A second aspect: the present-day counterpart of the word *dhalūl*.**

The earth's being "tamed" is a notice of readiness: this planet **has been made** usable. And within the likeness of the domestic animal there is also a warning, because the relation with a domestic animal is reciprocal: whoever treats the animal badly loses his mount.

The verse does not say this, and I do not make the verse say something it does not say. But the word itself sets up a relation, and relations are not one-sided. I leave this as a direction for thought.

## 67/16-18 — ءَأَمِنتُم مَّن فِى ٱلسَّمَآءِ أَن يَخْسِفَ بِكُمُ ٱلْأَرْضَ فَإِذَا هِىَ تَمُورُ ۝ أَمْ أَمِنتُم مَّن فِى ٱلسَّمَآءِ أَن يُرْسِلَ عَلَيْكُمْ حَاصِبًا ۖ فَسَتَعْلَمُونَ كَيْفَ نَذِيرِ ۝ وَلَقَدْ كَذَّبَ ٱلَّذِينَ مِن قَبْلِهِمْ فَكَيْفَ كَانَ نَكِيرِ

*A-amintum man fi'l-samāʾi an yakhsifa bikumu'l-arḍa fa-idhā hiya tamūr — am amintum man fi'l-samāʾi an yursila ʿalaykum ḥāṣiban fa-sa-taʿlamūna kayfa nadhīr — wa laqad kadhdhaba'lladhīna min qablihim fa-kayfa kāna nakīr*
"Have you become secure from the One in the sky, that He should make the earth swallow you, so that it suddenly heaves? Or have you become secure from the One in the sky, that He should send against you a wind hurling stones? You shall soon know how My warning is. Those before them cried lies too; and how was My rejection!"

### ءَأَمِنتُم — "have you become secure?"

**Root: أ-م-ن.** To be safe, to be without fear. And from this root comes *īmān* — the central notion of the Qurʾān.

**This duality of the root is at work here.** *Amn* is safety; *īmān* is giving trust or trusting. And the verse questions **a sense of security placed in the wrong place**.

That is, the question is not "do you know that you are in danger?" The question is: **"on what are you relying that you are at ease?"**

**This touches a very basic side of human psychology.** The continuity of everyday life produces a feeling of safety: the ground is fixed, the sky stays calm, morning comes. This feeling does not rest on evidence; it rests on **repetition**. What has happened a thousand times is assumed to happen the thousand-and-first time.

The verse takes aim at this assumption. And in doing so it does not announce a disaster — it only asks **what the security rests on**.

### مَّن فِى ٱلسَّمَآءِ — an ambiguous expression

How this expression is to be understood is the same question as the *"in His hand"* of the first verse, and I handle it by the same method.

**The classical explanations:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **"Man" = God; "fi'l-samāʾ" conveys no place** | In Arabic the expression "the one in the sky" is an idiom used for loftiness and transcendence. Place is not ascribed to God; the expression is speaking in the language of the one addressed |
| **"Man" = the appointed angel** | The angel charged with bringing down the punishment may be meant; to say "in the sky" of angels is a statement of place and raises no difficulty |
| **Tafwīḍ** | The wording is left as it stands and its modality is consigned to God |

**I take no side.** All three positions are represented in the tradition and all three agree that God is beyond place.

I content myself with recording a linguistic datum: the Qurʾān itself says openly in other verses that God is present everywhere by His knowledge. This is the ground of the first view. But there is also a method that prefers not to alter the wording of the revealed text by interpretation, and that too is consistent within itself.

### يَخْسِفَ — to make the earth swallow

**Root: خ-س-ف.** A thing's **caving in, sinking into the ground, collapsing inwards.** *Khusūf* — the eclipse of the moon; the word's aspect of "disappearing, going out" comes from here.

**Its best-known use in the Qurʾān is in the story of Korah:** *"So We made the earth swallow him and his house"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/81 — *fa-khasafnā bihi wa bi-dārihi'l-arḍa*).

**And now see the link within the sūra.** In the preceding verse (15) the earth was *dhalūl* — a tamed, submissive mount. In this verse the same earth **caves in and heaves.**

**تَمُورُ — root م-و-ر.** To surge, to sway, to go back and forth. The concrete sense of the root is the **back-and-forth movement** of a liquid or a cloud of dust.

**The contrast of the two verses stands in the text:**

| Verse | The state of the earth |
|---|---|
| 15 | *dhalūl* — a docile mount, walked upon |
| 16 | *tamūr* — heaving, caving in |

**And the likeness completes itself, as my own reading:** a domestic animal is docile — but its docility comes not from itself but **from its having been broken in**. When the one who broke it in wishes, the same animal turns skittish. The verse says that the fixity of the earth is not a property owned but a **permission**.

The strength of this reading lies in the two verses coming one after the other. The fifteenth verse recalls the means, the sixteenth the source of the means.

### حَاصِبًا — a wind hurling stones

**Root: ح-ص-ب.** *Ḥaṣbāʾ* — gravel, small stones. *Ḥāṣib* — a thing that flings gravel; a violent wind carrying stones.

**Its uses in the Qurʾān:**

- *"We sent against them a wind hurling stones"* (al-Qamar 54/34) — concerning the people of Lot (Lūṭ).
- *"…or have you become secure from His sending against you a wind hurling stones?"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/68).

**The directions of the two threats are opposite and this is deliberate:**

| Verse | Threat | Its direction |
|---|---|---|
| 16 | *yakhsifa bikumu'l-arḍ* | **From below** — the earth caves in |
| 17 | *yursila ʿalaykum ḥāṣibā* | **From above** — stones from the sky |

**The place where the human being stands is between these two directions.** By closing both the verse leaves no space to escape into. And note: both are **ordinary** disasters — earthquake and storm. No extraordinary punishment is described; things that can always happen are enumerated.

**This is part of the sūra's method:** the proofs and the threats alike are chosen from the everyday. Birds, walking, provision, water — and earthquake, storm.

### كَيْفَ نَذِيرِ / كَيْفَ نَكِيرِ — the dropped yāʾ

The words at the end of the two verses call for attention: **نَذِيرِ** and **نَكِيرِ**. Normally they would be *nadhīrī* and *nakīrī* — the **yāʾ** at the end is the first-person pronoun meaning "my".

**The yāʾ has dropped and a kasra has been left in its place.** This is a known phenomenon in the Qurʾān in the regions of the verse-endings: so that the sounds of the ends of verses may agree, the letter of the pronoun is dropped and its vowel remains.

**Its effect is this:** the words *nadhīr* and *nakīr* fit the sūra's twenty-one verses of *rāʾ* rhyme. Had the pronoun been written, the sound would have broken.

I record this as an observation about form. But there is a semantic effect too: the dropping of the pronoun leaves the word for a moment **without an owner** — it comes close to a general expression like "how the warning is", and then its owner is understood from the context.

**نَكِير — root ن-ك-ر.** Not to recognise, to reject, to find strange. *Munkar* — the unrecognised, the disapproved (and the opposite of *maʿrūf*; the root of *maʿrūf* occurred in the eleventh verse by way of *iʿtirāf*). *Nakīr* — denial, rejection, and from there "punishment".

**And there is a symmetry:** they cried lies (*kadhdhaba*), and God's *nakīr* came. That is, the one who rejects is met with a rejection. The choice of the word carries this.

**And a deeper link:** in the eleventh verse there was *iʿtirāf* (recognising), in the eighteenth verse *nakīr* (not recognising). Both are the two ends of the same field of meaning — *maʿrifa* and *nukr*. The sūra sets up a movement along the axis of recognising and not recognising. **I record this as my own reading.**

### What this says to the present

**The source of the feeling of security** is worth thinking about.

Modern life is built on making risk calculable: insurance, backup, forecasting, early warning. These are good things and are not what the verse objects to — the verse questions not precaution but **the illusion precaution produces**.

The illusion is this: when risk can be calculated, it is supposed to be controlled. But to calculate is not to control; knowing the probability of an earthquake does not prevent the earthquake.

**The question the verse asks brings this difference to light: "have you become secure?"** Security is not the absence of danger but the presence of a guarantee against danger. And the verse asks whom the guarantee comes from.

**A second aspect: the ordinariness of the two threats.**

The verse describes neither an army coming down from the sky nor an unheard-of catastrophe. Earthquake and storm — the two most familiar disasters in human history. That is, what is said is not "something extraordinary may happen" but **"what is ordinary may happen at any moment."**

This takes the threat out of being abstract. For a person living on a fault line the verse is not a figure of speech.

## 67/19 — أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا۟ إِلَى ٱلطَّيْرِ فَوْقَهُمْ صَٰٓفَّٰتٍ وَيَقْبِضْنَ ۚ مَا يُمْسِكُهُنَّ إِلَّا ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ ۚ إِنَّهُۥ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍۭ بَصِيرٌ

*A-wa-lam yaraw ila'l-ṭayri fawqahum ṣāffātin wa yaqbiḍn, mā yumsikuhunna illa'l-raḥmān, innahu bi-kulli shayʾin baṣīr*
"Have they not looked at the birds above them, spreading their wings and closing them? None holds them up but the Merciful. He sees everything."

### أَوَلَمْ يَرَوْا۟ — "have they not seen?"

In the discussion of al-Ghāshiya the patterns *hal atā* and *a-lam tara* were compared. The finding there: *a-lam tara* **obliges the one addressed to accept** the event; the only thing opened to discussion is its detail.

Here the pattern is plural and in the third person: *a-wa-lam yaraw* — "have they not seen?" That is, the one addressed is not spoken to directly; he is mentioned as a third person. This is a distance repeated in this part of the sūra.

**And the verb is *raʾā* (to see), not *naẓara* (to look).** In al-Ghāshiya *naẓara* was used and the finding there was: the flaw is not failing to see but failing to look. Here *raʾā* is used and comes with the preposition **ilā**: *yaraw **ilā**.* In Arabic this construction adds the sense "to look towards a thing, to fix one's eye on it" — that is, not mere seeing but **turning towards**.

### ٱلطَّيْر — the birds

**Root: ط-ي-ر.** To fly. *Ṭayr* is a collective noun: flying things, the bird kind.

The Qurʾān frames this verse in almost the same way in another place:

> *"Have they not seen the birds made subject in the open sky? None holds them up but God."* (al-Naḥl 16/79)

**Comparing the two verses is instructive:**

| | **al-Naḥl 16/79** | **al-Mulk 67/19** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | *fī jawwi'l-samāʾ* — in the open of the sky | *fawqahum* — **above them** |
| How | *musakhkharāt* — made subject | *ṣāffātin wa yaqbiḍn* — **spreading and closing** |
| Who holds them | *illa'llāh* | *illa'l-raḥmān* |

**Two differences stand out.** The first is distance: al-Naḥl says "the open of the sky", al-Mulk says **"above them"** — that is, near, visible, there when you raise your head. The second is detail: al-Naḥl gives the bird's state with a general word (made subject), al-Mulk describes **the movement of the wing**.

Sūrat al-Mulk gives the same proof more closely and in more detail. This agrees with the sūra's general method: what is abstract is brought down to the concrete.

### صَٰٓفَّٰتٍ وَيَقْبِضْنَ — a point of grammar

**This is the most striking place in the verse and a place the classical exegetes dwelt on.**

Two words, in two different grammatical categories:

- **صَافَّات** — an **active participle**, plural. "Those spreading their wings" — it conveys a **state**.
- **يَقْبِضْنَ** — a **verb**, imperfect. "They close" — it conveys an **action**.

**In Arabic the active participle conveys continuity and fixity, while the verb conveys renewal and intermittent repetition.** This is a known distinction of grammar.

**And the verse has applied this distinction to the flight of the bird:**

| | **صافات** (active participle) | **يقبضن** (verb) |
|---|---|---|
| What | The wing's standing open | The wing's closing |
| What the grammar conveys | Continuity, the base state | Intermittent, repeated movement |

That is, the wing's **being open** is given as the base state, and its **closing** as the movement that intervenes.

**This description fits the real structure of a bird's flight.** In birds flying long distances gliding (wing open) is the dominant state; the beat is intermittent and costs energy. Keeping the wing open is the default, closing it is the intervention.

**Here one must be careful, and per the method I enter the reservation:** I do not present this as a scientific miracle. The flight of birds was something observable in the seventh century as well; no unknown information is being given in the verse. What is said is plainer and stronger: **the categories the grammar has chosen fit the structure of what is observed.** This is a fineness of language, not a claim of discovery.

**And the conjunction *wāw* itself carries a fine point:** between *ṣāffātin* and *yaqbiḍn* there is a **wāw**, that is, the two states are **joined to each other** yet not placed in the same category. The sentence is grammatically a little rough — a noun is joined to a verb. The classical grammarians explain this by saying "the verbal clause is in the position of a circumstantial accusative". The roughness produces the intended meaning itself.

### مَا يُمْسِكُهُنَّ إِلَّا ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ — "to hold"

**أَمْسَكَ — root م-س-ك.** To hold, to keep in hand, not to let go. *Misk* is not from the same root; but *imsāk* (holding, abstaining from food and drink in fasting) is.

**The choice of verb is striking.** The verse does not say "the one who lifts them", "who makes them fly", "who drives them in the air". It says **"the one who holds them"**.

The difference is this: to lift is to apply a force; to hold is **to prevent a fall**. That is, the bird's flying is told not as an achievement but its not falling as a **being held**.

**This changes the outlook of the verse, as my own reading:** the ordinary state is falling; flying is the exception. And the exception continues thanks to a holding.

The same verb is used in the Qurʾān for the standing of the heavens and the earth as well — the continuance of the universe is told as a "holding". The example of the bird is a visible instance of that great holding.

**And *illa'l-raḥmān*:** this name again. In the third verse the owner of the creating, here the owner of the holding. The sūra insistently mentions the disputed name together with acts.

### إِنَّهُۥ بِكُلِّ شَىْءٍۭ بَصِيرٌ — the owner of the gaze

**And the sūra's chain of looking is completed here.**

In the third and fourth verses the human being's gaze was sent out and came back exhausted. In the nineteenth verse people are again told "have they not seen?" And at the end of the verse the real seer is mentioned: **baṣīr.**

That is, after showing the limit of the human being's gaze, the sūra names the gaze that has no limit. The same root (*b-ṣ-r*), with two different subjects.

**And there is this too:** the verse does not say "He knows everything" (*ʿalīm*), it says **"He sees everything"** (*baṣīr*). Because the subject is seeing, the attribute of seeing was chosen. This is a typical example of the Qurʾān's verse-endings being chosen in accord with the context.

### What this says to the present

**The finding recorded in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya holds here too:** what is seen most becomes what is looked at least. There the example was the camel; here it is the bird.

And the bird is even more familiar than the camel. Even someone living in a city sees dozens of birds a day and looks at none of them.

**What the verse does is to turn something not looked at into a proof.** And the strength of the proof lies precisely in its ordinariness: had an extraordinary event been the proof, it would have been unrepeatable. The bird flies every day.

**A second aspect: the idea of "being held".**

The verse tells flying not as an achievement but as a being held. This outlook can be applied to a person's own life as well — and when it is applied it is uncomfortable.

A person's staying on his feet, his health, his work going on, his relationships continuing: all of these are generally told in the language of **success**. The language of the verse is different: these are a state of **not falling**, and not falling requires a holding.

The difference between the two languages changes how a person looks at himself. In one there is a position won; in the other a permission being maintained.

## 67/20-21 — أَمَّنْ هَٰذَا ٱلَّذِى هُوَ جُندٌ لَّكُمْ يَنصُرُكُم مِّن دُونِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ ۚ إِنِ ٱلْكَٰفِرُونَ إِلَّا فِى غُرُورٍ ۝ أَمَّنْ هَٰذَا ٱلَّذِى يَرْزُقُكُمْ إِنْ أَمْسَكَ رِزْقَهُۥ ۚ بَل لَّجُّوا۟ فِى عُتُوٍّ وَنُفُورٍ

*Amman hādha'lladhī huwa jundun lakum yanṣurukum min dūni'l-raḥmān, ini'l-kāfirūna illā fī ghurūr — amman hādha'lladhī yarzuqukum in amsaka rizqah, bal lajjū fī ʿutuwwin wa nufūr*
"Or who is this army of yours that is to help you apart from the Merciful? The deniers are in nothing but delusion. Or who is this that is to provide for you, if He withholds His provision? No — they persist in hardness and shying away."

### أَمَّنْ هَٰذَا ٱلَّذِى — the pattern itself

The two verses begin with the same pattern: **أَمَّنْ هَٰذَا ٱلَّذِى** — "or who is this?"

The pattern consists of three parts and each part has a function:

- **أَمْ** — "or"; it sets a second possibility against the preceding one.
- **مَنْ** — "who"; the question.
- **هَٰذَا ٱلَّذِى** — "this that"; **a demonstrative.**

**The third is the real fine point.** *Hādhā* (this) is a demonstrative, and pointing requires that there be something there to point at. The verse points at something that is not there: **"this army of yours"** — where is it, then?

**This is a pattern of mocking interrogation in Arabic.** The closest English is the expression "and where is this friend of yours?" — the speaker knows there is nobody there.

And the two verses' being framed with the same pattern turns them into a single question: **protection and feeding.** The two basic things a master provides for his slave, a patron for his dependant, a state for its citizen. The verse asks whom these two things come from.

### جُندٌ لَّكُمْ — "your army"

**جُنْد — root ج-ن-د.** A gathered military force, an army, a troop. Its plural is *junūd*.

**And its coming in the singular is striking:** *jundun* — an army. Not "your armies".

One more point of grammar: although *jund* is a collective noun, it is pointed at with *hādhā* (this, singular) and then *yanṣurukum* (he helps you — a singular verb) follows. That is, the army has been set up as a single entity.

**نَصَرَ — root ن-ص-ر.** To help — but in Arabic this word describes **help that comes to someone in a tight place**. *Naṣr* is support against an enemy. The English "help" is broader; *naṣr* is narrower and carries the smell of war.

**مِّن دُونِ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنِ — "apart from the Merciful"**

The word *dūn* in Arabic means both "below" and "apart from, short of". *Min dūni'llāh* is one of the constructions the Qurʾān uses most often, and it describes associating partners with God: those held **short of** God.

And again **al-Raḥmān.** The third use.

### إِنِ ٱلْكَافِرُونَ إِلَّا فِى غُرُورٍ — delusion

The pattern **إِنْ… إِلَّا** conveys **restriction** in Arabic: "only and only". That is, not "they are in delusion" but **"they are in nothing but delusion."**

**غُرُور — root غ-ر-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root is interesting: *ghirr* — **inexperienced, raw, easily taken in.** *Ghurra* — the most visible, brightest face of a thing; the white blaze on a horse's forehead. *Gharra* — he deceived; he showed the bright face and hid the inside.

**That is, in this Arabic root being deceived is a matter of appearance:** the deceiver does not lie; **he only shows the bright face.**

In the discussion of al-Infiṭār this root was taken up in the verse *"mā gharraka bi-rabbika'l-karīm"* (what deceived you about your generous Lord?). The use here is on the same line: nobody told them "you have a protector"; they looked at the bright face and supposed it themselves.

### إِنْ أَمْسَكَ رِزْقَهُۥ — the other face of holding

**And now one of the finest internal links of the sūra.**

In the preceding verse (19) the one who **holds** the birds in the air was mentioned: *mā yumsikuhunna illa'l-raḥmān.* The verb: **أَمْسَكَ.**

In this verse **the same verb** comes: *in **amsaka** rizqah* — "if He withholds His provision".

| Verse | Verb | What is held | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 19 | *yumsiku* | The birds | **They do not fall** |
| 21 | *amsaka* | The provision | **Nobody can give** |

**The same verb, two verses apart, with two opposite results.** Holding is in one place holding up, in another withholding. Both are the work of the same hand.

**I record this as a datum of the text; the contrast I set up between them is my own reading.** But the two verses' being so close and the verb's being the same is striking.

**And the order between the two questions is meaningful too:** first the army (protection), then the provision (feeding). The possibility of an army requires an enemy; the possibility of provision does not. That is, the second question is nearer and more unavoidable. The sūra comes down from threat to need — and in the thirtieth verse this descent will reach water.

### بَل لَّجُّوا۟ — persisting

**Root: ل-ج-ج.** And the concrete sense of the root is depth: **لُجَّة (*lujja*)** — the deep part of the sea, the open water. *Lujjiyy* — belonging to the deep sea. It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"like darknesses in a deep sea"* (this expression is used around al-Nūr 24/40).

**And the verb *lajja*: to plunge into a matter and persist in it, not to come out.**

The picture of the word is this: someone who has gone into deep water. He has moved away from the shore; to go back requires swimming, and to go on requires swimming too. And he chooses to go on.

**The English "obstinacy" does not quite give this.** Obstinacy is an attitude; *lajāj* is a **position** — a place entered and stayed in.

The verse uses the preposition *fī* (in): *lajjū **fī** ʿutuwwin wa nufūr.* That is, hardness and shying away are described as a place entered into. The root's sense of "depth", joined with the preposition, fits exactly.

### عُتُوّ — overstepping

**Root: ع-ت-و.** To overstep the limit, to run wild, to be arrogant.

**And the concrete sense of the root is instructive:** *ʿatā al-ʿūdu* — the branch dried out, hardened, and no longer bends. A green branch flexes; a dried branch does not flex, it only breaks.

**That is, *ʿutuww* is having lost one's flexibility.** Running wild is described here not as an excess but as a **hardening**.

In the discussion of al-Fajr the root *ṭughyān* was treated: there running wild was described as an **overflowing** (water going out of its vessel). **The two words give the same phenomenon in two different pictures:**

| Word | Its concrete picture | The quality of the wildness |
|---|---|---|
| **طغى** | Water overflowing its vessel | **Excess** — going outside the limit |
| **عتا** | A branch drying and hardening | **Rigidity** — not bending |

One is an excess of movement, the other a deficiency of movement. The Qurʾān describes running wild from two different directions and the two do not contradict: what has overflowed does not return to the vessel, the dried branch no longer bends. **The common outcome: irreversibility.**

### نُفُور — shying and bolting

**Root: ن-ف-ر.** And the proper place of the root is animals: *nafara'l-dābba* — the animal took fright and bolted. A horse or camel that starts and springs away at a sudden sound.

Its derivatives: *nafīr* (the call to go out to war — springing from one's place), *nafar* (a group that goes out on some business), *tanfīr* (to frighten away).

**And now see the link within the sūra — this is one of the finest internal links of the sūra and I record it as my own reading:**

In the fifteenth verse **the earth** was *dhalūl*: tamed, docile, not shying.

In the twenty-first verse **the human being** is in *nufūr*: frightened, bolting.

| | **The earth (67/15)** | **The human being (67/21)** |
|---|---|---|
| Attribute | *dhalūl* — tame, docile | *nufūr* — skittish, bolting |
| The animal likeness | Can be ridden | Cannot be ridden |
| Towards whom | Towards the human being | Towards the Merciful |

**The sūra compares the two sides in the same animal language: the inanimate has submitted, the intelligent shies and bolts.**

The support for this reading in the text is that both words are found in the same sūra and in the same field of meaning. The contrast I set up is an interpretation; the roots of the words are data.

**And the order of the three is meaningful too:**

1. **لجّوا** — they plunged into the deep (position)
2. **عتوّ** — they hardened (state)
3. **نفور** — they took fright and bolted (movement)

First the place entered, then the rigidity acquired there, then flight from everything that approaches. **The order describes a psychology:** a person who settles firmly into a position hardens, and the one who has hardened takes fright at everything that might shake him.

And the interesting side of shying is this: the animal that shies is **not a coward**; it looks for the danger in the wrong place. So it is in the verse too: what is fled from is the Merciful.

## 67/22 — أَفَمَن يَمْشِى مُكِبًّا عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِۦٓ أَهْدَىٰٓ أَمَّن يَمْشِى سَوِيًّا عَلَىٰ صِرَٰطٍ مُّسْتَقِيمٍ

*A-fa-man yamshī mukibban ʿalā wajhihi ahdā amman yamshī sawiyyan ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm*
"Is one who walks fallen forward on his face better guided, or one who walks upright on a straight path?"

The most visual verse of the sūra. And the place where the sound structure breaks: the twenty-one verses of *rāʾ* rhyme end here.

### Two walks, one verb

**The most important feature of the sentence is this: on both sides there is the same verb.** *Yamshī* — he walks. Both are walking.

This settles where the comparison lies. The verse is not comparing **one who walks with one who stands still**; it is comparing two who walk. That is, the matter is not moving but **how one moves.**

**And this connects with the fifteenth verse of the sūra:** there the command was *fa'mshū* (walk). The command was given; now the measure is given.

### مُكِبًّا عَلَىٰ وَجْهِهِۦ — on his face

**Root: ك-ب-ب.** To overturn a thing on its face, to turn it over and pour it out. *Kabba* — he overturned it, threw it face down. *Akabba* — he fell forward on his face.

**Its pattern calls for attention: *mukibb* is the active participle of Form IV.** One function of this form in Arabic is **entering into a state** (*aṣbaḥa* — he entered upon the morning, *amsā* — he entered upon the evening). That is, *mukibb* means "one who has entered the state of being on his face".

**What is the picture? — there are three readings and all three occur in the classical sources:**

| Reading | The picture | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **One walking bent over, not seeing ahead** | Head fallen forward, going along looking at his toes; he does not see where he is going | Best suited to the context; agrees with the question "is he better guided?" |
| **One crawling, going along stumbling** | On rough ground, tripping, advancing by holding on with his hands | Close to the word's sense of "overturning" |
| **One dragged on his face in the hereafter** | The person gathered face down on the Day of Resurrection | This description exists in the Qurʾān; but the verb of the verse is imperfect and the context is the life of this world |

**My preference is the first view**, and its ground is the comparison itself: the other side walks *sawiyyan* (upright). The opposite of uprightness is not being dragged but **being bent**. **It is not binding;** the third view is also defensible and has support in the Qurʾān.

**وَجْه — face.** In the discussion of al-Ghāshiya it was dwelt on why the word "face" is chosen: the face is the part of a person that cannot be hidden, the seat of identity. Here one more function is added: **the face is where direction is determined.** The eye is in the face; where you look determines where you go.

And the face of the one who walks on his face is turned to the ground. That is, **the organ that determines direction is seeing not the direction but the floor.**

### أَهْدَىٰ — "better guided"

**Root: ه-د-ي.** Treated in the discussion of al-Fātiḥa: *hidāya* is the showing of the way that brings one to the goal; in the root there is **going ahead and leading**.

*Ahdā* is an elative: "the one more rightly on the way".

**And the comparative form carries a fine point here.** The verse does not say "which of them is on the right way?"; it says **"which of them goes better?"** That is, the question assumes that both are going somewhere; what is asked about is efficiency.

This agrees with the finding recorded for *mustaqīm* in the discussion of al-Fātiḥa: *"the shortest distance between two points is a straight line; the straight road is at the same time the shortest, most economical road. Crookedness does not only take you wrong; even when it takes you there it tires you and lengthens the way."*

The one who walks on his face is also advancing. But how.

### سَوِيًّا — upright

**Root: س-و-ي.** This root was treated at length in the discussion of al-Shams (91/7); I do not repeat it here. The summary of the finding there: the idea beneath the root is **equality and proportion**; in Form II (*sawwā*) the verb takes on the sense "to bring the parts of a thing into proportion with one another, to get its ratios right, to make it complete and flawless". And the Qurʾān uses this verb insistently for the creation of the human being: *fa-sawwāka* (al-Infiṭār 82/7), *fa-sawwāhā* (al-Shams 91/7).

**Now the word *sawiyy* is the adjectival form of that root: brought into balance, complete, well-formed.**

**And from here comes one of the strongest links of the sūra, as my own reading:**

The human being was created by being made *sawwā* — by having his parts brought into balance. In the twenty-second verse the walk that is asked for is *sawiyy* — from the same root. That is, **the walk that is asked for is the walk that preserves the balance of one's creation.**

To walk on one's face is the breaking of this balance. That a human being stands upright is a feature of his creation; to bend is to abandon that feature.

This is akin to the matter of *aḥsan-i taqwīm* treated in Sūrat al-Tīn: the being created in the finest form is asked whether it preserves that form.

### عَلَىٰ صِرَٰطٍ مُّسْتَقِيمٍ — the way

**This construction was treated in the discussion of al-Fātiḥa; I do not repeat it here.** The summary of the findings there:

- **صراط** — a broad, open, main road; a well-travelled route that everyone can pass along. This is its difference from *sabīl* and *ṭarīq*: not a private path but an open highway.
- **مستقيم** — root *q-w-m*: to stand, to be erect, to straighten. The word says two things at once: **straightness** and **upright stance.**

**And now al-Mulk 67/22 joins these two findings.**

In al-Fātiḥa it was recorded that the word *mustaqīm* carries the sense of "upright stance", but in that verse it was a quality of the road. Here the quality of **the one who walks** is stated separately: *sawiyyan*.

| | The quality of the road | The quality of the walker |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Fātiḥa 1/5** | *mustaqīm* — straight, and holding one upright | (not stated) |
| **al-Mulk 67/22** | *mustaqīm* | *sawiyy* — upright, balanced |

**What emerges when the two verses are read together is this, as my own reading: being on the right road and walking rightly are separate things.**

The verse does not count the road's being right as sufficient; it counts the walker's posture too. One can walk on one's face on a right road as well.

**And the reverse is also true:** the verse of the one who walks on his face does not say which road he is on. Perhaps he is on the same road. The difference is not in the road but in the posture.

**I do not present this reading as a settled exegesis.** Most classical exegetes divide the two sides as "denier" and "believer", and this too suits the text. The layer I add is that the two sides' doing **the same act** (walking) says something.

### The breaking of the sound structure

The first twenty-one verses of the sūra ended with *rāʾ*. This verse ends with *mīm*: **mustaqīm.**

And after this the verse-endings change: *tashkurūn, tuḥsharūn, ṣādiqīn, mubīn, taddaʿūn, alīm, mubīn, maʿīn.* Now *nūn* and *mīm* predominate.

**I record this as an observation and construct no claim of miracle.** But this aspect of the observation is worth recording: the verse in which the sound changes is the verse of the sūra that **lays down the measure**. For twenty-one verses proofs and threats had been set out; here a question is asked, and the question calls the one addressed to look at his own posture.

The sound too changes direction at that point. I set up no relation of cause and effect between the two; I only record that the two changes meet in the same verse.

### What this says to the present

**The distinction the verse produces is the distinction between "movement" and "direction"** — and this distinction works particularly well today.

The strongest feeling of modern life is busyness. A person is constantly doing something, constantly advancing, constantly moving on to the next task. Movement is not questioned; stillness is questioned.

The verse does not question movement — both sides are walking anyway. What it questions is **where the head is.**

And the feature of the one who walks on his face is that he does not see ahead. This may be a result not of laziness but of **intensity**: the person who looks at his toes is generally the one who is in a hurry. He attends to his steps and does not look at the horizon.

**The concrete question that comes out of this is:** how many times a year does a person raise his head in his own life and look at where he is going? Taking steps is easy and rewarding; checking direction slows the step.

**A second aspect: the moral counterpart of the word *sawiyy*.**

To walk upright is not only a posture. The sense carried by the English expression "to stand up straight" overlaps with the Arabic *sawiyy*: to do what befits one, not to bend, to keep one's balance.

And the finding recorded in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya works here in reverse: there *khushūʿ* (lowering) could be of two kinds — voluntary and compelled. Here too uprightness can be towards two things: a person may bend before God and stand straight before people, or the reverse. The uprightness the sūra asks for is the uprightness that is **on the road** — that is, an uprightness that has accepted the direction.

## 67/23-24 — قُلْ هُوَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَنشَأَكُمْ وَجَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ ۖ قَلِيلًا مَّا تَشْكُرُونَ ۝ قُلْ هُوَ ٱلَّذِى ذَرَأَكُمْ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ وَإِلَيْهِ تُحْشَرُونَ

*Qul huwa'lladhī anshaʾakum wa jaʿala lakumu'l-samʿa wa'l-abṣāra wa'l-afʾida, qalīlan mā tashkurūn — qul huwa'lladhī dharaʾakum fi'l-arḍi wa ilayhi tuḥsharūn*
"Say: It is He who brought you into being and gave you hearing, eyes and hearts. How little you are grateful! Say: It is He who scattered you over the earth, and to Him you will be gathered."

For the first time in the sūra the command **قُلْ** (say) comes, and after this it will come five times more (24, 26, 28, 29, 30). That is, six of the sūra's last eight verses begin with this command.

**This settles the character of the last part of the sūra:** up to here the speaker was the owner of the text directly; now the messenger comes in between. And what the messenger is made to say are the answers to be given to the objections of the one addressed.

### أَنشَأَ — to bring into being by growing

**Root: ن-ش-أ.** And this root differs from *khalaqa*; the difference needs seeing.

*Nashaʾa* — **to come into being little by little, to come about by growing.** *Nashʾa* — coming into being, birth. *Nāshiʾ* — a growing youth. The Qurʾān says *nāshiʾata'l-layl* (al-Muzzammil 73/6) — the "rising" of the night, that is, the time that comes about little by little.

| Verb | What it describes |
|---|---|
| **خلق** | To bring into being according to a measure — giving form |
| **فطر** | To split out of nothing and begin — the first opening |
| **أنشأ** | **To bring into being and grow** — a process |
| **ذرأ** | To multiply and scatter — distribution |

**All four verbs are used in the Qurʾān for creating and each brings a different aspect to the fore.** In this verse two of them come one after the other: *anshaʾakum* (23) and *dharaʾakum* (24).

**And the choice of the verb *anshaʾa* agrees with the context:** the verse is going to count the organs that were given. Organs come about not in a moment but by developing. The verb contains the process.

### ٱلسَّمْعَ وَٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ وَٱلْأَفْـِٔدَةَ — the triad and the matter of singular and plural

**What is striking is this: *samʿ* is singular, *abṣār* and *afʾida* are plural.**

This is a repeated pattern in the Qurʾān — the triad is in the same form almost everywhere it occurs. The classical lexicographers and exegetes have tried to explain it and several accounts are given:

| Explanation | Its ground |
|---|---|
| **The kind of thing heard is one** | The ear takes in only sound; the eye takes in colour, shape, movement, distance — many kinds of thing. The singular comes from the singleness of the subject |
| ***Samʿ* is a verbal noun** | The word is not the name of an organ but the name of an act (hearing); verbal nouns are not pluralised. *Abṣār*, by contrast, is the plural of the name of an organ |
| **Hearing is passive, seeing active** | The ear does not close, it cannot select; the eye turns, it selects. The plurality reflects the variety of selection |

**All three explanations are reasonable and they do not exclude one another. The second is the soundest linguistically;** *samʿ* really is in the pattern of a verbal noun. The others add a layer of meaning.

**The ordering is meaningful too:** hearing, sight, heart. That is, **from outside inwards.** Sound comes, image comes, and the two are gathered in one place.

**And this order connects with the tenth verse of the sūra** — it was recorded earlier: there it was said *"had we listened or used our reason"*. Here hearing and the heart are counted as things that were given.

### ٱلْأَفْـِٔدَة — hearts

**Root: ف-أ-د.** This word was treated in the discussion of al-Humaza (104/7); I do not repeat it here. The summary of the finding there: a difference is observed between *fuʾād* and *qalb* — *fuʾād*, because of the idea of "burning, kindling" in its root, brings to the fore the **moving, passionate, burning** side of the heart.

**I add a layer here.** That *afʾida* and not *qulūb* was chosen in the verse suggests that what was given is not an organ but a **centre of comprehension**. And its standing at the end of the triad shows that what the two senses bring is processed somewhere.

That is, the verse counts three instruments: **two collectors and one processor.**

### قَلِيلًا مَّا تَشْكُرُونَ — little gratitude

**A point of word order:** *qalīlan* has been brought forward. The normal order would be *tashkurūna qalīlan*. The element brought forward carries the emphasis — the emphasis falls on **the littleness**.

**شَكَرَ — root ش-ك-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root is worth dwelling on: the lexicographers say *shakara'l-dābba* — the animal grew fat on little fodder. That is, in the root there is **making little seem much, bringing out what has been given**.

From here comes the definition of gratitude, and it differs from the English "thanks": gratitude is not a word but **the making visible of what has been given.** To use what was given, to produce something from it, to show its effect.

**And now the two halves of the verse come together:** hearing, sight and heart were given. Gratitude is to **use** these. The verse does not say "you do not say thank you"; it says you do not put the instruments given to you to work.

**This reading overlaps exactly with the tenth verse of the sūra:** there regret was expressed because the instruments had not been used. Here the name of not using them is given: **ingratitude.**

### ذَرَأَ — to multiply and scatter

**Root: ذ-ر-أ.** To create and multiply, to scatter like seed. The word's relation to *dharra* is disputed; but its closeness in meaning to *dhurriyya* (offspring, progeny) is plain.

**The distinguishing side of the verb is multiplying and dispersal.** *Khalaqa* brings a thing into being; *dharaʾa* **spreads** it.

**And the two halves of the verse set up a contrast:**

| First half | Second half |
|---|---|
| *dharaʾakum fi'l-arḍ* — He **scattered** you over the earth | *ilayhi **tuḥsharūn*** — to Him you will be **gathered** |

**حَشَرَ — root ح-ش-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root gives exactly this: **to drive what is scattered together into one place.** *Ḥashr* — to drive a community to a place and gather it; for war, for migration, for a census. In the word there is compulsion: the one gathered does not come of his own will, he is **driven**.

**Scattering and gathering:** in the fifteenth verse of the sūra there was *nushūr* (opening out); here *ḥashr* (gathering). The two words complete each other — opening out and gathering.

**And the structure of the two verses is identical:** each begins with *qul huwa'lladhī…*, each counts a favour, each ends with a sentence of consequence. One is tied to **gratitude**, the other to **the reckoning**. Two different outcomes in the same pattern.

## 67/25-27 — وَيَقُولُونَ مَتَىٰ هَٰذَا ٱلْوَعْدُ إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰدِقِينَ ۝ قُلْ إِنَّمَا ٱلْعِلْمُ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ وَإِنَّمَآ أَنَا۠ نَذِيرٌ مُّبِينٌ ۝ فَلَمَّا رَأَوْهُ زُلْفَةً سِيٓـَٔتْ وُجُوهُ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوا۟ وَقِيلَ هَٰذَا ٱلَّذِى كُنتُم بِهِۦ تَدَّعُونَ

*Wa yaqūlūna matā hādha'l-waʿdu in kuntum ṣādiqīn — qul innama'l-ʿilmu ʿinda'llāhi wa innamā anā nadhīrun mubīn — fa-lammā raʾawhu zulfatan sīʾat wujūhu'lladhīna kafarū wa qīla hādha'lladhī kuntum bihi taddaʿūn*
"They say: 'When is this promise, if you are truthful?' Say: 'The knowledge is with God alone; I am only a clear warner.' When they see it close at hand, the faces of those who denied will darken, and it will be said: 'This is what you kept calling for.'"

### مَتَىٰ هَٰذَا ٱلْوَعْدُ — the character of the question

This question is often repeated in the Qurʾān and every time it comes out of the same mouths.

**And the question is not, as it appears, a request for information.** A *date* is being asked for; but it is known that the date cannot be given. That is, the question is asked because it cannot be answered — the lack of an answer will be counted as the refutation of the claim.

This is a tactic of debate and it is widespread today too: to demand from the other side a detail he cannot supply, and when it is not supplied, to bring down the whole claim.

**إِن كُنتُمْ صَٰدِقِينَ** — "if you are truthful". This reservation brings out the intention of the question: the question has been tied to a test of truthfulness.

**And note: the question is put to a plural addressee** (*kuntum* — you). That is, not only to the Prophet but to those who follow him.

### قُلْ إِنَّمَا ٱلْعِلْمُ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ — the withholding of an answer

**And the answer is to give no answer.**

This is a point that must be dwelt on. The messenger says *"I do not know"* to the question put to him — and says it not as a weakness but as **a statement of position**.

The particle **إِنَّمَا** has been used twice and both convey restriction:

1. *innama'l-ʿilmu ʿinda'llāh* — the knowledge is **only** with God.
2. *wa innamā anā nadhīrun mubīn* — I am **only** a warner.

**The two restrictions draw the job description of the messenger.** In the discussion of al-Ghāshiya this description was treated: *"You are only a reminder; you are not one who compels them"* (88/21-22). The finding there was: not being a *musayṭir* is not a deficiency but an **exemption**.

Sūrat al-Mulk draws the same boundary from another direction: the messenger is neither a compeller nor one who knows everything.

**نَذِيرٌ مُّبِين — "a clear warner".**

*Mubīn* — root *b-y-n*: to separate, to be clear. *Bayān* — explanation; *bayna* — between. In the root there is **opening a space between two things**; clarity is separatedness.

The pattern of the word (the active participle of Form IV) can be understood in two ways and both occur in the classical sources: **one who is himself clear** or **one who makes clear.** Both suit the context.

### فَلَمَّا رَأَوْهُ زُلْفَةً — to see it close at hand

**زُلْفَة — root ز-ل-ف.** Nearness, drawing near. *Zulaf* — a portion of the night; *Muzdalifa* — the place drawn near to, one of the stations of the pilgrimage. In the root there is **the closing of distance**.

**And the word's syntactic place here is disputed:** *zulfatan* is in the accusative, but of what is it the circumstantial qualifier? Of the thing seen (the punishment being near), or of the act of seeing (seeing from close up)? Both readings are possible and both give the same result: **the distance has closed.**

**And now an internal link:** in the eleventh verse *suḥqan* (away with them) was said. Here *zulfa* (nearness). In the same sūra, two faces of the same event: the punishment draws near, the one who undergoes the punishment is put far away.

**The form of the verb is striking too:** *fa-lammā raʾaw* — **the perfect.** An event that has not yet happened is told as though it had happened. This is a form repeated in the Qurʾān's scenes of the hereafter and its effect is this: what is to come is presented, at the level of language, as settled. The same was said of *aʿtadnā* (We have prepared) in the fifth verse.

### سِيٓـَٔتْ وُجُوهُ — the darkening of faces

**Root: س-و-أ.** To be bad, to become ugly. *Sayyiʾa* — an evil deed; *sūʾ* — badness, ugliness.

**The verb is passive: *sīʾat* — "were made bad".** That is, the faces do not change of themselves; they are changed.

And again **faces.** In the discussion of al-Ghāshiya this choice was treated: the face is the part of a person that cannot be hidden; a person's state is read there first. The Qurʾān speaks insistently of faces in the scenes of the day of reckoning.

**There is an added fine point here:** before the verse there is the verb *raʾaw* (they saw). That is, **the seer is in the face and the seen is opposite.** Seeing and the face in the same sentence: they looked, and the trace of the look fell on their faces.

### كُنتُم بِهِۦ تَدَّعُونَ — two readings

There is disagreement over the reading and the meaning of this verb:

| Reading | Pattern | Its meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **تَدَّعُونَ** | Form VIII (د-ع-و) | "What you kept calling for" — asking in haste; or "what you claimed" |
| **تَدْعُونَ** | Form I | "What you called for, what you asked for" |

**The difference of meaning is not great and both suit the context.** In the twenty-fifth verse they had asked *"when?"* — that is, they had been in haste. The sentence answers that question: **this is what you asked for.**

**I record the detail of the variant reading, but because I cannot give with certainty which reading belongs to which imam, I write no name.**

**And the irony of the sentence is sharp:** when the thing people asked about mockingly, saying "when will it come?", does come, they are told *"this is what you wanted"*. The request has been granted.

## 67/28-29 — قُلْ أَرَءَيْتُمْ إِنْ أَهْلَكَنِىَ ٱللَّهُ وَمَن مَّعِىَ أَوْ رَحِمَنَا فَمَن يُجِيرُ ٱلْكَٰفِرِينَ مِنْ عَذَابٍ أَلِيمٍ ۝ قُلْ هُوَ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ ءَامَنَّا بِهِۦ وَعَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْنَا ۖ فَسَتَعْلَمُونَ مَنْ هُوَ فِى ضَلَٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ

*Qul a-raʾaytum in ahlakaniya'llāhu wa man maʿiya aw raḥimanā fa-man yujīru'l-kāfirīna min ʿadhābin alīm — qul huwa'l-raḥmānu āmannā bihi wa ʿalayhi tawakkalnā, fa-sa-taʿlamūna man huwa fī ḍalālin mubīn*
"Say: 'Tell me — if God destroys me and those with me, or has mercy on us, who will protect the deniers from a painful punishment?' Say: 'He is the Merciful; we have believed in Him and relied on Him. You shall soon know who is in clear error.'"

### أَرَءَيْتُمْ — "tell me"

This pattern was treated in the discussion of al-Māʿūn (107/1). The summary of the finding there: *a-raʾayta* means literally "have you seen", but its function is **"tell me, what do you say?"** — it puts the one addressed into a thought experiment.

**And here a thought experiment really is set up.** The verse counts two possibilities and both are either against the Prophet or in his favour:

1. *"If God destroys me and those with me"* — what the addressees expect and hope for.
2. *"Or has mercy on us"* — the reverse.

**And then the question comes: in either case, who will save you?**

**The structure of the argument is this, and it is extremely sharp:** the addressees are waiting for the Prophet to be removed; as though the matter would be over if he went. The verse cuts this off: **my own fate does not change your situation.**

This takes aim at one of the most widespread mistakes in a debate: **confusing the silencing of the one who defends a claim with the refutation of the claim.** Even if the speaker dies, what was said still stands.

**I record this as a point of logic;** the wording of the verse does not lay down this general principle, but this is the argument it produces.

### يُجِير — to take under protection

**Root: ج-و-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root is **neighbourliness**. *Jār* — neighbour. *Jiwār* — neighbourliness, nearness.

**And *ijāra* was an institutional practice in pre-Islamic Arab society:** a member of a tribe could take under his "protection" someone not of his own tribe, and that person was thereafter counted as someone who could not be harmed. This was an institution of law; the protector was the *mujīr*, the protected the *jār*.

**This historical weight of the word increases the force of the verse.** In Meccan society protection was secured by entering the patronage of someone powerful. The verse acknowledges that institution and asks: **in the face of this punishment, who is your *mujīr*?**

That is, the question is not an abstract theological question; it is a question asked in the language of the addressee's own social order.

The Qurʾān uses this root of God as well — expressions occur that He gives protection and that no protection can be given against Him. The question in the verse is within this frame.

### قُلْ هُوَ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ — the declaration of the name

**The fourth and last use of *al-Raḥmān* in the sūra. And this time the name comes not together with a verb but on its own as a sentence.**

In the third verse *khalqi'l-raḥmān*, in the nineteenth *illa'l-raḥmān*, in the twentieth *min dūni'l-raḥmān*. In all of them the name was part of a construct. Here: **هُوَ ٱلرَّحْمَٰنُ** — "He is the Merciful."

**In the discussion of al-Fātiḥa it was recorded that this name was disputed in Mecca,** and the Qurʾān mentions this dispute openly: *"When they are told 'prostrate to the Merciful', they say 'and what is the Merciful?'"* (al-Furqān 25/60).

**Now the placement in Sūrat al-Mulk takes on meaning, and I record this as my own reading:** through the sūra this name was mentioned three times together with an act of power — creating, holding, protecting. At the end the name is uttered on its own and a declaration of faith is added after it: *āmannā bihi.*

That is, after answering the question "who is the Merciful?" **with acts**, the sūra declares the name. The proof first, the name after.

### ءَامَنَّا بِهِۦ وَعَلَيْهِ تَوَكَّلْنَا — two verbs

**آمَنَ — root أ-م-ن.** The same root as *a-amintum* in the sixteenth verse. **And this is an internal link of the sūra:**

| Verse | Word | Towards whom or what |
|---|---|---|
| 16, 17 | *a-amintum* — have you become secure? | Towards a false guarantee |
| 29 | *āmannā* — we have believed, we have trusted | Towards the true one |

**The same root, two trusts looking in two different directions.** One is questioned, the other declared. That the root carries both the senses "to be safe" and "to believe" makes this contrast possible.

**تَوَكَّلَ — root و-ك-ل.** To commit a task to another, to appoint an agent. *Wakīl* — the one to whom a task is left.

**And the fine point of the word is here: reliance is not the giving up of work but the committing of the outcome of the work.** The person who appoints an agent does not abandon the work — he shares the responsibility. This distinction is central to the Qurʾān's understanding of reliance and is very open to being misunderstood.

**The ordering is meaningful too:** first *āmannā* (we have believed), then *tawakkalnā* (we have relied). Belief first, reliance after. One does not give agency to someone one does not know.

### فَسَتَعْلَمُونَ مَنْ هُوَ فِى ضَلَٰلٍ مُّبِينٍ — an answer to the ninth verse

**And here the sūra closes an account within itself.**

In the ninth verse those in Hell had repeated the words they said to the messengers in the world:

> *"…we said: God has sent down nothing, **you are in great error** (fī ḍalālin **kabīr**)."* (67/9)

The twenty-ninth verse answers with the same word:

> *"You shall soon know who is **in clear error** (fī ḍalālin **mubīn**)."*

| | **67/9** | **67/29** |
|---|---|---|
| Who says it | The deniers (to the messengers) | The messenger (to the deniers) |
| The shared word | *fī ḍalālin* | *fī ḍalālin* |
| The adjective | *kabīr* — great | *mubīn* — clear |
| The form | A settled verdict: "you are in it" | Deferred knowledge: "**you shall know**" |

**The last row is the most important.** The deniers pass judgement: "you are in error." The messenger does not pass judgement: "you shall know who is in error."

**That is, the answer is not to throw the same accusation back; it is to leave the verdict to time.** And this is on the same line as the distinction recorded in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya: the idea of being called to account and the appetite for calling others to account are separate things. The messenger does not declare the other side to be in error; he says **that the authority to declare it does not belong to him.**

The choice of word supports this too: *mubīn* — what is clear. That is, it is not clear now; it will become clear.

## 67/30 — قُلْ أَرَءَيْتُمْ إِنْ أَصْبَحَ مَآؤُكُمْ غَوْرًا فَمَن يَأْتِيكُم بِمَآءٍ مَّعِينٍۭ

*Qul a-raʾaytum in aṣbaḥa māʾukum ghawran fa-man yaʾtīkum bi-māʾin maʿīn*
"Say: Tell me — if your water were to sink away into the ground, who would bring you flowing water?"

The last verse of the sūra. And the place where the whole argument comes down.

### أَصْبَحَ — "when morning came"

**Root: ص-ب-ح.** The same root as *maṣābīḥ* (lamps) in the fifth verse. That the same root is found in the first and last parts of the sūra is an observation.

The verb *aṣbaḥa* has two functions in Arabic: **"he entered upon the morning"** and — as one of *kāna*'s sisters — **"he came into a state."** The second sense holds here.

**But the first sense also stands within the verb and adds a colour:** the water's having sunk away when one gets up in the morning. That is, the disaster happens in a night; a person wakes and there is no water.

**This is a structure that will be repeated exactly in the story of the garden in Sūrat al-Qalam:** there too the owners of the garden undergo a disaster in the night and the word *muṣbiḥīn* (having entered upon the morning) is used again and again. I record this similarity of structure between the two sūras as an observation.

### غَوْرًا — to sink into the ground

**Root: غ-و-ر.** And beneath the root there is **caving inwards, going down deep**. *Ghār* — a cave, a hollow going into a mountain. *Ghawr* — a hollow, a low place; the sunken part of a region. *Ghāra'l-māʾ* — the water sank beneath the ground.

**The word is a verbal noun and stands as a predicate:** *aṣbaḥa māʾukum **ghawran*** — literally "your water became a sinking-away". In Arabic the use of a verbal noun in place of an adjective conveys intensification: the water has not sunk; it has become **sinking itself**.

**And now note the link within the sūra:** in the sixteenth verse there was *yakhsifa bikumu'l-arḍ* — the earth's caving in, taking you into itself. Here the same direction: the water is drawn into the earth.

In both the threat comes **from below** and **silently**. There is no army and no thunder. Only something goes down and does not come back.

### مَعِين — flowing water

**The root of the word is disputed and there are two views:**

| Root | Derivation | Its meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **ع-ي-ن** | *ʿayn* (spring, eye) → *maʿīn* | **Water coming from a spring, water that has a source.** The mīm is additional |
| **م-ع-ن** | *maʿana* (to flow, to flow easily) → *maʿīn* | **Water that flows along, easily reached.** The mīm is radical |

**Both derivations are defended in the classical sources and both come out in the same field of meaning:** water that is at the surface, that can be seen, that can be reached. Its opposite is *ghawr* — withdrawn, unreachable.

**And precisely for this reason the two words of the verse form a perfect pair:**

| **غَوْر** | **مَعِين** |
|---|---|
| Drawn downward | Coming to the surface |
| Unreachable | Reachable |
| Disappearing | Visible |

The words are exact opposites of each other and they stand in the same sentence.

**A note:** in the discussion of al-Māʿūn the view that the word *māʿūn* may mean "water" was recorded and found weak; the ground given was that *māʿūn* and *maʿīn* are different words. That record holds here too; the two words must not be confused.

### The close of the sūra: why water?

**This is the most striking architectural move of the sūra and it is worth dwelling on.**

Follow the scale of the sūra from beginning to end:

1. **The dominion** — ownership of everything (1)
2. **Death and life** — the whole of existence (2)
3. **Seven heavens** — the structure of the universe (3)
4. **Hell** — the scene of the hereafter (6-11)
5. **The earth** — the whole planet (15)
6. **The birds** — a living thing seen with the eye (19)
7. **Provision** — daily livelihood (21)
8. **Walking** — the posture of a body (22)
9. **Water** — a cup (30)

**The sūra starts at the highest point and comes down to the lowest, and stops there.**

**In the discussion of al-Māʿūn the same structure was identified and treated under the heading "from a cosmic claim to a cooking pot".** The finding there was: *"The sūra starts at the highest point and comes down to the lowest, and stops there… great claims are tested in small things."*

**Al-Mulk uses the same technique but for another purpose, and the difference is important:**

| | **al-Māʿūn 107** | **al-Mulk 67** |
|---|---|---|
| What is above | The day of reckoning | The ownership of the dominion |
| What is below | A bucket, a cooking pot | A cup of water |
| The function of the descent | **Moral**: belief is tested in small conduct | **Evidential**: the claim of power is tested in the most everyday need |
| The one addressed | The one who prays | The one who denies |

**In al-Māʿūn the descent is a test of morals; in al-Mulk it is a test of dependence.**

And the object al-Mulk chooses is smaller even than al-Māʿūn's — because a bucket can be lent, but water cannot be produced. The sūra chooses a thing the human being **can get from nobody**.

### The logic of the question

The verse does not announce a disaster; it sets up a **condition**: *in aṣbaḥa* — if it should happen.

And the unanswerability of the question is the strength of the question. What is done if the water sinks away? A well is dug — but if there is no water the well is empty. It is brought from elsewhere — but what is asked about is the **source** of the water, not its transport.

**Today this question is even more understandable, and I record this as an observation:** modern technology can transport water, purify it, take the salt out of it, bring it up from great depths. The one thing it cannot do is **create water.** All water technology is a rearranging of water that exists.

The question of the verse stands exactly on this distinction: you can carry it, you can bring it up, you can clean it. **You cannot bring it into being.**

**I do not present this as a claim of scientific miracle.** The verse makes no prediction about technology; it only shows a dependence, and that dependence holds independently of technology.

### The first verse of the sūra and the last

**I record this as an observation:**

| **67/1** | **67/30** |
|---|---|
| *bi-yadihi'l-mulk* — the dominion is in His hand | *fa-man yaʾtīkum bi-māʾin maʿīn* — who will bring you water |
| A claim of ownership | The everyday test of the ownership |
| Abstract, comprehensive | Concrete, particular |

**The sūra has proved a single sentence in thirty verses — and it does the proving by bringing it down to a cup of water.**

The sentence "the dominion is His" is a claim that can be disputed. The question "if your water sank away, who would bring it?" is a situation that cannot be disputed. The sūra ties what can be disputed to what cannot.

**As my own reading I add one last thing:** the verse's saying *māʾukum* — **"your water"** — is the finest place in the sentence. The water is mentioned as "yours"; the ownership is granted. And then it is asked: if it is yours, bring it back.

The claim of ownership is not refused; **it is tested.** That a thing is yours is measured by your being able to bring it back.

## The sūra as a whole

### A sūra of questions

It is instructive to count the sentences in Sūrat al-Mulk that carry a question mark:

| Verse | Question |
|---|---|
| 3 | *hal tarā min fuṭūr* — do you see any crack? |
| 8 | *a-lam yaʾtikum nadhīr* — did no warner come to you? |
| 14 | *a-lā yaʿlamu man khalaqa* — shall the one who created not know? |
| 16 | *a-amintum* — have you become secure? |
| 17 | *am amintum* — or have you become secure? |
| 19 | *a-wa-lam yaraw* — have they not seen? |
| 20 | *amman hādha'lladhī* — who is this? |
| 21 | *amman hādha'lladhī* — who is this? |
| 22 | *a-fa-man yamshī… ahdā* — which of them goes better? |
| 25 | *matā hādha'l-waʿd* — when is this promise? (from the addressees) |
| 28 | *fa-man yujīr* — who will protect? |
| 30 | *fa-man yaʾtīkum* — who will bring you? |

**Twelve questions in thirty verses.** And all but one come from the owner of the text; only the question in the twenty-fifth verse comes from the one addressed.

**This distribution announces a method, as my own reading:** the sūra tries less to give information than **to make one find it**. And most of the questions are left unanswered — because the answer will appear when the one addressed looks at his own situation.

**And note the answer not given to the questions that were asked:** the addressee's single question (25) is not answered. The sūra asks dozens of questions and answers none.

### The main idea of the sūra

**I gather up here the reading I stated at the outset, and I state again that it is my own reading:**

Sūrat al-Mulk opens with a claim of ownership and, instead of **proving** that claim, **tests** it.

To prove is to present evidence to the other side. To test is to make the other side see his own situation.

And the method the sūra uses is the same from beginning to end: **it asks after the source of a means.**

- The sky is sound — who holds it?
- The earth is docile — who tamed it?
- The bird flies — who holds it?
- Provision comes — who gives it?
- Water flows — who brought it?

**Every question has the same structure: show that what is in your hand did not come from you.**

And the sūra does this not as an accusation but as an **inventory**. Through thirty verses nobody is scolded as "ungrateful" — only in the twenty-third verse is it said *"how little you are grateful"*, and that is all.

### Death and water

I want to set side by side the two words at the two ends of the sūra, as my own reading:

**Death in the second verse** — the limit of life, the finishing line of the test.
**Water in the thirtieth verse** — the continuation of life, the most everyday need.

Both are the work of the same hand. One is given, the other taken away; and the source of both is the same.

**The sūra gathers the most abstract and the most concrete under the same owner.** To speak about death is a matter of philosophy; to speak about water is a matter of the kitchen. The sūra puts the two at the two ends of the same sentence.

## What this says to the present — an overall assessment

### First: the weight of the measure "the best"

The distinction treated in the second verse is the most practical side of the sūra and deserves repeating.

**There are two ways of measuring oneself: looking at how much you did, and looking at how you did it.** The first is comforting because it can be counted; the second is uncomfortable because it cannot.

And the measure that cannot be counted has one property: **nobody can measure it in your place.** Whether a work was given its due nobody can fully know except the one who did it. This makes the measure both harder and something nobody can take out of your hands.

### Second: the tiring of the gaze and the limit of knowledge

The scene of observation in the third and fourth verses proposes a way of relating to knowledge.

The verse keeps away from both extremes:

- It does not say **"do not look, do not question"** — on the contrary, it commands looking again and again.
- Nor does it say **"if you look you will solve everything"** — it says from the outset that the gaze will come back exhausted.

**The position that stands between is this: investigate, but know the limit of your instrument.**

This position is against both being closed to knowledge and worshipping knowledge. And both are widespread today: one side counts asking a danger, the other believes every question can be answered.

### Third: two channels — hearing and using reason

The pair in the tenth verse is, for today, an instrument of self-examination.

**The question is: which of these two channels is open in me?**

Some listen but do not think: they take what they hear as it is and never test it. Some think but do not listen: they trust their own reasoning and take nothing from outside.

The measure the verse produces is that either of the two is counted sufficient on its own — but **the presence of even one** is required. A mind in which both are closed cannot correct itself.

### Fourth: the ground of security

The question the sixteenth and seventeenth verses ask — "have you become secure?" — is asked not in order to produce fear but **in order to make a ground visible**.

Every day, without being aware of it, a person lives by a series of assumptions: the ground will stay fixed, the air will be breathable, the heart will go on beating. These are not conscious beliefs; they are assumptions **running in the background**.

The verse brings the background to the front. And when the background becomes visible, two things appear at once: the fragility of the situation, and the fact that that fragility has been sustained until now.

### Fifth: the posture of the walk

The twenty-second verse is the sentence of the sūra that looks most directly at the present.

**Both people are walking.** The difference is that one looks at his toes and the other at the horizon.

Its concrete counterparts:

**At work.** To do a job and to know where the job is going are separate things. Most busyness does not require asking the second, and so it is not asked.

**In education.** To complete a curriculum and to bring up a human being are separate things. The first can be measured, the second cannot.

**In religious life.** To perform an act of worship and to know what it serves are separate things. In Sūrat al-Māʿūn this distinction was treated in its sharpest form.

**And note the form of the verse's question: "goes better" — that is, both are going.** The sūra does not criticise stillness; it criticises **movement without direction**.

### Sixth: a cup of water

The side of the last verse that looks at the present is the place that needs explaining least.

Water is the human being's most basic need and the thing he most overlooks. The tap is turned, the water comes. Behind this operation there is a source, a cycle, a climate, a geology — and none of them is thought about.

**What the verse does is to make one imagine that cycle being cut somewhere.** And it does this not as a threat but as a **question**: who will bring it?

**The sinking away of water is not a figure of speech today.** The falling of groundwater levels, drought, the exhaustion of water sources — these are things that are measured and reported. The verse does not announce them; **it has no need to announce them,** because the question it asks holds independently of these events.

**And looking at the sūra as a whole, the place of the close becomes clear:** after seven heavens, Hell, the resurrection and the birds, the last sentence is about a cup of water. The sūra ends by tying the greatest claim to the smallest need.

Because a human being can dispute whom the dominion belongs to. When he is thirsty he cannot dispute.

## Where this section does not speak with certainty

At several points throughout the text certain language was deliberately not used; the reader needs to know these:

- **No occasion of revelation was given.** Because I am not sure that a definite incident is attached to this sūra, no report at all was related.
- **The reports about the merit of the sūra** (protection against the punishment of the grave, the names *al-Wāqiya* / *al-Munjiya*) were recorded with the statement that they are widespread; **no hadith source and no chain of transmission was ascribed,** because I cannot give them with certainty.
- For the expression **بِيَدِهِ** (verse 1) and the expression **مَن فِى ٱلسَّمَآءِ** (verses 16-17) the positions of *tafwīḍ* and *taʾwīl* were given in a table; **no side was taken between them.**
- **The difference of variant reading in the word تَفَاوُت** and **the difference of reading in the word تَدَّعُونَ** were recorded, but **which reading belongs to which imam was not written,** because I cannot give the distribution with certainty.
- For **the expression "seven heavens"** no matching with a modern layering was made; the possibility that the number "seven" conveys multitude in Arabic was also recorded, and no preference was adopted between the two possibilities.
- For **the expression رُجُومًا لِّلشَّيَاطِينِ** (verse 5) three readings were given and **no ruling was issued among them;** no matching with meteors was made, and the ground for this was written out openly.
- **To whom the pronoun لَهُمْ refers** (end of verse 5) is disputed; a preference was stated and it was recorded that it is not binding.
- **Whether كَرَّتَيْنِ conveys a number or a multitude** (verse 4) is disputed; two views were given, a preference was stated with its ground, and it was written that it is not binding.
- **The two readings in the sentence أَلَا يَعْلَمُ مَنْ خَلَقَ** (verse 14) were given and **no preference was adopted,** because both come out at the same conclusion.
- The two readings of **the expression بِٱلْغَيْبِ** (verse 12) were given; **no preference was adopted,** and it was stated that both come out at the same principle.
- What **the word مَنَاكِب** (verse 15) means is disputed; three views were given, a leaning was stated, and it was written that it is not binding.
- **The picture of مُكِبًّا** (verse 22) is disputed; three readings were given, a preference was stated with its ground, and it was recorded that it is not binding.
- **The root of the word مَعِين** (verse 30) is disputed; two derivations were given and **no preference was adopted,** it being stated that both come out at the same meaning.
- **No claim of scientific miracle was constructed about the flight of the birds.** The distinction *صافات / يقبضن* was presented as **a point of grammar**; while its agreement with observation was recorded, it was written openly that this was something observable in the seventh century as well.
- **No conclusion in any direction was drawn from the word ذَلُولًا about the shape or the motion of the earth;** the record for *firāsh* at al-Baqara 2/22 was relied on, and it was stated that forcing in either direction is the same mistake.
- **From the verse about the sinking away of water** no claim of miracle regarding modern water technology was constructed; it was stated that the question of the verse holds independently of technology.
- **The root-link between سِرّ / سُرُور** was related as an explanation transmitted by the lexicographers; it was stated that it carries no claim of certainty (in agreement with the same record in the discussion of al-Ghāshiya).
- It was stated openly that the following readings are **my own reading**: the main idea of the sūra as "the turning of the claim of ownership into an inventory of dependence"; death's being brought forward making life bounded; *fāṭir* and *fuṭūr* coming face to face in the same root; the measure of *aḥsan* equalising the test; the contrast of the animal likeness between *ذلول* and *نفور*; the two opposite uses of the verb *أمسك* in verses 19 and 21; the link between *sawwā* and *sawiyy* joining creation and walking; the cycle of the word *nadhīr* through four verses; the axis of recognising and not recognising between *iʿtirāf* and *nakīr*; the construction of the chain of looking (3-4-19-23); the difference between the sūra's descent of scale and that of al-Māʿūn; the construct *māʾukum* in the last verse testing ownership.
- **The observations about sound and structure** (the twenty-one verses of *rāʾ* rhyme and the break in verse 22; the number of questions; the table of internal links) were recorded not as a claim of miracle but as an agreement of form with content, and it was written openly that no relation of cause and effect was set up.
