# 75. Sūrat al-Qiyāma

Forty verses. On its being Meccan there is something close to agreement: short verse-endings, the argument over the resurrection, a manner that answers the objection of the one addressed head-on, the absence of legal content.

It takes its name from its second word: **ٱلْقِيَٰمَة** — the rising.

## How the sūra is built

The sūra turns around one question, and that question is asked twice, at two different depths.

**The third verse:** *"Does man suppose that We shall not gather his bones?"*
**The thirty-sixth verse:** *"Does man suppose that he will be left to himself?"*

The two questions open with the same pattern — *a-yaḥsabu'l-insān* — and thirty-two verses lie between them. The first question is about the **body**: can a thing that has scattered be gathered? The second is about **life**: can a thing that has been lived be left without a return? The sūra moves from the first to the second and says that the real matter is the second.

The blocks:

**I (1-2).** Two oaths. One a day outside, one a mechanism inside.

**II (3-6).** Man's objection, the intention beneath the objection, and the question that is the instrument of that intention.

**III (7-15).** The scene opens: the eye is dazzled, the moon is swallowed, sun and moon are brought together. A place of escape is sought and not found. And the reckoning is set up from an unexpected quarter: **from man himself.**

**IV (16-19).** A parenthesis: the moment of receiving revelation. Why these four verses were set into the middle of the sūra becomes clear along with the twentieth verse.

**V (20-25).** The diagnosis (loving *al-ʿājila*) and two faces: the face that looks, the face that is soured.

**VI (26-30).** The moment of death. The narrowest scene in the sūra: a room, a bed, a question.

**VII (31-35).** The portrait in retrospect: what this man was, how he walked.

**VIII (36-40).** The closing question and the proof. The sūra returns to the third verse, but it is no longer standing in the same place.

The sentence that carries the whole weight of the sūra is in the fourteenth verse: **بَلِ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِۦ بَصِيرَةٌ** — *"Man is a clear sight upon himself."* And that sentence is the counterpart of the oath in the second verse: the soul that reproaches itself and the man who is proof against himself are two names for the same thing. The sūra is built between these two.

The basmala was treated in earlier sections and is not taken up again here; the verse numbers are counted without the basmala.

## 75/1 — لَآ أُقْسِمُ بِيَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ

*Lā uqsimu bi-yawmi'l-qiyāma*
"I swear by the Day of Rising."

### The لَا at the head

This pattern was treated in detail in the section on Sūrat al-Balad and four views were set out in a table: that the *lā* is redundant (an intensifier), that it rejects something said before, that it magnifies the oath (*"there is no need to swear, it is that plain"*), and that it is a shortened form of *alā*. The conclusion recorded there was this: none of the views is certain, the debate has not closed since the classical period, and the outcome that reaches the translation ("I swear") is the same on every reading.

I do not repeat it here. What is to be added here is that **this sūra uses the pattern differently from al-Balad.**

In al-Balad there was a single *lā uqsimu*. Here there are two, and in the second the *lā* is repeated:

> **لَآ أُقْسِمُ** بِيَوْمِ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ • **وَلَآ أُقْسِمُ** بِٱلنَّفْسِ ٱللَّوَّامَةِ

In Arabic it was not obligatory to repeat the *lā* for the second oath; *wa bi'n-nafsi'l-lawwāma* could have been said. Its repetition shows that the two oaths **are not reduced to one another**: these are not two elements of a single oath but two separate oaths.

And this distinction is needed in order to understand what the second verse does. The sūra swears by two things separately — one in the future, outside; one now, inside.

**The debate in al-Balad has a further side that belongs to this sūra in particular.** The second of the four views — that the *lā* rejects something said before — was found weak in al-Balad, because al-Balad did not begin in the middle of an argument. In al-Qiyāma the situation is different: in the third verse the sūra quotes an objection directly (*"does he suppose that We cannot gather his bones?"*), and in the sixth a question (*"and when is that day?"*). That is, here there really is something to be rejected, and it is visible in the text.

This makes the second view stronger for this sūra than it was in al-Balad. **I write this not as a preference but as a record;** the debate still does not close.

### يَوْم — day

**Root: ي-و-م.** In Arabic *yawm* is not only a stretch of twenty-four hours; it also means **the span of time over which a matter runs**. The Arabs call a battle *yawm Badr*, *yawm Dhī Qār*; what is meant is the duration of that fighting, not a day on the calendar.

This breadth is a datum of the language to be kept in mind when reading the Qurʾān's expressions for "day". The word itself imposes no measure of duration.

### ٱلْقِيَٰمَة — the rising

**Root: ق-و-م.** This root was treated in five separate places in this commentary and I do not repeat them:

- **al-Fātiḥa 1/6** — *mustaqīm*: an attribute of the path; both straightness and upright bearing.
- **al-Baqara 2/3** — *iqāma*: keeping the prayer standing; the root's act in the servant.
- **al-Tīn 95/4** — *taqwīm*: setting straight, giving form; causation and intensity in the *tafʿīl* pattern.
- **al-Bayyina 98/3, 5** — *qayyima*: that which is itself straight and straightens others.
- **al-Takwīr 81/28** — *yastaqīm*: what was an attribute of the path turning into an act of the person.

The summary of the root: **to stand, to be set upright, to be straightened.**

**What is to be added here is that in this sūra the word is the *name of an event*.** In the five uses above the root qualified a path, a religion, or a servant's act. Here it names a day — and in naming it, it says what happens on that day: **there will be a rising.**

As was recorded in the section on al-Muṭaffifīn, the Qurʾān draws this picture explicitly in one place:

> **يَوْمَ يَقُومُ ٱلنَّاسُ لِرَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِينَ** — *"The day when people stand before the Lord of all the worlds."* (al-Muṭaffifīn 83/6)

The finding recorded there was this: the verb is imperfect, that is, not standing up and sitting down again but **remaining standing** — waiting. And that the word *qiyāma* comes from this root shows that the day's name is "the rising".

**The rising has two faces**, and both are contained in the root:

| Face | Picture | Its counterpart in the root |
|---|---|---|
| **The lying rise** | The dead are raised upright from the grave | *qāma* — to get to one's feet |
| **The risen wait** | One stands in the Presence | *qiyām* — the state of standing |

The first is a moment, the second a duration. The word carries both at once.

**And an observation, as my own reading:** the same root also gives one of God's names in the Qurʾān — **ٱلْقَيُّوم** (al-Baqara 2/255): the one who himself stands and holds everything else standing. So the absolute state of standing belongs to Him, and the event of rising to man. The one who rises will rise, but the rising itself is also a thing that is held up. I draw this link from the shared root; the sharing of the root is a datum of the language, the conceptual relation between them is my own reading.

### Why is the oath sworn by this day?

An oath is sworn by something the one addressed accepts. Here, what is sworn by is exactly what the one addressed **rejects**.

This increases the strangeness of the pattern and joins with the debate in the section on al-Balad. If *lā uqsimu* means "there is no need to swear by this, it is plain in itself", then the sentence is saying: **that day is not something to be supported by an oath; it is clear enough to be proof itself.**

And the rest of the sūra does just this: it does not defend the resurrection as a piece of news, it derives it from man's own constitution. This is exactly why the second element of the oath comes.

## 75/2 — وَلَآ أُقْسِمُ بِٱلنَّفْسِ ٱللَّوَّامَةِ

*Wa lā uqsimu bi'n-nafsi'l-lawwāma*
"And I swear by the soul that reproaches itself."

One of the densest verses in the sūra, and one of the most striking sentences in the Qurʾān's account of man.

### ٱللَّوَّامَة — the great reproacher

**Root: ل-و-م.** Its meaning is **to blame, to reproach, to throw a thing in someone's face, to rebuke.** It is a verbal act: to say something to a person because of something he has done.

Its derivatives:

- **لَوْم** (*lawm*) — the act of blaming itself.
- **لَائِم** (*lāʾim*) — the one who blames.
- **مَلَامَة** (*malāma*) — being blamed, a state deserving blame. The English *Malāmatī*, the name of a later Sufi current, comes from this root: it became the name of a stance that accepts blame — but that is a usage laid over the word's lexical sense afterwards.
- **مَلُوم** (*malūm*) — the one who has been blamed.

**The most striking use of the root in the Qurʾān is in a speech made on the Day of Rising:**

> **فَلَا تَلُومُونِى وَلُومُوٓا۟ أَنفُسَكُم** — *"Do not blame me; blame yourselves."* (Ibrāhīm 14/22)

This sentence is spoken by Satan, on the day of the reckoning. And it contains exactly the subject of this verse: **the object of blame is turned from outside to inside.**

### The pattern: فَعَّالَة

The word is not *lāʾima* (blaming) but **لَوَّامَة** (*lawwāma*).

The **فَعَّال** pattern in Arabic conveys **intensity**: one who performs an act much, who has made it a habit. *Kadhdhāb* (who lies much), *ghaffār* (who forgives much), *jabbār*. The ة at the end makes the adjective agree with the word *nafs*, which is feminine.

So the word is not "a soul that reproaches" but **"a soul that has made reproach its habit, that reproaches without ceasing"**. The act is not incidental but continuous.

**This detail carries the entire weight of the verse.** What is sworn by is not a single regret; it is **a mechanism standing in working order** inside a person.

### What does it reproach?

The explanation widely transmitted in the classical commentaries is this: this soul reproaches **in two directions at once**. When it has done evil it says "why did you do it?"; when it has done good it says "why did you not do more?" That is, the reproach is not an assessment of outcome but an account that stands permanently open.

This explanation has a support in the language too: the *faʿʿāl* pattern conveys that the act is not broken off. And the object of an act that is not broken off is not exhausted either.

### The real point of the oath: what is sworn by?

Now set the two verses side by side.

| | **75/1** | **75/2** |
|---|---|---|
| What is sworn by | **ٱلْقِيَٰمَة** — the Day of Rising | **ٱلنَّفْس ٱللَّوَّامَة** — the soul that reproaches itself |
| Its place | Outside | Inside |
| Its time | In the future | Now |
| Its extent | One event for everyone | Separately in every person |
| Its visibility | Never seen | Being lived |

**The two oaths are set down as two states of the same thing.** And the move made here builds the whole evidential structure of the sūra:

The one addressed rejects the first element — he does not believe there is a day of reckoning. But he cannot reject the second, because he lives it every day. That voice inside him — the one that turns back on him after something he has done, that accuses him, that troubles him although no one has seen — he cannot deny.

And the verse names the two in the same sentence.

**This is a reading and I record it as my own reading;** but the placing of the two oaths side by side is the text's own move. The sūra swears by a day of judgement and sets beside it, at once, the court already established inside man.

### The standing of conscience as "proof"

Care is needed here, because this verse is open to being misread in two directions at once.

**The first misreading:** "Conscience is the proof of the hereafter." The verse does not say this. An oath is not an operation of proof; an oath produces a witness in order to mark the weight of what is about to be said. A link is being made between the two, but neither is offered as the demonstration of the other.

**The second misreading:** "Conscience is enough, no reckoning is needed." The verse does not say this either. The soul's reproach of itself is not a judgement; it issues no ruling, produces no consequence, makes nothing good. It only **informs.**

What the verse does is finer: it establishes that man is a being able to judge himself. And this finding will be used again in the fourteenth verse, where the sūra describes how the reckoning is set up.

### The second verse and the fourteenth

The inner architecture of the sūra is built here, and this link is the key to understanding it.

> **ٱلنَّفْس ٱللَّوَّامَة** (75/2) — the soul that reproaches itself.
> **بَلِ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِۦ بَصِيرَةٌ** (75/14) — man is a clear sight upon himself.

The two sentences build the same idea:

| | **75/2** | **75/14** |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | The soul | Man |
| Object | Itself | His own soul |
| Act | Reproaching (*lawm*) | Seeing / being proof (*baṣīra*) |
| Particle | — | **عَلَىٰ** — against him |
| Its time | Now | On that day |

**The only difference is in time.** The mechanism working now in the second verse is working in that day's court in the fourteenth. The sūra describes the day of reckoning not as an institution foreign to man but as **the coming into the open of something already at work.**

And this is the real answer the sūra gives to the mocking question of the sixth verse. The man asks "when is that day?" The sūra gives no date; it tells him to look inside.

### A record about "the stations of the soul"

In the Qurʾān the word *nafs* is qualified by an adjective in three places:

| Adjective | Place | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **أَمَّارَةٌۢ بِٱلسُّوٓءِ** | Yūsuf 12/53 | Commanding evil much |
| **ٱللَّوَّامَة** | al-Qiyāma 75/2 | Reproaching much |
| **ٱلْمُطْمَئِنَّة** | al-Fajr 89/27 | Settled, come to security |

A record was made on this matter in the section on al-Fajr, and I repeat it here word for word, because consistency requires it:

**These three adjectives were turned in the later Sufi tradition into a graded classification under the name "the stations of the soul", and further rungs were added. No such ordering of degrees is explicitly established in the Qurʾān.** The three adjectives occur in three separate sūras, in three separate contexts; that there is a line of ascent between them is an interpretation drawn out of the text.

Something must be added to this as regards this sūra. If we assume a graded ladder, *lawwāma* becomes the middle rung — a stopping place passed on the way from bad to good. But the verse's own context does not support this: here *lawwāma* is something sworn by. One does not swear by an intermediate state that is meant to be left behind.

**As my own reading:** in the verse *lawwāma* stands not as a station but as a **capacity** — a structural part of being human. This reading does not reject the classification; it only says that the classification cannot be derived from this verse.

### The verse-ending

The two verses end on the same sound: *al-qiyāma* / *al-lawwāma*. The same measure (**فِعَالَة** and **فَعَّالَة**), the same close.

And the third verse will end on the same sound too: *ʿiẓāmah*. The fourth: *banānah*. The fifth: *amāmah*. The sixth: *al-qiyāmah*.

**The first six verses of the sūra all close on a single sound.** The ear hears the first block together with its boundary: at the seventh verse the sound will change.

## 75/3-4 — أَيَحْسَبُ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ أَلَّن نَجْمَعَ عِظَامَهُۥ · بَلَىٰ قَٰدِرِينَ عَلَىٰٓ أَن نُّسَوِّىَ بَنَانَهُۥ

*A-yaḥsabu'l-insānu allan najmaʿa ʿiẓāmah · Balā qādirīna ʿalā an nusawwiya banānah*
"Does man suppose that We shall not gather his bones? Yes indeed — We are able to set even his fingertips in order."

### أَيَحْسَبُ — "does he suppose?"

**Root: ح-س-ب.** And the relation between the two branches of this root is the finest thing in the verse.

- **حَسَبَ** (*ḥasaba*) — **he counted, he reckoned.** *Ḥisāb* (reckoning), *ḥāsib* (one who reckons), *iḥtisāb*, *ḥasīb*. English happens to keep the two branches together in one word: *to reckon* is both "to calculate" and "to suppose" — "I reckon" says both.
- **حَسِبَ** (*ḥasiba*) — **he supposed, he thought.**

The two senses are two separate verbs carrying separate vowels, but they come from the same root. The explanation the lexicographers transmit is this: to suppose something is **the result of a reckoning** a person has made in his head. Opinion comes out of what has been counted.

**From here a point arises for the verse.** In describing the man who denies the day of reckoning, the verse uses a verb from precisely the root of "reckoning": *a-yaḥsabu* — "is he reckoning?" The man has made a reckoning and it has come out wrong. And the subject of the reckoning that came out wrong is reckoning itself.

I record this as an observation about the language; the two branches of the root are lexical data, the point drawn between them is mine.

**The interrogative particle أ (hamza)** is here a **denying question**: it does not ask for information, it rejects an attitude. It answers to the tone of the English "does he really think so?"

### ٱلْإِنسَٰن — the subject of the sūra

The word occurs **six times** in this sūra: 3, 5, 10, 13, 14, 36. Apart from this no human group is named — there is no expression such as "those who disbelieve", "the wrongdoers", "the mushrikūn".

**The sūra works with a single subject: man, as a genus.**

This use was treated in the section on al-Inshiqāq: the *al-* at the head conveys **totality** (*istighrāq*), covering the whole of the genus. There it stood in an address (*yā ayyuhā'l-insān*); here the telling is in the third person. But the extent is the same.

This has a consequence, and it matters in reading the sūra: the one in verse 5 who "wants to break open what lies ahead of him", the one in verse 33 who "walks with a swagger", the one in verse 36 who "supposes he will be left to himself" — all are named with the same word. The sūra describes not a group but a **tendency**. Whoever carries that tendency is included in it.

### أَلَّن نَجْمَعَ — "that We shall not gather"

**أَلَّنْ** is a compound of *an* + *lan*. **لَنْ** in Arabic is a **categorical** negation directed at the future — it was treated at al-Baqara 2/23 in the discussion of *wa lan tafʿalū*.

So the man's opinion is not a weak guess: he says "you cannot gather them", not "you might not be able to gather them". **The claim to certainty is his.**

**Root: ج-م-ع.** To gather, to bring together, to unite what is scattered. Its derivatives: *jamʿ* (gathering), *jamāʿa* (a community), *jāmiʿ* (that which gathers; the place of gathering), *jumuʿa* (the day of gathering), *ijmāʿ*, *majmūʿ*.

**And now one of the most striking word-patterns in the sūra.** This root occurs **three times** in the sūra, and each time its object changes:

| Verse | Expression | What is gathered |
|---|---|---|
| **75/3** | *najmaʿa **عِظَامَهُ*** | Scattered **bones** |
| **75/9** | ***جُمِعَ** ٱلشَّمْسُ وَٱلْقَمَرُ* | **Sun and moon** |
| **75/17** | *إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا **جَمْعَهُ*** | **The Qurʾān** |

The man says "you cannot gather my bones". The sūra first shows that it has brought sun and moon together, then says that the gathering of the Qurʾān belongs to itself. Three gatherings, three scales: a man's skeleton, the heavenly bodies, a text.

**I offer this as my own reading;** the three occurrences themselves are data of the text and can be counted.

### عِظَام — bones

**Root: ع-ظ-م.** And the second branch of this root is famous: **عَظِيم** (*ʿaẓīm*) — great, exalted; **تَعْظِيم** (*taʿẓīm*) — magnifying, exalting.

The explanation the lexicographers transmit: bone is the body's **frame**, its carrier, its hardest and most lasting part; the sense of "greatness" was born of this bearing function. I transmit this explanation; I issue no definite ruling on the historical development of the root.

**Why does the verse say "bone"?**

Because bone is what **remains** after death. The flesh dissolves, the bone stays; and in the end the bone too crumbles. Bone, then, is the name of the last stage of decay. The man who objects is not pointing to the least durable place but to the vanishing of even the most durable one.

The Qurʾān transmits this objection elsewhere with the same word:

> *"He has coined a likeness for Us and forgotten his own creation: 'Who will give life to these **bones** when they have crumbled?' he said."* (Yāsīn 36/78)

> *"Look at the **bones**, how We join them together and set them in their places, then clothe them with flesh."* (al-Baqara 2/259)

The second verse is especially striking: there too the verb is about **joining**, and there too it is shown to the objector with his own eyes.

### بَلَىٰ — the answer that breaks a negation

This particle was treated in the discussion of al-Inshiqāq 84/15. The finding recorded there was this: **balā** must not be confused with *naʿam* (yes); *naʿam* affirms a positive question, whereas *balā* **breaks** a **negative** sentence or question. "Did he not come?" — "*Balā* (no, he came)." The Qurʾān's most famous *balā* is at al-Aʿrāf 7/172.

The negation broken here is the **لَّن** in the preceding verse (*allan najmaʿa*) — "that We shall never gather". The verse takes that *lan* and snaps it.

**The structural likeness to al-Inshiqāq:** there too a person was framing a negative sentence (*ẓanna an lan yaḥūra* — he supposed he would not return), and the next verse opened with *balā*. The two sūras make the same move: a wrong opinion is transmitted, then broken with a single word.

The difference is here: after its *balā*, al-Inshiqāq says *"his Lord was seeing him"* — it turns to the past. Al-Qiyāma says *"We are able"* — it turns to power. One answers with knowledge, the other with might.

### قَٰدِرِينَ — grammar

The word is **accusative** and **plural**: *qādirīna*. Why?

The grammarians' explanation: after *balā* a verb is understood — *balā najmaʿuhā qādirīna* ("yes, We shall gather them — being able to"). *Qādirīna* is the **circumstantial qualifier** of that understood verb. Its being plural comes from the subject of the sentence being a plural of majesty (*najmaʿa* — "We gather").

Its counterpart in meaning is this: the answer is not "We shall gather" but **"We shall gather — and with power to spare."** A circumstantial qualifier states the manner in which the act is done. That is, the work is done not with strain but with a surplus of power.

### بَنَان — fingertips

**Root: ب-ن-ن.** *Banān* is used in Arabic for the **fingertips** or the **fingers**; the lexicographers transmit that the word points in particular to the end joint of the finger.

**The logic of the verse: an *a fortiori* proof.**

The man says the bones cannot be gathered. The answer comes by saying something greater than that: far from gathering the bones, We are able **to set the fingertips in order**.

Why the fingertip? Because that is the smallest place in the body, the most finely detailed, the one made of the most parts. There is a difference of scale between putting a skeleton roughly together and seating the finger-joints one by one in their places. The verse makes its proof not out of the largest thing but out of **the smallest**.

**Two explanations given by the classical exegetes:**

| Reading | Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Rebuilding the detail** | "We are able to restore even the fingertips to what they were" — the repair of the finest structure | The sense of the verb *sawwā* as "to set in order, to get the proportions right"; the *a fortiori* logic |
| **Levelling** | "If We wished We would make his fingers a single undivided piece, like a camel's hoof" — that is, the fingers being separate is already a favour | The sense of the verb *sawwā* as "to level, to make equal"; the "equivalence" branch of the root |

Both readings are found in the classical sources and both come out of the root's real semantic field. **The first reading fits the context better**, because the verse stands inside an argument about resurrection and the subject is rebuilding. But the second is not contrary to the text either, and it has a surprising side: as a proof of power it shows not destruction but **the fineness of what has been given**. I state my preference, but **it is not binding.**

### The matter of the fingerprint

In the modern period this verse has been widely tied to the uniqueness of the fingerprint. Here one must speak briefly and plainly.

The wording of the verse does not speak of the **print** of the finger; *banān* is the finger itself, not the mark it leaves. Nor is the proof the verse builds about uniqueness; it is about **scale**: the one who can set the small in order is *a fortiori* able to gather the large.

In the section on Sūrat al-ʿAlaq, on the occasion of the word *ʿalaqa*, why such readings cannot be counted as proof was treated in detail on five grounds, and I keep the same position here; I do not repeat the grounds. In brief: to nail a verse to a current scientific description is to make it share the fate of that description; and the verse's own work is not to give information.

**To sum up:** the meaning of the word is "fingertips", and the proof of the verse is a proof from scale. That a reader, reading this verse, should think of the fineness of the fingertips and draw from it a measure of wonder is his own right. But this cannot be presented as the verse's claim, nor turned into a proof of miracle. **I mention it as an attempted reading and pass on; it is not proof.**

### نُسَوِّىَ — to set in order

**Root: س-و-ي.** This root was treated in detail in the discussion of al-Shams 91/7. The summary of the finding there: the idea beneath the root is **equality and equivalence**; in Form II (*sawwā*) the verb becomes transitive and intensive — **to bring the parts of a thing into correspondence with one another, to get its proportions right, to make it complete and without flaw.**

I do not repeat it here. What belongs to this place is the verb's **object**.

In the Qurʾān the object of *sawwā* is generally a whole: *fa-sawwāka* (al-Infiṭār 82/7 — "He proportioned you"), *wa nafsin wa mā sawwāhā* (al-Shams 91/7 — the soul). Here the object is a **part**, and the smallest at that: *banānah*.

The scale is made smaller and the proof grows stronger. The verb is the same, the claim is the same; the only thing that changes is the size of the place pointed to.

**And this verb will come once more at the end of the sūra:** in verse 38, *fa-khalaqa fa-sawwā*. There the object is a whole again — man himself. That is, the sūra opens with *sawwā* (the fingertip) and closes with *sawwā* (man himself). I return to the link between the two at verse 38.

## 75/5 — بَلْ يُرِيدُ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ لِيَفْجُرَ أَمَامَهُۥ

*Bal yurīdu'l-insānu li-yafjura amāmah*
"In truth, man wants to break open what lies ahead of him."

The sūra's verse of diagnosis. And one of its hardest to translate.

### بَلْ — *iḍrāb*

**بَلْ** in Arabic is the particle of *iḍrāb*: leaving what has been said and passing to something else. It works in two ways — either it cancels the previous judgement, or it drops it and moves to what is more important.

Here the second is at work. The third verse had described an **opinion**: the man supposes the bones cannot be gathered. The fifth verse strikes out that opinion and says what lies beneath it: **the real matter is not what he supposes but what he wants.**

**This is the sharpest move in the sūra.** Denial is described not as a lack of knowledge but as a **choice of the will**. The man does not reject because he can find no proof; he does not look for proof because he wants to reject.

The finding recorded for the root of *kufr* in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/6 sits exactly here: the root means **to cover**, not to be ignorant; *kufr* is covering over what one knows. Al-Qiyāma 75/5 states the **motive** of that act of covering.

### يُرِيدُ — he wants

**Root: ر-و-د.** Its concrete sense is interesting: **to go back and forth in search of a place, to scout.** The *rāʾid* is the scout who goes ahead of the tribe looking for pasture and water. From the same root come *irāda* (willing) and *ruwayd* (gently).

So at the root of the verb "to want" there is a **movement of searching**: the one who wants tests out the road to what he wants.

This suits the verse: the man wants something and is looking for a road that suits that want. The road he is looking for will appear in the sixth verse — a question.

### لِيَفْجُرَ — to break open

**Root: ف-ج-ر.** This root was treated in detail in two places in this commentary and I do not repeat them:

- **In the sections on al-Shams and al-Infiṭār:** its concrete sense is **to split, to split and make flow, to burst out in a gush.** *Fajr* — the moment when the night is split and the light gushes out. *Tafjīr* — making water burst out by splitting. And in the moral branch: **fujūr** — the tearing of the limit that holds a person; the *fājir* is the one who splits the covering over him and comes out, who breaches the boundary that holds him in. The finding recorded there was this: *fujūr* is not a deficiency but an **overflow** — like water that will not fit its vessel breaking the dam.
- **In the section on al-Fajr:** the comparison of the root with *falq*.

**What belongs to this place is that the verb has no object.**

The verse does not say "he wants to sin". The verb *yafjura* has no object; in its place there is an **adverbial**: **أَمَامَهُ** — "what is ahead of him". So what is split is not a prohibition, a limit or a rule — it is **time.**

### أَمَام — ahead

**Root: أ-م-م.** And the family of this root is striking: *umm* (mother), *umma* (a community), *imām* (the one standing in front, the one who goes ahead), *amām* (the space in front).

The shared idea: **priority, being in front, being at the source.** The mother is the one who comes first; the imam is the one who stands in front; *amām* is the place that is in front.

So *amāmahu* — "his front", the space ahead of him, future time.

### The sense of the expression

Now the table of disagreement:

| Reading | Explanation | Ground | Weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Filling the time ahead with sin** | "He wants to commit *fujūr* throughout the life ahead of him" — the reason he rejects the resurrection is to keep his front clear | The moral sense of *fujūr*; the context is looking for the motive of denial | The verb's coming without an object is not fully accounted for on this reading |
| **Keeping his front open, deferring the reckoning** | By pushing the resurrection further off he wants to leave an empty space ahead of him; "splitting" here is getting past an obstacle | The verb's lack of an object; the word *amām* denoting time; verse 6 showing this directly | It lightens the settled moral weight of *fujūr* |
| **Lying, slandering** | One branch of *fujūr* is lying too; "to throw a lie out ahead of him" | This branch of the root really does exist | Weak link with the context; the adverbial *amām* is left unexplained |

**The second reading is the most consistent when read together with the sixth verse**, and its ground is in the text itself: the fifth verse says "he wants to break open what lies ahead of him", and the sixth shows immediately afterwards how that want comes to speech — *"and when is that Day of Rising?"* That is, the concrete form of breaking open one's front is to make the date uncertain. **I state my preference; it is not binding**, and the first reading is the most widespread in the classical sources.

**Let us describe the picture.** There is a wall in front of a person: the reckoning. The verb *fajara* speaks of splitting that wall, of piercing it and passing through, of leaving an open space in front of oneself. What is split is neither a rock nor a sky; it is **the person's own future.** The man wants to pierce what he knows stands ahead of him and make an open road beyond it.

And the root's real sense works exactly here: *fajr* is the splitting of the night and the gushing of light — that is, the opening of what was closed. The man too wants an opening, but what he opens gives on to emptiness, not light.

**I record this as my own reading;** the semantic field of the root and the function of the adverbial are data of the language.

### A parallel in the reverse direction

The same idea is set up from the other side in Sūrat al-Insān:

> **وَيَذَرُونَ وَرَآءَهُمْ يَوْمًا ثَقِيلًا** — *"And they leave a heavy day behind them."* (al-Insān 76/27)

There what is left is placed **behind** (*warāʾ*); here what is split stands **in front** (*amām*). The two verses tell the same movement from two directions: piercing the reckoning from the front and throwing it behind are two names for the same act.

And the two sūras meet not only in this picture but in a word: in both, this life is called **al-ʿājila**. I return to this at verse 20.

## 75/6 — يَسْـَٔلُ أَيَّانَ يَوْمُ ٱلْقِيَٰمَةِ

*Yasʾalu ayyāna yawmu'l-qiyāma*
"He asks: 'And when is that Day of Rising?'"

### أَيَّانَ — "when"

Arabic has several particles for asking about time, and **مَتَىٰ** (*matā*) is the most common. The verse does not use it; it uses the word **أَيَّانَ** (*ayyāna*).

The distinction some of the lexicographers transmit is this: *ayyāna* is used for asking about a time that is far off and great; it carries a weight in it. **I transmit this distinction and build no ruling on it**, because it cannot be presented as a distinction settled among the lexicographers.

Looking at the places where the Qurʾān uses this word, a pattern appears: the word occurs almost always in **mocking questions about the resurrection** — *"ayyāna yawmu'd-dīn"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/12), *"ayyāna mursāhā"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/187; al-Nāziʿāt 79/42). That is, in the Qurʾān's own usage the word has become the sign of a type of question.

### The mood of the question

The first thing to notice in the sentence is this: the question is not **"will it happen?"** but **"when?"**

On the face of it this is an acceptance. Someone who asks the time of a thing is taken to have accepted that it will happen. But there is no acceptance here; the question comes **in the guise of acceptance**.

The finding recorded for the particle *idhā* in the section on al-Inshiqāq looks straight at this: the Qurʾān opens its scenes of the resurrection with *idhā* almost without exception, because what is in dispute is not "will it happen" but "when". Now note: **the one addressed is also asking "when".**

The same question, in two mouths:

| | **The Qurʾān's mood** (*idhā*) | **The addressee's question** (*ayyāna*) |
|---|---|---|
| What is accepted | That it will happen, for certain | That it will happen, apparently |
| What is unknown | Its time | Its time |
| Its tone | Statement | Challenge |
| What its answer does | Changes nothing | Changes nothing at all |

The difference is in tone; and the difference in tone determines whether the answer will be taken or not.

### The question as an instrument of deferral

In the discussion of al-Baqara 2/67-71 — the story of the cow — this matter was treated in detail. The summary of the finding there was this:

**Asking questions is not itself condemned.** In the same sūra the angels asked a question and their questions were answered. What is condemned is not curiosity but **deferral**: here the question is not an instrument of knowledge but an instrument of delay — and the production of a new question each time an answer comes shows this as well. And in that story what made the command heavier was not the one who gave it but the one who received it.

I do not repeat it here. What belongs to this place is this: **in Sūrat al-Qiyāma the link between question and deferral is not drawn out of a story; it is set up by placing two verses side by side.**

> Verse 5: **he wants to break open what lies ahead of him.**
> Verse 6: **he asks, "and when is it?"**

The fifth verse gives the intention, the sixth the instrument. There is not even a conjunction between them — the sentences have been set side by side and the reader makes the relation.

And it is not hard to see how the question serves deferral: if the date of a thing is uncertain, that thing does not bind today. As long as the answer to "when?" is not given, the one who asks may not count himself under any judgement. The question suspends the judgement.

### The Qurʾān does not answer the question

And now the most meaningful move in the text.

The sixth verse asks a "when" question. The seventh verse gives no date. It gives no year, no sign, no token. It opens **the scene** directly:

> **فَإِذَا بَرِقَ ٱلْبَصَرُ** — "So when the eye is dazzled…"

That is, the question is asked as "when?" and the answer comes as "how it will be".

This is not an evasion but a diagnosis. The text gives no information because it sees that the question is not a request for information. Instead it shows that the answer to the question is not a date: what is being asked about is not an entry in a calendar but a **state**.

The Qurʾān explicitly reserves knowledge of the time of the resurrection to itself elsewhere: *"Its knowledge is only with my Lord"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/187). So the withholding of an answer is not a stylistic choice peculiar to this sūra; it is the text's consistent position.

**And an observation, as my own reading:** the sūra does not answer this man's question — but in the tenth verse it **makes the same man ask a question.** This time the question is not "when" but "where":

> *"On that day man says: 'Where is the place of escape?'"* (75/10)

The first question was a challenge; the second is helplessness. And the one who asks them both is, in the sūra's own wording, the same person named with the same word: **ٱلْإِنسَٰن**. The sūra places the scene of the resurrection between one man's two questions.

## 75/7-9 — فَإِذَا بَرِقَ ٱلْبَصَرُ · وَخَسَفَ ٱلْقَمَرُ · وَجُمِعَ ٱلشَّمْسُ وَٱلْقَمَرُ

*Fa-idhā bariqa'l-baṣar · Wa khasafa'l-qamar · Wa jumiʿa'sh-shamsu wa'l-qamar*
"When the eye is dazzled, and the moon is swallowed, and sun and moon are brought together."

### فَإِذَا — a condition whose answer comes

In the section on al-Inshiqāq the difference between *idhā* and *in* was treated: *in* is used for what is doubtful, *idhā* for what will certainly happen and whose time alone is unknown. And as was recorded there, the Qurʾān opens its scenes of the resurrection with *idhā* almost without exception.

The difference here is this: **in al-Inshiqāq the answer to *idhā* was not given**, the sentence was left hanging. In al-Qiyāma the answer comes openly — the tenth verse: *"On that day man says…"*

That is, the same structure, two different uses in two sūras. Al-Inshiqāq left a gap and drew the reader into it; al-Qiyāma leaves no gap, because in the sixth verse a question has been asked and that question has someone who asked it.

The **فَ** at the head shows this too: it ties back to the preceding verse. You ask "and when is it?" — **then** this is what will happen.

### بَرِقَ ٱلْبَصَرُ — the dazzling of the eye

**Root: ب-ر-ق.** Its concrete sense is **lightning, flashing, a sudden light.** *Barq* — lightning; *bāriqa* — a thing that flashes; *ibrīq* (a shining vessel) is also related to this root.

The expression *bariqa'l-baṣar* describes the eye's **being dazzled, frozen, made unable to blink** before a sudden light or a terror. Think what the eye does when lightning strikes: for an instant sight is cut off, the eye stays open but can make nothing out.

**Two variant readings are transmitted for the word**, and both turn on the senses of dazzling and freezing; no great difference arises in the meaning that results. I do not say which reading belongs to which authority of the readings, because I am not sure.

**The Qurʾān describes the same state with another word as well:**

> *"He only defers them to a day on which the eyes will **stare fixedly** (*tashkhaṣu fīhi'l-abṣār*)."* (Ibrāhīm 14/42)

Both descriptions look at the same place: the eye is open, but it has lost the function of seeing.

**And the order of the scene is striking.** The verse describes not the sky first but **the eye**. Descriptions of the resurrection generally come from outside inward — the sky splits, the earth quakes, then it turns to man. Here it is reversed: first the eye, then the moon, then the sun.

That is, the scene opens **from the eye of the one who looks**. What is told is not an event but an **experience**. I record this as my own reading; the ordering is the text's.

### خَسَفَ ٱلْقَمَرُ — the swallowing of the moon

**Root: خ-س-ف.** And the concrete sense of this root is far harsher than what the English "eclipse" conveys: **to collapse, to sink, to be buried in the ground, to cave in.**

The Qurʾān uses the same root in an account of destruction:

> **فَخَسَفْنَا بِهِۦ وَبِدَارِهِ ٱلْأَرْضَ** — *"So We made the earth swallow him and his house."* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/81)

There the earth **swallows** a man and his house. The same verb.

Used of the heavenly bodies, *khusūf* is the moon's losing its light, going dark, sinking. But the concrete weight of the root is always the same: a thing's disappearing by collapse.

**The choice of verb says something.** The verse does not say "the moon's light went", nor "the moon was extinguished". The root's real sense is collapse: the moon is swallowed as the earth swallows a man.

### وَجُمِعَ ٱلشَّمْسُ وَٱلْقَمَرُ — the bringing together of sun and moon

**A point of grammar.** The verb is **جُمِعَ** — masculine singular, passive. Yet there are two subjects, and the first (*al-shams*) is feminine. What would be expected is *jumiʿati'sh-shamsu wa'l-qamar*.

The grammarians' explanation: when the verb comes before its subject it does not take the dual or plural marker, and it may also agree with **the nearer subject**, or stay singular when the two subjects are counted as one whole. Both are at work here: the two bodies are handled as the object of a single operation, as though a single whole.

**The verb is also passive:** *jumiʿa* — "they were brought together". Who brought them is not said. As was recorded in the section on al-Takwīr, the concealing of the agent in descriptions of the resurrection is a settled preference of the Qurʾān.

**What does "being gathered" mean?** The explanations given by the classical exegetes:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Union in light** | Both lose their light; the difference between them disappears, the two become one |
| **Union in position** | The two are gathered in one place; the order that separates night from day is broken |
| **Union in outcome** | The two are brought together and driven to the same end |

All three are in the classical sources, and there is no indication requiring a preference among them. **I state no preference.**

But it is worth dwelling on what the three have in common: **the difference between sun and moon is the difference by which time is measured.** What separates night from day, month from year, what makes the calendar, is that these two move separately. When the two are brought together, no time is left to measure.

**And this is the silent answer the sūra gives to the question in the sixth verse.** The man asked "when?" The scene opens with the joining of the two instruments by which time is measured. The scale on which an answer to the question could be given disappears at the moment the question is answered.

I record this as my own reading; that the two bodies are a measure of time is the Qurʾān's own statement: *"He made the sun a radiance and the moon a light, and determined its stages that you might know the number of the years and the reckoning"* (Yūnus 10/5).

## 75/10 — يَقُولُ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ يَوْمَئِذٍ أَيْنَ ٱلْمَفَرُّ

*Yaqūlu'l-insānu yawmaʾidhin ayna'l-mafarr*
"On that day man says: 'Where is the place of escape?'"

### Two questions, one man

Here the sūra sets down the counterpart of the sixth verse and brings the two questions face to face:

| | **75/6** | **75/10** |
|---|---|---|
| Interrogative | **أَيَّانَ** — when | **أَيْنَ** — where |
| Who asks | *al-insān* (subject implicit) | *al-insān* (stated openly) |
| Its time | Now | On that day |
| Its subject | The time of the event | His own condition |
| Its tone | Challenge | Helplessness |
| Its answer | Not given | **Given:** "There is no refuge" |

**The sūra's verdict is hidden here.** The man asked the first question and got no answer; he asks the second and gets one. That is, the matter is not whether the information is given but when the question is asked. The same man, the same subject, two different moments — and in the second moment the information is of no use.

And note: **both questions ask about place and time.** Right up to the last moment the man is looking for a coordinate. What he wants to find is not a truth but a way out.

### ٱلْمَفَرّ — escape / a place of escape

**Root: ف-ر-ر.** To flee, to run away. *Fārr* — one who flees; *firār* — flight. (In Turkish the borrowed word *firar*, "desertion, absconding", comes straight from this root.)

The word **مَفَرّ** can be read in two ways, and both are possible in the language:

| Reading | Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **مَفَرّ** (noun of place) | *mafʿal* | The **place** to flee to |
| **مَفَرّ** (verbal noun with *mīm*) | *mafʿal* | The **act of fleeing** itself |

On the first reading the question is "where shall I flee to?"; on the second, "where is escape, is there such a thing as escape?" The answer in the next verse (*lā wazara* — there is no refuge) fits the first reading better; but the second does not contradict the answer either.

**The most striking use of the same root in the Qurʾān is a command:**

> **فَفِرُّوٓا۟ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ** — *"So flee to God."* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/50)

The same word, the opposite direction. In this world it is said "flee to God"; on that day it is asked "where shall I flee from God?"

And the twelfth verse will close this tension: the address sought as a place to flee to is the very address one is asked to flee to.

## 75/11-12 — كَلَّا لَا وَزَرَ · إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْمُسْتَقَرُّ

*Kallā lā wazar · Ilā Rabbika yawmaʾidhini'l-mustaqarr*
"No! There is no refuge. That day, the place of rest is with your Lord alone."

### كَلَّا — the particle that cuts off

**كَلَّا** in Arabic is the particle that cuts off a statement and rejects it: "no, it is not so." In the Qurʾān it comes almost always in Meccan sūras and after an objection.

**It occurs three times in this sūra:** 11, 20, 26. And all three stand at the head of a block:

| Verse | What it cuts off | What it opens |
|---|---|---|
| **11** | The question "where is the place of escape?" | The judgement that there is no refuge |
| **20** | The distance of the scene | Direct address to the one addressed |
| **26** | The whole argument | The scene of the moment of death |

So the particle is the sūra's section-mark. When the ear hears *kallā* it knows it has entered a new block.

### وَزَر — refuge

**Root: و-ز-ر.** And this word meets us for the second time in this commentary — but with an entirely different meaning.

The same root was treated in the discussion of al-Inshirāḥ 94/2: **وِزْر** (*wizr*) — a **heavy burden**, and from there **sin**. What was recorded there: the *wazīr* is the one who shares the ruler's burden; when Moses (Mūsā) asked his Lord for a *wazīr* he was asking for a partner in the load; and in al-Inshirāḥ the *wizr* was a weight that was taken down.

**Here the same root comes in the sense of "refuge".** *Wazar* — the rock on a mountain that one shelters behind, a stronghold, a place of retreat.

**How are the two senses connected?**

The explanation the lexicographers transmit is this: at the centre of the root there is the idea of **weight and mass**. The *wazar* is the steep and **heavy** mass of rock on the mountain — the thing a person shelters behind. The *wizr* is the **weight** that is carried. The *wazīr* is the one who shares the weight.

That is, the same family of words names both **what is loaded on you** and **what stands under you as support**. Because both are weight: one rests on top, one underneath.

**I offer this joining as my own reading;** that both senses are present in the root is lexical data, the division of labour between them is a link I have made.

**And now a confirmation from within the Qurʾān:**

> **أَلَّا تَزِرُ وَازِرَةٌ وِزْرَ أُخْرَىٰ** — *"No bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another."* (al-Najm 53/38)

In this sentence three derivatives of the root stand together: *taziru* (bears), *wāzira* (a bearer of burden), *wizr* (burden). And what it says is: **the burden cannot be transferred.**

Now set the two verses side by side:

| | **al-Qiyāma 75/11** | **al-Najm 53/38** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *lā wazara* | *lā taziru wāziratun wizra ukhrā* |
| What is denied | **A refuge** | **The transfer of the burden** |
| Root | و-ز-ر | و-ز-ر |

**The same root carries two denials at once: on that day there is neither anything to hide behind, nor anyone on whom to load your burden.** There are two roads of escape — to hide, or to put the responsibility on someone else — and the Qurʾān closes both with a word from the same root.

I record this as an observation; the sharing of the root is a datum of the text.

### The structure of the sentence

**لَا وَزَرَ** — the grammarians call this use of *lā* the ***lā* that negates the genus**: it negates the whole class. Not "there is no refuge" but **"there is no such thing as a refuge"**. The *fatḥa* at the end of the noun (*wazara*) is the sign of this structure.

The word's coming indefinite works in the same direction: no kind is specified, and no kind of it exists.

### إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ — fronting

The word order of the sentence is: **ilā Rabbika** — *yawmaʾidhin* — **al-mustaqarr.**

The expected order would be *al-mustaqarru ilā Rabbika yawmaʾidhin*. The verse draws the element that states place **to the head of the sentence**.

In Arabic this fronting conveys **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*): the place of arrival is **only** He, and no other. And it fits the context exactly — because the previous sentence had said "there is no other place". In the tenth verse it was asked "where?"; the eleventh said "not there"; the twelfth says "here".

**There is one further grammatical detail.** *Mustaqarr* is "the place where one comes to rest", and it normally takes *fī* (in) — one comes to rest *in* a place. The verse uses *ilā*: the particle of **direction**. That is, the place of settling is described not as a location but as an **arrival**.

### ٱلْمُسْتَقَرّ — the place of rest

**Root: ق-ر-ر.** To settle, to stand fixed, to stay in a place. Its derivatives are wide — and English happens to hold the same double sense in *settle*: a thing settles, and a matter is settled:

- **قَرَار** (*qarār*) — both "fixity, coming to rest" and "a decision reached". Both from the same idea: a thing's sitting down, coming into its place.
- **ٱسْتِقْرَار** (*istiqrār*) — settledness, stability.
- **مُسْتَقَرّ** (*mustaqarr*) — a noun of place from Form X: the place where rest is taken.
- **قُرَّة** (*qurra*) — coolness of the eye. The expression *qurratu ʿayn* comes in Arabic from the picture of the eye's staying in place and not roving: an eye that seeks nothing else to look at.

This last derivative is striking. The same root names both peace (the eye no longer searches) and arrival (the journey is over).

**And a symmetry is set up within the sūra.** This sentence will be repeated almost word for word in the thirtieth verse:

> **إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْمُسْتَقَرُّ** (75/12)
> **إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْمَسَاقُ** (75/30)

The same three elements in the same order; the only word that changes is the last. I return to this at verse 30.

### The link with al-Inshiqāq

At al-Inshiqāq 84/6 this occurred: **إِنَّكَ كَادِحٌ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ كَدْحًا** — *"You are toiling towards your Lord with much toil."*

The finding recorded there was this: the crucial element of the verse is the particle *ilā*; whatever man does, the place he goes to is the same, and this is not a command but a statement of fact.

**Three verses, the same two words:**

| Place | Expression | What it states |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Inshiqāq 84/6** | *kādiḥun **ilā Rabbika*** | The direction of the journey |
| **al-Qiyāma 75/12** | ***ilā Rabbika** al-mustaqarr* | The end of the journey |
| **al-Qiyāma 75/30** | ***ilā Rabbika** al-masāq* | The driving of the journey |

Al-Inshiqāq states the walking, al-Qiyāma the arrival and the being driven. Together the three build a single sentence: the direction is given, the arrival is settled, and the going is not in the person's hands.

I draw this link from the expression the two sūras share; the sharing of the expression is in the text.

## 75/13 — يُنَبَّؤُا۟ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ يَوْمَئِذٍۭ بِمَا قَدَّمَ وَأَخَّرَ

*Yunabbaʾu'l-insānu yawmaʾidhin bimā qaddama wa akhkhar*
"On that day man is informed of all that he sent ahead and all that he left behind."

### يُنَبَّأُ — he is informed

**Root: ن-ب-أ.** And this root is distinct from the general Arabic word for "news".

The distinction the lexicographers draw is this: **خَبَر** (*khabar*) is any kind of report; **نَبَأ** (*nabaʾ*) is a report that is **important, consequential, of great benefit**. Every *nabaʾ* is a report, but not every report is a *nabaʾ*.

From the same root comes **نَبِىّ** (*nabī*) — the one who brings news, or the one to whom news is brought. (Its being related instead to the root *nabwa*, "high ground", is also transmitted; the lexicographers differ between the two derivations and I state no preference here.)

**The pattern: تَفْعِيل (*nabbaʾa*), passive (*yunabbaʾu*).** Form II here conveys **going into detail**: not a summary report but item-by-item notification. And the verb is passive: who gives the report is not said.

**What the verse does looks like a transfer of information.** But the next verse will turn this around: the one who is informed already knows.

### بِمَا قَدَّمَ وَأَخَّرَ — "what he sent ahead and what he left behind"

One of the most discussed expressions in the sūra.

**The root ق-د-م:** to go in front, to be ahead. And at the concrete centre of the root is an organ: **قَدَم** (*qadam*) — **the foot.** The foot is the part of the body that goes forward. From the same root, *taqdīm* (putting forward), *muqaddima* (what is placed in front), *qadīm* (what is earlier, old).

**The root أ-خ-ر:** to leave behind, to put off to later. From the same root, *ākhir* (last), *ākhira* (what comes after), *taʾkhīr* (deferral), *ukhrā* (the other one).

**The two verbs set up an exact opposition: to send forward / to leave behind.**

The views:

| Reading | Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **What he did and what he did not do** | *Qaddama*: the deeds he performed. *Akhkhara*: what he ought to have done and left off, deferring it | The opposition of the two verbs; omission counts as a deed too |
| **What he did first and what he did later** | The whole of his life from beginning to end; no period is left outside | The comprehensiveness of the pattern: name the two ends and the middle fills in |
| **What he did himself and what he left behind him** | *Qaddama*: his own deed. *Akhkhara*: the effect he left behind him, a good or a bad precedent | Yāsīn 36/12: *"We write what they did and their **traces**"* |

**All three readings are in the classical sources and they do not exclude one another.** The third is worth recording separately because it finds direct support from the Qurʾān:

> **إِنَّا نَحْنُ نُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ وَنَكْتُبُ مَا قَدَّمُوا۟ وَءَاثَٰرَهُمْ** — *"It is We who give life to the dead, and We write what they sent ahead and the traces they left."* (Yāsīn 36/12)

The same verb (*qaddamū*), the same structure. And that verse begins with the expression **نُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ** ("We give life to the dead") — with which Sūrat al-Qiyāma will end exactly (75/40: *an yuḥyiya'l-mawtā*). The two verses hold the same subject from two ends.

### The link with the fifth verse

And now one of the finest connections inside the sūra.

> **Verse 5:** *li-yafjura **أَمَامَهُ*** — he wants to break open **what lies ahead of him**.
> **Verse 13:** *bimā **قَدَّمَ** wa akhkhar* — he is informed of **what he sent ahead**.

The two verses use the same language of space: **front and back.**

In the fifth verse the man wanted to open up the space ahead of him — to empty out the future. In the thirteenth he is informed of the things he has **sent** ahead of him.

So the space he thought was empty was not empty. While the man was labouring to clear his front, he was sending things on ahead of him.

**I record this link as my own reading;** that the two verses use the same language of space is a datum of the text.

## 75/14-15 — بَلِ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِۦ بَصِيرَةٌ · وَلَوْ أَلْقَىٰ مَعَاذِيرَهُۥ

*Bali'l-insānu ʿalā nafsihī baṣīra · Wa law alqā maʿādhīrah*
"In truth man is a clear sight upon himself — even if he flings out his excuses."

**The heart of the sūra.** And one of the densest sentences in the Qurʾān about man.

### بَلْ — the second *iḍrāb*

The sūra uses this particle for the second time (the first was in verse 5). And it does the same work again: it leaves what has been said and passes to the real matter.

The previous verse had said "man is informed". This verse strikes that sentence out: **there is no need to inform him — he already knows.**

This is no ordinary correction. The thirteenth verse had set up the reckoning as a **notification**: one side reports, the other side learns. The fourteenth breaks that structure and carries the reckoning **into the person himself.**

### بَصِيرَة — clear sight

**Root: ب-ص-ر.** *Baṣar* — the eye, sight. *Baṣīr* — the one who sees. *Ibṣār* — seeing. And **بَصِيرَة** (*baṣīra*) — **inner sight, discernment; and proof, plain evidence.**

The Qurʾān uses this word in one place as the name of a stance:

> **قُلْ هَٰذِهِۦ سَبِيلِىٓ أَدْعُوٓا۟ إِلَى ٱللَّهِ عَلَىٰ بَصِيرَةٍ** — *"Say: This is my way; I and those who follow me call to God upon clear sight."* (Yūsuf 12/108)

There *baṣīra* is the name of a call made not blindly but **with sight.**

### A grammatical question: why بَصِيرَة and not بَصِير?

The verse does not say "man is *baṣīr* upon himself", it says **baṣīra**. Although the subject (*al-insān*) is masculine, the predicate is feminine. This is a point the grammarians have dwelt on at length.

| View | Explanation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **The ة is for intensity** | In Arabic the ة at the end of certain adjectives conveys not the feminine but **excess**: *rāwiya* (who transmits much), *ʿallāma* (who knows much), *nassāba* | "Man sees himself **very well**" |
| ***Baṣīra* means "proof"** | Here the word is not an adjective but a noun: *ḥujja*, plain evidence. "Man is a **proof** against himself" | A person is a witness in his own case |
| **There is an understood noun qualified** | *ʿalā nafsihī* [ʿayn] *baṣīra* — "there is a seeing eye upon him", or his limbs are meant | It requires an ellipsis |

**The first and the second readings are both strong in the classical sources, and both work in the text.**

But the second joins far better with the next verse. Because the fifteenth verse speaks of **excuses** — and an excuse is a courtroom word. A defendant offers an excuse. If man is a **proof**, then the uselessness of an excuse is the natural consequence: a proof is not removed by a defence.

**My preference is the second reading**, and my ground is the courtroom language of the fifteenth verse. **It is not binding;** the first reading also suits the text, and in fact the two readings come out at the same place — a person who sees himself very well is already a proof against himself.

### عَلَىٰ — "upon" or "against"?

The finest word in the verse is this particle.

In Arabic the difference between **عَلَىٰ** (*ʿalā*) and **لِـ** (*li-*) is a legal difference: *lahu* "in his favour", *ʿalayhi* "against him". English makes the same distinction with **for** and **against**: a witness *for* the defence, a witness *against* him.

The verse says **عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِ**: **against himself.**

That is, the sentence does not say "man sees himself". It says **"man sees against himself / man is proof against himself"**. The knowledge is not neutral knowledge; it is knowledge that takes a side — and the side it takes is opposite its owner.

**The Qurʾān says this explicitly in another place:**

> **ٱقْرَأْ كِتَٰبَكَ كَفَىٰ بِنَفْسِكَ ٱلْيَوْمَ عَلَيْكَ حَسِيبًا** — *"Read your book! Today you are enough as a reckoner against yourself."* (al-Isrāʾ 17/14)

The same particle: **عَلَيْكَ.** And the same structure: a person is the officer of his own reckoning.

In the section on al-Inshiqāq this verse was cited to complete the scene of the giving of the book. Here it goes one step further: even if the book is not read, the person already knows.

### The joining with the second verse

And now the place where the sūra closes upon itself.

> **ٱلنَّفْس ٱللَّوَّامَة** (75/2) — the soul that reproaches itself.
> **ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ عَلَىٰ نَفْسِهِۦ بَصِيرَةٌ** (75/14) — man is proof against himself.

The relation between the two verses carries the whole argument of the sūra:

**What is sworn by in the second verse turns into a verdict in the fourteenth.**

If the soul reproaches itself, then in order to reproach it must **know**. Reproach is an operation of knowledge: the person knows what he did, knows what that act was, and compares the two. *Lawwāma* is an accounting at work.

And if that accounting is working, then the day of reckoning brings nothing new — **it opens what is already there.**

The only difference between the two verses is **time**:

| | **75/2** | **75/14** |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Reproaches | Becomes proof |
| When | Now, continually | On that day |
| Who knows | Only the person | Everyone |
| Its effect | Disquiet | Verdict |

What gives disquiet now gives a verdict on that day. The mechanism is the same; what changes is that its result becomes binding.

**This is the real answer the sūra gives to the question in the sixth verse.** The man had asked "when is that day?" The sūra gave no date. Instead it says this: **that day's court is already set up and running inside you.**

I offer this as my own reading; that the two verses pair the same object (*nafs*) with the same subject (man) is a datum of the text.

### مَعَاذِير — excuses

**Root: ع-ذ-ر.** *ʿUdhr* — an acceptable reason for a thing's not being done, or for its being done. *Maʿdhūr* — the one held excused. *Iʿtidhār* — apologising. (In Turkish the borrowed words *özür*, "apology", and *mazeret*, "excuse", come straight from this root.)

**The singular of the word is disputed**, and this dispute affects the meaning directly:

| Singular | Its meaning | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **مَعْذِرَة** (*maʿdhira*) | An excuse, a plea | *Maʿādhīr* — "excuses" |
| **مِعْذَار** (*miʿdhār*) | **A curtain, a covering** | *Maʿādhīr* — "curtains" |

The second reading is transmitted by some of the lexicographers and it is striking: when the word means "curtains", the verse is saying — **"even if he lets down his curtains."**

That is, an excuse is in the word's own logic an act of **covering**. The person sees himself; he draws a curtain in front of what he sees. The curtain does not remove what he saw, it only shuts it in.

**I transmit this second reading and build no ruling on it;** it cannot be presented as a settled preference among the lexicographers. But both readings come out at the same place: an excuse changes not the knowledge but its visibility.

And the finding recorded for the root of *kufr* at al-Baqara 2/6 works here once more: *kufr* is **covering**. Drawing a curtain is covering too. The two acts do the same work — one over the truth, the other over one's knowledge about oneself.

### أَلْقَىٰ — he flung

**Root: ل-ق-ي.** This root was treated in the discussion of al-Inshiqāq 84/4. The summary of the finding there: the root works in two directions — *laqiya* (he met, he came upon) and *alqā* (he threw, he cast, he brought out to face). **That is, "to throw" and "to meet" are from the same root: to throw a thing is to bring it out before you.**

I do not repeat it here. What belongs to this place is **the use of the same verb with two different subjects in two sūras:**

| Place | Sentence | Who throws | What is thrown | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Inshiqāq 84/4** | *wa **alqat** mā fīhā wa takhallat* | **The earth** | Everything in it | **It was emptied** |
| **al-Qiyāma 75/15** | *wa law **alqā** maʿādhīrah* | **Man** | His excuses | Nothing changed |

The two verbs are in the same pattern (Form IV, perfect). The earth threw out what was inside it and was emptied; man throws out his excuses and is not emptied.

**The difference lies in what is thrown.** The earth really does throw out what is inside it — the dead, the wealth, the traces. Man does not throw out what is inside him; he throws something **in front of** what is inside him. An excuse is not a thing taken out but a thing put in place.

I record this as my own reading; the verb's occurrence in the two places is a datum of the text.

### وَلَوْ — "even if"

**وَلَوْ** is in Arabic what is called *waṣliyya*: it states that a judgement holds even under the most extreme condition. In English, "even if…", "even though…".

This has two consequences:

**First: the verse does not prevent the production of excuses.** Man will produce excuses — that is not disputed, it is even set down as expected. The only thing said is that what is produced will be of no use.

**Second: the abundance of excuses is implied.** The word comes in the **plural** (*maʿādhīrah* — his excuses). Not a single defence but a heap.

And when these two details are put together a familiar picture appears: the person knows, but piles layer upon layer of explanation on top of what he knows. The verse does not deny the pile; it says **that what lies beneath it goes on being visible.**

### The change of verse-ending

The fourteenth and fifteenth verses depart from the sound-order of the preceding seven.

**Verses 7-13:** *al-baṣar, al-qamar, al-qamar, al-mafarr, wazar, al-mustaqarr, wa akhkhar* — seven verses in a row ending in **-ar**. A hard, short, closed series.

**Verses 14-15:** *baṣīrah, maʿādhīrah* — the long **ī** enters.

When the sound changes the subject changes too: the scene ends, the verdict begins. And the next block (16-19) will move to an entirely new sound.

## 75/16-19 — لَا تُحَرِّكْ بِهِۦ لِسَانَكَ لِتَعْجَلَ بِهِۦٓ · إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا جَمْعَهُۥ وَقُرْءَانَهُۥ · فَإِذَا قَرَأْنَٰهُ فَٱتَّبِعْ قُرْءَانَهُۥ · ثُمَّ إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا بَيَانَهُۥ

*Lā tuḥarrik bihī lisānaka li-taʿjala bih · Inna ʿalaynā jamʿahū wa qurʾānah · Fa-idhā qaraʾnāhu fa'ttabiʿ qurʾānah · Thumma inna ʿalaynā bayānah*
"Do not move your tongue with it to hurry it. Its gathering and its recitation are Ours. When We recite it, follow its recitation. Then its explanation too is Ours."

### What are these four verses doing here?

The sūra was telling a scene of the resurrection. In the fifteenth verse the verdict was given. And in the sixteenth, all at once, with no transitional sentence, comes **an instruction addressed to the Prophet.**

The grammarians call this a **جُمْلَة اعْتِرَاضِيَّة** (*jumla iʿtirāḍiyya*) — a parenthesis: a piece inserted into the telling with no grammatical link to what precedes or follows it.

In the classical commentaries the reason for these verses is explained thus: as the revelation was coming down, the Prophet, anxious not to forget or not to miss it, was **repeating the words simultaneously**; the verses forbid this. This explanation is widespread in the sources and agrees directly with the wording of the verse (moving the tongue).

**But the real question is this: why was it put here?**

The answer stands in the twentieth verse, and in a single root:

> **Verse 16:** *li-**تَعْجَلَ** bih* — to take it in **haste**.
> **Verse 20:** *bal tuḥibbūna'l-**عَاجِلَة*** — you love what is **at hand in haste**.

**The same root: ع-ج-ل.**

The parenthesis is tied to the sūra by one word: **haste.** And the manner of the tie is striking — because the two hastes are not the same:

| | **The Prophet's haste (16)** | **Man's haste (20)** |
|---|---|---|
| What is hurried | Receiving the revelation | The life that is at hand |
| Its motive | Fear of missing it | The pull of what is near |
| The text's attitude | **It is corrected** | **It is rejected** (*kallā*) |
| How it is met | By giving a guarantee | By making a diagnosis |

That is, the sūra sets two hastes from the same root side by side. One is well-meant, the other not; but both make the same mistake: **trying to seize the result before its time.**

**I offer this as my own reading;** the presence of the root in the two verses is a datum of the text and can be counted.

And this reading explains why the parenthesis does not stand there like a parenthesis. The subject of the sūra is the relation man builds with time: a man who in the fifth verse wants to break open his front, who in the sixth asks for a date, who in the twentieth loves what is at hand. The sixteenth verse shows the same matter in a flawless addressee and in its most innocent form.

### لَا تُحَرِّكْ بِهِ لِسَانَكَ

**The root ح-ر-ك:** motion, stirring. Form II (*ḥarraka*): to set moving. (In Turkish the borrowed words *hareket*, "movement", and *hareketli*, "lively", come from this root.)

The imperative is in the **prohibitive** mood: "do not move."

**The concreteness of the description is striking.** The verse does not say "do not hurry"; it says "do not move your tongue". What is forbidden is named not as an attitude but as a **movement of muscle**. This kind of concreteness in the Qurʾān was recorded for *ʿabasa* (he frowned) in the section on ʿAbasa as well: an inward state is named from the place where it shows, in the face or on the tongue.

To what does the pronoun **بِهِ** refer? There is no explicit antecedent in the text — the Qurʾān has not yet been mentioned. The classical explanation: because the indication is clear the pronoun refers to **the revelation**; the one addressed already knows what it is. This is the same structure as the use of *"We sent it down"* in Sūrat al-Qadr: an object known without being named.

### إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا جَمْعَهُ وَقُرْءَانَهُ

**The fronting of عَلَيْنَا.** The normal order of the sentence would be *inna jamʿahū wa qurʾānahū ʿalaynā*. The verse draws the element "upon Us" to the front — it conveys restriction: **this work belongs to Us, not to you.**

And this fronting comes **once more in the nineteenth verse**: *thumma inna ʿalaynā bayānah.*

**Four tasks are counted in four verses, and three are given to one side, one to the other:**

| Task | Word | To whom it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| Gathering | *jamʿ* | **To Us** |
| Reciting / causing to recite | *qurʾān* | **To Us** |
| Following | *ittibāʿ* | **To you** |
| Explaining | *bayān* | **To Us** |

**The only task that falls to man is to follow.** And that task too comes after the second place — that is, it is given first and followed afterwards.

This table is consistent with the sūra's whole account of man. In the third verse man supposed we could not do a certain thing; here the list of what will be done is drawn up and man's share is reduced to one.

### The root ق-ر-أ: the confirmation of the analysis in al-ʿAlaq

**Root: ق-ر-أ.** This root was analysed in detail in the section on Sūrat al-ʿAlaq and summarised at al-Aʿlā 87/6. The finding there was this:

**There are two basic senses in the root: to gather, to bring together (the original sense), and to read, to give voice. The second is counted a special case of the first — to read is to bring letters and words together in the mouth. The name "Qurʾān" is from this root too.**

I do not repeat it here. But something remarkable happens here and it must be pointed out.

**The verse sets the two senses of the root side by side in the same sentence:**

> **إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا جَمْعَهُۥ وَقُرْءَانَهُۥ**
> *"Its **gathering** and its **recitation** are Ours."*

If *qurʾān* already means "gathering", this would be a repetition — "to gather and to gather". Since the verse distinguishes the two, *qurʾān* here belongs to the **reading** side of the root: first it is gathered, then it is read.

**But this very distinction confirms the analysis in al-ʿAlaq.** Because in distinguishing the two tasks the verse at the same time sets them **side by side**. Gathering and reading are given, in one sentence, as two phases of one work. The two senses inside the root appear one after the other in the text's own word order.

That is, a root analysis drawn from the lexicon in the section on al-ʿAlaq is met here by the text's own sentence structure: **gathering first, *qurʾān* after.** The order of the senses in the dictionary and the order of the words in the verse are the same.

**I offer this as my own reading;** the two senses of the root are lexical data, the order of the two words in the verse is a datum of the text, and it is I who set up the correspondence between them.

### فَإِذَا قَرَأْنَٰهُ — "when We recite it"

The verb is in the **first person plural**: "when We recite". The classical explanation is this: the act of reciting belongs directly to the angel, but is ascribed to God with the plural of majesty — because the angel does not recite of himself but by command.

This explanation is widespread in the sources and follows from the Qurʾān's general language of ascription. **I do not present it as a settled ruling.**

**فَٱتَّبِعْ — follow.** The root **ت-ب-ع**: to go after someone, to follow his track. *Tābiʿ* — the one who goes after. Form VIII (*ittabaʿa*) intensifies the verb: to follow closely, insistently.

And the picture in the word suits the verse: the one who follows is **behind** the one who goes in front. The sixteenth verse forbade trying to go in front; the eighteenth shows the right position.

### The link with al-Aʿlā 87/6

In the section on al-Aʿlā this verse was treated:

> **سَنُقْرِئُكَ فَلَا تَنسَىٰ** — *"We shall make you recite, and you will not forget."* (al-Aʿlā 87/6)

The finding recorded there was this: in al-ʿAlaq there was a command (*iqraʾ* — recite); in al-Aʿlā the same root comes in a **causative** pattern and in the **indicative** mood (*sanuqriʾuka* — We shall make you recite). That is, the command turns into a promise, and the owner of the act of reciting changes: *"the act of reciting is not yours; it is recited to you."*

**Al-Qiyāma 75/17 repeats the same promise in other words.** Let us set the three side by side:

| | **al-ʿAlaq 96/1** | **al-Aʿlā 87/6** | **al-Qiyāma 75/17** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expression | *iqraʾ* | *sanuqriʾuka fa-lā tansā* | *inna ʿalaynā jamʿahū wa qurʾānah* |
| Mood | Command | Statement (future) | Statement (undertaking) |
| Whose work | The addressee's | God's | God's |
| The anxiety removed | — | **Forgetting** | **Missing it** |

**Three texts draw a single line.** In al-ʿAlaq the work is asked for; in al-Aʿlā it is promised that the work will be done; in al-Qiyāma it is said openly **to whom the work belongs.**

And the difference between the anxieties removed is fine: in al-Aʿlā the fear was that what had been received would be lost; in al-Qiyāma the fear is that the moment of receiving will slip away. One has to do with memory, the other with speed.

In making this link I make no claim about the order of revelation — the same record was made in the section on al-Aʿlā too. The shared root of the texts is in the text; the line between them is drawn by me.

### ثُمَّ إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا بَيَانَهُ

**The root ب-ي-ن** was treated in detail in the section on Sūrat al-Bayyina. The summary of the finding there: at the centre of the root there is **separation and interval** — *bayn* is the space between two things. To *bayān* a thing is to make it clear by **separating** it out from where it was mixed. *Bayyina* — the proof that distinguishes. *Tabyīn* — explanation.

I do not repeat it here. Two things belong to this place.

**First: ثُمَّ (then).** There are two readings of this particle:

| Reading | Explanation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Order of time** | The explanation comes after the recitation; the text is given first, its meaning opened afterwards | The receiving of revelation and its being understood are separate phases |
| **Order of rank** | *Thumma* here conveys not time but importance and degree: "moreover, further" | The explanation is not at a different time from the recitation but at a different rank |

Both readings are in the classical sources. **I state no preference**, but the first reading has a practical consequence worth recording: taking in a text and understanding it do not have to be completed at the same moment.

**Second: to whom the *bayān* belongs.** The verse gives the explaining to "Us" as well. This has two sides:

- The **explanation** of the text comes from the text's source. That is, the Qurʾān is positioned as a text that explains itself; and this is one of the verses on which the method called "the exegesis of the Qurʾān by the Qurʾān" rests.
- And the same sentence draws a **limit**. If the explanation does not belong to man, then the explanation man makes is not final. Every explanation, this commentary included, is an attempt.

I record this second inference as my own reading.

### The verse-ending

Three of the four verses end on the same sound: *qurʾānah, qurʾānah, bayānah.* And the sixteenth verse ends with *bih* — short, cut off.

The block builds a closed island of sound within itself. Before it *-ar* and *-īrah*, after it *-ila/-ira*. The ear hears the parenthesis as a parenthesis.

## 75/20-21 — كَلَّا بَلْ تُحِبُّونَ ٱلْعَاجِلَةَ · وَتَذَرُونَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةَ

*Kallā bal tuḥibbūna'l-ʿājila · Wa tadharūna'l-ākhira*
"No! In truth you love what is at hand in haste, and you leave the hereafter."

### The address changes direction

Here the sūra does something it had not done for nineteen verses: **it turns directly to the one addressed.**

Until that moment the subject had always been in the third person — *al-insān*, "man". Now the verbs move to the second person plural: *tuḥibbūna*, *tadharūna* — "you love", "you leave".

The books of rhetoric call this **iltifāt** (a shift in the direction of address). Its effect is this: the diagnosis stops being talk about a type observed from a distance and becomes a word said **to the person in the room**.

And its timing is striking. The sūra first set up the diagnosis in the third person — the reader could follow it comfortably. In the twentieth verse the curtain lifts: the one being described is you.

**The sūra will do this once more:** in verses 34-35 the address will turn to the second person singular (*awlā **laka***), and then in verse 36 it will go back to the third person. That is, the sūra moves back and forth between three layers of address.

### ٱلْعَاجِلَة — what is at hand in haste

**Root: ع-ج-ل.** Its meaning is **to hurry, to want a thing before its time, to bring it forward.** Its derivatives:

- **عَجَلَة** (*ʿajala*) — haste. (Turkish took it over directly as *acele*.)
- **عَاجِل** (*ʿājil*) — what is immediate, what is at hand. (It lives on in Turkish as *acil*, but with a narrowed sense.)
- **تَعْجِيل** (*taʿjīl*) — speeding up, bringing forward.
- **ٱسْتِعْجَال** (*istiʿjāl*) — hurrying, growing impatient.
- **عِجْل** (*ʿijl*) — **a calf.** The explanation the lexicographers transmit: because the animal begins to move early, or because it is "unripe, early". I transmit this derivation; I do not present it as a settled link.

**The form of the word:** *al-ʿājila* is the feminine of the active participle — "the thing that is in haste". The noun it qualifies is not stated; in the classical explanation what is understood is *al-dāru'l-ʿājila* or *al-ḥayātu'l-ʿājila*: the dwelling, or the life, that is at hand.

The word set against it has the same structure: **ٱلْـَٔاخِرَة** — "the thing that comes after", its qualified noun likewise unstated.

**Both words are adjectives and both have hidden the noun they qualify.** That is, the sentence names the two lives not by their names but by **their positions in time.**

### The names of this life

And now the contribution of this sūra.

In the discussion of al-Baqara 2/4 this was recorded: the counterpart of *al-ākhira* is *al-dunyā*, and *dunyā* comes from the root **د-ن-و**: **what is near, what is low.** The conclusion reached there was this: the names the Qurʾān uses for the two lives state not a value judgement but a **position** — this world is not called "this world" because it is bad, but because it is near.

And in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/61 the same root came up once more: *adnā* — "lower" or "nearer"? The finding there: **what is near and what is low are named by the same word**; what is tangible and immediately reachable is for that very reason counted low in value.

**Al-Qiyāma 75/20 gives this same life a third name.** And this name stands on a different axis from the others:

| Name | Root | What it states | Axis | Where it was treated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ٱلدُّنْيَا** | د-ن-و | What is near / what is low | **Distance** | al-Baqara 2/4, 2/61; al-Aʿlā 87/16 |
| **ٱلْأُولَىٰ** | أ-و-ل | What is first, what comes before | **Order** | al-Ḍuḥā 93/4; al-Aʿlā 87/17 |
| **ٱلْعَاجِلَة** | ع-ج-ل | What is in haste, what is at hand | **Speed** | **al-Qiyāma 75/20** |

Three names, the same life. And all three are words of **comparison**: near (relative to the far), first (relative to what follows), immediate (relative to the deferred). The Qurʾān never names this life on its own; always in its relation to the other.

**And what *al-ʿājila* adds is this:** this world is not only near, it is also **fast**. It comes early, it ends early, it does not wait.

From here comes the sūra's diagnosis, and the diagnosis differs from the usual: **the verse does not say that man chooses the wrong thing; it says he chooses the fast thing.**

The choice is not made as the outcome of a comparison of values. Nobody sits down and reckons "the hereafter is worth less". The choice follows the reach of the hand: whichever comes first is the one taken.

And the Qurʾān records this feature of man in another place as an attribute:

> **وَكَانَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ عَجُولًا** — *"Man was created hasty."* (al-Isrāʾ 17/11)

The same root: ع-ج-ل, in the intensive pattern (*ʿajūl*). That is, haste is by Sūrat al-Qiyāma a fault of conduct; by Sūrat al-Isrāʾ an attribute of man. Read together, the two verses say: the attribute is acknowledged, but the verdict the attribute gives is not accepted.

**I offer this comparison as my own reading;** the root meanings of the three names and the usage at al-Isrāʾ 17/11 are transmitted.

### تُحِبُّونَ — you love

**Root: ح-ب-ب.** And at the concrete centre of the root stands something: **حَبّ** (*ḥabb*) — **a grain, a seed, a kernel.** *Ḥabba* — a single grain. In the Qurʾān: *"the weight of a mustard seed"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/47).

The explanation the lexicographers transmit: *ḥubb* (love) is what settles in the heart like a seed and puts down roots there. The name of love comes from the name of what is sown.

I transmit this explanation; I issue no definite ruling on the historical development of the root. But the picture the word gives suits the verse: love of *al-ʿājila* is not a decision but something settled — a root that has to be pulled up.

**The same root will come once more in Sūrat al-Insān, but turned around:** *wa yuṭʿimūna'ṭ-ṭaʿāma **ʿalā ḥubbihī*** — "they give food though they love it" (al-Insān 76/8). There love is what measures the worth of what is given; here, of what is held back.

### تَذَرُونَ — you leave

**Root: و-ذ-ر.** Its meaning is **to leave, to abandon, to take no interest in.**

This verb has a striking feature of language, treated in the discussion of al-Insān 76/27; I do not repeat it here.

**What belongs to this place is the verb's weight of meaning.** The verse does not say *tukadhdhibūna* (you call it a lie), nor *tunkirūna* (you deny it). It says **you leave it.**

The difference matters. To call something a lie is to take a position; to leave it is to take none. The one who calls it a lie has engaged with the matter, has set himself against it, has fixed a position. The one who leaves it has not engaged.

**The verse names the real fault committed against the hereafter not as denial but as indifference.**

And this joins with the fifth verse. There man wanted to "break open his front"; here he "leaves" the hereafter. The two are two descriptions of the same movement: pushing it forward and leaving it behind.

### The word order of the sentence

The two verbs are built in parallel and their objects answer each other:

| | Verb | Object |
|---|---|---|
| **20** | *tuḥibbūna* — you love | *al-ʿājila* — what is in haste |
| **21** | *tadharūna* — you leave | *al-ākhira* — what comes after |

To love and to leave are not exact opposites — the opposite would be "to hate". The verse deliberately sets up a weak opposition: on one side a strong bond (love), on the other the bond's never being formed at all (leaving).

That is, man does not hate the hereafter. He takes no interest in it. And the verse's diagnosis lies precisely in that asymmetry.

## 75/22-23 — وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَّاضِرَةٌ · إِلَىٰ رَبِّهَا نَاظِرَةٌ

*Wujūhun yawmaʾidhin nāḍira · Ilā Rabbihā nāẓira*
"There are faces that day, radiant; they are looking to their Lord."

### وُجُوهٌ — an indefinite plural

This pattern was treated in the section on al-Ghāshiya: *wujūhun yawmaʾidhin* + adjective. There two tableaux opened with the same pattern (*khāshiʿa* / *nāʿima*), and the finding recorded was this: the same subject, the same adverb of time, the same place, one word different — in Arabic this is called *muqābala*, and its effect is this: the difference between the two groups is presented not as a difference of treatment coming from outside but as **two different fillings of the same sentence.**

Al-Qiyāma uses the same pattern (22 and 24), but with one difference: in al-Ghāshiya the adjectives stood alone; here a further sentence is added behind each adjective (23 and 25). So the tableau has two layers: the state of the face, and the cause of that state.

**The word's being indefinite** is meaningful too: "certain faces". No number is given, no proportion. As was recorded for the opening verses in the section on al-Baqara, the Qurʾān gives no proportion when it classifies people.

### نَّاضِرَة — freshness

**Root: ن-ض-ر.** This root was treated in detail in the discussion of al-Muṭaffifīn 83/24 (*naḍrata'n-naʿīm*). The summary of the finding there:

**The word's proper field is plant life** — *naḍira'sh-shajaru*, the tree grew fresh, put out green. *Naḍīr* — fresh, green, bright. *Nuḍār* — pure gold (because of its brightness). The word carries **the look of a plant that has taken water** over to the human face. And instead of naming an emotion — instead of saying happiness, peace, joy — the Qurʾān says **something seen in the face.**

I do not repeat it here. This verse was already cited there as an example.

What is to be added here: the word is an **active participle** here (*nāḍira*), and a **verbal noun** in al-Muṭaffifīn (*naḍra*). That is, there it was a quality seen in the faces; here it is the face's own state. Al-Muṭaffifīn tells what someone looking from outside sees (*taʿrifu fī wujūhihim* — "you will know it in their faces"); al-Qiyāma speaks of the face itself.

### نَاظِرَة — looking

**Root: ن-ظ-ر.** To look, to turn the eye. Its derivatives: *naẓar* (a look), *manẓar* (an appearance), *intiẓār* (waiting), *nāẓir* (one who looks, who watches over), *naẓīr* (equal, counterpart — what stands opposite), *munāẓara* (looking at one another, disputation).

**The nearness in sound between ن-ض-ر and ن-ظ-ر** was treated in a table in the section on al-Muṭaffifīn, and this record was entered there: **that the two roots are separate is certain; the nearness of their sounds and their occurring together in two sūras are data of the text.** The warning made in the section on al-Falaq (nearness of sound is not unity of root) was repeated there as well.

I keep the same record here. The single letter of difference between the two words (ض / ظ) is an observation, not a claim of derivation.

But this can be said, and it was said in the section on al-Muṭaffifīn too: **the Qurʾān draws a link between "looking" and "shining."** The face that looks shines.

### إِلَىٰ رَبِّهَا — fronting

The complement of *nāẓira* (*ilā Rabbihā*) has been **brought forward.** The expected order would be *nāẓiratun ilā Rabbihā*.

And this fronting stands at the centre of one of the most disputed questions in exegesis — because if it is said to convey **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*), the meaning becomes: they are looking **only** to their Lord, and to nothing else.

---

## The question of the vision of God (*ruʾyat Allāh*)

These two verses are the most quoted text of one of the longest and hardest disputes in the history of kalām. **No side is taken in this commentary;** the positions are transmitted and given with their grounds.

First the matter itself must be set down: **will the believers see God in the hereafter?**

### The positions of the two sides

| | **Those who affirm the vision** | **Those who reject the vision** |
|---|---|---|
| Who they are | The main line of Sunnī kalām (the Ashʿarī and Māturīdī traditions) and the people of hadith | The Muʿtazila; and certain other groups besides |
| Basic claim | In the hereafter God will be seen; **without manner, without direction, without distance, and without being encompassed** | Seeing requires body, direction, distance and a facing. Since God is free of these, the vision is **impossible** |
| How it reads this verse | When the verb *naẓara* comes with **إِلَىٰ** it means in Arabic **to look with the eye**. The verse is plain | *Naẓara* in Arabic also means "to wait"; the verse says *"they are waiting for (the bounty of) their Lord"* |
| Second reading | — | The governing noun has been elided: *ilā **thawābi** Rabbihā* — "they are looking to their Lord's reward" |

### The verses advanced as proof

**What those who affirm the vision rely on:**

- **al-Qiyāma 75/22-23** — this verse; it is counted the plainest proof.
- **Yūnus 10/26** — *"For those who do good there is the best, and something **more**."* It is transmitted that the *ziyāda* here has been interpreted as the vision.
- **al-Aʿrāf 7/143** — Moses' request, *"My Lord, show me Yourself (**arinī anẓur ilayka**)"*. The proof is built thus: a prophet does not ask for something impossible.
- In a report transmitted widely in the hadith sources, it is stated that the believers will see their Lord as they see the moon on the night it is full, and that there will be no crowding or pressing in this. It is stated that this report is found in the sound hadith collections; **I give no definite attribution as to its chain of transmitters or its full text.**

**What those who reject the vision rely on:**

- **al-Anʿām 6/103** — **لَّا تُدْرِكُهُ ٱلْأَبْصَٰرُ وَهُوَ يُدْرِكُ ٱلْأَبْصَٰرَ** — *"Sight does not grasp Him, and He grasps all sight."*
- **al-Aʿrāf 7/143** — the answer given to Moses' request: **لَن تَرَىٰنِى** — *"You will never see Me."* And the **لَنْ** here is, as was recorded in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/23, a **categorical** negation directed at the future.
- A rational proof: seeing requires that what is seen be in a direction and at a distance; and that means corporeality.

### The answers exchanged

**The answer given to al-Anʿām 6/103.** Those who affirm the vision distinguish thus: **إِدْرَاك** (*idrāk*) and **رُؤْيَة** (*ruʾya*) are not the same thing. *Idrāk* is to grasp a thing by **encompassing** it together with its limits. The verse rejects encompassing, not seeing. To see a man and to comprehend the whole of him are different things.

**The answer given to al-Aʿrāf 7/143.** It is maintained that the sentence *lan tarānī* was said **in this world** and carries no ruling about the hereafter. The other side says that the categorical force of *lan* cannot be narrowed in this way.

**The answer given to al-Qiyāma 75/23.** The strongest linguistic objection of those who affirm the vision is this: when the verb *naẓara* is used in the sense of "to wait", it comes in Arabic generally **without a preposition** or with some other construction (*unẓurūnā* — "wait for us", al-Ḥadīd 57/13). When it comes with **إِلَىٰ** it states looking with the eye. And in the verse there is *ilā*.

The other side meets this on the ground that *ilā* conveys "turning towards", and that hope is a turning too.

### A record

**The place where the two sides agree** must be dwelt on, because it narrows the scope of the dispute:

- Both sides agree that God **is not a body.**
- Both sides agree that God **holds no direction and no place.**
- Both sides agree that He is not seen in this world.

**The disagreement is over whether, under these conditions, there can be such a thing as "seeing" at all.** Those who affirm the vision say that a seeing whose reality is unknown is possible, and enter the qualification "without manner". Those who reject it say that a seeing without manner is a meaningless expression.

So the dispute is not, as it appears at first sight, a dispute over "is God a body". It is about the limits of a concept — of seeing.

### Assessment

**No preference is stated in this commentary**, and the ground for this is as follows:

The **wording** of the verse stands closer to one side: the settled sense in Arabic of the pattern *naẓara ilā* is looking with the eye, and this is a strong support in terms of language. Not to record it would not be honest.

But the whole of the matter is not closed by the wording of one verse. Both sides rely on other verses, on hadith reports and on rational arguments; and the matter is a heading that both sides count as one of *uṣūl* (the fundamentals of belief). A ruling given in a section of a commentary is not enough to close that heading.

**What is said in this section about this matter is not binding.** The reader has seen the supports of the two sides; I leave the preference to his own inquiry.

One thing more must be added. The harshness of the dispute arises from both sides carrying the same anxiety: one fears likening God to the created (*tashbīh*), the other fears voiding the plain wording of the Qurʾān (*taʿṭīl*). **Both fears are legitimate**, and this is why the dispute has lasted so long.

## 75/24-25 — وَوُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍۭ بَاسِرَةٌ · تَظُنُّ أَن يُفْعَلَ بِهَا فَاقِرَةٌ

*Wa wujūhun yawmaʾidhin bāsira · Taẓunnu an yufʿala bihā fāqira*
"And there are faces that day, drawn tight; they know that something back-breaking is to be done to them."

### بَاسِرَة — drawn, contracted

**Root: ب-س-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root comes from a fruit: **بُسْر** (*busr*) — **a date that has not yet ripened.** The stage at which the colour has turned but the fruit has not softened, hard and sharp to the taste.

From here the verb is born: *basara* — he soured his face, he drew his features tight, he wrinkled them. So "a sour face" is named by the hardness of an unripened date.

**The Qurʾān uses this word together with another verb:**

> **ثُمَّ عَبَسَ وَبَسَرَ** — *"Then he frowned and scowled."* (al-Muddaththir 74/22)

In the section on ʿAbasa the root **ع-ب-س** was treated: to wrinkle the face, to knit the brows, the hardening of the features. *ʿAbūs* — sour-faced. When the two roots come side by side they say the same movement twice.

**And one comparison is striking.** In Sūrat ʿAbasa the one who frowned was **a man**. In Sūrat al-Insān what frowns is **the day** (*yawman ʿabūsan qamṭarīrā* — al-Insān 76/10). In al-Qiyāma what is soured is a face again, but this time not by its owner's will.

That is, the same movement in three places: what a man does to another, what the day does to man, and what happens to a man's face without his choosing.

### The opposition of the two adjectives

The opposition between *nāḍira* and *bāsira* is built **in the language of plants**:

| | **نَّاضِرَة** (22) | **بَاسِرَة** (24) |
|---|---|---|
| Root | ن-ض-ر | ب-س-ر |
| Concrete field | A plant that has taken water, freshened | An unripened, hard date |
| Its counterpart in the face | Opened, shining | Contracted, wrinkled |
| Its touch | Soft | Hard |

The opposition in al-Ghāshiya (*khāshiʿa* / *nāʿima*) was built on the same axis: dried earth / a soft surface. **The Qurʾān's description of the face uses not the names of emotions but the names of textures.**

### تَظُنُّ — "supposes" or "knows"?

**Root: ظ-ن-ن.** This word was treated in two separate places in the section on al-Baqara, in two opposite senses:

- **al-Baqara 2/46** — *yaẓunnūna annahum mulāqū Rabbihim*: here **firm conviction** is meant; the verse says it as praise.
- **al-Baqara 2/78** — *wa in hum illā yaẓunnūn*: here **groundless guessing** is meant.

The finding recorded there was this: the root in Arabic can answer to both a doubtful guess and a firm conviction — in language this is called a contronym — and which sense is intended is always determined by the context.

**Then which is it here?**

| Reading | Meaning | Its effect |
|---|---|---|
| **He knows for certain** | "He knows with certainty what is to befall him" | The terror is complete: he knows there is no escape |
| **He guesses** | "He senses that something is to befall him, but does not know what" | The torment of uncertainty: an unknown waiting |

**Both readings suit the text** and both are in the classical sources. I state no preference.

But an observation can be recorded within the sūra. The fourteenth verse had described man as *baṣīra* — possessing **certain** knowledge about himself. Here the same man is *taẓunnu* — a state of unsteady knowledge. The one who knows himself for certain does not know for certain what will befall him.

**This distinction is consistent:** a person knows what he did, and does not know what its return will be. The sūra preserves the difference between knowledge and verdict.

I record this as my own reading; the presence of the two verbs in the sūra is a datum of the text.

### فَاقِرَة — back-breaking

**Root: ف-ق-ر.** And the concrete centre of this root is a bone: **فَقَار** (*faqār*) — **the spine, the backbone**; its singular *faqāra* — a vertebra.

From here comes the verb: *faqara* — to break the spine, to crack the back. And **فَاقِرَة** (*fāqira*) is the active participle: **the calamity that breaks the back.**

The other branch of the root is far better known: **فَقِير** (*faqīr*) — poor. The explanation the lexicographers transmit: the poor man is one who is as though **his back had been broken**; poverty crushes a man as though he were under a load he cannot carry.

The Turkish word **"fıkra"** comes from this root too: the separately standing parts of a text, jointed pieces like the segments of a spine. This is the sense used in legal language when one speaks of "the second *fıkra*" of an article.

**English has the same idiom:** to break someone's back, a back-breaking load. Two languages have fastened the same picture to the same place.

**The verse's indefiniteness is deliberate.** The sentence says *"something back-breaking"*; it does not say what. *Fāqira* is indefinite — unspecified. And the verb is passive: *yufʿala bihā* — "will be done to it", and who will do it is not said.

So neither the subject nor the object is stated. Only the result is stated: the back will be broken.

This runs in the same direction as the effect recorded in the section on al-Inshiqāq for the withholding of the answer to *idhā*: what is not described weighs heavier than what is described.

### The verse-ending: four verses, one sound

**Verses 20-25 all close on a single sound:**

*al-ʿājila, al-ākhira, nāḍira, nāẓira, bāsira, fāqira.*

Six verses in a row. And the last four are in the same measure (active participle, feminine): *nāḍira / nāẓira / bāsira / fāqira*. Four words, four roots, one pattern.

This is one of the tightest examples of the Qurʾān's ordering of verse-endings: two opposed tableaux drawn with four words of the same measure, and the ear hears the difference only from the root letters.

## 75/26-29 — كَلَّآ إِذَا بَلَغَتِ ٱلتَّرَاقِىَ · وَقِيلَ مَنْ رَاقٍ · وَظَنَّ أَنَّهُ ٱلْفِرَاقُ · وَٱلْتَفَّتِ ٱلسَّاقُ بِٱلسَّاقِ

*Kallā idhā balaghati't-tarāqiy · Wa qīla man rāq · Wa ẓanna annahu'l-firāq · Wa'ltaffati's-sāqu bi's-sāq*
"No! When it reaches the collarbones, and it is said, 'who will heal him?', and he knows that this is the parting, and leg is wound about leg…"

The narrowest scene in the sūra. The tableau of the resurrection was universal — sun, moon, all mankind. Here the scene comes down to one room, one bed, one body.

### كَلَّا — a third time

The third and last *kallā* of the sūra. And it does a different work from the others: it cuts off not a question but **the whole argument.**

Everything spoken of up to the twenty-sixth verse — the objection, the question, the scene, the two tableaux — is left behind and the text turns to the most concrete thing there is: **the moment of death.**

There is a logic to this. The matter argued over throughout the sūra was the reality of a distant day. By saying *kallā* the text says this: there is no need to argue over that day, because there is a reality far nearer than it and nobody argues over that one.

### إِذَا بَلَغَتِ — a sentence without a subject

The subject of the sentence **is not stated.** *Balaghati* — "when it reached"; there is no "it".

The classical explanation: the elided subject is **ٱلرُّوح** (*rūḥ*) or **ٱلنَّفْس** (*nafs*); the verb's coming in the feminine shows this (*nafs* is feminine). It was not stated because the indication is clear.

**And the ellipsis has an effect.** The sentence opens with a gap: "when it reached" — what? The reader halts for a moment. Then *al-tarāqī* comes and the gap fills of itself: something is going up.

In describing a man who is dying, the verse does not use the word **death**. Throughout the whole scene (26-29) that word never occurs.

### ٱلتَّرَاقِى — the collarbones

**Root: ت-ر-ق.** *Tarquwa* — **the collarbone**; its plural *tarāqī*.

**Why this bone?**

The collarbone stands just below the neck, at the very top of the chest. Anatomically it is **the place where the trunk ends**. When the soul has come up against it, no road is left.

That is, the verse states not a time but a **limit**. And in naming the limit it chooses not an abstract word but a bone that can be touched.

**The Qurʾān draws the same limit once more with another bone:**

> **فَلَوْلَآ إِذَا بَلَغَتِ ٱلْحُلْقُومَ** — *"When it reaches the throat (**ḥulqūm**)…"* (al-Wāqiʿa 56/83)

The same verb (*balaghat*), the same absence of a subject, a different organ. Read together, the two verses show the Qurʾān's description of death: a movement of rising, and a stopping place at the top of the body.

### وَقِيلَ مَنْ رَاقٍ — "who will heal him?"

**The verb is passive:** *qīla* — "it was said". Who says it is not specified. Those in the room, his kin, those around him — no name is given.

**And the words are said not to the sick man but about him.** The one who is dying is no longer the one addressed; he is the subject. This is one of the finest details of the scene: the person is still there, but he has turned into a third person.

**رَاقٍ — root: ر-ق-ي.** And this root has two branches; both have been maintained for this verse:

| Branch | Meaning | Derivatives |
|---|---|---|
| **To rise** | To go up, to climb | *ruqiyy*, *irtiqāʾ*, *mirqāt* (a ladder) |
| **Ruqya** | To treat by reciting and breathing over | *ruqya*, *rāqin* (one who recites) |

Two readings:

| Reading | Who says it | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **The healer** | Those around him | "Who is there, who will cure this man?" — the search for a last resort |
| **The one who takes up** | The angels | "Who will take this soul up?" |

**The first reading suits the context better** and is more widespread in the classical sources: the scene is a sickroom, and those around are looking for a remedy. The second is not contrary to the text either and comes legitimately out of the root's other branch. **I state my preference; it is not binding.**

One side of the first reading is worth dwelling on. The question is asked as *"who will cure him?"* — but those who ask it also know there is no answer. This is the last expression of helplessness: when there is nothing left to do, still to want something done.

### وَظَنَّ أَنَّهُ ٱلْفِرَاقُ

**ظَنَّ again.** The sūra's second *ẓann* (the first was in verse 25). And here the context is plainly on the side of **firm conviction**: the man has now understood. To render it "he supposed" would be wrong; "he knew" is closer.

The same side as the usage at al-Baqara 2/46: there too *ẓanna* was in the sense of certain knowledge.

**ٱلْفِرَاق — root: ف-ر-ق.** To separate, to divide, to open a space between. Its derivatives are wide: *farq*, *firqa*, *tafriqa*, *farīq*, *iftirāq*, *mufāraqa*. And **فُرْقَان** (*furqān*) — that which separates; one of the names of the Qurʾān.

**The word's taking the definite article is striking:** *al-firāq* — "that parting", "the parting itself". As though it were something recognised, something with a name of its own. Partings are many; this is the one by which all the rest are named.

**And who is parting from whom?** The verse does not say. The soul's parting from the body, the man's parting from his kin, his parting from the world — all happen at the same moment and the verse does not choose one.

### The play of sound: رَاقٍ / ٱلْفِرَاقُ

And now the most striking side of this block.

Two verses come one after the other and end on almost the same sound:

> **مَنْ رَاقٍ** — *man **rāq***
> **أَنَّهُ ٱلْفِرَاقُ** — *annahu'l-**firāq***

The same three sounds: **r-ā-q.** The word *rāq* stands inside *firāq*.

The books of rhetoric call this *jinās*: the placing side by side of two words alike in sound and separate in meaning.

**And the meanings of the two words are exact opposites:**

| | **رَاقٍ** | **ٱلْفِرَاقُ** |
|---|---|---|
| Root | ر-ق-ي | ف-ر-ق |
| Meaning | The one who heals by reciting | Parting |
| What is hoped | **Staying** | **Going** |
| Who says it | Those around | The man himself |

In one verse those around him hope for staying; in the next the man himself knows he is going. **Two sentences end on the same sound and say exactly opposite things.**

**I record this as an observation about sound, not as a claim of miracle.** In the section on al-Inshiqāq for *kadḥ*, and in the section on al-Qāriʿa, similar findings were made and it was stated that correspondence between sound and sense is a phenomenon found in all languages. The same record holds here. But that the two words stand side by side in this sūra and carry opposite senses is a datum of the text.

### وَٱلْتَفَّتِ ٱلسَّاقُ بِٱلسَّاقِ

**The root ل-ف-ف:** to wrap, to wind, to entangle, to fold. *Lifāfa* — a wrapping cloth. *Alfāf* — entwined with one another (of gardens: *jannātin alfāfā*, al-Nabaʾ 78/16).

**Form VIII (إِلْتَفَّ):** it wound itself, it was wrapped together. Reflexive — a movement that happens of itself.

**سَاق — the shank, the leg** (below the knee, the bony part). The same word is used of plants as well: *sāq*, stem or stalk — it occurs in the description of the crop in Sūrat al-Fatḥ.

**The disagreement:**

| Reading | Explanation | Ground |
|---|---|---|
| **A physical description** | In the throes of death the legs wind about each other; the body contracts | The concrete sense of the word; the bodily character of the scene |
| **Hardship added to hardship** | *Sāq* here is figurative. In Arabic *kashafa ʿan sāqihi* ("he bared his shank") means "he took the matter seriously, he entered upon difficulty". The sentence: "one hardship wound about another" | The idiom's being settled in Arabic |
| **The joining of two worlds** | The last day of this world and the first day of the hereafter wind about each other | The scene's being a moment of transition |

All three have been maintained in the classical sources. **I state no preference**, because none of the three is contrary to the text and there is no indication requiring a choice among them.

But one side of the second reading is worth recording: the association of the word *sāq* with hardship is a known usage in Arabic, and on this reading the verse describes not the body but **the moment**. The third reading, for its part, agrees with the structure of the sūra: this block stands exactly at the threshold of the passage from individual death to the universal resurrection.

**A note on the language:** the sentence has the particle *bi-* — *iltaffati's-sāqu **bi**'s-sāq*. This particle conveys **adhesion** (*ilṣāq*). That is, the winding is not superficial but stuck fast.

## 75/30 — إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْمَسَاقُ

*Ilā Rabbika yawmaʾidhini'l-masāq*
"That day the driving is to your Lord alone."

### ٱلْمَسَاق — the driving

**Root: س-و-ق.** And the concrete sense of this root is a herdsman's work: **to drive, to put before oneself and take along** — especially to drive animals.

Its derivatives show where the root opens out:

- **سَائِق** (*sāʾiq*) — one who drives, who puts before himself. At Qāf 50/21 it is stated that on the Day of Rising every soul has with it a *sāʾiq* and a *shahīd*.
- **سُوق** (*sūq*) — **a market.** The place to which goods are driven and brought. The English *souk* is this word.
- **سِيَاق** (*siyāq*) — **context**, the flow of speech, its driving. The sub-heading "Context" used at every verse in this commentary is from this root: the meaning that a word drags along from in front of it and from behind it.
- **مَسَاق** (*masāq*) — the driving, or the place to which one is driven.

*Masāq* can be read in two ways — as a verbal noun with *mīm* ("the act of being driven") or as a noun of place ("the place to which one will be driven"). Both are possible and produce no great difference in meaning.

### The symmetry with the twelfth verse

Here the sūra repeats its own sentence:

> **إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْمُسْتَقَرُّ** (75/12)
> **إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْمَسَاقُ** (75/30)

The three elements are the same, in the same order. The only word that changes is the last.

| | **75/12** | **75/30** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | ٱلْمُسْتَقَرّ | ٱلْمَسَاق |
| Root | ق-ر-ر — to stay, to settle | س-و-ق — to drive |
| What it states | **Arrival** | **Journey** |
| The person's condition | Standing still | Being driven |
| Its context | The middle of the resurrection scene | The end of the moment of death |

**The two verses together build one sentence:** the place you are going to is settled (12) and you are not going there on your own feet (30).

And the position of the two verses in the sūra is meaningful. The first was answering the search for escape — it came immediately after the question "where is the place of escape?" The second comes immediately after the moment of death. That is, both when the question is asked and when there is no longer any state in which to ask it, the answer is the same.

**And the language of herding will appear once more in the sūra.** In the thirty-sixth verse the word *sudā* will come: an animal let loose without a herdsman. The two words are from the same field:

| | **75/30** | **75/36** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | *masāq* — being driven | *sudā* — being left loose |
| The animal's condition | Before the herdsman | Without a herdsman |
| The verse's verdict | You will be driven | You were not left loose |

The sūra gives both possibilities in the language of herding and eliminates one. **I record this as my own reading;** the semantic field of the two words is lexical data.

## 75/31-33 — فَلَا صَدَّقَ وَلَا صَلَّىٰ · وَلَٰكِن كَذَّبَ وَتَوَلَّىٰ · ثُمَّ ذَهَبَ إِلَىٰٓ أَهْلِهِۦ يَتَمَطَّىٰٓ

*Fa-lā ṣaddaqa wa lā ṣallā · Wa lākin kadhdhaba wa tawallā · Thumma dhahaba ilā ahlihī yatamaṭṭā*
"He neither affirmed nor prayed; but he called it a lie and turned away. Then he went off to his people with a swagger."

After the moment of death the sūra turns back: **who was this man?**

The ordering itself is meaningful. The Qurʾān showed the end first (26-30), then tells the life. Like the reading backwards of a file on which judgement has been given.

### فَلَا صَدَّقَ — "he neither affirmed"

**Root: ص-د-ق.** To be true, to correspond to reality. *Ṣidq* — truthfulness; *ṣādiq* — one who speaks truly; *taṣdīq* — affirming; *ṣiddīq* — one who has made truthfulness a habit.

And from the same root comes **صَدَقَة** (*ṣadaqa*), alms. The explanation the lexicographers transmit: alms is what **shows the truth** of a person's faith — what is said in words verified with property.

**This second branch gives rise to a disagreement in the verse:**

| Reading | Meaning | Ground | Weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **He did not affirm** | He did not hold what was brought to be true, he did not believe | The *kadhdhaba* (he called it a lie) of the next verse is its exact opposite; both are from the same form (*tafʿīl*) | — |
| **He did not give alms** | He gave nothing of his property | The second branch of the root; its pairing with *ṣallā* (property and prayer) | The opposition with *kadhdhaba* supports the first reading |

**The first reading is stronger**, because the thirty-second verse brings the opposite verb in the same measure: *ṣaddaqa* ↔ *kadhdhaba*. **I state my preference; it is not binding**, and the second reading is also in the classical sources; moreover the two readings do not exclude one another — as was recorded in the sections on al-Balad and al-Māʿūn, the Qurʾān often measures faith by property.

### وَلَا صَلَّىٰ — "nor prayed"

**Root: ص-ل-و.** In the section on al-Aʿlā a record was made on this matter and I repeat it here: **ص-ل-ي** (to enter the fire) and **ص-ل-و** (prayer) **are not the same root**; no meaning should be drawn from the likeness of sound.

In the verse two verbs stand side by side: **affirming and prayer.** That is, one from within (conviction), one from without (act). This is the Qurʾān's habitual pairing; at al-Bayyina 98/5 too, *ikhlāṣ* and *iqāmu'ṣ-ṣalāt* occurred in the same sentence.

### Word order: what he did not do and what he did

The three verses work in two parts, and the symmetry is exact:

| | **Verse 31: what he did not do** | **Verse 32: what he did** |
|---|---|---|
| First verb | **صَدَّقَ** — to affirm | **كَذَّبَ** — to call a lie |
| Second verb | **صَلَّىٰ** — to pray | **تَوَلَّىٰ** — to turn away |
| Mood | Negative (*lā … wa lā*) | Positive (*wa lākin*) |

**The opposition has two layers.** *Ṣaddaqa* and *kadhdhaba* are from the same form (*tafʿīl*) and come from exactly opposite roots — one to affirm, the other to call a lie. *Ṣallā* and *tawallā*, on the other hand, form an opposition of **direction**: one is turning towards, the other turning away.

**The root و-ل-ي** is striking: to be near, to be adjoining, to be a friend. *Walī* — near one, friend, protector. *Wilāya* — nearness, protection. And Form V, **تَوَلَّىٰ** (*tawallā*), carries two opposite senses:

- **He took charge, he took up** — the usage in the line of *"God is the walī of those who believe"*.
- **He turned his face away, he turned his back** — the usage here.

The same verb states both drawing near and moving away. The lexicographers' explanation: at the centre of the root is "to turn, to face one way"; the sense is determined by whom the turning is towards. To turn to one is to turn one's back on another.

**wa lākin** — the particle of *istidrāk*: after a negation it brings the contrary of what is expected. "He did not do it — but neither did he stand idle."

The function of this particle is fine: the sentence says the man was not in a state of deficiency. What filled the place of what he did not do was not emptiness but **other acts.**

### ثُمَّ ذَهَبَ إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ يَتَمَطَّىٰ

The most visual verse of the sūra, and its harshest diagnosis.

**يَتَمَطَّىٰ — the disputed root.** The lexicographers propose two derivations:

| Root | Meaning | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **م-ط-و** | To stretch, to extend | *Tamaṭṭī*, to walk stretching oneself out; to sway |
| **م-ط-ط** | To pull taut, to draw out | The same picture; with the second ط turning into ي |

Some lexicographers also relate the word to **مَطَا** (*maṭā*) — the back, the loins — and explain it as "to walk swinging the back, throwing the shoulders". **I transmit these derivations; I make no definite preference among them.**

The meaning is shared: **to walk stiffly, swaying, with a swagger.** English answers to it with *to swagger*, *to strut*.

**The choice of word must be dwelt on.** The verse could have been content with "he went". It adds a **circumstantial qualifier** beside the verb — a word telling how he went. And the word chosen is a manner of walking.

That is, denial is shown here not as an opinion but as a **bodily state**. The man does not merely call it a lie; he carries his calling it a lie.

**And this is consistent with the sūra's account of man.** The fourteenth verse had said that man is proof against himself. The thirty-third shows that this proof can be seen from outside as well: from his walk.

### إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ — to his people

**Root: أ-ه-ل.** This root was treated in the section on al-Bayyina and used again in the section on al-Inshiqāq. The summary of the finding there: at the centre of the root is **belonging and familiarity**; a person's *ahl* are his household; an *ahlī* animal is one accustomed to living with man; *ahliyya* is being the right man for a task.

I do not repeat it here. What belongs to this place is **that the same expression occurs twice in Sūrat al-Inshiqāq.**

Let us set three verses side by side:

| Place | Expression | Where | When | State |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Inshiqāq 84/9** | *yanqalibu **إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ** masrūrā* | In the hereafter | **After** the reckoning | **Joyful** — made joyful |
| **al-Inshiqāq 84/13** | *innahū kāna **فِىٓ أَهْلِهِ** masrūrā* | In this world | Without thinking of the reckoning | **Joyful** — of himself |
| **al-Qiyāma 75/33** | *dhahaba **إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِ** yatamaṭṭā* | In this world | **After** calling it a lie | **Swaggering** |

**The same place, three states.** And the finding recorded for this word in the section on al-Inshiqāq works here once more: the reward is given not as a place but as a **belonging** — not "he enters the Garden" but "he returns to his people".

Al-Qiyāma shows the other end of the same belonging. The man in al-Inshiqāq 84/13 was **joyful** among his people; the man in al-Qiyāma 75/33 goes to his people **with a swagger.** Al-Inshiqāq states the condition, al-Qiyāma the walk.

**And the shared diagnosis of the two sūras is this:** a man's denial takes its most visible form, at the moment when he assumes he will not be called to account, among those closest to him. How he stands where no one is judging him shows what he is.

**I offer this link as my own reading;** the use of *ahl* in the three verses is a datum of the text.

### ثُمَّ — the point in the sequence

The particle ranges three verbs in a sequence: he called it a lie → he turned away → **then** he went off to his people with a swagger.

This was not the expected order. When a person turns away from a truth, one would expect at least some unease. The verse shows the opposite: **denial is carried not as a loss but as a gain.**

And *thumma* stresses the distance between: time passed between the two acts, the man reached his house, and in that time nothing changed.

## 75/34-35 — أَوْلَىٰ لَكَ فَأَوْلَىٰ · ثُمَّ أَوْلَىٰ لَكَ فَأَوْلَىٰٓ

*Awlā laka fa-awlā · Thumma awlā laka fa-awlā*
"Woe to you, and woe! Then again woe to you, and woe!"

One of the strangest expressions in the Qurʾān, and the hardest to translate.

### The sudden change of address

Up to the thirty-third verse the telling was in the third person: *ṣaddaqa, ṣallā, kadhdhaba, tawallā, dhahaba*. In the thirty-fourth it turns all at once to the **second person singular**: **لَكَ** — "to you".

This is the sūra's second *iltifāt* (the first was in verses 20-21, to the second person plural). And its effect is harsher here: the narrative turns in an instant into a **confrontation**. The man who was being spoken about is taken face to face.

And in the thirty-sixth verse the address will turn back to the third person (*a-yaḥsabu'l-insān*). That is, these two verses are a short, sharp parenthesis within the sūra.

**The sūra uses three layers of address:**

| Verses | Address | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 3-19, 36-40 | Third person (*al-insān*) | Diagnosis, from a distance |
| 20-21 | Second person plural (*tuḥibbūna*) | Turning to the one addressed |
| 34-35 | **Second person singular** (*laka*) | Direct confrontation |

### أَوْلَىٰ — the root of the expression

There is disagreement over the expression, and the disagreement begins with the word's **root**:

| View | Root | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **An elative from the root و-ل-ي** | و-ل-ي | *Awlā* — "nearer", "more deserving". The sentence understood: "what is to befall you is nearer to you", or "this is more fitting for you" |
| **A settled formula of threat** | و-ل-ي | A threat pattern established in Arabic: *awlā laka* — "it nearly befell you!" or "it has come close to you!" |
| **Related to وَيْل** | و-ي-ل | Some lexicographers count the word as an imprecatory formula in the sense of *wayl* (woe) |

**The first and second views rest on the same root and in practice come together:** the expression is a threat formula saying that a bad outcome has drawn near to the person. The third view rests on nearness of sound and has been found weaker.

**It is hard to render into English**, because the formula has no one-to-one equivalent. Attempts: "Woe to you!", "It serves you right!", "It has come upon you!", "This is what you deserve!"

### The fourfold repetition

The expression occurs **four times**, and its arrangement is particular:

> *awlā laka* **فَـ** *awlā* · **ثُمَّ** *awlā laka* **فَـ** *awlā*

Two pairs, and the pairs are joined by different particles:

- Within the pair, **فَ** (*fāʾ*) — quick addition: "woe — and immediately again, woe".
- Between the pairs, **ثُمَّ** (*thumma*) — an interval: a pause, then again.

According to the lexicographers' explanation the *thumma* here states not time but **degree**: the second pair mounts on top of the first.

**What is the function of the repetition?** In rhetoric this is called *takrīr*, and it intensifies a threat. But there is a further side to the repetition here: **there is no verb in the sentence.** What will happen is not said, only that "it has drawn near".

That is, what is repeated four times is not the **content** of a threat but its **nearness**. And the withholding of the content — as was recorded in the section on al-Inshiqāq for the unanswered *idhā*, and in this sūra for the indefiniteness of *fāqira* — makes what is not described weigh heavier than what is.

### The occasion of revelation

Reports are transmitted that these verses were said about a particular person. **No name is given in this commentary**, because I do not possess enough knowledge to issue a ruling on the soundness of the report.

The measure of method does not require it in any case. As was recorded in the section on al-Balad: *al-ʿibratu bi-ʿumūmi'l-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣi's-sabab* — the ruling goes by the generality of the wording, not by the particularity of the occasion.

And the sūra's own language supports this: the text gives no name, it says *al-insān*.

---

## 75/36 — أَيَحْسَبُ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ أَن يُتْرَكَ سُدًى

*A-yaḥsabu'l-insānu an yutraka sudā*
"Does man suppose that he will be left to himself?"

**The sūra's concluding question.** And the return of the third verse.

### The symmetry with the third verse

The two questions open with the same pattern and thirty-two verses lie between them:

| | **75/3** | **75/36** |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | *a-yaḥsabu'l-insānu* | *a-yaḥsabu'l-insānu* |
| Content | *allan najmaʿa **عِظَامَهُ*** | *an yutraka **سُدًى*** |
| Subject | **The body** — can what is scattered be gathered | **Life** — can what is lived be left without return |
| Type of objection | An objection of possibility | An objection of meaning |
| Its answer | *balā, qādirīna* (4) | *a-laysa dhālika bi-qādirin* (40) |

**The sūra draws a circle**, but does not return to the same point. The first question was an engineering question: can scattered pieces be brought together? The second is a question of meaning: can a life end without being tied to any outcome?

And the whole advance of the sūra is the distance between these two questions. The thirty-two verses in between have been spent showing that the first question was not the real question.

**The fifth verse had already said this:** *"bal yurīdu'l-insān"* — the matter is not what he supposes but what he wants. The thirty-sixth verse gives that want its name: **to be left to himself.**

### أَن يُتْرَكَ — to be left

**The verb is passive:** *yutraka* — "to be left". Who will leave him is not said.

**Root: ت-ر-ك.** To leave, to abandon, to leave behind. From the same root comes *tarika* (an estate — what is left behind).

It must be compared with the verb in the twenty-first verse:

> **وَتَذَرُونَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةَ** (21) — "you **leave** the hereafter".
> **أَن يُتْرَكَ سُدًى** (36) — "that he will be **left** to himself".

Two different roots (و-ذ-ر and ت-ر-ك) but the same field of meaning: to abandon. And the direction turns around: **man leaves the hereafter, and in return hopes to be left.**

I record this as my own reading; the shared meaning of the two verbs is lexical data.

### سُدًى — loose, unattended

**Root: س-د-ي / س-د-و.** And the word's concrete field is **herding**: *ibilun sudā* — a herd of camels without a herdsman, let loose, left to itself. Nobody drives them, nobody follows behind them, nobody counts them and keeps a reckoning.

The lexicographers give the word as a synonym of *muhmal* (neglected). Another use of the same root in weaving is also transmitted (*sadā* — the lengthwise threads of the weave); this second use has little to do with the meaning in the verse and I build nothing on it.

**The picture the word carries is this:** a herd of animals, and no one at the head of the herd.

### The link with the thirtieth verse

The sūra had already used this picture six verses earlier:

> **إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ ٱلْمَسَاقُ** (30) — the driving.
> **أَن يُتْرَكَ سُدًى** (36) — being left loose.

**Both are the language of herding.** *Masāq* is being put in front and taken along; *sudā* is being left ungoverned. The same animal, two states.

And the sūra eliminates one: you were not left loose — you will be driven. As for the direction, the thirtieth verse had already said it: *ilā Rabbika*.

**I record this as my own reading;** the semantic field of the two words is lexical data, and it is I who make the link between them.

### Is "being left loose" a promise?

The question the verse asks looks at first sight like a threat: "does he suppose he will be left to himself?" — that is, you will not be left, there is a reckoning.

But the word's own meaning turns the question around.

To be *sudā* means **that nobody cares.** It is the state of an animal fallen out of the herd, not followed after, not counted. The word is not a name for freedom but for **abandonment.**

That is, the verse may also be saying this: what you want, to be left loose, is in fact that nobody should take any interest in you. Not to be called to account is not to be counted as mattering.

**I offer this as my own reading;** that the word comes from the field of herding is lexical data. The context of the verse is a warning; but the wording of the word is open to this direction too.

## 75/37-39 — أَلَمْ يَكُ نُطْفَةً مِّن مَّنِىٍّ يُمْنَىٰ · ثُمَّ كَانَ عَلَقَةً فَخَلَقَ فَسَوَّىٰ · فَجَعَلَ مِنْهُ ٱلزَّوْجَيْنِ ٱلذَّكَرَ وَٱلْأُنثَىٰ

*A-lam yaku nuṭfatan min manīyyin yumnā · Thumma kāna ʿalaqatan fa-khalaqa fa-sawwā · Fa-jaʿala minhu'z-zawjayni'dh-dhakara wa'l-unthā*
"Was he not a drop of sperm poured out? Then he was a clinging thing; then He created and set in order. And from it He made the two mates, the male and the female."

### أَلَمْ يَكُ — the lightened verb

**أَلَمْ يَكُنْ** would have been expected; the verse drops the final **ن**: *a-lam yaku*.

In Arabic this is called **taḫfīf** (lightening) and is common with the verb *kāna* in the jussive. Its effect is to speed the flow of sound: the sentence runs on unbroken.

**The interrogative particle is again a denying question** — as in the third and thirty-sixth verses. But the direction has changed: those questions rejected the addressee's opinion; this question **makes the addressee affirm his own past.**

### نُطْفَة — a drop

**Root: ن-ط-ف.** Its meaning is **to drip, to seep, to flow little by little.** *Nuṭfa* — a drop of water; the word is also used of clear water in small quantity.

**The choice of word must be dwelt on.** *Nuṭfa* is the name not of a substance but of a **quantity**: smallness. The verse does not use a biological term; it says "a drop".

And the weight of the proof lies exactly here: **the comparison is built not on the material but on the scale.** If a man has come out of a drop, why should it be impossible for a man to come out of decayed bone?

### مِّن مَّنِىٍّ يُمْنَىٰ

**Root: م-ن-ي.** And the semantic family of this root is surprisingly wide:

- **مَنَىٰ** (*manā*) — he determined, he measured, he apportioned.
- **مَنِىّ** (*manī*) — what is poured out.
- **مَنِيَّة** (*maniyya*), plural **مَنَايَا** — **death**; according to the lexicographers' explanation, "what has been determined".
- **أُمْنِيَّة** (*umniyya*), plural **أَمَانِىّ** — **wishing, vain fancy.**

This last derivative was treated in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/78: *amānī* — a conviction resting not on reality but on desire. The finding recorded there was this: the text is read but not known; what fills the gap is what the person wants to be the case.

**So the name of life's beginning, the name of vain fancy and the name of death all come from the same root** — because all three are tied to the idea of "what is determined, what is measured". **I transmit this link as a lexical observation;** I issue no definite ruling on the historical development of the root and build no interpretation on it.

**يُمْنَىٰ** — passive imperfect: "poured out / spilled". A passive again: man is not the agent even at his own beginning.

**Two variant readings are transmitted for the word** (*yumnā* / *tumnā*); no great difference arises in the resulting meaning — one refers the pronoun to *manī*, the other to *nuṭfa*. I do not say which reading belongs to which authority, because I am not sure.

### عَلَقَة — *ʿalaqa*

**Root: ع-ل-ق.** This word was analysed in detail in the section on Sūrat al-ʿAlaq. The summary of the finding there: the meaning of the root is **to hang, to cling, to stick**; *ʿalaqa* — a thing that hangs, that clings; *ʿalaq* also means a leech (the animal that hangs on) and is used of a clot as well.

And there, the claim that the word matches modern embryology was treated in detail on **five grounds** and rejected. **I keep the same position here and do not repeat the grounds.** In brief: the meaning of the word is given and not diminished; but this cannot be presented as the verse's claim, nor turned into a proof of miracle. The verse's own purpose is not to give information; it is **to set up a bringing-low and a proof of power.**

**What belongs to this place is the word's grammatical position.**

| | **al-ʿAlaq 96/2** | **al-Qiyāma 75/38** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *khalaqa'l-insāna min **عَلَقٍ*** | *thumma **كَانَ عَلَقَةً*** |
| Word | *ʿalaq* — generic/plural | *ʿalaqa* — singular, indefinite |
| Particle/verb | *min* — states material | *kāna* — states state |
| What it says | What he was made of | What he **became** |

In Sūrat al-ʿAlaq the word was a **material**: man was created from this. In al-Qiyāma it is a **stage**: man came to this state, then passed to another.

The difference is not trivial. Al-Qiyāma sets up a series: drop → *ʿalaqa* → creating → setting in order. Four steps, and at each step something different from the one before.

### فَخَلَقَ فَسَوَّىٰ — the change of subject

**In the middle of the verse the agent changes, and the change is not stated.**

- *kāna ʿalaqatan* — "**he** became a clinging thing". Subject: man.
- *fa-khalaqa fa-sawwā* — "**He created and set in order**". Subject: God.

Within the sentence, with no conjunction and no warning, there is a passage from a passive becoming to an active doing.

In the classical commentaries this is explained as a hidden change of subject, and the indication is clear: the subject of the verb "to create" cannot be man.

**Its effect is this:** in the first two steps man is inside a **becoming** — he became a drop, he became a clinging thing, he did nothing himself. At the third step an agent enters. That is, man is not there at the beginning of his story; he is not there even in the middle of it.

**The two فَ particles (*fāʾ*)** reinforce this as well: *fa-khalaqa fa-sawwā* — quick and unbroken succession. There is no gap between the steps.

### فَسَوَّىٰ — the sūra's second *sawwā*

And now one of the sūra's most marked structural closings.

**This verb had occurred in the fourth verse of the sūra as well:**

| | **75/4** | **75/38** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *an **نُّسَوِّىَ** banānah* | *fa-**سَوَّىٰ*** |
| Object | **The fingertips** — the smallest part | **Man himself** — the whole |
| Its time | **In the hereafter** — a setting in order again | **In this world** — the first setting in order |
| Mood | Imperfect (We shall do) | Perfect (He did) |
| Its function | A claim | A proof |

**The sūra opens and closes with the same verb.** In the fourth verse there was a claim: "We are able to set the fingertips in order." The thirty-eighth verse shows **that claim already realised**: He has already set in order.

That is, the sūra shows that the work said to be done in the future has already been done in the past. The claim carries its own proof within it.

The finding recorded for this root in the discussion of al-Shams 91/7 — **to bring the parts of a thing into correspondence with one another, to get its proportions right** — is the same in both verses. The only thing that changes is the size of what is proportioned.

**I offer this symmetry as my own reading;** the presence of the verb in the two verses is a datum of the text.

### فَجَعَلَ مِنْهُ ٱلزَّوْجَيْنِ

**Root: ز-و-ج.** And the word **زَوْج** (*zawj*) has a subtlety in Arabic: it does not mean "a pair" but **one member of a pair.** One shoe of a pair is a *zawj*; the two together are *zawjān*.

This is why the Qurʾān says *zawj* of both man and woman; the word is "mate", "one of a pair".

**ٱلزَّوْجَيْن** — the dual: two mates, the two sides of a pair.

**To what does the pronoun مِنْهُ refer?** There are two readings: to *nuṭfa* (two sexes from the same drop) or to *al-insān* (two sexes from the same kind). Both are possible and produce no great difference in meaning.

**ٱلذَّكَرَ وَٱلْأُنثَىٰ** — the explanation (apposition) of *al-zawjayn*: "the two mates — the male and the female."

**A point of word order:** first the unity (*al-zawjayn* — one pair), then the division (*al-dhakar wa'l-unthā*). The verse first sets down the twoness as a whole, then breaks it apart.

The relation between the root **ذ-ك-ر** and *dhikr* (remembrance) is disputed among the lexicographers; no settled link has been established and I build nothing on it here. As for the root **أ-ن-ث**, the explanation the lexicographers transmit is that there is an idea of **softness** in the root; I transmit this explanation and make no claim to certainty.

### Why is this verse here?

A question may be asked: the proof of the resurrection had been completed in the thirty-eighth verse (*fa-khalaqa fa-sawwā*). Why is the thirty-ninth added?

The classical explanation is this: the distinction of sex is a separate proof of power — **two different results from the same material.** The drop is one, what comes out is two sexes. That is, the matter is not only the bringing of a thing into being but its being **differentiated** as it is brought into being.

And this is the continuation of the line of proof in the fourth verse. There too the matter was detail: setting the fingertips in order. Here too: drawing two separate orders out of a single drop.

**The sūra always proves power from detail,** not from magnitude. I record this as my own reading.

## 75/40 — أَلَيْسَ ذَٰلِكَ بِقَٰدِرٍ عَلَىٰٓ أَن يُحْۦِىَ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ

*A-laysa dhālika bi-qādirin ʿalā an yuḥyiya'l-mawtā*
"Is the one who did this not able to give life to the dead?"

The last verse of the sūra. And the answer to the fourth.

### أَلَيْسَ — the question that makes one affirm

This is the kind of question called *istifhām taqrīrī*: it asks for no information, it makes the one addressed say the answer himself.

English has its counterpart: the tag question, "is He not?" The sentence is built so that the answer can be nothing but "yes".

And this choice is meaningful. The sūra could have ended with a statement: "He is able to give life to the dead." Instead it ends with a question — **leaving the last word to the one addressed.**

The sūra had begun with a question (verse 3: *a-yaḥsabu'l-insān*) and it ends with a question. But the two questions run in opposite directions: the first rejected the addressee's opinion, the last asks the addressee for his assent.

### بِقَٰدِرٍ — the redundant *bāʾ*

The particle **بِـ** is here **redundant**: not required grammatically, present for reinforcement. In Arabic negative sentences built with *laysa*, this particle often comes at the head of the predicate and strengthens the negation.

It is hard to carry into English; it adds an emphasis like "is He not indeed able?"

### The close with the fourth verse

Here the sūra closes its own opening:

| | **75/4** | **75/40** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *balā **قَٰدِرِينَ** ʿalā an nusawwiya banānah* | *a-laysa dhālika **بِقَٰدِرٍ** ʿalā an yuḥyiya'l-mawtā* |
| Word | *qādirīn* — plural, circumstantial qualifier | *qādir* — singular, predicate |
| Structure | *ʿalā an* + imperfect | *ʿalā an* + imperfect |
| Mood | **Statement** — "We are able" | **Question** — "is He not able?" |
| Who says it | The text | The one addressed (by giving the answer) |

**The same word, the same structure, two different moods.** At the beginning of the sūra the text builds the claim itself; at the end it makes the one addressed build the same claim.

And what happened in between? Over thirty-six verses the proof was laid out: a drop, a stage, a setting in order, a distinction of sex. The one addressed meets the question after seeing these proofs.

**This is an example of the Qurʾān's way of building a proof:** the claim is put first, the proof laid out after, and the conclusion made to be spoken by the one addressed.

### Comparison with al-Baqara 2/28

In the section on al-Baqara this verse was treated:

> *"How do you deny God? You were dead and He gave you life; then He will make you die, then give you life again, then to Him you will be returned."* (al-Baqara 2/28)

The finding recorded there was this: the verse contains a reasoning in **four steps** — you were dead, He gave life, He will make die, He will give life. The proof is this: **the fourth step you object to is a repetition of the second step, which has already happened once.** And the force of the argument lies in its using the addressee's own existence as evidence; it asks for no proof from outside.

**Al-Qiyāma sets up the same proof, but on a different axis:**

| | **al-Baqara 2/28** | **al-Qiyāma 75/37-40** |
|---|---|---|
| Axis | **Time** — four steps | **Material** — four stages |
| Steps | Dead → living → dead → living | Drop → *ʿalaqa* → creating → setting in order |
| Form of the proof | "It happened once; why should its repetition be impossible?" | "Look where you began" |
| Emphasis | The possibility of repetition | The smallness of the beginning |
| Close | *"then to Him you will be returned"* — statement | *"is He not able?"* — question |

**Both use the same method:** making the addressee's own existence the proof. And neither asks for evidence from outside — no miracle, no witness, no book. All that is asked is that a person remember where he came from.

**The difference is here:** al-Baqara moves back and forth in time; al-Qiyāma descends in scale. One says "this happened before", the other says "this is what you began from".

And the Qurʾān joins these two proofs in a single sentence in another place:

> **وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى يَبْدَؤُا۟ ٱلْخَلْقَ ثُمَّ يُعِيدُهُۥ وَهُوَ أَهْوَنُ عَلَيْهِ** — *"It is He who begins the creation, then repeats it; and that is easier for Him."* (al-Rūm 30/27)

### ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ — the last word

The sūra ends with the word **"the dead"**.

And this crosses once more with the verse from Yāsīn cited at the thirteenth verse:

> **إِنَّا نَحْنُ نُحْىِ ٱلْمَوْتَىٰ وَنَكْتُبُ مَا قَدَّمُوا۟ وَءَاثَٰرَهُمْ** (Yāsīn 36/12)

The same two elements — *nuḥyi'l-mawtā* (to give life to the dead) and *mā qaddamū* (what they sent ahead) — stand at the two ends of Sūrat al-Qiyāma: "what he sent ahead" in verse 13, "to give life to the dead" in verse 40. Yāsīn gathers them into a single sentence.

I record this as a crossing; the shared expressions of the two verses are data of the text.

## The architecture of the sūra

### Structure

| Block | Verses | Content | Key |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | 1-2 | Two oaths | *al-qiyāma*, *al-lawwāma* |
| **II** | 3-6 | Objection, intention, question | *a-yaḥsabu*, *yafjura amāmah*, *ayyāna* |
| **III** | 7-12 | The scene and the search for escape | *ayna'l-mafarr*, *lā wazara*, *al-mustaqarr* |
| **IV** | 13-15 | The setting up of the reckoning | ***baṣīra***, *maʿādhīr* |
| **V** | 16-19 | Parenthesis: revelation | *jamʿahū wa qurʾānah*, *bayānah* |
| **VI** | 20-21 | Diagnosis | *al-ʿājila*, *tadharūna* |
| **VII** | 22-25 | Two faces | *nāḍira / nāẓira*, *bāsira / fāqira* |
| **VIII** | 26-30 | The moment of death | *al-tarāqī*, *man rāq*, *al-firāq*, *al-masāq* |
| **IX** | 31-35 | The portrait in retrospect | *yatamaṭṭā*, *awlā laka* |
| **X** | 36-40 | The closing question and the proof | *sudā*, *fa-sawwā*, *bi-qādirin* |

### Repeated words

The sūra's most marked technique is to set up a frame by using the same word at two distant points:

| Word / root | First occurrence | Second occurrence | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **أَيَحْسَبُ ٱلْإِنسَٰن** | 3 (bones) | 36 (being left loose) | Question of possibility → question of meaning |
| **قَادِر** | 4 (statement) | 40 (question) | Claim → making one affirm |
| **سَوَّىٰ** | 4 (fingertip) | 38 (man himself) | Future claim → past proof |
| **ج-م-ع** | 3 (bones), 9 (sun and moon) | 17 (the Qurʾān) | Gathering on three scales |
| **ع-ج-ل** | 16 (the Prophet) | 20 (man) | Ties the parenthesis to the sūra |
| **إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ يَوْمَئِذٍ** | 12 (*al-mustaqarr*) | 30 (*al-masāq*) | Arrival / driving |
| **ظَنَّ** | 25 (the faces) | 28 (the dying man) | Uncertain knowledge / certain knowledge |
| **نَفْس** | 2 (*al-lawwāma*) | 14 (*ʿalā nafsihī*) | The reproacher / the proof |
| **ٱلْإِنسَٰن** | 3, 5, 10 | 13, 14, 36 | Six times: the sūra's only subject |
| **كَلَّا** | 11, 20 | 26 | Three doors of blocks |
| **ت-ر-ك / و-ذ-ر** | 21 (*tadharūna*) | 36 (*yutraka*) | The one who leaves / the one who hopes to be left |

In a text of forty verses an economy of repetition at this density is striking. Instead of bringing in a new word the sūra **turns** the word it has already brought — and at each turn it changes its mood or its scale.

I offer this as my own reading; the repetitions themselves are data of the text and can be counted.

### The texture of sound

The ordering of verse-endings in al-Qiyāma is one of the most regular in the Qurʾān. **There are seven regions of sound and each corresponds to a block of subject matter:**

| Verses | Verse-ending | Words | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1-6** | **-āma(h) / -āna(h)** | *al-qiyāmah, al-lawwāmah, ʿiẓāmah, banānah, amāmah, al-qiyāmah* | Six verses, long and open; oath and objection |
| **7-13** | **-ar** | *al-baṣar, al-qamar, al-qamar, al-mafarr, wazar, al-mustaqarr, akhkhar* | Seven verses, hard and short; the scene |
| **14-15** | **-īrah** | *baṣīrah, maʿādhīrah* | Two verses, long *ī*; the verdict |
| **16-19** | **-ānah** | *bih, qurʾānah, qurʾānah, bayānah* | A closed island; the parenthesis |
| **20-25** | **-ila(h) / -ira(h)** | *al-ʿājilatu, al-ākhirata, nāḍiratun, nāẓiratun, bāsiratun, fāqiratun* | Six verses; the last four in the same measure |
| **26-30** | **-āq** | *al-tarāqiya, rāq, al-firāq, al-sāq, al-masāq* | Five verses; the moment of death |
| **31-40** | **-ā** | *ṣallā, tawallā, yatamaṭṭā, awlā, awlā, sudā, yumnā, sawwā, al-unthā, al-mawtā* | **Ten verses**, a single long *ā*; the close |

**The last ten verses of the sūra all end on one sound.** The ear hears that the sūra is closing before the sense does.

And one detail: in the block 26-30, four of the five verses end in *-āq*, and two of them (*rāq* / *firāq*) are almost the same word. The sound of the moment of death is the narrowest range of sound in the sūra — as the scene narrows, so does the sound.

I offer these observations as my own reading; the verse-endings themselves are countable data.

## What this says to the present

**First: the self-reproaching voice is not to be counted a fault.**

The second verse swears by man's capacity to accuse himself. Today this capacity is most often handled as a problem: guilt, the inner critic, harshness towards oneself. A language aimed at easing these is widespread, and in many cases rightly so.

What the verse says is something else. *Lawwāma* is named not as an illness that paralyses a person but as **a mechanism at work** — valuable enough to be sworn by.

The measure of this for today lies here: the voice that reproaches a person and the voice that wears him down are not the same thing. The first gives **information** — it tells him what he did. The second gives no information, only pain. The sūra is speaking of the first; the fourteenth verse already changes the word and says *baṣīra* (seeing).

**Second: an excuse does not remove knowledge.**

The distinction the fifteenth verse makes is about one of the most familiar operations of daily life.

A person does something and immediately produces an explanation. The explanation is most often true — the conditions really were hard, the other side really was in the wrong, there really was no time. The verse does not say these explanations are false. It says **they do not change the knowledge.**

And the practical measure here is this: a person can look at **how much** he needs the explanations he produces for himself. Nobody produces an explanation for an act that requires none. If one is being produced, there is something underneath — and that something the person already knows himself.

**Third: the question used as an instrument of deferral.**

The fifth and sixth verses describe a method: to escape a judgement by asking about its **time**.

Its present-day counterparts can be multiplied and none of them requires a religious context. When a responsibility is not to be accepted, the argument is shifted from the responsibility itself to procedure: what exactly is the definition, how far does the boundary run, whose measure is the measure, how certain are the data. Each of the questions is legitimate on its own. But their sum performs a function: **the judgement stays suspended until the argument ends.**

And the measure recorded in the story of the cow in al-Baqara holds here too: what is condemned is not curiosity but the production of a new question each time an answer comes.

**Fourth: the choice made on speed and not on value.**

The diagnosis of the twentieth verse is not a customary moral sentence. It does not say "you love what is wrong"; it says **"you love what is in haste."**

Its counterpart for today is something directly measurable. When a choice is made, the two options are generally compared not by their values but by **when they arrive**. A small return to be had now is preferred to a large return to be had later — and the one who makes the choice takes it for a judgement of value.

What the verse sets down is not a prohibition but a **diagnosis**: before you look at the quality of what you love, ask whether you are looking at its timing.

And the choice of verb in the twenty-first verse completes this: the hereafter is not **called a lie**, it is left. That is, the matter is not a decision of belief but a distribution of attention.

**Fifth: bearing gives belief away.**

The thirty-third verse ends a three-verb portrait with a single description of a walk: *yatamaṭṭā* — with a swagger.

This is the sūra's most practical measure. Belief or denial is expressed in a **bearing** before it is expressed in a sentence. And that bearing takes its plainest form where a person feels he is not being judged — at home, among those close to him.

Read together with al-Inshiqāq a measure emerges: both sūras show the person beside his "people". The question the sūras ask is this: how do you stand where no one is watching you?

**Sixth: being left loose is not freedom.**

The thirty-sixth verse says something by the word's own meaning. *Sudā* is the animal without a herdsman.

Today "answering to no one" is mostly spoken of as a gain, and there is a just side to this: not having to answer to arbitrary authority really is a gain. But the word the verse uses looks somewhere else.

What is not called to account is what is **not considered to matter**. If nobody asks after the outcome of a piece of work, then that work is not thought to have an outcome. This is where the sūra turns the question round: to be called to account is not a punishment but a form of **being counted**.

**Seventh: "receive first, understand after."**

The sixteenth and nineteenth verses set down an order for learning: receiving, following, then explanation.

Its value for today lies in its standing between two widespread attitudes. The first says that nothing not understood should be accepted. The second says that understanding is not needed at all.

The order the verses set up falls into neither: **the explanation is promised** (*inna ʿalaynā bayānah*) — that is, it is not something that will not be understood. But the completion of the explanation at the moment of receiving is not made a condition.

And from here a limit also emerges: if the *bayān* belongs to the source of the text, then every explanation man makes is an attempt. This commentary included.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

- **The function of the *lā* at the head of verse 1** is not certain; the four views in the section on al-Balad were relied on, and it was recorded that in this sūra the second view (rejection of something said before) is the stronger, but no preference was imposed.
- **The conceptual link I set up between ٱلْقَيُّوم and ٱلْقِيَٰمَة** is my own reading; the sharing of the root is a datum of the language, the relation between them is not.
- **That *lawwāma* is a "capacity" and not a "station"** is my own reading. That the ordering among the soul's three adjectives is an interpretation developed in the later tradition is a finding recorded in the section on al-Fajr as well.
- **The relation I set up between the two oaths** (the day outside / the mechanism inside) is my own reading; the placing of the two oaths side by side is a datum of the text.
- **The point drawn between the two branches of the root ح-س-ب** (reckoning and supposing) is an observation about the language; no ruling was issued on the historical development of the root.
- **Two classical readings of *banān* in verse 4** were given; the first reading was preferred and it was written that it is not binding.
- **The fingerprint claim was not entered into.** The meaning of the word was given, it was stated that the verse's proof is a proof from scale, and the modern reading was mentioned and passed over with the note "an attempted reading, not proof". For the grounds, the section on al-ʿAlaq was relied on.
- **Three readings of *li-yafjura amāmah* in verse 5** were given; the second reading was preferred, and it was written that it is not binding and that the first reading is the more widespread in the classical sources.
- **The difference between *ayyāna* and *matā*** was transmitted from some of the lexicographers and it was stated that no ruling was built on it.
- **The variant reading at verse 7** was recorded, but which reading belongs to which authority was not given, because I am not sure. It was stated that no great difference arises in the resulting meaning.
- **Three explanations of "the gathering of sun and moon" in verse 9** were given; no preference was stated.
- **The explanation of how the senses "refuge" and "burden" in the root و-ز-ر are joined** was transmitted from the lexicographers; the conceptual division of labour I set up between the two senses is my own reading.
- **Three readings of *qaddama wa akhkhar* in verse 13** were given; it was stated that they do not exclude one another, and no preference was imposed.
- **Three explanations of *baṣīra*'s coming in the feminine in verse 14** were given; the second reading (proof, *ḥujja*) was preferred and it was written that it is not binding.
- **Two views on the singular of the word *maʿādhīr*** were given; the "curtain" reading was transmitted and it was stated that no ruling was built on it.
- **That the block 16-19 is tied to the sūra through the root ع-ج-ل** is my own reading; the presence of the root in the two verses is a datum of the text.
- **The ascription of the expression "when We recite it" in verse 18 to the angel** was transmitted as the classical explanation, not presented as a settled ruling.
- **Two readings of whether the *thumma* of verse 19 states time or rank** were given; no preference was stated.
- **The conclusion I drew from the *bayān*'s belonging to God, that "every explanation is an attempt",** is my own reading.
- **The distinction of axes I set up among the three names of this life (*al-dunyā*, *al-ūlā*, *al-ʿājila*)** — distance / order / speed — is my own reading; the root meanings are transmitted.
- **The derivation linking *ʿijl* (calf) and *ʿajala*** was transmitted, not presented as a settled link.
- **The explanation linking *ḥubb* and *ḥabb* (grain)** was transmitted from the lexicographers; no ruling was issued on the historical development of the root.
- **No side was taken on the question of the vision of God.** The positions of the two sides, the verses they rely on and the answers they exchange were given in a table; it was recorded that the wording of the verse stands closer to one side, but no preference was stated and it was written explicitly that **it is not binding.** The hadith report about the vision was mentioned with the note "transmitted widely in the hadith sources"; **no definite attribution was given as to its chain of transmitters or its full text.**
- **That the *ziyāda* at Yūnus 10/26 has been interpreted as the vision** was conveyed as transmitted material, not as a ruling.
- **In which sense the *ẓann* of verse 25 stands** was left open; two readings were given, no preference was stated.
- **Two readings of *rāqin* in verse 27** were given; the first reading was preferred and it was written that it is not binding.
- **Three explanations of *iltaffati's-sāqu bi's-sāq* in verse 29** were given; no preference was stated.
- **The nearness in sound of *rāq* / *firāq*** was recorded as an observation, not as a claim of miracle; the same record in the sections on al-Inshiqāq and al-Qāriʿa was relied on.
- **Whether *ṣaddaqa* in verse 31 means "he did not affirm" or "he did not give alms"** was given in two readings; the first was preferred and it was written that it is not binding.
- **Three derivations of the root of *yatamaṭṭā* in verse 33** were transmitted; no preference was made among them.
- **Three views on *awlā laka* in verses 34-35** were given; it was stated that the first two come together in practice.
- **No name was given in the report transmitted as the occasion of revelation of verses 34-35**, because I do not have knowledge enough to issue a ruling on the soundness of the report. The measure of method (*al-ʿibratu bi-ʿumūmi'l-lafẓ*) was relied on.
- **The use of *sudā* in verse 36 in the field of weaving** was found weak and nothing was built on it.
- **The reading of *sudā* as "abandonment"** is my own reading; it was also stated separately that the context of the verse is a warning.
- **The link of herding language I set up between *masāq* and *sudā*** is my own reading; the semantic field of the words is lexical data.
- **The variant reading at verse 37** was recorded but no attribution to an authority was given.
- **The kinship of *manī*, *maniyya* and *amānī* in the root م-ن-ي** was transmitted as a lexical observation; no interpretation was built on it.
- **The embryology claim was not entered into;** the five grounds in the section on al-ʿAlaq were relied on and the same position was kept.
- **The referent of the pronoun *minhu* in verse 39** was left open; both readings were held possible.
- **The relation between ذ-ك-ر and *dhikr*** was found disputed and nothing was built on it. **The explanation of "softness" in the root أ-ن-ث** was transmitted, with no claim to certainty.
- **The observations about the sūra's regions of sound, its economy of repetition and the frames 3-36 / 4-40** were offered as my own reading, not as classical transmitted material.
