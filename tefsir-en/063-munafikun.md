# 63. Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn

Eleven verses. Belonging to the Medinan period. It takes its name from the word in its first verse: **al-Munāfiqūn**.

## What the sūra does

In the opening of Sūrat al-Baqara the hypocrites were described across thirteen verses (2/8-20), and that passage was treated in this commentary. The picture established there was this: the gap between word and condition, self-deception, the corrupter's seeing himself as a reformer, two settings and two languages, and at the end two long comparisons — the fire that goes out, and the man walking in a storm.

This sūra does not repeat that passage. It does something else.

The description in al-Baqara was made **from the inside**: the sickness in the heart, the not noticing, the floundering. Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn, by contrast, looks **from the outside**: how they appear, how they speak, what impression their bodies leave, how they turn their heads, what sentences they utter.

So al-Baqara was a psychological description; this sūra is a **behavioural** one. Neither takes the place of the other; they complete one another.

And the sūra has a claim of its own, not found in al-Baqara. It pictures hypocrisy not as **a deficiency of belief** but as **a technique for protecting a position**: oaths are used as a shield, economic pressure becomes an instrument, superiority becomes something to fight over. What is related is not a state of the heart but a strategy.

Then the sūra, just as in Sūrat al-Māʿūn and Sūrat al-Jumuʿa, turns at the ninth verse and changes the one it addresses: *"O you who believe!"* It says that what has been related over eight verses has a source — wealth and children — and that this source is present in everyone.

This is the sūra's real idea: **hypocrisy begins with wealth and children drawing the attention; and that beginning is open to everyone.**

## The name of the sūra

The root **ن-ف-ق**. Beneath this root there are senses that look hard to join to one another at first sight, but all of them come out of a single centre: **being used up, coming to an end, passing away.**

- **نَفِقَ (nafiqa)** — the using up of wealth, the coming to an end of a thing.
- **نَفَقَ (nafaqa)** — goods being in circulation, a coin passing current. A thing's "passing", its going out of the hand.
- **إِنْفَاق (infāq)** — to let wealth go out of one's hand, to spend.
- **نَفَقَة (nafaqa)** — what is spent on maintenance.
- **نَافِقَاء / نَفَق (nāfiqāʾ / nafaq)** — the **second exit hole** in the burrow of the jerboa (*yarbūʿ*). The animal makes its burrow with two mouths: one visible, the other covered by a thin layer of earth. When danger comes at the front door, it breaks through the second mouth with its head and escapes.

That the word **munāfiq** comes from this last sense is the common record of the classical dictionaries. *Nifāq*, in the word's own logic, is **the condition of having two doors.** A visible entrance, a hidden exit.

This derivation says in advance the whole content of the sūra:

- The second mouth **is not always opened**; it is broken through only when needed. That is, *nifāq* is not a continuous act but a possibility held in readiness.
- The second mouth **is not visible**; a thin layer of earth covers it. One looking from outside sees a single door.
- And most importantly: the burrow **really is a burrow.** The animal lives there. The existence of the second door does not mean that the first door is false.

This last point is needed in order to understand the fine distinction in the sūra's first verse.

There is also the sūra's link with the "spending" branch of the root: in the seventh verse the sentence the hypocrites utter is about **infāq** (*lā tunfiqū*), and in the tenth verse the command given to the believers is again **infāq** (*anfiqū*). So the sūra uses both branches of the root it takes its name from: a group's second door, and the wealth a community lets go out of its hand. Both are forms of "passing away".

## Meccan or Medinan

The sūra is **Medinan**, and there is no disagreement on this. Its ground is the subject itself.

Hypocrisy, as a phenomenon, was not possible in Mecca. The finding recorded for ostentation in Sūrat al-Māʿūn holds here too, and more strongly: *"For ostentation to be able to exist, a precondition is required: that piety be socially rewarded."*

The same holds for hypocrisy, and in a sharper form. For joining a community in appearance to be meaningful, joining that community must carry **a return**. In Mecca becoming a Muslim brought loss: exclusion, boycott, torture. No one becomes a Muslim in appearance — because the appearance has a cost and no return.

In Medina the picture turned around. The Muslim community became the party that determined the political and military structure of the city. From that moment on, the cost of not joining and the cost of joining changed places. Hypocrisy is precisely the product of that exchange.

This is a piece of sociological reasoning; it is not a transmitted report. But it needs recording, because it explains why the phenomenon appeared in a particular place and at a particular time — and it also shows where the same mechanism will work today.

## The reports of the occasion of revelation — and a caution

There is a detailed report in the body of hadith about the occasion of revelation of the sūra, and of its eighth verse in particular.

According to what is transmitted in al-Bukhārī from Zayd b. Arqam: during an expedition Zayd heard ʿAbd Allāh b. Ubayy b. Salūl — mentioned as the leading name among the hypocrites of Medina — say *"Do not spend on those who are with the Messenger of God, so that they disperse"* and *"If we return to Medina, the mightier will drive out the lowlier from it"*, and he reported this to the Prophet. Ibn Ubayy denied the matter; Zayd's truthfulness was established by the sending down of these verses.

In the sources a dispute that arose at a watering place during the expedition is mentioned as the event that led to these words being said. This detail comes by separate lines of transmission; its presentation as a single joined narrative is widespread, but it is not part of one single report.

**A few qualifications must be entered about this report:**

**One.** That the report is found in the sound collections is true. But there are differences of detail among its variants (how the dispute arose, who was present, during which expedition the event took place). I do not go into the detail here, and I adopt no preference among the variants.

**Two.** The name that occurs in the report (ʿAbd Allāh b. Ubayy) is a name repeated in the sources, and it is a piece of historical information for understanding the sūra's first addressees. But note: **the verse gives no name.** The verse says *"they"*; it says *"those who say, if we return"*. The Qurʾān's not giving a name is a deliberate choice, as was recorded at al-Baqara 2/8: *"The Qurʾān describes this group; it does not expose it. And there is a reason for this — a description lasts, an exposure passes. Had a name been given, the text would have been buried in history along with those persons."*

**Three — and this is the most important.** This sūra **cannot be used today to identify particular persons or groups.**

This must be written openly, because it is one of the most abused texts in all of history. The sūra makes **a description of an attribute**. Whoever carries that attribute, the verse is speaking of him. But the attribute itself is, by definition, something **that cannot be seen from outside**: the second door is hidden. Therefore the use of these verses to pass judgement on someone else runs against the verse's own logic.

There is evidence for this from within the text as well: at the ninth verse the sūra turns the address and calls out to the believers. That is, the text itself takes the reader out of the position of "them" and drives him to look at his own condition. What was recorded at the end of Sūrat al-Māʿūn holds here too: *"To pick up these verses and say 'that man too falls under the description' is to turn the sūra's own work upside down. The sūra is an instrument of diagnosis, and a diagnosis is made on oneself first."*

## 63/1 — إِذَا جَاءَكَ الْمُنَافِقُونَ قَالُوا نَشْهَدُ إِنَّكَ لَرَسُولُ اللَّهِ ۗ وَاللَّهُ يَعْلَمُ إِنَّكَ لَرَسُولُهُ وَاللَّهُ يَشْهَدُ إِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ لَكَاذِبُونَ

*Idhā jāʾaka'l-munāfiqūna qālū nashhadu innaka la-rasūlu'llāh. Wa'llāhu yaʿlamu innaka la-rasūluh, wa'llāhu yashhadu inna'l-munāfiqīna la-kādhibūn*

"When the hypocrites come to you they say, 'We bear witness that you are indeed the Messenger of God.' God knows that you are indeed His Messenger; and God bears witness that the hypocrites are liars."

### The centre of the verse: what it is that is false

This verse establishes one of the Qurʾān's finest distinctions, and the distinction is very easy to miss.

The verse consists of three sentences:

1. **They say:** "You are the Messenger of God."
2. **God says:** You are indeed His Messenger.
3. **God bears witness that:** the hypocrites are liars.

Now note: **the second sentence confirms the first.** What they say is true. The verse does not dispute this; on the contrary it affirms it.

What, then, is the "lie" in the third sentence?

The answer lies in the **first word** of the first sentence: *nashhadu* — "we bear witness".

What is declared false is not the **content** of the sentence but **their own bearing of witness.** That is:

> "You are the Messenger of God" — this is true.
> "To this we bear witness" — this is false.

What they say is true; what is false is the claim that they themselves believe what they say.

The classical exegetes establish this distinction openly, and the formulation has settled thus: the lie lies not in the report itself but **in the claim to bear witness.** A person may utter a true sentence and still have lied — if he presents that sentence as his own conviction and it is not his conviction.

### Why this matters so much

At first sight this distinction looks like a technical nicety. It is not. It has a central place in the understanding of the sūra, and of the concept of hypocrisy in general.

**First:** the distinction shows that hypocrisy is **not a deficiency of knowledge**. The verse does not claim that the hypocrites say something wrong. They utter the true sentence. The finding recorded for *taḥrīf* at al-Baqara 2/75 holds here too: *"the matter is not ignorance but what is done with what is known."*

**Second:** the distinction says that **a true word cannot by itself be a measure**. That a sentence is true says nothing at all about the one who says it. This is a very hard finding, and it lowers the value of speech.

**Third:** the distinction explains why the whole of the rest of the sūra proceeds by way of **behaviour**. When speech ceases to be a measure, what is left? In the fourth verse an appearance, in the fifth a movement, in the seventh the consequence of a sentence, in the eighth a plan. Because the sūra took speech out of being a measure in its first verse, in the rest it is obliged to use other measures.

### شَهَادَة — bearing witness

The root **ش-ه-د**: **to be present.** The *shāhid* is the one who was there. *Shahāda* is the report of one who has been there. From the same root come *mashhad* (the place seen, the place witnessed), *mushāhada* (observation) and *shahīd*.

At al-Jumuʿa 62/8 this root occurred as the opposite of *ghayb*: *ʿālimu'l-ghaybi wa'sh-shahāda* — the knower of the unseen and of the seen. As was recorded there, the opposition is not "known / unknown" but **"what is hidden / what is present"**.

Return to the verse with this in mind. To say *nashhadu* is to say **"we were there, we saw, we were present"**. That is, the hypocrites are claiming to rest on an observation.

And the lie is exactly here. They have not seen. Their hearts have not made that observation. Not because they heard the sentence from others — perhaps they have really seen — but because they have **not bound themselves** to that seeing.

The legal side of *shahāda* works here too. In Arabic and in Islamic law *shahāda* is **a binding** declaration: the witness takes on responsibility for his declaration. To bear witness to a thing is to establish a bond between yourself and that thing. What the hypocrites do is to appear to have established the bond without establishing it.

**And note:** in the verse's last sentence the same root is used of God — *wa'llāhu **yashhadu***. That is, the verse sets a true bearing of witness against the hypocrites' false one. The same word, in two different mouths, with two different weights.

This is the most elegant structure in the sūra's first verse: the word carries its own opposite inside itself.

### إِنَّكَ لَرَسُولُ اللَّهِ — an excess of emphasis

Look at the grammar of the sentence: **إِنَّ** (*inna*) + **لَ** (the *lām* of inception). In Arabic this is one of the **strongest** forms of emphasis with which a statement can be given.

In the classical tradition of rhetoric the emphasising of a statement is divided into three according to the condition of the one addressed:

| The condition of the one addressed | The name of the address | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| He has no opinion about the matter | *Ibtidāʾī* | No emphasis is needed |
| He is hesitant | *Ṭalabī* | One emphasis is fitting |
| He denies it | *Inkārī* | Strong emphasis is needed |

Now the question is this: in saying to the Prophet "you are the Messenger of God", what need is there to use the strongest pattern of emphasis?

The one addressed is neither hesitant nor denying about his own messengership. By the rule of rhetoric, there is no need for emphasis here.

**As my own reading** — and recording openly that this is a reading — I think this: the excess of emphasis says something about the sentence itself. If a person is trying to convince someone he does not need to convince, the person he is trying to convince is not the one in front of him.

This is a familiar pattern in everyday life: too much swearing, too much stress, too much repetition. When the force of what is said is not enough, the force of the saying is increased.

I do not attribute this reading to any classical exegete. But that the verse continues in the second verse with *"they have taken their oaths as a shield"* suggests that there is a note in this direction — because an excess of emphasis and an oath belong to the same family.

### Word order: the sequence of the three sentences

The sequence of the sentences is not chance:

1. *Qālū nashhadu innaka la-rasūlu'llāh* — **the claim.**
2. *Wa'llāhu yaʿlamu innaka la-rasūluh* — **the content of the claim is confirmed.**
3. *Wa'llāhu yashhadu inna'l-munāfiqīna la-kādhibūn* — **the maker of the claim is declared a liar.**

Had the middle sentence not been there, the verse would have said something very different: the hypocrites say something and they are lying. That reading would have implied that what they said was wrong too.

The middle sentence closes off that possibility. And in closing it, it does one more thing: **the confirmation of the messengership is stated separately, because it does not depend on the hypocrites' approval.** That is, the verse cuts off the logic of "they affirmed it, so it must be true". The truth comes from somewhere else.

One more detail: in the second sentence it says **رَسُولُهُ** (His Messenger), not *rasūlu'llāh* as in the first. The shift to a pronoun makes the expression closer. In ascribing the Messenger to Himself, God uses a pronoun.

**And a grammatical note:** in the third sentence *inna* is again emphasised with a *lām*: *la-kādhibūn*. That is, the pattern of emphasis the hypocrites used is repeated exactly in the verdict passed on them. The sentence answers with their own weapon.

### What this says to the present

**That a true word is not a measure.** This is the side of the verse that looks most directly at the present, and it is uncomfortable. That a sentence is true says nothing at all about the one who says it. This holds all the more in a period in which knowledge is abundant and true sentences are cheap: saying true things has ceased to be an indicator of a position.

The measure the verse sets down is not the content of the word but **the bond the speaker establishes with that word**. And this bond is not visible from outside. The verse is therefore not an instrument of identification but an instrument of self-examination.

**The weight of bearing witness.** The second side has to do with the legal aspect of the word *shahāda*. To bear witness to a thing is to take on responsibility for it. Today declaration is very cheap: supporting a view, announcing which side one stands on, joining a cause, most often brings no burden at all. The verse gives a name to the declaration that carries no burden.

## 63/2 — اتَّخَذُوا أَيْمَانَهُمْ جُنَّةً فَصَدُّوا عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّهُمْ سَاءَ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

*Ittakhadhū aymānahum junnatan fa-ṣaddū ʿan sabīli'llāh. Innahum sāʾa mā kānū yaʿmalūn*

"They have taken their oaths as a shield and turned others from the way of God. Evil indeed is what they have been doing!"

### أَيْمَان — oaths

The root **ي-م-ن**. At the centre of the root is **the right hand**: *yamīn* is the right hand. From the same root come *yumn* (blessing, good fortune) and *maymana* (the right side).

That the oath carries this name comes from the Arabs' manner of swearing: the parties would strike their right hands together, or clasp hands. That is, *yamīn*, in the word's own logic, is **the handshake.** The sealing of a word by a physical movement.

This makes the word's use here sharper: an oath is in fact an instrument for **establishing a bond**. It binds two parties to one another. In the verse it is being used as an instrument for **separating**.

### جُنَّة — a shield

The root **ج-ن-ن** was fully analysed at al-Baqara 2/25; I do not repeat it here. The centre of the root: **to cover, to conceal.** Its derivatives are surprisingly wide, and all of them turn around "invisibility":

- *janīn* — the one covered in the womb
- *jinn* — the being that is not seen
- *majnūn* — one whose reason is covered
- *mijann* — a shield (the thing one hides behind)
- *janna* — the garden that covers the ground with its trees

Here the **shield** branch of the root is used. **جُنَّة (*junna*)** means a shield — in the same field of meaning as *mijann*.

It is worth dwelling on what a shield is, because the whole force of the comparison lies there:

**A shield is an instrument of defence.** It does not attack; it protects. One hides behind it. And most importantly: **the one holding the shield does not become invisible** — the shield does not conceal its holder, it stops **the blow coming at him**.

This distinction matters. The verse does not say "they took their oaths as a curtain"; it says "as a shield". A curtain conceals; a shield **meets.** That is, it is not that the hypocrites are invisible — they are visible, but they stop with an oath the questioning that comes at them.

**And the oath's turning into a shield corrupts what an oath is.**

Normally the function of an oath is this: because a word may be untrustworthy, a guarantee is added to the word. The oath is the weight placed behind the word.

When it is used as a shield this relation is reversed. The oath no longer supports the word; **it blocks the question.** When someone asks "is it really so?", the answer is not a proof but an oath. And an oath, by definition, cannot be questioned — because to question it would be to accuse the other of lying.

So the oath works by making questioning **socially costly**. This is exactly what a shield is: it is not itself a proof, but it makes asking for a proof impossible.

**The same expression occurs once more in the Qurʾān.** Al-Mujādala 58/16: *"They have taken their oaths as a shield and turned others from the way of God; for them there is a humiliating punishment."* The first sentence is **word for word the same** as this verse; the difference lies only at the end. Al-Mujādala ends with an announcement of punishment, al-Munāfiqūn with a reproach.

A sharing of this degree between two sūras suggests that there are other links between them as well — and indeed there are, and they will be taken up at the fifth verse.

### فَصَدُّوا عَنْ سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ

The root **ص-د-د**: to turn away, to hold back, to block; and to turn one's own face away.

**There is a real disagreement here**, and it turns on whether the verb is transitive or intransitive:

| Reading | Its meaning | Who is affected |
|---|---|---|
| **Intransitive** (*lāzim*) | "They themselves turned away from the way of God" | Only themselves |
| **Transitive** (*mutaʿaddī*, with its object dropped) | "They held people back from the way of God" | Others |

The verb *ṣadda* is used in both ways in Arabic, and both uses occur in the Qurʾān.

In the classical commentaries the second sense is mostly preferred, and the ground is the context: if an oath is being used as a **shield**, there is a conflict at hand; and a shield, while it protects its holder, also holds an area. Moreover the rest of the verse says *"what they have been doing"* — an activity is being spoken of.

In my judgement the two do not exclude one another, and the dropping of the word's object is probably deliberate. The finding recorded for *yurāʾūna* at al-Māʿūn 107/6 works here too: the dropping of the object widens the scope. They themselves turn from the way, and they also hold others back. This is a preference; it is not binding.

**سَبِيل** — root **س-ب-ل**: a road that stretches out. *Sabīl* is a road that is travelled on, open and definite. In the word's concrete sense there is a flowing — *isbāl* is a thing's hanging down and extending.

### إِنَّهُمْ سَاءَ مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ

**سَاءَ** — one of the Arabic **verbs of blame**: "how evil it is". In the same function as *biʾsa*. At al-Jumuʿa 62/5 *biʾsa mathalu'l-qawm* occurred.

**مَا كَانُوا يَعْمَلُونَ** — *kāna* + the imperfect: a habit that has been going on. Not a single event, but what has come to be done.

And note: the verdict is passed on **deed**, not on belief. The verse does not say "how evil is what they believe"; it says **"how evil is what they do"**.

This accords with the sūra's general method. In the first verse speech was taken out of being a measure; in the second verse the verdict is bound directly to the act.

### What this says to the present

**Instruments that block questioning.** The side of the verse that works most today is **what the shield does**: it offers no proof; it makes asking for a proof costly.

This mechanism is not limited to oaths. In an argument, an answer by the party being questioned that turns the questioning itself into a moral offence does the same work. "Are you accusing me of lying?", "Are you questioning my intention?", "How can you not believe this?" — all of these are shields. They do not answer; **they make the question invalid.**

The verse's diagnosis is this: the person holding the shield is not winning the argument; he is **ending** it. And ending is not winning.

**The inflation of the oath.** The second side has to do with the oath itself. The more often an oath is used in a community, the less weight it carries. An oath's working depends on its being rare. An oath used as a shield wears away both its own function and the function of every oath in the community.

## 63/3 — ذَٰلِكَ بِأَنَّهُمْ آمَنُوا ثُمَّ كَفَرُوا فَطُبِعَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ فَهُمْ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

*Dhālika bi-annahum āmanū thumma kafarū fa-ṭubiʿa ʿalā qulūbihim fa-hum lā yafqahūn*

"That is because they believed, then denied. So their hearts were sealed; and now they cannot grasp."

### The four steps of a process

The verse builds a chain, and the connectives between the links of the chain differ — this shows how the process works:

| Step | Verb | Connective | What it denotes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *āmanū* — they believed | — | The beginning |
| 2 | *kafarū* — they denied | **ثُمَّ** (*thumma*) | Time passed in between |
| 3 | *ṭubiʿa* — they were sealed | **فَ** (*fāʾ*) | Without interval, a direct consequence |
| 4 | *lā yafqahūn* — they do not grasp | **فَ** (*fāʾ*) | Again a direct consequence |

The two connectives say two different things.

**ثُمَّ** (*thumma*) stands between the first and second steps: **there is time in between.** A period passed between the believing and the denying. That is, what is related is not a sudden turn but **an erosion**.

**فَ** (*fāʾ*) stands between the second and third and between the third and fourth steps: **there is no interval.** As soon as the denial comes, the seal comes; as soon as the seal comes, the grasping goes.

This difference of connectives describes how the process works: **the beginning slow, the end fast.** A person moves away little by little, then a threshold is crossed and the rest happens of itself.

At al-Jumuʿa 62/5 the same connective did the same work: *ḥummilū **thumma** lam yaḥmilūhā* — they were loaded with it, then did not carry it. There too there was a period in between, and what was related was not a refusal but a wearing down. Two neighbouring sūras describe the same process with the same connective.

### آمَنُوا ثُمَّ كَفَرُوا — "they believed, then denied"

This expression is the most disputed place in the verse, and one must be honest: had they really believed?

In the classical commentaries there are three views:

| View | Ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **They believed with their tongues and denied with their hearts** | This is the definition of hypocrisy; *āmanū* here means "they said they had believed" | The verse says *āmanū*, not "*qālū āmannā*" — no qualification has been entered as it was at al-Baqara 2/8 |
| **They really believed for a time, then turned back** | The plain sense of the verb; that the connective *thumma* denotes time in between | It does not agree with the usual definition of hypocrisy |
| **The first for some of them, the second for others** | That the group is not a single person | It may look like an evasion |

I adopt no preference. But this must be recorded: **whichever view is taken, the result is the same.** The verse is not writing a psychological history; it is announcing a **result**. And the cause of that result is the same in both cases: at some point contact was made, and then it was cut.

The first comparison at al-Baqara 2/17 appears to support this reading. There too a light **was seen for a moment**: *"when the fire lit up what was around him…"* As was recorded there: *"so there had been a moment of light."* That is, in describing this type the Qurʾān does not picture him as someone who never made contact.

### فَطُبِعَ عَلَىٰ قُلُوبِهِمْ — the seal

The root **ط-ب-ع**: to press on a thing so as to leave a mark, to stamp, to take an impression. From the same root come *ṭabʿ* (an impression, and also character — what has been stamped upon a person), *ṭabīʿa* (nature, disposition) and *maṭbaʿa* (a printing house).

At al-Baqara 2/7 a different root was used for the same work: **خ-ت-م** (*khatama* — to seal).

The distinction the lexicographers make between the two words is this:

| Root | Its concrete sense | What it implies |
|---|---|---|
| **خ-ت-م** (*khatm*) | To seal the mouth of a vessel, to close it | The closing of entry |
| **ط-ب-ع** (*ṭabʿ*) | To press a stamp onto a surface, to leave a mark | The fixing of a shape |

I do not present this distinction as a firm rule; there are places in the sources where the two words are counted as synonyms. But that the root *ṭabʿ* also carries the sense of "character" is meaningful: the stamp pressed onto a thing becomes that thing's **lasting shape**.

The balance recorded for the seal at al-Baqara 2/7 holds here too: this is not an arbitrary obstruction but **the consequence of a choice.** The word order of the verse shows this openly — the seal comes **after** the verb *kafarū*, and with the connective *fāʾ*. The cause first, the consequence after.

Moreover the verb is in the **passive**: *ṭubiʿa*. The agent is not stated. This is a structure the Qurʾān uses often in places of this kind, and it does not make responsibility vague — on the contrary, the contrast with the verb in the preceding clause (*kafarū*, active) shows which side did what.

### فَهُمْ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ — they do not grasp

The root **ف-ق-ه**: **to go down into the depth** of a thing, to grasp its subtlety, to understand what lies beneath a word.

This root differs from *ʿilm* and *fahm*, and the difference matters:

| Root | What it does |
|---|---|
| **ع-ل-م** (*ʿilm*) | To know — the acquiring of knowledge |
| **ف-ه-م** (*fahm*) | To understand — the grasping of what is said |
| **ف-ق-ه** (*fiqh*) | To go into the depth — to grasp what lies **behind** what is said |

The lexicographers most often describe *fiqh* thus: to draw out from a word what that word does not say openly but requires. That Islamic law carries this name comes from here: *fiqh* is the work of drawing out the ruling that lies beneath the texts.

Now return to the verse: *fa-hum lā yafqahūn* — "they cannot grasp".

The verse does not say "they do not know". **They do know.** We saw it in the first verse: they utter the true sentence. The knowledge is in place.

What is missing is the capacity to **see behind** the knowledge. The condition of not being able to see what a word amounts to, where it arrives, what it asks of them.

And this fits the metaphor of the seal exactly. A surface that has been stamped is not fit for something new to be stamped upon it — its shape has been fixed. The knowledge comes but changes nothing, because the place that would change has already taken the mould.

**And the sūra will use this word once more.** The seventh verse will again end with *lā yafqahūn*. The eighth verse, on the other hand, with *lā yaʿlamūn*. Two different words, for two different subjects — this distinction will be taken up at the eighth verse.

At al-Baqara 2/12-13 there was a distinction of the same kind: verse 12 ended with *lā yashʿurūn* (they do not sense), verse 13 with *lā yaʿlamūn* (they do not know). As was recorded there, the change of word was not idle. Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn uses the same technique.

### Context

The third verse gives **the ground** of the first two. *Dhālika bi-annahum* — "that is because they…"

So the sequence is this:

1. **63/1** — What they say (and the gap between what they say and their condition).
2. **63/2** — What they do (turning an oath into a shield).
3. **63/3** — What the cause of this is (a contact cut at some point, and the seal that comes as its consequence).

And the fourth verse will relate **how all this looks from outside**. The sūra moves from the inside outwards: word, act, cause, appearance.

## 63/4 — وَإِذَا رَأَيْتَهُمْ تُعْجِبُكَ أَجْسَامُهُمْ ۖ وَإِنْ يَقُولُوا تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ ۖ كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُسَنَّدَةٌ ۖ يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ ۚ هُمُ الْعَدُوُّ فَاحْذَرْهُمْ ۚ قَاتَلَهُمُ اللَّهُ ۖ أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ

*Wa idhā raʾaytahum tuʿjibuka ajsāmuhum, wa in yaqūlū tasmaʿ li-qawlihim. Ka-annahum khushubun musannada, yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥatin ʿalayhim. Humu'l-ʿaduwwu fa'ḥdharhum, qātalahumu'llāh, annā yuʾfakūn*

"When you see them their bodies please you; and if they speak you listen to their words. They are like timbers propped up. They think every cry is against them. They are the enemy, so beware of them. May God destroy them! How are they turned away!"

### The sūra's longest verse

This verse is the sūra's centre, and it consists of six separate sentences. Each sentence adds something to the one before it, and the picture is built step by step:

1. **The appearance** — their bodies are impressive.
2. **The voice** — their speech is listened to.
3. **The comparison** — timbers propped up.
4. **The inner state** — thinking every cry is against them.
5. **The verdict** — they are the enemy.
6. **The close** — an imprecation and an expression of astonishment.

The order is meaningful: it runs from the outside inwards. First how they appear, then what they are, then what they feel.

### تُعْجِبُكَ أَجْسَامُهُمْ — their bodies please you

The root **ع-ج-ب**: a thing's surprising a person, being outside the ordinary, arousing wonder. From the same root come *ʿajīb* (astonishing), *taʿajjub* (wonder) and *ʿujb* (self-admiration).

In Form IV (*aʿjaba*) it becomes transitive: "it pleased him, it won his admiration, it impressed him".

**أَجْسَام** — the plural of *jism*. Root **ج-س-م**: the body of a thing, its volume, its mass.

The choice of word is particularly striking here. The verse does not say *"their appearance"* (*ṣuwaruhum*) or *"their faces"* (*wujūhuhum*). It says **"their bodies"**.

At the centre of the word *jism* is **volume**: that which takes up space. A face carries identity; a body carries only mass. That is, the verse says that what is impressive is not a personality but a **weight of presence**.

In the context of the period this has a concrete equivalent: height, bearing, imposing presence, good clothing. That leading men in a community should arouse respect by their physical presence was an ordinary thing in that period, and it still is today.

**And there is something to notice:** the verse does not deny this effect. It does not say *"you suppose them impressive"*; it says **"they please you"**. The effect is real. The verse is not correcting an illusion; it is saying **what a real impression does not measure**.

This distinction matters. Had the sūra claimed that the appearance was deceptive, the remedy would have been to look carefully. The verse says something harder: the appearance is a true appearance, and even so it says nothing.

### وَإِنْ يَقُولُوا تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ — if they speak, you listen

**A nicety of word order:** the sentence is built not as *tasmaʿu qawlahum* (you hear their words) but as **تَسْمَعْ لِقَوْلِهِمْ** (you give ear to their words). A *lām* has come in between.

In Arabic, when the verb *samiʿa* takes a direct object it means "to hear"; when it comes with a *lām* it gains the sense of **giving ear, listening, being inclined to accept**. The difference is the difference between passive hearing and active listening.

So the verse does not say "their words reach your ear"; it says **"you listen to their words"**. Their words are found worth listening to.

This joins with the finding of the first verse. There it was recorded that what they say is **true**. Here it is recorded that their manner of saying it is **impressive** as well. Together the two verses draw the portrait of a persuasive speaker.

And the structure of the conditional clause is interesting: *wa in yaqūlū* — "**if** they speak". That is, their speaking is not regular; when they do speak they are effective. This accords with the image of immobility in the verse's next sentence: timbers do not always speak.

### كَأَنَّهُمْ خُشُبٌ مُسَنَّدَةٌ — timbers propped up

The sūra's central comparison, and one of the densest images in the Qurʾān.

**خُشُب** — the plural of *khashab*. Root **خ-ش-ب**: wood, timber, a felled tree.

The choice of word is critical. In Arabic **شَجَر** (*shajar*) is used for a living tree. *Khashab*, by contrast, is a **felled** tree: a log, timber, firewood.

So the comparison is made not to a tree but to **a dead tree**. And the difference between the two words is exactly the sūra's subject:

| | Shajar | Khashab |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | Living | Felled |
| Its root | In the soil | None |
| Does it grow | Yes | No |
| Does it stand | Of itself | Only if propped |

And in the Qurʾān *shajar* is mentioned together with a root: *"A good word is like a good tree, its root firm and its branches in the sky"* (Ibrāhīm 14/24). There the quality of the tree is **the firmness of its root**.

*Khashab* is precisely the tree that has no root.

**A note on the variant reading:** it is recorded in the sources that this word is read as *khushub* (with *ḍamma* on the *shīn*) and as *khushb* (with the *shīn* vowelless). There is no difference of meaning between the two readings; both are two forms of the same plural. I make no attribution to particular imāms of the variant readings.

**مُسَنَّدَة** — root **س-ن-د**: to lean on a thing, to rest against it; and to support a thing.

The word is a **passive participle**: the passive adjective of Form II. That is, **"propped up"** — not leaning, but made to lean.

This passivity is the vital point of the comparison. The timbers are not leaning of themselves; **someone has propped them.** Their standing is not their own doing.

**A relative of the root:** from the same root comes **إِسْنَاد** (*isnād*) — in the science of hadith, the chain of transmitters on which a report rests; and **سَنَد** (*sanad*) — a support. A report's *sanad* is what holds that report up.

This is a fine link established at the level of the word: *musannada* means "having a support". And the timbers in the comparison are things that do not carry their own weight and stand only by a support. Looked at from outside they stand upright; when the support is withdrawn they fall.

### The comparison unpacked

Now let us look at what the comparison says. It says three things at once:

**One. Complete from outside, empty within.** A log carries all the outward properties of the tree: the same weight, the same height, the same grain. The only thing missing is **life** — and that is not visible from outside.

**Two. To be standing is not to be able to stand.** The passivity of the word *musannada* says this. To stand inside a community does not mean to belong to it. A support can take the place of belonging.

**Three. Uselessness.** A felled tree serves two purposes: either it is burned or it is used in a building. The log propped against a wall does neither. It is neither alive nor in use. **It takes up space and does no work.**

And this third point joins with the verse's first sentence: *tuʿjibuka ajsāmuhum* — their bodies, that is, their **volumes**, please you. A log too is volume. The same idea is said twice: taking up space is taken for a sign of presence.

### A comparison with the two comparisons in al-Baqara

At al-Baqara 2/17-20 two long comparisons were established for the hypocrites, and they were treated in this commentary. Now we have a third comparison in hand, and the comparison is instructive.

| | **al-Baqara 2/17-18** | **al-Baqara 2/19-20** | **al-Munāfiqūn 63/4** |
|---|---|---|---|
| The picture | A man who kindles a fire, its light taken away | A man walking in a downpour | Timbers propped against a wall |
| Movement | It happened once, then stopped | **Intermittent** — he walks in the lightning, stands still in the dark | **None** |
| The senses | Deaf, dumb, blind | Fingers in the ears, the sight about to be taken | The body impressive, the voice listened to |
| Life | Alive but useless | Alive, in fear | **Dead** |
| What it awaits | Nothing — *lā yarjiʿūn* | The next flash of lightning | A cry |

**The greatest difference is movement.** In both pictures in al-Baqara there is a movement: in one a movement that has stopped (*"they will not return"*), in the other an intermittent movement (*"whenever it lights up for them they walk in it, and when darkness falls over them they stand still"*).

In al-Munāfiqūn there is no movement at all. The log does not walk, cannot halt, does not return. It only stands.

This difference has a meaning. The pictures in al-Baqara related a **process** — the taking away of the light, the interruption of the walking. The picture in al-Munāfiqūn relates a **result**: the place where the process ends.

Another difference: both comparisons in al-Baqara were built on **light**. In the comparison in al-Munāfiqūn there is no light at all. Light has to do with showing direction; for a thing that has no direction, the question of light has fallen away as well.

And a third difference, perhaps the most important: the men in al-Baqara were **on their own**. It was he who had kindled the fire, he who was walking in the storm. The timbers in al-Munāfiqūn have been **propped up** — passive. That is, al-Baqara describes an individual, al-Munāfiqūn a **position**.

Read together, the three comparisons yield a sequence: the one who loses his light, the one who walks intermittently, and at last the one who does not move at all. The three are like three points on one road.

### يَحْسَبُونَ كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ عَلَيْهِمْ — thinking every cry is against them

The sūra's sharpest observation, and psychologically an extremely accurate finding.

**يَحْسَبُونَ** — root **ح-س-ب**: to count, to reckon; and from there "to suppose, to think". The link is this: to suppose is to make a reckoning with the data in one's hands. From the same root come *ḥisāb* and *ḥasīb*.

The verb is imperfect: a continuous, repeated condition.

**صَيْحَة** — root **ص-ي-ح**: to shout aloud, to cry out. A *ṣayḥa* is a single cry.

To look at this word's use in the Qurʾān adds a further colour to the verse. *Ṣayḥa* is used regularly in the Qurʾān for **the cry that destroys**: the punishment that annihilated Thamūd and other peoples is in most places called *al-ṣayḥa* (Hūd 11/67, 11/94; al-Ḥijr 15/73, 15/83). That is, in the Qurʾān's lexicon the word is a sound of ruin.

This makes the verse sharper: they take every sound for a *ṣayḥa* — that is, in every noise they expect ruin.

**كُلَّ صَيْحَةٍ** — "every cry". Without exception. No distinction is made.

**عَلَيْهِمْ** — "against them". A predicate has been dropped here (*maḥdhūf*): "they think every cry is [something] against them". The classical grammatical sources supply it with words such as *wāqiʿatun* (falling) or *kāʾinatun* (being).

In Arabic the particle *ʿalā* denotes what comes from above and what is against. Had it been *lahum* (in their favour) the sense would have been the exact opposite. That is, every sound is perceived as something coming down upon them.

### The condition of being constantly on the alert

The condition this sentence describes is worth dwelling on.

What is related is not cowardice. A coward is afraid when there is something to be afraid of. What is described here is different: **making a reckoning of danger even when nothing is happening.**

The reason for this is not in the verse itself but is there in the sūra as a whole: in the first verse a gap was established between word and condition; in the second verse a shield that protects that gap. That is, these people **are carrying something that has to be protected.**

And everyone who has a secret to protect makes the same reckoning: *"Has it been found out?"* Once this question settles in the mind it is applied to every stimulus. A glance, a silence, the sound of a door, a summons to a meeting — all become data for the same question.

Today this is called **hypervigilance**, or the state of being on the alert, and it is a condition well described in the psychological literature. The observation the verse makes agrees with it. But I draw no claim from this that "the Qurʾān knew psychology in advance" — that would be a forcing of the kind the method (`USLUP.md`) forbids. What can be said is this: **the verse correctly describes the price of carrying a hidden contradiction**, and that description agrees with observation.

Let us note what the price is: **there is no ease.** The punishment the sūra assigns to this type is not a punishment seen from outside. Their position is secure, their bodies impressive, their words listened to. But every sound comes down upon them.

This accords with the comparison too: for a propped-up log every tremor is a threat, because its remaining upright depends not on its own strength but on the support.

### هُمُ الْعَدُوُّ فَاحْذَرْهُمْ

**A nicety of word order:** *humu'l-ʿaduww* — subject and predicate, both definite. In Arabic this structure denotes **restriction (*ḥaṣr*)**: "**they** are the enemy", that is, "the real enemy is not someone else, it is they".

At al-Baqara 2/13 there was a similar structure: *"they are the foolish ones."* The same pattern, the same work.

**الْعَدُوُّ** — it comes singular, not plural (it does not say *aʿdāʾ*). In Arabic the word *ʿaduww* can be used for both singular and plural; and in its definite form (*al-*) it denotes **the class**: "the thing called enemy".

So the verse does not say "they are enemies"; it says **"the enemy — this is it"**. A description of a category.

The root **ع-د-و**: to pass, to cross, to cross a boundary. From the same root come *ʿadā* (he ran, he passed), *ʿudwān* (overstepping the bound, aggression), and the uses carrying the sense of transgression. And what is interesting: *ʿaduww*, in the word's own logic, is **"the one who crosses the boundary"**.

This explains why the verse chose this word. Enmity here is not a declaration of war; it is **the crossing of the boundary from within.** The enemy outside is already outside; the one who has crossed the boundary is inside.

**فَاحْذَرْهُمْ** — root **ح-ذ-ر**: to be on one's guard, to beware, to stay awake.

It is necessary to notice what the command is not. The verse does not say *"fight them"*, does not say *"drive them out"*, does not say *"punish them"*. It says **"beware"**.

In Arabic *ḥadhar* denotes not fighting an enemy but **being watchful** against him. At the centre of the word is not attack but attention.

This accords with the sūra's whole stance, and it is an important qualification: **the command given about a type that has been identified is not a command to take action but a command to pay attention.** And a command to pay attention asks the one addressed to change his own condition, not the other's.

And indeed this is how it was historically: that no prosecution was begun against the persons described in this sūra, and that on the contrary they were allowed to remain within the community, is the common record of the sources.

### قَاتَلَهُمُ اللَّهُ — an idiom

The root **ق-ت-ل**: to kill. In Form III (*mufāʿala*, *qātala*) it normally means "to fight one another".

But here the word is used **idiomatically**. In Arabic the expression *qātalahu'llāh* is a settled pattern with two functions:

1. **Imprecation:** "may God destroy him", "may God curse him".
2. **An expression of astonishment:** it is sometimes used even in a positive context, as a reaction to something surprising — as with the English "well I'll be damned", or an admiring "damn him", where the face of the phrase and its function come apart.

Here the first sense is the primary one. Some of the classical exegetes explain it as *"may He curse them"*.

**And that this pattern was chosen is meaningful.** Form III requires two parties. A human being's entering into "mutual combat" with God is unthinkable. The force of the expression lies exactly in that impossibility: one of the parties is counted as having entered a fight in which he has no chance at all.

### أَنَّىٰ يُؤْفَكُونَ — "how are they turned away"

**أَنَّىٰ** — an interrogative particle: "how", "from where", "in what way". Here it is not a real question; it denotes **astonishment**.

**يُؤْفَكُونَ** — root **أ-ف-ك**: to turn a thing from its direction, to divert it from the course it ought to take.

The best-known derivative of the root is **إِفْك** (*ifk*): a great lie, a slander. The link is plain — *ifk* is the truth in a state of having been **turned from its direction**. That falsehood should be named from this root describes falsehood not as a "fabrication" but as a **diversion**: there is an existing truth, and it is being turned.

From the same root comes *muʾtafika* — used in the Qurʾān for the cities that were overturned (al-Najm 53/53, al-Tawba 9/70). That is, the root carries "being turned out of its place" in its most concrete form as well.

**The verb is passive:** *yuʾfakūn* — "they are turned away". The agent is not stated. They are not turning of themselves; they are being turned.

This passivity belongs to the same family as the *musannada* of the verse. Two passive forms, one at the beginning of the verse and one at its end: they are propped up, and they are turned away. Both denote a position outside their own will.

And the verse ends with a question — a question whose answer is not given. This resembles the technique in the opening of Sūrat al-Māʿūn: the question does not **impose** the answer, it **makes one find** it.

### What this says to the present

**One. That the appearance is true but empty.** The most useful side of the verse is that it does not count the appearance false. Their bodies really are impressive, their words really are worth listening to. The verse is not correcting an illusion; it is saying that a true impression **measures nothing**. This holds all the more in a period in which the management of impressions has become a profession: the bond between the quality of presentation and the quality of content grows weaker as the technical means increase.

**Two. A support taking the place of belonging.** The passivity of the word *musannada* says that standing inside an institution or a community is not the same thing as belonging to it. And this distinction is not visible from outside — because as long as the support stays in place, the log too stands upright. The distinction comes out only when the support is withdrawn.

**Three. The price of a hidden contradiction.** "Thinking every cry is against them" describes the bill that inconsistency presents to a person. And the bill is not visible from outside: the position secure, the standing intact. What is paid is **inner ease**. This is the sūra's most practical observation, and it can be verified without requiring any religious frame.

**Four. The limit of the command "beware".** The verse's command is *fa'ḥdharhum* — be watchful. It is not a command to punish. This is a binding limit for the sūra's use today: the only command given against an attribute that has been identified is attention, and attention begins with oneself.

## 63/5 — وَإِذَا قِيلَ لَهُمْ تَعَالَوْا يَسْتَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ لَوَّوْا رُءُوسَهُمْ وَرَأَيْتَهُمْ يَصُدُّونَ وَهُمْ مُسْتَكْبِرُونَ

*Wa idhā qīla lahum taʿālaw yastaghfir lakum rasūlu'llāhi lawwaw ruʾūsahum wa raʾaytahum yaṣuddūna wa hum mustakbirūn*

"When they are told, 'Come, and the Messenger of God will ask forgiveness for you', they twist their heads away; and you see them turning aside in arrogance."

### The record of a movement

Here the sūra does something not often met with in the Qurʾān: it puts **a movement of the body** on record.

In the preceding verse there was an appearance (bodies, timbers). Here there is a movement: **the turning of the head.**

And the way the movement is recorded is striking. The verse does not say "they refused", does not say "they did not accept", does not say "they did not want it". It says they twisted their heads away.

That is, the refusal is given not with a word but **with the body**. This accords with the sūra's general method: in the first verse speech was taken out of being a measure; here the only meaningful thing is what the body does.

### تَعَالَوْا — "come"

Root **ع-ل-و**: to rise, to be high. From the same root come *aʿlā* (the most high), *ʿuluww* (height) and *taʿālā* (He is exalted).

The verb has an interesting history. The command *taʿāla* (come) means, by the root of the word, **"come up"**. According to what the lexicographers transmit, this use comes from a person standing in a high place calling to someone below: "come up". Later it became fixed and gained the general sense of "come".

This past of the word says something fine here: the call is a call **upward**. And the attitude described at the end of the verse is *mustakbirūn* — those who make themselves great. That is, the people called up refuse by counting themselves already up there.

I record this link as a nicety of language; it carries no value as a ruling, but it shows the picture behind the word.

### يَسْتَغْفِرْ لَكُمْ — the content of the offer

The root **غ-ف-ر**: **to cover.** *Mighfar* is the war-helmet that covers the head. *Maghfira* is the covering of a sin — not its erasure but the closing over of it.

In Form X (*istaghfara*): "to ask that it be covered".

And now note what the offer is: what is asked of them is not **to repent**, not **to confess**, not **to apologise**. They are only asked **to come**. Someone else will do the asking for forgiveness.

So the threshold has been lowered as far as it will go. The same technique recorded at al-Māʿūn 107/3: *"the threshold has been pulled down as far as it will go."* There what was asked was not the giving but the urging; here it is not the doing of something but only the coming to a place.

And precisely for this reason the meaning of the refusal grows heavier. If an offer whose cost is almost nothing is refused, the reason for the refusal cannot be cost. One reason alone remains: **accepting must mean giving up a position.**

And indeed the end of the verse says this: *wa hum mustakbirūn*.

### لَوَّوْا رُءُوسَهُمْ — they twisted their heads away

The root **ل-و-ي**: **to bend, to twist, to turn.** To twist a rope, to turn a thing about its own axis.

The derivatives of the root show its centre:

- *liwāʾ* — a banner (a cloth twisted and wound)
- *ilwiyya* — a thing twisted and coiled
- *layy* — bending, and **the putting off of a debt** (in Arabic *layyu'l-wājid* — a rich man's delaying of his debt)

The last sense is interesting: postponement is, in the word's logic, a **bending**. The warping of something that ought to go straight.

**A note on the variant reading:** the word has been read as *lawwaw* (with *shadda* on the *wāw*, Form II) and as *lawaw* (without *shadda*, Form I). The reading with *shadda* denotes **repetition and multiplicity**: turning not once but again and again; or the turning not of one person but of many. The reading without *shadda* gives the plain verb. The direction of the meaning does not change. I make no attribution to particular imāms of the variant readings.

### The root's network within the Qurʾān — and its link with taḥrīf

This root occurs in several other places in the Qurʾān, and in all of them it does the same work: **the bending of what ought to go straight.**

**Āl ʿImrān 3/78:** *"And among them there is a group who **twist their tongues** with the Book (*yalwūna alsinatahum bi'l-kitāb*), so that you may suppose it to be from the Book. But it is not from the Book."*

**al-Nisāʾ 4/135:** *"…If **you bend and twist** (*talwū*) or turn away, know that God is aware of what you do."* — This verse is in the context of **bearing witness**.

Now set the three uses side by side:

| Place | What is bent | Context |
|---|---|---|
| **Āl ʿImrān 3/78** | The tongue | The transmission of the text |
| **al-Nisāʾ 4/135** | The word (testimony) | Bearing witness |
| **al-Munāfiqūn 63/5** | The head | An answer to an invitation |

All three make the same movement: **the bending of what ought to be straight.**

And from here there is a bridge to al-Baqara 2/75. There **تَحْرِيف** (*taḥrīf*) was taken up, and what was recorded was this: the root **ح-ر-ف** means "edge, tip, boundary"; *taḥrīf*, in the word's own logic, is **"to bend a thing from its edge"**. *"Not to tear the text down the middle; to pull it slightly from its margin, to shift its direction. A small crookedness produces a large deviation at a distance."*

**ل-و-ي** and **ح-ر-ف** are two names for the same work. One relates the bending, the other the pulling from the edge. Neither breaks; both **bend.**

And there is an order of intensity between the two concepts:

1. **Taḥrīf** (2/75) — the text is bent.
2. **Layy al-lisān** (3/78) — the tongue is bent; the text stays as it is, but its recitation is twisted.
3. **Layy al-raʾs** (63/5) — the body is bent. There is no longer even a need to deal with the text; the direction is changed directly.

The third is the plainest, and precisely for that reason the most honest: to turn one's head away is not an interpretation but an answer.

**And another link:** in Sūrat al-Mujādala (58/8) the hypocrites' corrupting of the greeting when they come to the Prophet is spoken of — *"When they come to you they greet you with a greeting with which God has not greeted you."* The same structure: *wa idhā jāʾūka*. Al-Munāfiqūn 63/1 also opens with the same pattern: *idhā jāʾaka'l-munāfiqūn*.

In both sūras what is related is **the bending of a gesture**: in one the greeting, in the other the movement of the head. This is taken up in detail in `058-mucadele.md`; it may be consulted there.

### وَرَأَيْتَهُمْ يَصُدُّونَ — "you see them turning aside"

The root **ص-د-د** occurred in the second verse. There its object had been dropped and two readings were possible. Here the verb is used intransitively: they themselves are turning aside.

**رَأَيْتَهُمْ** — "you see them". The verb is perfect but the sense is continuous. And note: the address is again in the second person singular — the same as the *raʾaytahum* of the fourth verse.

In these two verses the sūra gives the one addressed the role of **an observer**: when you see them, this is what you see, and this. That is, the verses do not merely give information; they teach **a way of looking**.

This does the same work as the first verse of Sūrat al-Māʿūn: *a-raʾayta* — "have you seen?" As was recorded there: *"the matter is not to make a list but to gain a way of looking."*

### وَهُمْ مُسْتَكْبِرُونَ — in arrogance

The root **ك-ب-ر**: to be great. In Form X (*istakbara*) it can take two senses:

1. **To seek:** to want greatness, to arrogate greatness to oneself.
2. **To count:** to see a thing as great.

Here the first. A *mustakbir* is a person who appropriates to himself a greatness he does not deserve.

At al-Muddaththir 74/23 this root was taken up, and what was recorded there was this: in that sūra **ك-ب-ر** occurs in three positions — greatness that is commanded (*kabbir*), greatness that is usurped (*istakbara*), greatness that comes as recompense. That is, the root turns around the question of who is great.

**A nicety of word order:** *wa hum mustakbirūn* is a **circumstantial clause**: it states how the act of turning aside is performed. That is, arrogance is recorded not as the cause of the turning aside but as its **manner**.

The difference is fine but important. The verse does not say "they turned aside because of their arrogance"; it says **"they turned aside in a state of arrogance"**. That is, the arrogance shows within the movement. The very act of turning the head away is a display of arrogance.

This accords with everyday observation: how a refusal is made most often says more than what is refused.

### The link with Iblīs's ground

At al-Baqara 2/34 Iblīs's not prostrating was explained by *istikbār*, and at al-Baqara 2/90 it was recorded: *"The same structure as Iblīs's ground: there too the matter was not the command itself but whose favour it was in. Envy, like arrogance, is born of a comparison."*

The same structure works here too. What is offered (forgiveness) is not in itself something to be refused. What is refused is **whom the offer comes from** and what accepting it would mean. To accept that forgiveness be asked for you is to accept that you need forgiveness.

Arrogance blocks exactly this: the admission of a need.

## 63/6 — سَوَاءٌ عَلَيْهِمْ أَسْتَغْفَرْتَ لَهُمْ أَمْ لَمْ تَسْتَغْفِرْ لَهُمْ لَنْ يَغْفِرَ اللَّهُ لَهُمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْفَاسِقِينَ

*Sawāʾun ʿalayhim astaghfarta lahum am lam tastaghfir lahum lan yaghfira'llāhu lahum. Inna'llāha lā yahdi'l-qawma'l-fāsiqīn*

"It is all one for them whether you ask forgiveness for them or do not ask forgiveness for them; God will not forgive them. God does not guide the people who have gone outside the bound."

### The same pattern as al-Baqara 2/6

In pattern this verse is exactly the same as al-Baqara 2/6, and the comparison is instructive:

| | **al-Baqara 2/6** | **al-Munāfiqūn 63/6** |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | *sawāʾun ʿalayhim* | *sawāʾun ʿalayhim* |
| Condition | *a-andhartahum am lam tundhirhum* | *astaghfarta lahum am lam tastaghfir lahum* |
| About whom | *alladhīna kafarū* — those who deny | *al-munāfiqūn* |
| Result | *lā yuʾminūn* — they do not believe | *lan yaghfira'llāhu lahum* — they will not be forgiven |

The same grammatical skeleton is used for two different groups and two different verbs.

**And the difference is meaningful.** In al-Baqara what is equalised is **warning**: whether you warn them or do not warn them, they do not believe. Here what is equalised is **asking forgiveness**.

That is, in al-Baqara the ineffectiveness of speech is stated; here, the ineffectiveness of prayer. Both announce the closing of a door — but the one in al-Munāfiqūn is heavier, because there it was the addressee's own reaction (not believing) that was at issue; here it is the fruitlessness of something someone else does for him.

**And its place within the sūra:** in the fifth verse the offer was refused. The sixth verse announces the consequence of the refusal. The sequence is: the offer came, it was refused, and now the offer has no meaning left.

This accords with the metaphor of the seal (63/3). The door has been closed from inside.

### سَوَاءٌ عَلَيْهِمْ — "it is all one for them"

The root **س-و-ي**: equality, evenness, equivalence. From the same root come *taswiya* (levelling), *musāwāt* (equality) and *sawī* (even).

**Word order:** *sawāʾun* is the subject, and the two conditional clauses that follow stand in the position of its predicate (or the reverse; there are two explanations in the grammatical sources). *ʿAlayhim* — "for them", that is, the equality is on their side.

The emphasis lies here: the equality is not **in the result** but **from their side**. That is, the verse does not say "asking forgiveness is of no use"; it says "for them it makes no difference". The nuance matters — because the verse announces not the invalidity of a rule but the imperviousness of a particular condition.

### أَسْتَغْفَرْتَ — a note on the spelling and the reading

In the word **أَسْتَغْفَرْتَ** at the head of the verse, the interrogative *hamza* and the verb's connecting *hamza* have merged. Its original form is *a-istaghfarta*; when the two *hamza*s come together the connecting *hamza* drops and it becomes *astaghfarta*.

This is a technical matter of spelling and it does not affect the meaning; I record it because, in reading the text, it needs to be seen that the word is a question.

**أَمْ** — "or". A connective denoting equivalence between two alternatives. The same connective was there at al-Baqara 2/6.

### لَنْ يَغْفِرَ اللَّهُ لَهُمْ

**لَنْ** — the definite negation of the future. As was recorded at al-Jumuʿa 62/7, it is more definite than *lā*, and it looks to the future.

**There is a question here, and one must be honest:** does this expression mean that the door of forgiveness has closed absolutely?

In the classical commentaries two answers are given to this question:

| View | Ground |
|---|---|
| The ruling holds **as long as the condition of nifāq continues**; if a person changes his condition, the ruling changes too | The Qurʾān's keeping the door of repentance open in general; the presence at al-Nisāʾ 4/146 of an exception for the hypocrites, on the conditions of repentance, amendment and sincerity |
| The ruling is **a definite announcement** about those who persist in this attitude | The plain sense of the expression |

Al-Nisāʾ 4/146 is decisive in this discussion: there it is said of the hypocrites, *"Except those who repent, and amend, and hold fast to God, and make their religion pure for God"*. This exception shows that the ruling is not absolute.

In my judgement the first view is the stronger, and its ground is this exception within the Qurʾān itself. But this preference **is not binding**, and this qualification matters: what the verse announces is what the outcome will be if that condition is persisted in.

It should also be compared with **al-Tawba 9/80**: *"Ask forgiveness for them or do not ask forgiveness for them; even if you ask forgiveness for them seventy times, God will not forgive them."* The same ruling, with an emphasis of number. "Seventy" here is not a definite number but a use denoting multiplicity in Arabic.

### إِنَّ اللَّهَ لَا يَهْدِي الْقَوْمَ الْفَاسِقِينَ

The root **ف-س-ق**. Its concrete sense is very distinct: **a thing's coming out of its skin.** In Arabic one says *fasaqati'r-ruṭaba* — the ripe date came out of its skin.

That is, *fisq*, in the word's own logic, is **going outside the bound**. And note: the place gone out of is a skin — a protective covering. A fruit that comes out of its skin is left unprotected.

This is an interesting side of the root. *Fisq* is pictured not as the breach of a rule but as **a coming out of a protection**. The rule is thought of as the skin that wraps the fruit.

**The word's place here:** al-Jumuʿa 62/5 and 62/7 ended with *al-ẓālimīn*. Al-Munāfiqūn 63/6 ends with *al-fāsiqīn*. Two neighbouring sūras use two different roots:

| Root | Its concrete sense | What it describes |
|---|---|---|
| **ظ-ل-م** (al-Jumuʿa) | To put a thing somewhere other than its place | Misplacement |
| **ف-س-ق** (al-Munāfiqūn) | To come out of the skin | Going outside the bound |

Both presuppose an order but describe different breaches: one a misplacement inside the order, the other a going out of the order.

**لَا يَهْدِي** — the same expression was there at al-Jumuʿa 62/5: *wa'llāhu lā yahdi'l-qawma'ẓ-ẓālimīn*. The two sūras use the same pattern with a different attribute.

And here too the same balance holds: the withholding of guidance is placed **after** the attribute. First the being *fāsiq*, then the absence of guidance.

## 63/7 — هُمُ الَّذِينَ يَقُولُونَ لَا تُنْفِقُوا عَلَىٰ مَنْ عِنْدَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ حَتَّىٰ يَنْفَضُّوا ۗ وَلِلَّهِ خَزَائِنُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

*Humu'lladhīna yaqūlūna lā tunfiqū ʿalā man ʿinda rasūli'llāhi ḥattā yanfaḍḍū. Wa li'llāhi khazāʾinu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ, wa lākinna'l-munāfiqīna lā yafqahūn*

"They are the ones who say, 'Do not spend on those who are with the Messenger of God, so that they may disperse.' Yet to God belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth; but the hypocrites cannot grasp it."

### هُمُ الَّذِينَ — restriction

In the fourth verse there was *humu'l-ʿaduww*; here *humu'lladhīna yaqūlūn*. The same structure of restriction: "those who say this — they are the ones".

For this structure it was recorded at al-Māʿūn 107/2: *"The description is stuck to the person. This man may have other qualities as well. The verse throws them all aside and makes a single attribute his identity."*

Here the attribute made into an identity is a sentence that was said.

### لَا تُنْفِقُوا — "do not spend"

The root **ن-ف-ق** — that is, the root of the sūra's own name. It was opened out above in the discussion of the sūra's name: at the centre of the root are **being used up and passing away**; *infāq* is the letting of wealth go out of one's hand.

Now note: the sūra's name is **munāfiq** (the one with a second door), and the verb in this verse is **infāq** (the letting of wealth go out of the hand). The same root, two branches.

And the relation does not look like chance. In both there is a "passing away":

- *Nifāq*: a person's slipping secretly out of his own position.
- *Infāq*: wealth's going out of the hand.

The difference is this: one is a **hidden** exit, the other an **open** one. And the people the verse describes are trying to block the open exit, *infāq*.

The sūra will return to this root once more in the tenth verse — this time in the imperative: *wa anfiqū*. So the same root occurs in three positions in the sūra: an identity (*munāfiqūn*), a prohibition (*lā tunfiqū*), a command (*anfiqū*). This is one of the sūra's hidden backbones.

### عَلَىٰ مَنْ عِنْدَ رَسُولِ اللَّهِ — "those who are with the Messenger"

The most striking side of the sentence is this phrase.

What would one have expected to be said? "Do not spend on the Muslims", "do not spend on the emigrants", "do not spend on them". What is said is: **"on those who are with the Messenger of God."**

There are two things here.

**One. The method of definition.** People are described not by their own identity but by **whom they are with**. This shows the picture in the mind of the one who framed the sentence: the group opposite him is not a community of belief but a crowd around one man. That is, the matter is seen not as a cause but as **a circle**.

This explains why the plan of economic pressure looked reasonable. A circle can be dispersed; a belief cannot. The plan's reckoning follows from its wrong definition of what stood opposite it.

**Two. The use of the title.** In the sentence it says **رَسُولِ اللَّهِ** — "the Messenger of God". That is, the person framing the plan uses the title of the man he is opposing.

This is exactly the same as the picture in the first verse. There too they uttered the true sentence: *"You are the Messenger of God."* Here too they use the title — but they do not draw the conclusion the title requires.

By this repetition the sūra shows something: **to use the right word is not to accept the word's meaning.** This is the behavioural equivalent of the *nashhadu* distinction in the first verse.

### حَتَّىٰ يَنْفَضُّوا — "so that they may disperse"

The root **ف-ض-ض** was taken up at al-Jumuʿa 62/11. Its concrete sense: **to break a thing and scatter its pieces**, to separate what is joined. In Form VII it is reflexive: to scatter of oneself, to break up, to empty out.

And the table recorded there is completed here:

| Place | Sentence | Mood |
|---|---|---|
| **Āl ʿImrān 3/159** | *la'nfaḍḍū min ḥawlik* | Conditional — **it could have happened** |
| **al-Jumuʿa 62/11** | *infaḍḍū ilayhā* | Perfect — **it happened** |
| **al-Munāfiqūn 63/7** | *ḥattā yanfaḍḍū* | Imperfect, of purpose — **it is wanted to happen** |

Three verses use the same verb about the same community.

And the link between the two neighbouring sūras is established exactly here. In Sūrat al-Jumuʿa the sound of a caravan had scattered the congregation — with no one's plan, of itself. In Sūrat al-Munāfiqūn the same dispersal appears as **the aim of a strategy**.

That is, al-Jumuʿa records a fragility; al-Munāfiqūn shows how that fragility was meant to be used. Read side by side, the two sūras yield this: **the place where a community can scatter of itself can also be targeted from outside.**

**حَتَّىٰ** — here it denotes purpose: "so that…". The aim of the plan is stated openly.

### The cutting off of infāq as economic pressure

The method this verse describes is worth dwelling on, because it is a simple but effective mechanism.

**Historical background.** In Medina after the emigration the emigrants had come leaving their property behind in Mecca. Their livelihood depended in large part on the support of the *anṣār* and on shared resources. That is, one part of the community was economically dependent on the other.

This made the plan described actually workable. If the support is cut off, those who are dependent cannot stay; those who cannot stay disperse; and with those who disperse the community disperses too.

**The character of the method:** no prohibition, no violence, no open hostility. Only **the not doing of something** is asked for. And the thing asked not to be done is something nobody is obliged to do — *infāq* is voluntary.

That is, the plan is built entirely within a **lawful** field. No one will commit a crime; only generosity will be stopped.

This is the dark side of the finding recorded at al-Māʿūn 107/3. There it was said that even the failure to urge the feeding goes on record: *"Not doing evil is not counted as enough; not being the voice of the good goes on record too."*

Here a step further is taken: not the failure to be the voice of the good, but **the attempt to silence the voice of the good.** And the mechanism works from the same place: the not giving of something that does not have to be given breaks no rule at all — but its outcome is the scattering of a community.

### وَلِلَّهِ خَزَائِنُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضِ

**Note the form of the answer.**

The verse could have said: "This plan will not work", "The believers will not go hungry", "God will provide for them". It says none of these.

What it says is: **the treasuries belong to God.**

That is, the answer is not a promise but **a correction of ownership.** The plan's error is not in its reckoning but in its assumption: the person framing the plan supposes that the source is in his own hand.

The root **خ-ز-ن**: to store a thing, to accumulate it, to take it into safekeeping. A *khazīna* is the place where what is stored is kept. From the same root comes *khāzin* (the keeper of a treasury).

At the centre of the word is **stock**: what is not used at once, what is stored, what is brought out at the moment of need.

And this answers the plan's logic directly. The plan rested on this: if the support is cut off, the source runs out. The answer: **the source is not the stock in your hands.**

**This expression occurs in several places in the Qurʾān**, and it generally does the same work: correcting where the source is. What is particular about the use at al-Munāfiqūn 63/7 is that it has been set against a very concrete economic calculation.

### وَلَٰكِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ لَا يَفْقَهُونَ

The verb that occurred in the third verse comes round again: *lā yafqahūn* — they do not grasp.

And the choice of the word is particularly apt here. **ف-ق-ه**, as was recorded above, is "to grasp what lies behind a word" — not knowledge but inference.

What is it they do not grasp? The verse is plain: whose the treasuries are. But this is not a matter of knowledge. Probably, if they heard the sentence "to God belong the treasuries of the heavens and the earth", they would not object either — as we saw in the first verse, they are able to utter the true sentences.

What is not grasped is the sentence's **consequence**: if the source is there, the reckoning here is empty. Not being able to establish the bond between the knowledge and the reckoning — this is exactly what the lack of *fiqh* is.

This is connected with the sūra's next verse too. There as well there will be a similar error of reckoning, and the verb used there will be different: *lā yaʿlamūn*. This difference of word between the two verses will be taken up at the eighth verse.

### What this says to the present

**Being wrong about where the source is.** The verse's answer is neither a threat nor a consolation; it is **a correction of position**. And its mechanism can be generalised: one person's power over another most often rests on the assumption that the source is in his own hand. If the assumption is wrong the power is wrong too — but this is not seen until the power is tried.

**Pressure built within the lawful field.** The most striking side of the method the verse describes is that it breaks no rule. This is the most widespread form of pressure today as well: withdrawing support, cutting off the relationship, drying up the source. None of them is forbidden, and all of them work. That the verse recorded this shows that moral judgement passes **beyond legality**.

## 63/8 — يَقُولُونَ لَئِنْ رَجَعْنَا إِلَى الْمَدِينَةِ لَيُخْرِجَنَّ الْأَعَزُّ مِنْهَا الْأَذَلَّ ۚ وَلِلَّهِ الْعِزَّةُ وَلِرَسُولِهِ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَلَٰكِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ

*Yaqūlūna la-in rajaʿnā ila'l-Madīnati la-yukhrijanna'l-aʿazzu minha'l-adhall. Wa li'llāhi'l-ʿizzatu wa li-rasūlihi wa li'l-muʾminīna wa lākinna'l-munāfiqīna lā yaʿlamūn*

"They say, 'If we return to Medina, the mightier will surely drive out the lowlier from it.' Yet might belongs to God, and to His Messenger, and to the believers; but the hypocrites do not know it."

### The framing of the sentence: a claim that does not name itself

The most striking side of this sentence is **that it does not say who is who**.

*"The mightier will drive out the lowlier"* — who is the mightier? Who is the lowlier? The sentence does not state it.

And its not stating it is a choice. The speaker does not name himself "the mightier"; he frames a sentence stating a general rule and places himself somewhere inside that rule. This is the safest way to advance a claim: it does not look like a direct claim, it looks like an observation.

The same technique is widespread today: instead of praising oneself, to set down a criterion and leave unsaid who stands where on it.

**And the Qurʾān transmits this sentence as it is.** It adds no name, does not say "he meant himself". The sentence is recorded just as it was framed — and the answer is given in the sentence's own terms.

**A nicety of word order:** *la-in* + *la-yukhrijanna* — Arabic's strongest oath-and-condition structure. The conditional particle beginning with a *lām* (*la-in*) carries the sense of an oath, and its answer too comes with a *lām* + the heavy *nūn* (the *nūn* of emphasis).

That is, the sentence is not an ordinary prediction; it is **a threat reinforced with an oath**. And this connects with the finding of the second verse: in the mouths of these people an oath is an instrument.

### الْأَعَزُّ and الْأَذَلُّ — two roots

The root **ع-ز-ز**. It was opened out at al-Jumuʿa 62/1 in the discussion of the name *al-ʿAzīz*: the concrete sense of the root is **hardness, impenetrability, resistance to wear**; and from there **fewness, rarity.** In Arabic *arḍun ʿazāz* means "hard ground that cannot be dug".

The bond between the two branches of the root matters: what is hard does not wear away, what does not wear away does not diminish, what is few becomes valuable. *ʿIzza*, in the word's own logic, is **not being able to be worn away**.

The root **ذ-ل-ل**: softness, docility, the state of having been made to submit.

And the most concrete use of this root in the Qurʾān is in Sūrat al-Baqara. At al-Baqara 2/71, in the counting out of the qualities of the cow that was to be slaughtered: **لَا ذَلُولٌ تُثِيرُ الْأَرْضَ** — "not broken to the yoke, not having ploughed the soil". This passage was taken up in `002-bakara.md`, and the table was recorded there thus: *"Not broken to the yoke, not having ploughed a field, not having watered a crop, without blemish, without a spot — very nearly a single animal."*

**ذَلُول** (*dhalūl*) is **the animal that has been broken in, domesticated, brought under the yoke**. This is the concrete sense of the root.

That is, *al-adhall*, in the word's own logic, is not "worthless" but **"the one made to submit"**. The opposition comes to this:

| | Root | Concrete sense | Figurative sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| **الْأَعَزّ** | ع-ز-ز | Hard ground that cannot be dug | What cannot be worn away, what cannot be made to submit |
| **الْأَذَلّ** | ذ-ل-ل | The animal accustomed to the yoke | The one made to submit |

The two words describe a **relation of domination**. The matter is not standing but who has made whom submit.

**The elative pattern.** Both are on the *afʿal* pattern: "the more mighty", "the more lowly". That is, not an absolute attribute but **a comparison** is being established. The whole logic of the sentence rests on a comparison.

And this comes out at the same place as the finding recorded at al-Baqara 2/34 and 2/90: *"Envy, like arrogance, is born of a comparison."* So it is here too: the claim is not a claim of worth in itself but a claim of ranking.

### وَلِلَّهِ الْعِزَّةُ — the form of the answer

The answer takes the sentence's own word and changes its place.

What would the expected answer have been? *"The truly mighty are the believers."* That would have been to play the same game — merely to reverse the ranking.

The verse does not do this. It says: **might belongs to God.**

That is, the answer does not correct the ranking; it corrects **whom the thing being ranked belongs to**. *ʿIzza* is not goods to be shared out between the parties. Its owner is known.

**A nicety of word order — and an important one:** *li'llāhi'l-ʿizzatu* — in the sentence *li'llāhi* (belonging to God) has been **brought forward**, and *al-ʿizzatu*, the subject, has been left behind.

In Arabic the bringing forward of the predicate denotes **restriction (*ḥaṣr*)**. That is, the expression is not "might belongs to God" but **"might belongs to God alone"**.

And this structure is exactly the same as the answer in the seventh verse: *wa **li'llāhi** khazāʾinu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ*. Two verses one after the other, with the same word order, doing the same work:

| Verse | What was claimed | The answer |
|---|---|---|
| **63/7** | The source is in our hands | *Li'llāhi* the treasuries |
| **63/8** | The might is ours | *Li'llāhi* the *ʿizza* |

Both plans rest on an assumption of ownership; both answers correct the ownership. The sūra shows the same error in two different fields.

### وَلِرَسُولِهِ وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ — the ordering

The sentence continues with a *lām* repeated in three places: *li'llāhi… wa li-rasūlihi… wa li'l-muʾminīn.*

**The repetition of the *lām* each time** is meaningful. It could have said *li'llāhi wa rasūlihi wa'l-muʾminīn*; that would have been shorter. The repetition makes each of them counted separately.

**The order too is meaningful:** God, the Messenger, the believers. The *ʿizza* is given first to its owner, then downwards in two steps.

And the conclusion that follows is important: the believers' *ʿizza* does not come **from themselves**. They are third in the list, and they are there by way of connection. That is, the verse does not give the believers a superiority; it counts them as **bound to the source** of the *ʿizza*.

This distinction prevents the verse from being turned into a claim of group superiority. The measure recorded at al-Baqara 2/62 holds here too: **a label does not save.** *ʿIzza* is not the automatic output of a membership; it is the consequence of remaining bound to the source.

### وَلَٰكِنَّ الْمُنَافِقِينَ لَا يَعْلَمُونَ — why "they do not know" this time?

The seventh verse ended with *lā yafqahūn* (they do not grasp). The eighth verse ends with *lā yaʿlamūn* (they do not know).

Two verses one after the other, the same structure, a different verb. This is the same technique as at al-Baqara 2/12-13, and it was recorded there: *"The change of word is not idle."*

Let us try to establish the difference:

| Verse | Subject | Verb | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **63/7** | The treasuries belong to God | *lā yafqahūn* — they do not grasp | This knowledge requires **an inference**: if the source is there, the reckoning here is empty. *Fiqh* is the seeing of what lies behind a word. |
| **63/8** | The *ʿizza* belongs to God | *lā yaʿlamūn* — they do not know | This is **a basic piece of knowledge**; it requires no inference. If it is not known, it is not known from the very beginning. |

That is, in the first verse what is missing is the capacity for inference; in the second, the knowledge itself.

I present this as my own reading. Differences of verse-ending of this kind are dwelt on in the classical sources; the establishing of the distinction here in this way is my own inference, and it is not binding.

Another reading is also possible: one may look for no meaningful difference between the two verbs, and it may be said that the verse-ending changed for the harmony of sound. I do not exclude this possibility.

### The structure of the fight over might

The dispute this verse describes is worth dwelling on, because its structure is very familiar.

The claim is this: in a place there are two parties, one of them is the mightier, and the mightier drives out the other.

The assumptions inside this sentence:

1. **Might is something measurable.**
2. **Might confers the right to exclude.**
3. **The place belongs to the mightier.**

The verse's answer does not target all three assumptions; it only changes the **subject** of the first. And this is enough: if might is something measurable and belongs to neither of the parties, the second and third assumptions collapse of themselves.

As a way of framing an argument this is striking. The verse does not enter into the dispute and say "no, it is we who are the mightier". It pulls away the **ground** of the dispute.

## 63/9 — يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا لَا تُلْهِكُمْ أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَلَا أَوْلَادُكُمْ عَنْ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ ۚ وَمَنْ يَفْعَلْ ذَٰلِكَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْخَاسِرُونَ

*Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū lā tulhikum amwālukum wa lā awlādukum ʿan dhikri'llāh. Wa man yafʿal dhālika fa-ulāʾika humu'l-khāsirūn*

"O you who believe! Let neither your wealth nor your children distract you from the remembrance of God. Whoever does that — they are the losers."

### The place where the sūra turns

For eight verses "they" were spoken of. In the ninth verse the address changes: **"O you who believe!"**

This is the same manoeuvre as at al-Māʿūn 107/4, and the sibling of the transition at al-Jumuʿa 62/9. All three sūras do the same work:

| Sūra | How many verses of "they" | The point of turning | How it turns |
|---|---|---|---|
| **al-Māʿūn** | 3 | 107/4 | *Fa-waylun li'l-muṣallīn* — hard, sudden |
| **al-Jumuʿa** | 8 | 62/9 | *Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū* — with a command |
| **al-Munāfiqūn** | 8 | 63/9 | *Yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū* — with a warning |

In al-Māʿūn it was recorded: *"the sūra first sets up a 'they', puts the reader at ease, and then draws the line across to the reader's own side. This is not a text that points outward; it is a mirror."*

**And the turn in al-Munāfiqūn is the heaviest of the three.** Because what is related over eight verses is a type nobody would want to find in himself. For eight verses the reader is safe.

Then the ninth verse comes and shows **the beginning** of that type: wealth and children.

This is the sūra's most important claim, and it must be said openly: **the sūra says that hypocrisy is not a type of character but the end of a process.** And the beginning of the process lies in two things everyone has: the wealth he owns and the children he loves.

### لَا تُلْهِكُمْ — let them not distract you

The root **ل-ه-و** was taken up in detail at al-Takāthur 102/1; I do not repeat it here. In summary: its primary sense in the lexicon is **to turn away from a thing, to move away from it, to be occupied with something else.** *Lahw* is other than what the English word "amusement" carries: **an occupation that holds a person back from his real business, that takes his attention from there and fastens it elsewhere.** At the centre of the word lies not cheerfulness but **displacement**.

**And the verb pattern is exactly the same.** Al-Takāthur 102/1: *alhākum* — Form IV, transitive. Al-Munāfiqūn 63/9: *lā tulhikum* — the same form, in the negative imperative.

In the discussion of al-Takāthur it was recorded for this pattern: *"not 'he was distracted' but 'it distracted' — transitive. That is, there is a subject at hand, and that subject distracts the human being. Here the human being is in the passive position: not the one distracting himself but the one who has been distracted."*

The same structure holds here too, but with one difference: **al-Takāthur was making a finding; al-Munāfiqūn gives a command.**

| | al-Takāthur 102/1 | al-Munāfiqūn 63/9 |
|---|---|---|
| Mood | Perfect, a statement | Imperfect, a prohibition (negative imperative) |
| What it says | *Alhākum* — it distracted you (it is done) | *Lā tulhikum* — let it not distract you (it must not be) |
| Subject | *al-takāthur* (competing in abundance) | *amwālukum wa awlādukum* |

And there is a nicety of word order: the prohibition is directed **at the wealth and the children**. Not "do not be distracted" but "let them not distract you". That is, the grammatical addressee of the command is the wealth and the children.

This is a known manner in Arabic, and its effect is this: **the responsibility is still with the one addressed, but the source of the danger has been named.** The command is framed not as "pay attention" but as "do not let this thing do this to you". This implies that the danger is continuous and active — wealth and children are distracting of themselves; something has to be done for them not to distract.

**Another thing recorded in the discussion of al-Takāthur is completed here:** it was recorded there that the question *"abundance of what"* was left unanswered, and that this was deliberate. And this note was entered: the verse that supplies the lexicon of Sūrat al-Takāthur (al-Ḥadīd 57/20) fills the gap by saying *"in wealth and children"*.

Al-Munāfiqūn 63/9 counts out the same two things: **amwāl and awlād.**

### أَمْوَالُكُمْ وَلَا أَوْلَادُكُمْ — wealth and children

This pair occurs together regularly in the Qurʾān, and each time it serves the same function.

**أَمْوَال** — the plural of *māl*. Root **م-و-ل**: what is owned. What is interesting is the comment the lexicographers make on the root's connection with *mayl* (inclination): wealth is what one inclines towards. I do not count this derivation as certain; but it relates the word's function well.

**أَوْلَاد** — the plural of *walad*. Root **و-ل-د**: to give birth.

**Why these two?** Because both of them are:

1. **Legitimate.** The Qurʾān condemns neither the acquiring of wealth nor the having of children. Both are counted as favours.
2. **Loved.** Both are things a person naturally turns towards.
3. **Reaching into the future.** Wealth represents tomorrow, children the day after tomorrow. Both are a promise of **continuity**.

The third is perhaps the most important. In accumulating wealth and in raising children, a person is in fact trying to extend himself into the future. And precisely for this reason these two are the strongest rivals of the thought of death.

The sūra's next verse will take death itself as its subject. So the sequence is this: the instruments of extension into the future (9) → the real future (10-11).

**The network within the Qurʾān:**

- **al-Taghābun 64/15** — the very next sūra: *"Your wealth and your children are only a trial; and with God is a great reward."*
- **al-Anfāl 8/28** — the same expression: *"And know that your wealth and your children are a trial."*
- **al-Ḥadīd 57/20** — in the description of the life of this world: *"…and boasting of abundance in wealth and children."*
- **al-Kahf 18/46** — *"Wealth and sons are the adornment of the life of this world."*

Three neighbouring sūras — al-Jumuʿa, al-Munāfiqūn, al-Taghābun — turn around this matter. In al-Jumuʿa a trading caravan, in al-Munāfiqūn the warning about wealth and children, in al-Taghābun the naming of the same pair as *fitna*.

And note: nowhere does the Qurʾān count this pair as **evil**. *Fitna* (a trial), *zīna* (adornment), the thing that can be a cause of *lahw* — all of them are neutral or positive words. What is condemned is not the object but **the place the object takes.**

### عَنْ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ — from the remembrance of God

The particle **عَنْ**. This particle was taken up in detail at al-Māʿūn 107/5, and it was recorded there: *"The basic function of the particle ʿan in Arabic is mujāwaza: parting from a thing, moving away from it, leaving it behind."*

The same particle does the same work here: the distraction works in the direction of **moving away** from the remembrance.

And there is a parallel between the two verses:

| Place | Structure | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Māʿūn 107/5** | *ʿan ṣalātihim sāhūn* | Moving away from the prayer |
| **al-Munāfiqūn 63/9** | *ʿan dhikri'llāh* | Moving away from the remembrance |

In both, the same particle, the same direction.

**ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ** — it occurred at al-Jumuʿa 62/9 as well: *fa'sʿaw ilā dhikri'llāh*. There the remembrance was a **destination**, and one was to hasten towards it. Here it is a **position**: the place from which one must not move away.

Two neighbouring sūras use the same phrase in two directions: in one it is gone towards, in the other one is asked not to move away from it.

### وَمَنْ يَفْعَلْ ذَٰلِكَ فَأُولَٰئِكَ هُمُ الْخَاسِرُونَ

The root **خ-س-ر**: diminution, loss, the loss of capital. This root was taken up in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr (103/2).

The commercial side of the word works particularly here. *Khusr* is the diminution of the principal in a transaction — not a failure to make a profit but **the losing of what one had in hand.**

And when the sūra's context is considered, this word falls exactly into place. In the preceding verses reckonings were being made over wealth: cut off the *infāq* so that they disperse; let the mightier drive out the lowlier. Now an answer comes in the same language: **the real loss is here.**

The metaphor of trade established for the hypocrites at al-Baqara 2/16 should be recalled: *"This transaction brought them no profit."* As was recorded there, the Qurʾān uses this language often, and the description of the transaction should be read carefully — **they had something in hand and they let it go.**

**A nicety of word order:** *fa-ulāʾika **humu'l**-khāsirūn* — a definite subject + a definite predicate + the pronoun *hum* in between. This is one of the strongest structures of restriction in Arabic: "those, precisely those, are the losers".

For the pronoun *hum* it was recorded at al-Māʿūn 107/5: *"a pronoun written separately although grammatically not obligatory… it is for assignment and reinforcement."*

**And note the structure of the conditional clause:** *wa man yafʿal dhālika* — "whoever does that". General. No name, no group, no membership. After the type called "the hypocrites" has been related over eight verses, the ruling is left open-ended in the form **"whoever does it"**.

This is the basic qualification of the method (`USLUP.md`) found within the text itself: what is described is **an attribute**, and whoever carries that attribute is included in it.

## 63/10 — وَأَنْفِقُوا مِنْ مَا رَزَقْنَاكُمْ مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَأْتِيَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ فَيَقُولَ رَبِّ لَوْلَا أَخَّرْتَنِي إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ قَرِيبٍ فَأَصَّدَّقَ وَأَكُنْ مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ

*Wa anfiqū min mā razaqnākum min qabli an yaʾtiya aḥadakumu'l-mawtu fa-yaqūla rabbi lawlā akhkhartanī ilā ajalin qarībin fa-aṣṣaddaqa wa akun mina'ṣ-ṣāliḥīn*

"Spend of what We have provided for you, before death comes to one of you and he says, 'My Lord! If only You had granted me respite for a short term, so that I might give in charity and be among the righteous.'"

### The place of the command: the answer given to the prohibition

In the seventh verse the hypocrites' sentence was this: **لَا تُنْفِقُوا** — "do not spend".

The command in the tenth verse: **وَأَنْفِقُوا** — "spend".

The same root, the same form, one a prohibition and one a command. Three verses later the sūra gives the exact counterpart of that sentence.

And the counterpart is **not a direct refutation.** The verse does not say "do not do what they say". It only gives the command. That is, the answer is not at the level of argument but at the level of behaviour.

This accords with the sūra's general method: in the first verse speech was taken out of being a measure; from beginning to end the sūra speaks not with words but with acts.

### مِنْ مَا رَزَقْنَاكُمْ — "of what We have provided for you"

This expression occurred at al-Baqara 2/3 as well: *wa mimmā razaqnāhum yunfiqūn* — "and they spend of what We have provided for them". There it was the third attribute of the godfearing.

**And it says two things:**

**One. مِنْ (*min*) — the partitive.** "Of the provision", that is, **of a part of it.** The whole is not asked for. This is the same particle and the same function as the *min faḍli'llāh* at al-Jumuʿa 62/10: a measure is set both on the seeking and on the giving.

**Two. رَزَقْنَاكُمْ — "We provided".** The subject of the verb is the first person plural. That is, the thing to be given is not what the giver produced himself but what was given to him.

This comes out at the same place as the reading recorded for the phrase *"the food of the needy"* at al-Māʿūn 107/3. It was recorded there: *"the food given is not the giver's in the first place; it is the needy man's. The giver is not bestowing a favour; he is handing over another's property to its owner."*

Here the same idea is established by way of the source of ownership: what is in your hand is not yours; it was given to you. And this accords with the answer in the seventh verse too: *the treasuries belong to God.*

### مِنْ قَبْلِ أَنْ يَأْتِيَ أَحَدَكُمُ الْمَوْتُ — a qualification of time

The command has an **expiry date**. And the date is an unknown date.

**أَحَدَكُمُ** — "to one of you". Singular. In a command addressed to a crowd, death comes in the singular. This is something the Qurʾān does regularly in describing death: life is collective, death singular.

The finding recorded at al-Baqara 2/48 — the loneliness of the reckoning — is tied in here.

**يَأْتِيَ** — "to come". Death is described not as a place one goes to but as **something that comes**. Man does not go to it; it comes to man. This is the same conception as the *mulāqīkum* (it will meet you) at al-Jumuʿa 62/8.

### فَيَقُولَ — and then he will say

Here the verse sets up a scene: the man at the moment of death, and the sentence he will say.

This is a technique the Qurʾān uses often — the relating of an outcome **from the mouth** of the one who undergoes it. In the discussion of al-Māʿūn it was recorded for al-Muddaththir 74/42-46: *"one as a confession from the mouths of those in Hell, the other as a diagnosis made while still in this world."*

The same work is done here: the ground of the command is given not as a ruling but as **a sentence of regret**.

And the effect of this is different. Had it said "if you do not give you will regret it", it would have been a threat. Instead the regret's own sentence is transmitted, and the reader tries that sentence in his own mouth.

### رَبِّ لَوْلَا أَخَّرْتَنِي — "My Lord, if only You had granted me respite"

**رَبِّ** — the shortened form of *rabbī* (in the vocative). The address is direct.

**لَوْلَا** — a particle with two functions in Arabic:

1. **Conditional:** "had it not been for" (*lawlā* + a noun).
2. ***Taḥḍīḍ / tandīm*:** when it comes with a perfect verb it denotes **regret and reproach** — "if only you had done thus".

Here the second. And in Arabic this use is not **a request** but a reproach directed at the past. That is, the sentence means not "grant me respite" but **"why did You not grant me respite"**.

This fine difference matters: the man is not making a request, he is making a complaint. And the one addressed by the complaint is the one who could have granted the respite.

**أَخَّرْتَنِي** — root **أ-خ-ر**: to leave behind, to put off to a later time. *Ākhir* (the last) and *ākhira* (what comes after) are from the same root.

And this verb will be refused in the next verse **with the same root**: *wa lan yuʾakhkhira'llāhu nafsan*. Request and refusal, with the same word. The sūra sets what is asked for and what is not given face to face with two verbs from the same root.

### إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ قَرِيبٍ — "for a short term"

This is the verse's most striking and most often passed-over place.

**أَجَل** — root **أ-ج-ل**: an appointed period, a term, a final date. At the centre of the word is **a limit**: an *ajal* is not the period itself but the point at which the period ends. In Arabic the *ajal* of a debt is its date of payment.

**قَرِيب** — "near". Root **ق-ر-ب**: nearness.

Now consider: a man at the moment of death asks for an additional period. **How much does he ask for?**

*A short term.* A little. Brief.

**And this establishes a complete opposition with al-Baqara 2/96.**

At al-Baqara 2/94-96 the test of wishing for death was taken up, and what was recorded there was this: *"you will find them the greediest of people for life"* and *"each of them wishes to live a thousand years."* The figure was dwelt on there: *"The figure of a thousand years matters. What is wanted is not immortality — that is already known to be impossible. What is wanted is the longest of what is possible."*

Now set the two pictures side by side:

| | **al-Baqara 2/96** | **al-Munāfiqūn 63/10** |
|---|---|---|
| Who | The person in life | The person at the moment of death |
| What he asks for | *Alfa sana* — a thousand years | *Ajalin qarīb* — a short term |
| What for | Not stated (in order to live) | *Fa-aṣṣaddaqa* — in order to give in charity |
| The scale of the request | The longest of what is possible | **The shortest** |

This opposition is one of the sūra's sharpest observations, and it is worth dwelling on.

**While alive a person asks for much time; at the moment of death he asks for little.**

Why? Because the value of time depends on what it will be used for. The man in life wants time **in order to live** — living is a work that never finishes, and so however much there is, it is little. The man at the moment of death wants time **in order to do one piece of work** — and because that work is definite, the period needed is definite too.

And exactly here the bitterest side of the picture comes out: **the period asked for is short because the work to be done is small.** Giving in charity does not take long. The man is not asking for a whole lifetime; he is asking for a few days.

That is, what is noticed at the moment of death is this: **the opportunity missed was not a great opportunity.** What could have been done was small, was easy, would have taken a short time — and it was not done.

This comes out at the same place as the architecture of Sūrat al-Māʿūn. It was recorded there: *"The sūra begins from the highest point and comes down to the lowest… The last sentence is a matter of pots and pans. This descent carries the sūra's real claim: great claims are tested in small things."*

Al-Munāfiqūn 63/10 makes the same drop in scale by way of **time**: after eight verses of a grand narrative of claim and strategy, the last scene is a request for an additional period of a few days.

### فَأَصَّدَّقَ — "that I might give in charity"

**A note on morphology.** The word's original form is **أَتَصَدَّقَ** (*ataṣaddaqa* — Form V). The letter *tāʾ* has been assimilated (*idghām*) to the *ṣād* that follows it, and it has become *أَصَّدَّقَ*. Assimilations of this kind are common in Arabic and make pronunciation easier.

The root **ص-د-ق** was taken up at al-Jumuʿa 62/6: soundness, truth. A *ṣadaqa* is the thing given — and that it carries this name is meaningful: **the wealth given is the evidence that confirms the giver's faith.** A word proves its truth with wealth.

**And the choice of the verb is very important.**

What does the man say? He does not say "let me pray". He does not say "let me believe". He does not say "let me repent".

He says **"let me give in charity"**.

This choice accords exactly with the verse's context. The command was a command of *infāq* (*wa anfiqū*); and the regret too is over *infāq*. But there is something deeper: throughout the sūra wealth had been used as an instrument of power — the plan to cut off the *infāq*, the claim of might. In the last scene the same wealth becomes a matter of regret because it was not given.

So wealth appears twice in the sūra: **a weapon when it is held, a regret when it is not given.**

### وَأَكُنْ مِنَ الصَّالِحِينَ — a grammatical question

Here there is a structure much treated in the classical books of grammar.

*Fa-aṣṣaddaqa* is in the **subjunctive** (*manṣūb*) — because of the *fāʾ* in the answer to a clause expressing a request. What one would expect is that the verb coordinated with it should be in the subjunctive as well: *wa akūna*.

But the text has **وَأَكُنْ** — in the **jussive** (*majzūm*).

The explanation given in the grammatical sources is this: the verb is coordinated not with the **wording** of *fa-aṣṣaddaqa* but with its **position**. That is: the expression *lawlā akhkhartanī fa-aṣṣaddaqa* is equivalent in meaning to a conditional clause (*in akhkhartanī aṣṣaddaq* — "if You grant me respite I will give in charity"). The answer of a conditional clause is in the jussive. Therefore *akun* too has come in the jussive.

This is the structure called **ʿaṭf ʿalā'l-maḥall** (coordination with the position) in Arabic, and it is a subject the lexicographers treat often.

**A note on the variant reading:** it is stated that the reading **وَأَكُونَ** (in the subjunctive) is also recorded in the sources; this reading coordinates the verb with the wording. The meaning does not change. I make no attribution to particular imāms of the variant readings.

**الصَّالِحِين** — root **ص-ل-ح**. This root was taken up at al-Baqara 2/11-12: *"to be fitting, to be in its right place, to be repaired. From the same root come ṣulḥ (peace) and ṣāliḥ."*

And what was recorded there finds an ironic echo here: the hypocrites' claim about themselves was *"we are only reformers"* (*innamā naḥnu muṣliḥūn*). Now the man at the moment of death says *"let me be among the righteous"*.

The same root, in two different positions: one a claim (*muṣliḥ* — a reformer), the other a wish (*ṣāliḥ* — one who is in right order). And between them lies a whole lifetime.

### What this says to the present

**Understanding the value of time too late.** The most practical side of the verse is the difference of scale between the two requests: a thousand years while alive, a few days while dying. This difference shows how time is valued — time gains value when what it is to be used for becomes definite. And that definiteness most often comes late.

No special comment is needed for the present-day equivalent of this: we notice how short a task would have taken only when we understand that we cannot do it.

**The missing of what is small.** The shortness of the period asked for shows the smallness of what was missed. And this is not a consolation but an aggravating finding: had a great opportunity been missed, it would have had an excuse. The missing of a small opportunity has no excuse.

## 63/11 — وَلَنْ يُؤَخِّرَ اللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِذَا جَاءَ أَجَلُهَا ۚ وَاللَّهُ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ

*Wa lan yuʾakhkhira'llāhu nafsan idhā jāʾa ajaluhā, wa'llāhu khabīrun bimā taʿmalūn*

"God will not grant respite to any soul when its term has come. And God is aware of what you do."

### The refusal of the request

What was asked for in the preceding verse was **taʾkhīr** (respite). This verse answers with the same root: *lan yuʾakhkhir*.

| | 63/10 | 63/11 |
|---|---|---|
| Root | **أ-خ-ر** | **أ-خ-ر** |
| Verb | *akhkhartanī* — if only You had granted me respite | *lan yuʾakhkhira* — He will not grant respite |
| Who | The servant (a request) | God (a ruling) |

**لَنْ** — the definite negation of the future. As was recorded at al-Jumuʿa 62/7, it is more definite than *lā*.

And note: the answer is given not to the man's request but **to the whole class of requests**. *Nafsan* — "any soul". An indefinite noun in a negative sentence denotes generality: there is no exception.

### نَفْسًا — any soul

The root **ن-ف-س**: breath, breathing; and from there "life, person, self". At the concrete origin of the word is **the drawing of breath** — the most visible sign of being alive.

And that this word was chosen here is meaningful: the term comes to the thing that draws breath. The word says within itself what it is that stops.

### إِذَا جَاءَ أَجَلُهَا — "when its term has come"

Again the verb *jāʾa* (to come). In the tenth verse *an yaʾtiya* (its coming), here *jāʾa* (it came). Both denote "coming".

**أَجَل** — opened out in the tenth verse: an appointed period, a term, the point at which the period ends.

And the word occurs in the two verses in two different forms:

| Place | Expression | Whose |
|---|---|---|
| **63/10** | *ajalin qarīb* — indefinite, "a short term" | The period asked for, **not given** |
| **63/11** | *ajaluhā* — definite, bound to a pronoun, "its term" | The period given, **appointed** |

That is, in one an indefinite period that is asked for, in the other a definite and appointed period. The verse says that the second makes the first invalid: when the period that was given comes to an end, the period asked for is not given.

**And from here a conclusion follows that looks to the sūra as a whole.** The command in the tenth verse (*wa anfiqū*) was given with a qualification of time: "before death comes". This verse says why that qualification is serious: there is no possibility of making it up afterwards.

That is, the command is not a piece of advice but **a work that has to be done within a period**. And the end of the period is not announced.

### وَاللَّهُ خَبِيرٌ بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ — the sūra's last sentence

The root **خ-ب-ر**. It has an interesting origin that the lexicographers record: *khabara'l-arḍa* — he ploughed the ground, turned it over, brought its inside out. From the same root comes *khabrāʾ* (soft, ploughed earth).

On this reckoning **khibra**, in the word's own logic, is **knowing the inside of a thing**: not from the surface but by turning its underside up. A *khabīr* is one who knows not a thing's outside but **its inside**.

From the same root comes *khabar* — news. But the distinction the lexicographers make between *khabīr* and *ʿālim* is this: *ʿilm* is general knowledge, whereas *khibra* is **the knowledge of the hidden detail**.

**And that the sūra ends with this name is not chance.**

This sūra was built from beginning to end on a **distinction between inside and outside**:

- What is said is true, but it is not the speaker's belief (63/1).
- The oaths are a shield — there is something behind it (63/2).
- The bodies are impressive, their insides are timber (63/4).
- The head is turned away — no word is said (63/5).

What is related over eight verses is a condition that cannot be seen from outside. And the sūra closes with precisely the name of **the one who knows the inside**.

This is the sūra's most elegant structure, and it forms a frame with the first verse:

| | The opening (63/1) | The close (63/11) |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *wa'llāhu **yaʿlamu*** ... *wa'llāhu **yashhadu*** | *wa'llāhu **khabīrun*** |
| What it does | He knows and He bears witness | He knows the inside |

The sūra opens with God's knowledge and closes with God's knowledge. The nine verses in between relate a field in which people's knowledge of one another is not enough.

**بِمَا تَعْمَلُونَ** — "of what you do". And note: the verb is in the **second person plural** — "what you do", not "what they do".

That is, the last sentence is directed not at the hypocrites but **at the believers addressed in the ninth verse**. The sūra does not end with "they"; it ends with "you".

This completes the sūra's manoeuvre of turning. For eight verses it was "they", in the ninth verse it became "you", and the last word stays with "you".

**And there is the letter *bāʾ*:** *khabīrun **bi**mā taʿmalūn*. As was recorded for *ʿalīmun bi'ẓ-ẓālimīn* at al-Jumuʿa 62/7, this structure denotes that the knowledge **touches its object**, that it goes down into its detail.

## The sūra as a whole

### The architecture

| Verse | Section | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **1-3** | Word and cause | The lie of bearing witness, the oath-shield, the seal |
| **4-5** | Appearance and movement | The timbers, the state of being on the alert, the turning of the head |
| **6** | Consequence | The closing of the door |
| **7-8** | Two plans | Economic pressure and the claim of might — both an error of ownership |
| **9-11** | The turn | The warning about wealth and children, the command of *infāq*, the term that is not put off |

The most striking side of the structure is that the sūra moves **from outside inwards, then outwards again**:

1. **Word** (what they say) → **cause** (the seal on the heart) → **appearance** (the bodies) → **inner state** (thinking every cry is against them) → **movement** (the turning of the head).
2. Then outwards: **the plans** (cutting off the *infāq*, driving out).
3. Then to the one addressed: **the source** (wealth and children) → **the remedy** (*infāq*) → **the limit** (the term).

### The roots that weave the sūra

**One: ن-ف-ق** — the root of the sūra's own name, in three positions:

| Verse | Word | What |
|---|---|---|
| 63/1, 7, 8 | *al-munāfiqūn* | Identity: the one with a second door |
| 63/7 | *lā tunfiqū* | Prohibition: do not spend |
| 63/10 | *anfiqū* | Command: spend |

The two branches that come out of the same root — the hidden exit and wealth's going out of the hand — make up the sūra's two poles. And the sūra sets the open exit against the hidden one: *infāq*.

I present this link as my own reading; that the two branches of the root are joined in Arabic is the common record of the dictionaries, but the claim that they are deliberately set face to face in the sūra is my own inference.

**Two: ف-ق-ه / ع-ل-م** — two verses one after the other, two different verbs of knowing (63/7, 63/8). The sibling of the *lā yashʿurūn / lā yaʿlamūn* distinction at al-Baqara 2/12-13.

**Three: ص-د-د** — in two verses (63/2, 63/5): first with its object dropped, then intransitive. Holding back, and turning aside.

**Four: أ-خ-ر** — in two verses (63/10, 63/11): request and refusal.

**Five: ل-ه-و** — once (63/9), but by way of the link it establishes with al-Takāthur it carries the sūra's whole point of turning.

### The link between two neighbouring sūras: al-Jumuʿa and al-Munāfiqūn

These two sūras stand side by side in the muṣḥaf, and it is transmitted in the reports that they were recited together in the Friday prayer.

The links at the level of the text:

| Link | al-Jumuʿa | al-Munāfiqūn |
|---|---|---|
| **ف-ض-ض** | 62/11 *infaḍḍū* — a dispersal of itself | 63/7 *ḥattā yanfaḍḍū* — a planned dispersal |
| **ل-ه-و** | 62/11 *lahwan* — what draws the attention | 63/9 *lā tulhikum* — let it not draw the attention |
| **ذِكْرُ اللَّه** | 62/9 *ilā dhikri'llāh* — hasten towards it | 63/9 *ʿan dhikri'llāh* — do not move away from it |
| **Anxiety about provision** | 62/11 *khayru'r-rāziqīn* | 63/7 *khazāʾinu's-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* |
| **A claim** | 62/6 *zaʿamtum annakum awliyāʾu li'llāh* | 63/1 *nashhadu innaka la-rasūlu'llāh* |
| **ع-ز-ز** | 62/1, 62/3 *al-ʿAzīz* (a divine name) | 63/8 *al-aʿazz / al-ʿizza* (a thing fought over) |
| **The manoeuvre of turning** | 62/9 *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū* | 63/9 *yā ayyuha'lladhīna āmanū* |

The most striking is the first: the dispersal recorded in al-Jumuʿa as a weakness appears in al-Munāfiqūn as an aim. **One sūra shows the fragility, the other shows how that fragility was meant to be used.**

The second is no less important: in both sūras the same word (*lahw*) is the name of the thing that scatters attention. In al-Jumuʿa the sound of a caravan, in al-Munāfiqūn wealth and children. One a stimulus coming from outside, the other the centre of a person's own life.

And the third: in the ninth verse of both sūras the address changes, and the same pattern is used. This may be chance; but combined with the practice of reciting the two sūras together it appears worth recording.

### The relation to al-Baqara 2/8-20: what was added

What this sūra adds to the passage in al-Baqara must be separately recorded, because together the two give a complete picture:

| Subject | **al-Baqara 2/8-20** | **al-Munāfiqūn** |
|---|---|---|
| Point of view | From the inside — the state of the heart | From the outside — behaviour |
| Word | *"They say, we have believed"* — the word is false | *"We bear witness"* — the word is true, the bearing of witness false |
| Heart | A sickness (*maraḍ*) — living, progressing | A seal (*ṭabʿ*) — fixed |
| Comparison | The fire that goes out, the man walking in a storm — **movement** | Timbers propped up — **immobility** |
| Inner state | *Yashʿurūn* — they do not notice | *Yaḥsabūna kulla ṣayḥa* — constantly on the alert |
| Act | Mockery, an attempt at deception | Economic pressure, a plan of exclusion |
| Source | Not stated | **Wealth and children** (63/9) |
| Remedy | Not stated | ***Infāq*** (63/10) |

The two most important differences are at the end: al-Baqara describes the type and leaves it there; al-Munāfiqūn states **its source** and **the counter-move** as well.

And the difference between seal and sickness is worth dwelling on. It was recorded at al-Baqara 2/10: *"A sick body is alive; it is neither healthy nor dead… by the definition of sickness it has a direction: it heals or it progresses."*

At al-Munāfiqūn 63/3, by contrast, there is a seal — a fixed shape. That is, al-Baqara relates the process, al-Munāfiqūn a point in the process. The two texts do not contradict; they show two points on the same road.

### What the sūra does not say

**One: a list for identification.** The sūra does not give a way of understanding who is a hypocrite. All the descriptions it gives — an impressive body, a word that is listened to, a firm bearing — are not distinguishing for someone looking from outside. On the contrary: these are qualities that can be found in anyone. The sūra does not give an instrument of diagnosis; it asks for **a watchfulness** (*fa'ḥdharhum*).

**Two: a command to punish.** The only command in the fourth verse is *fa'ḥdharhum* — beware. There is no command to take action. And this is a binding limit for the sūra's use today.

**Three: the evil of wealth and children.** The ninth verse condemns neither of them; it limits **the place they take**. In other places in the Qurʾān both are counted as favours.

**Four: the absolute closing of the door of forgiveness.** Concerning the sixth verse, the exception at al-Nisāʾ 4/146 was recorded; the ruling holds when that condition is persisted in.

### What this says to the present

**One. A true word is not a measure.** The distinction in the sūra's first verse — what is said is true, the bearing of witness false — is the finding that works most today. That a sentence is true says nothing at all about the one who says it. And this holds all the more in a period in which true sentences are cheap: saying true things is no longer an indicator of a position.

The practical consequence of this is: to appraise a person by what he says is the weakest appraisal that can be had. And the alternative the verse offers is not a method of diagnosis — it is to ask about the bond between one's own word and oneself.

**Two. Answers that block the question.** The metaphor of the shield in the second verse describes how an argument is ended: without offering a proof, by making the asking for a proof costly. This mechanism is not limited to oaths today. Every answer that turns questioning into a moral offence does the same work. And the verse's diagnosis is clear: the person holding the shield is not winning the argument, he is ending it.

**Three. Standing, and being able to stand.** The passivity of the word *musannada* (propped up) in the fourth verse describes the difference between **being** in an institution, a community, a position, and **belonging** to it. And the difference is not visible from outside — as long as the support stays in place, the log too is upright. The distinction comes out only when the support is withdrawn. This is a question that holds for a person about himself as well: what is it that holds me here, and what happens if that thing is withdrawn?

**Four. The bill for a hidden contradiction.** "Thinking every cry is against them" describes the price of carrying an inconsistency. And the price is not visible from outside: the position secure, the standing intact, the words listened to. What is paid is only **inner ease.** This is the most concrete punishment the sūra assigns to this type, and it is an observation that can be verified without requiring any religious frame.

**Five. That what is lawful is not thereby moral.** The plan in the seventh verse breaks no rule: nobody commits a crime, only a voluntary help is cut off. And its outcome is the scattering of a community. This is one of the cleanest examples showing that moral judgement passes beyond legality. No special effort is needed for its present-day equivalents: withdrawing support, cutting off the relationship, drying up the source — none of them is forbidden, and all of them work.

**Six. Pulling away the ground of the dispute.** The answer in the eighth verse is striking as a way of framing an argument. It does not say "it is we who are the mightier"; it corrects whom the might belongs to. This is the most effective answer that can be given to a fight over superiority — because it makes the fight invalid without entering into it. And at the same time it does not protect its own side either: the believers are third in the list, and they are there by way of connection.

**Seven. The beginning of the process is in everyone.** The sūra's heaviest side is the ninth verse. After a type nobody would want to find in himself has been related over eight verses, the beginning of that type is shown: wealth and children. Both legitimate, both loved, both present in everyone.

The meaning of this is: the sūra does not describe a class of people, it describes **a direction**. And the road leading in that direction passes in front of everyone's door.

**Eight. The shortness of the period asked for.** The last scene in the tenth verse — the man asking for an additional period of a few days — is the sūra's quietest but most lasting image. Compared with the request for "a thousand years" at al-Baqara 2/96, the difference of scale between the two requests shows how time is valued. And the smallness of what was missed does not excuse the missing of it — it aggravates it.

- **The meaning of *āmanū thumma kafarū* at 63/3**: three views were transmitted; no preference is adopted.
- **The distinction between *khatm* and *ṭabʿ* at 63/3**: it was transmitted as the lexicographers' distinction, and was not counted as a firm rule.
