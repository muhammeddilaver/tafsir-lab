# 79. Sūrat al-Nāziʿāt

Forty-six verses. It takes its name from its first word: **ٱلنَّٰزِعَٰت.** In the tradition it is also called *al-Sāhira* and *al-Ṭāmma*.

The sūra opens with **five oaths**, and in none of the five is it said what is being sworn by. Five active participles are set in a row — *those that wrench out, those that loosen, those that swim, those that outstrip, those that direct the affair* — and all of them are feminine plural. Who or what is meant is not in the text; the exegetes have debated it for fourteen centuries.

The sūra begins with this indeterminacy and **ends with indeterminacy**: in the last five verses the Prophet is told that he does not know the answer to a question that is being asked. *"Where are you in the mention of it? Its final end belongs to your Lord."*

In between there are three things: **two quakings**, **a narrative** and **a table of creation.**

And the backbone of the sūra lies in a single word: **طَغَىٰ.** The word occurs three times. First it is used of a particular person (*"Go to Pharaoh; he has run beyond all bounds"*), then it becomes an attribute (*"whoever runs beyond bounds and prefers the life of this world"*). The sūra recounts a historical figure and turns him into a **measure.**

## Meccan or Medinan

There is something close to unanimity on its being Meccan. The style supports this: the opening with a series of oaths, the short verse-endings, the debate over the resurrection, the use of the narrative as evidence.

The basmala was treated in earlier sections and is not taken up again here; the verse numbers have been counted without the basmala.

## The skeleton of the sūra

| Verses | Part | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1-5 | Five oaths | A series of oaths whose answer is not given |
| 6-9 | Two quakings | The onset of the Hour and the state of hearts |
| 10-12 | The objection | *"Are we to be brought back?"* |
| 13-14 | The answer | A single cry |
| 15-26 | Moses – Pharaoh | The historical example of running beyond bounds |
| 27-33 | Table of creation | Heaven, night, earth, mountain — and *"for you"* |
| 34-36 | Al-Ṭāmma | The great calamity and remembering |
| 37-41 | Two groups | The one who runs beyond bounds and the one who restrains his self |
| 42-46 | The question of the Hour | *"You cannot know"* |

## 79/1-5 — Five oaths

**وَٱلنَّٰزِعَٰتِ غَرْقًا · وَٱلنَّٰشِطَٰتِ نَشْطًا · وَٱلسَّٰبِحَٰتِ سَبْحًا · فَٱلسَّٰبِقَٰتِ سَبْقًا · فَٱلْمُدَبِّرَٰتِ أَمْرًا**

*Wa'l-nāziʿāti gharqā · Wa'l-nāshiṭāti nashṭā · Wa'l-sābiḥāti sabḥā · Fa'l-sābiqāti sabqā · Fa'l-mudabbirāti amrā*

"By those that wrench out to the utmost · By those that loosen and draw out gently · By those that swim along · Then by those that outstrip · Then by those that direct the affair."

### The matter of the oath

The subject of the oath (*qasam*) was set up in this commentary in the section on Sūrat al-ʿAṣr and widened out to the long series in the section on Sūrat al-Shams. The findings made there are not repeated.

In brief: in the Qurʾān an oath is not made in order to prove one's truthfulness — for the one addressed is already disputing the truthfulness of the one swearing — but in order **to draw attention to something**. The thing sworn by is at the same time a piece of evidence.

One datum recorded in the section on al-Shams is worth mentioning again here: **the longest unbroken series of oaths in the Qurʾān is in Sūrat al-Shams** (seven oaths). Al-Nāziʿāt opens with five oaths; al-Fajr, al-Mursalāt and al-Ṣāffāt are of similar length.

### The grammatical structure of the series

The structure common to the five verses is this: **a definite active participle (feminine plural) + a word in the accusative.**

But within the structure there are **two breaks**, and both are worth recording.

**The first break — the connecting particles.**

| Verse | Particle |
|---|---|
| 1 | **وَ** |
| 2 | **وَ** |
| 3 | **وَ** |
| 4 | **فَ** |
| 5 | **فَ** |

The first three oaths are joined with **wāw**, the last two with **fāʾ**.

In Arabic the difference between these two particles is clear:

- **وَ** — absolute conjunction. It announces no order; it sets up no temporal relation among the things counted.
- **فَ** — announces **succession and order**: "then, immediately after". Sometimes it announces cause and effect as well.

That is, the series of verses says this: **the first three acts stand side by side, and the last two come after them.**

This is a structure seen in Sūrat al-ʿĀdiyāt too: *"by those that run · **fa** those that strike sparks · **fa** those that raid · **fa** those that raise dust · **fa** those that plunge into the midst"* (100/1-5). There there was one *wāw* and four *fāʾ*s — and as was recorded in the section on al-ʿĀdiyāt, the *fāʾ*s were setting out a chain of action.

In Sūrat al-Mursalāt, on the other hand, there is a mixed arrangement (77/1-5).

**What is to be recorded in this commentary is this:** the difference of particle is in the text and it carries meaning; but since what the five oaths describe remains indeterminate, it is not possible to say with certainty what the order is ordering.

**The second break — the accusative words.**

| Verse | Active participle | Accusative word | From the same root? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **ٱلنَّٰزِعَٰت** (ن-ز-ع) | **غَرْقًا** (غ-ر-ق) | **No** |
| 2 | **ٱلنَّٰشِطَٰت** (ن-ش-ط) | **نَشْطًا** (ن-ش-ط) | Yes |
| 3 | **ٱلسَّٰبِحَٰت** (س-ب-ح) | **سَبْحًا** (س-ب-ح) | Yes |
| 4 | **ٱلسَّٰبِقَٰت** (س-ب-ق) | **سَبْقًا** (س-ب-ق) | Yes |
| 5 | **ٱلْمُدَبِّرَٰت** (د-ب-ر) | **أَمْرًا** (أ-م-ر) | **No** |

**The middle three oaths are strengthened by a verbal noun from their own root** — in Arabic this is called the **absolute object**, and it reinforces the verb. English answers it with an intensifying adverb or a doubling: *"loosening and loosening", "swimming and swimming", "outstripping and outstripping".*

**The first and the fifth oaths stand outside this pattern.** In the first there is a word from a different root (*gharqā*); in the fifth there is an object (*amrā*).

That is, the series is built thus: **two different on the outside, three the same on the inside.** The five verses form a framed structure.

I record this as an observation. The grammatical data (which are absolute objects and which are not) can be verified; that they form a frame is an order I see.

### What are the things sworn by? — the great disagreement

**This is one of the oldest and most open disagreements in Qurʾānic exegesis.** There is no noun in the text; there are only five adjectives.

A fact recorded in the section on al-ʿĀdiyāt meets us again here: in some of the Qurʾān's series of oaths the thing sworn by is named **not by its name but by its attribute** (al-Mursalāt 77/1-5; al-Nāziʿāt 79/1-5; al-Ṣāffāt 37/1-3). And in all of these sūras the same disagreement has occurred.

**The views:**

| View | Who / what | How the five oaths are read | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Angels** (the most widespread) | The angels who take souls and carry out the command | *Nāziʿāt*: those who draw out the soul of the denier violently. *Nāshiṭāt*: those who take the believer's soul gently. *Sābiḥāt*: those who descend and ascend between heaven and earth as though swimming. *Sābiqāt*: those who outstrip one another in bearing the command. *Mudabbirāt*: those who carry out the task given | **The fifth verse is decisive:** *"those that direct the affair"* is hard to apply to any but angels. Moreover the Qurʾān ascribes the taking of souls to angels: *"The angels stretching out their hands: give up your souls [they say]"* (al-Anʿām 6/93); *"The angel of death takes your souls"* (al-Sajda 32/11) | There is no direct indication in the Qurʾān that the first three attributes are used of angels |
| **The stars** | The heavenly bodies | *Nāziʿāt*: those drawn along in their constellations / those that rise and set. *Sābiḥāt*: those that swim in the sky. *Sābiqāt*: those that outstrip one another | **The Qurʾān uses the verb "to swim" of the heavenly bodies:** *"Each swims in an orbit"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/33; Yāsīn 36/40). This is a direct indication for the third oath | The first and fifth oaths (wrenching out, directing the affair) remain forced for the stars |
| **The souls of the dying** | The souls leaving the body | *Nāziʿāt*: those drawn out of the body. *Sābiḥāt*: those that swim after coming out | The root's sense of "drawing out" suits the scene of the taking of the soul | The souls' "directing the affair" (verse 5) cannot be explained |
| **Warriors and their horses** | The bows and horses of the fighters | *Nāziʿāt*: those who draw the bow to its limit (in Arabic an archer is called *nāziʿ*). *Sābiḥāt*: running horses. *Sābiqāt*: those that get ahead in the race | All the words are used in Arabic in the context of war and horses; Sūrat al-ʿĀdiyāt is a table of horses | The fifth verse again remains unexplained. Moreover the sūra is Meccan; a scene of war is far from the context |
| **The angel of death alone** | ʿAzrāʾīl | The five attributes are five tasks of the same being | Simplifying | A feminine plural pattern for a single being is forced |

### Assessment — and not imposing a preference

**No preference is imposed in this commentary**, and the ground for that needs to be written:

**One.** There is no noun in the text. The Qurʾān has not chosen to give a name. To make one reading definitive would be to close a place the text deliberately left open.

**Two.** Every one of the views has a ground and a weak side. None of them explains all five verses with equal ease.

**Three.** The words themselves — to be treated one by one below — do not require a particular subject. *To wrench, to loosen, to swim, to outstrip, to direct*: all of these can be applied to more than one agent.

**This much I can record, and I offer it as an observation:** the fifth verse (**فَٱلْمُدَبِّرَٰتِ أَمْرًا**) is the strongest indication in favour of the angels, because "directing the affair" requires an agent with will. And the third verse (**ٱلسَّٰبِحَٰت**) is the strongest indication in favour of the stars, because the Qurʾān uses this verb explicitly of the heavenly bodies.

Two indications point in two different directions. This shows why the disagreement has not been resolved.

**One further possibility needs to be recorded:** the views do not have to exclude one another. That the Qurʾān in some places chooses comprehensive and many-sided words has been recorded several times in this commentary (the choice of *ghāsiq* in the section on al-Falaq was an example). It is possible that the five attributes have been left **deliberately open** — and in that case to look for a single equivalent would be to narrow the scope of the word.

I offer this as a possibility, not as a preference.

### نَزَعَ — to wrench and tear out

**Root: ن-ز-ع.** The concrete sense of the root is **to pull a thing out of the place it is in, to tear it out**. Not a gentle taking; a taking that **tears loose**.

Its derivatives and its uses in the Qurʾān show the force of the root:

- **نَزَعَ يَدَهُ** — he drew out his hand. In the narrative of Moses: *"He drew out his hand, and there it was, white to the beholders"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/108; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/33). **This is a verb that occurs in this sūra's own narrative.**
- **تَنزِعُ ٱلنَّاسَ** — it was tearing people out. Of the wind that destroyed the people of ʿĀd: *"tearing them out as though they were the trunks of uprooted palm trees"* (al-Qamar 54/20).
- **نَزَّاعَةً لِّلشَّوَىٰ** — that which scorches and strips off the skin. In the description of the Fire (al-Maʿārij 70/16).
- **وَنَزَعْنَا مَا فِى صُدُورِهِم مِّنْ غِلٍّ** — We tore out the rancour in their breasts (al-Aʿrāf 7/43; al-Ḥijr 15/47).
- **تَنزِعُ ٱلْمُلْكَ مِمَّن تَشَآءُ** — You take sovereignty away from whom You will (Āl ʿImrān 3/26).
- **تَنَازُع** — contention; two parties pulling at a thing against one another. *"Do not contend with one another, or your strength will go"* (al-Anfāl 8/46).

**The common core of the root: to separate a thing from the place it is stuck to.** And separation always meets a resistance — this is why the verb conveys force.

**The last item is also worth recording:** *tanāzuʿ* (contention) is of the *tafāʿul* form and conveys reciprocity. The first word of Sūrat al-Nabaʾ (*tasāʾul*) was of the same form. Two neighbouring sūras describe two different relations with the same form — but in this verse there is no contending, only a one-directional pulling.

### غَرْقًا — to the utmost

**Root: غ-ر-ق.** To drown, to sink in water. **أَغْرَقَ** — he drowned. In the Qurʾān the end of Pharaoh is spoken of with this root: *"We drowned them"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/136; al-Anfāl 8/54).

**On the word's place in the sentence** the grammarians differ:

| Analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Absolute object** (from a different root) | A verbal noun in the sense of *ighrāq*: "those who draw taking it to the limit" |
| **Circumstantial accusative** | "Those who draw as though drowning, as though submerging" |
| **Specifying accusative** | "Those who draw in respect of submerging" |

**And an idiom the lexicographers record explains the word:**

**أَغْرَقَ ٱلنَّازِعُ فِى ٱلْقَوْسِ** — *"the archer drew the bow [to its limit]."*

In this idiom two things are present at once: the word **نَازِع** is used in the sense of archer, and **إِغْرَاق** describes the bow's being drawn to its furthest point.

That is, the idiom holds two words of the verse together.

**The picture the idiom gives is this:** the archer drawing the bow goes to a certain point; a good archer draws to **the furthest point** — beyond it the bow breaks. *Ighrāq* is that furthest point.

**The word's meaning here thus becomes clear:** *gharqā* announces not the violence of the drawing but **its being carried to the end**. Not a half draw; all the way down, leaving nothing behind.

I give this idiom as a usage recorded by the lexicographers; I do not ascribe it to any particular exegete.

### نَشَطَ — to draw gently

**Root: ن-ش-ط.** And this root sets up the exact opposite of the first verse.

**The senses of the root:**

- **نَشَطَ ٱلدَّلْوَ** — he drew the bucket from the well. So here too there is a drawing — but of a different kind: even, flowing, without snagging.
- **أَنْشَطَ ٱلْعِقَالَ** — he untied the knot. Opening the knot of the rope on a camel's leg.
- **نَشِيط** — lively, brisk, vigorous. (In Turkish the borrowed words *neşe* and *neşeli*, "cheer, cheerful", come from this root — but the sense proper to the Arabic is **liveliness and briskness**.)
- **نَشَاط** — liveliness, activity.

**The core beneath the root: to move, or to set in motion, without snagging, easily, in a flowing manner.**

And the sense of untying a knot is especially important: to untie a knot is to separate the rope without breaking it. Breaking also separates the rope — but it can never be tied again.

**The root appears to occur only here in the Qurʾān.** I do not offer this finding as the result of an exhaustive search; what I record is that the word is rare.

### The contrast between the two verbs

**Now let us set the two verses side by side.** This is one of the finest places in the sūra.

| | **79/1 — ٱلنَّٰزِعَٰت** | **79/2 — ٱلنَّٰشِطَٰت** |
|---|---|---|
| Sense of the root | To tear out, to wrench | To untie, to draw easily |
| Resistance | **Present** — what is drawn is stuck fast | **Absent** — a knot is being untied |
| Speed | Sudden | Flowing |
| Result | Tearing loose | Parting |
| Intensifier | **غَرْقًا** — to the utmost | **نَشْطًا** — from its own root |
| English rendering | To wrench and tear out | To loosen and draw out |

**The two verses describe the same task in two different manners: a thing's being separated from the place it is in.**

And the difference is not in the **task** but in the **manner**. The same task is done in two distinct ways.

**This is the strongest side of the "angels" view**, and it is commonly explained in the classical commentaries thus: the soul of the denier is drawn out violently, the soul of the believer is taken gently. The two verses describe two manners of death.

The Qurʾān sets up this distinction elsewhere:

*"If only you could see when the angels take the souls of those who disbelieved, striking their faces and their backs!"* (al-Anfāl 8/50; Muḥammad 47/27)

*"Those whom the angels take while they are in a goodly state…"* (al-Naḥl 16/32 — *ṭayyibīn*)

Two verses describe the same event (the taking of the soul) in two different manners.

**I record this as the reading that best fits the contrast between the two verses. But I do not impose a preference**, because this reading requires accepting the "angels" view, and that view is not certain.

**And this must be recorded as well — it is my own reading:** what the two verses say remains the same whichever view is accepted. **The same operation takes two different forms according to whom it is done to.** This is the governing idea of the rest of the sūra: in verses thirty-seven to forty-one two groups will meet two different ends within the same day.

That is, the sūra sets up the idea of separation in its very first two verses, by the difference between two verbs.

### سَبَحَ — to swim

**Root: س-ب-ح.** This root was treated in the section on Sūrat al-Naṣr and the finding there will be relied on.

What was recorded there was this: the root's original sense is **to swim**; when it passes into the *tafʿīl* form (**سَبَّحَ**) it takes on the sense of **tanzīh** — holding God apart from everything unbefitting Him. And the connection between the two senses is the lexicographers' interpretation: swimming is moving swiftly away from and through a medium; *tasbīḥ* likewise holds God away from deficiencies. The connection is not forced, but neither is it certain.

**What is to be added here is this: in this verse the root is used in its original sense — swimming.**

And the Qurʾān uses this verb explicitly of the heavenly bodies:

*"It is He who created the night and the day, the sun and the moon. **Each swims in an orbit.**"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/33)

*"It does not befit the sun to overtake the moon, nor can the night outstrip the day. **Each swims in an orbit.**"* (Yāsīn 36/40)

**The second verse is striking**, because it contains together the third and the fourth oaths of this sūra: *to swim* (*yasbaḥūn*) and *to outstrip* (*sābiq*). In Yāsīn it says *"the night cannot **outstrip** the day"* — the verb is **سَابِقُ**, the same root as this sūra's fourth oath.

**This is the strongest support for the "stars" view** and it was recorded in the table.

**What the verb of swimming describes.** To swim announces three things at once:

1. **Being within a medium** — what is swum in is a liquid or an air.
2. **Moving by one's own power** — not being dragged.
3. **Going evenly and without obstruction** — without snagging, without friction.

And in Arabic *sabḥ* is not a disorderly thrashing; it is **even and continuous advance**.

### سَبَقَ — to outstrip

**Root: س-ب-ق.** To get ahead, to leave behind, to go forward in a race.

Its derivatives:

- **سَابَقَ** — he raced. *"They raced"* (in Yūsuf 12/17, *nastabiqu* — we were racing).
- **سَابِق** — one who goes ahead. In the Qurʾān within a classification: *"…and those foremost in good deeds"* (Fāṭir 35/32; also al-Wāqiʿa 56/10: *"the foremost, the foremost"*).
- **سَبْق** — going ahead.
- **سَبَقَتْ كَلِمَتُنَا** — Our word has gone ahead (al-Ṣāffāt 37/171) — being prior in respect of time.

**The word has two directions:** getting ahead in a race (place) and being earlier (time). Both come out of the same idea.

**Why is the fourth oath joined with *fāʾ*?** As recorded above, *fāʾ* announces succession. That is, the outstripping comes **after** the swimming.

This ordering is meaningful: first the movement (swimming), then the result of the movement (outstripping). A thing that swims gets ahead because it swims.

### دَبَّرَ — to direct the affair

**Root: د-ب-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root is **the back**.

- **دُبُر** — the back, the end of a thing. In the Qurʾān: *"They will turn their backs and flee"* (al-Qamar 54/45).
- **إِدْبَار** — to turn one's back, to go away. **It will occur in this sūra:** *"Then he turned his back, hurrying"* (79/22 — *adbara*).
- **تَدْبِير** — **to manage a matter with its end in view**; to plan, to administer.
- **مُدَبِّر** — one who governs, who administers.

**The connection is this: *tadbīr* is acting with a view to the back — that is, to the end — of a matter.** English carries the same thought in *making provision* for something: taking beforehand into account what is going to happen.

That is, the word names **foresighted management**. Not doing a thing at random; carrying it out knowing the outcome.

**In the Qurʾān the subject of this verb is for the most part God:**

*"He directs the affair from the heaven to the earth."* (al-Sajda 32/5)

*"He directs the affair; there is no intercessor — save after His leave."* (Yūnus 10/3)

*"…and He is the one who directs the affair."* (al-Raʿd 13/2)

**And here there is a tension, and it needs to be recorded.** In these verses *tadbīr* is ascribed to God; whereas in al-Nāziʿāt 79/5 a plural agent is named, "those that direct the affair".

The exegetes' way of resolving this tension is: **the directing is God's; the angels carry out His direction.** That is, the agents in the verse are not the party that decides on its own but the party that carries out a decision given.

This explanation is another datum supporting the "angels" view.

**أَمْرًا — "an affair".** It comes indefinite: "an affair". The indeterminacy shows that the kind of affair is not stated.

**The word's place within the sūra.** *Amr* in the Qurʾān means both "command" and "affair". In this verse the second sense comes forward.

### Where is the answer of the oath?

**And now we come to the most striking feature of the series: the oath has no answer.**

In Arabic an oath (*qasam*) consists of two parts: the **muqsam bihi** (the thing sworn by) and the **muqsam ʿalayhi** (the answer of the oath — that is, the "surely such-and-such is so" part).

In this sūra the first part runs for five verses; **the second part never comes at all.**

The sixth verse begins with **يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ ٱلرَّاجِفَةُ** — an adverb of time. Not a clause of answer.

**The grammarians' solutions:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The answer has been elided** | Understood: *"Surely you will be raised"* or *"Surely the Hour will come to pass."* It was not said because it is understood from the context |
| **The answer is implicit in verses 6-7** | The sentence *"on the day the quaking one quakes"* is taken to be the answer itself |
| **The answer is in verses 13-14** | *"It is but a single cry; and at once they are upon the wakeful plain"* |

**The first view is the most widespread.**

**And the elision itself is worth recording.** The Qurʾān carries a series of oaths through five verses and does not give the answer.

Its effect is this: **the tension is set up and not discharged.** Through five verses the reader waits, asking "what will it say?", and instead of an answer a scene arrives.

I record this as my own reading. But that the elision is present in the text is a datum the grammarians accept unanimously.

**And a connection:** Sūrat al-Nabaʾ was doing the same thing. There a question was asked, its answer was not given, and in its place a table was set up. Two neighbouring sūras use the same technique in two different ways: one an unanswered question, the other an unanswered oath.

I record this parallel as an observation.

## 79/6-7 — يَوْمَ تَرْجُفُ ٱلرَّاجِفَةُ · تَتْبَعُهَا ٱلرَّادِفَةُ

*Yawma tarjufu'l-rājifa · Tatbaʿuha'l-rādifa*
"On the day the quaking one quakes; the one that comes behind follows it."

### رَجَفَ — to quake

**Root: ر-ج-ف.** **To be shaken violently, to tremble, to be moved from one's place.**

Its derivatives and its uses in the Qurʾān:

- **ٱلرَّجْفَة** — a quaking, an earthquake. In the Qurʾān it is used of the peoples destroyed: *"the quaking seized them"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/78 — Thamūd; 7/91 — Madyan; 7/155 — the seventy chosen from the people of Moses).
- **تَرْجُفُ** — will quake. *"On the day the earth and the mountains quake"* (al-Muzzammil 73/14).
- **ٱلْمُرْجِفُون** — those who shake things up, who spread unrest. *"The hypocrites, those in whose hearts is sickness, and those who spread agitating rumours in the city…"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/60).

**The last item is a linguistic datum worth recording.** In Arabic **إِرْجَاف** means to spread baseless and shaking news — to shake things up with rumour.

That is, the root names both physical quaking and social quaking. This is an observation about the language; the use in the verse is plainly physical and I draw no allusion from it.

### ٱلرَّاجِفَة — the quaking one

**The word is an active participle and it is feminine**: "the one that quakes (feminine)".

And this is an instance of a pattern recorded in the section on al-Qāriʿa. There the names the Qurʾān uses for the Hour were given in the form of a table and this finding was made: *"All of these names have a grammatical feature in common: nearly all of them are on the active participle pattern and are feminine. That is, all of them stand in the position of 'the one that does'."*

**ٱلرَّاجِفَة and ٱلرَّادِفَة are added to that list.** Both on the *fāʿila* measure, both feminine, both in the position of "the one that does".

And a third will come in the same sūra: **ٱلطَّامَّة** (79/34) — which was already in the table in al-Qāriʿa.

**The noun qualified has been elided.** *Al-rājifa* is an adjective; the noun it qualifies is not stated. This is the same situation as in verses one to five: there is an adjective, there is no noun described.

That is, in its first seven verses the sūra **speaks throughout by attribute.** I record this as an observation.

### رَدِفَ — to come behind

**Root: ر-د-ف.** To come behind someone; and especially **to mount behind someone.**

Its derivatives:

- **رَدِيف** — the one riding pillion; the one behind on the mount. A known position in Arab culture: two people ride a camel or a horse, the one in front drives, the one behind is the *radīf*.
- **مُرْدِفِين** — coming one after another. *"I shall help you with a thousand angels — one following another"* (al-Anfāl 8/9).
- **رَدِفَ لَكُم** — it has drawn near you, it has come up behind you. *"Say: perhaps a part of what you seek to hasten has come up behind you"* (al-Naml 27/72).

**The picture the word gives is this: the second one has mounted behind the first. That is, the two do not come separately; they come joined.**

The English "hard on its heels" comes near to this, but it does not give the idea of contact that is in *radīf*.

### What are the two quakings? — the disagreement

| View | Explanation | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The two blasts of the trumpet** (the most widespread) | The first blast kills, the second raises | al-Zumar 39/68 describes the two blasts explicitly. Moreover in the neighbouring sūra, al-Nabaʾ 78/18, it says *"the trumpet is blown"* |
| **The earth quakes, then the heaven splits** | The first quaking concerns the earth and the mountains, the second the heaven | al-Muzzammil 73/14: *"On the day the earth and the mountains quake"* |
| **Death, then resurrection** | Two stages in general | — |

**The first view is the most widespread** and the explicit expression in al-Zumar 39/68 is its strongest support.

**And the connection with the neighbouring sūra is worth recording.** In al-Nabaʾ 78/18 a single blast was mentioned: *"the day the trumpet is blown."* Al-Nāziʿāt speaks of two quakings.

The two sūras do not conflict: al-Nabaʾ mentions the second blast (the resurrection), because it goes on to say *"you will come"*. Al-Nāziʿāt mentions both together.

**An observation: the difference of word-choice.**

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/18** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/6-7** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **نَفْخ** — a blast | **رَجْف** — a quaking |
| Sense | **Hearing** | **Balance / touch** |
| Source | An instrument (the trumpet) | Unnamed |
| Number | One | **Two** |

**The two sūras describe the same event through two different senses:** one is heard, the other is felt.

I record this as an observation. That the two sūras are neighbours and describe the same event with different words is a feature often seen in the Qurʾān's scenes of the Hour.

### The link between the two verses

The seventh verse comes without a connecting particle: **تَتْبَعُهَا ٱلرَّادِفَةُ** — "the one that comes behind follows it."

The dropping of the particle (*faṣl*), as was treated in the section on al-Ikhlāṣ, shows that two sentences are **too tightly bound to be joined** by a connective.

And here this coincides exactly with the sense of the word: *radīf* is the one who **mounts** behind. That is, there is no gap between the two quakings — and there is no particle between the two verses either.

**The text performs in its grammar the adjacency it describes.**

I record this as my own reading; but the dropping of the particle and the sense of the word *radīf* are both in the text.

## 79/8-9 — قُلُوبٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ وَاجِفَةٌ · أَبْصَٰرُهَا خَٰشِعَةٌ

*Qulūbun yawmaʾidhin wājifa · Abṣāruhā khāshiʿa*
"On that day hearts will be throbbing; their eyes downcast."

### قُلُوبٌ — its coming indefinite

**The word is indefinite**: not "al-qulūb" (the hearts) but **"qulūbun"** (certain hearts).

This is a choice that has to be attended to. The verse does not speak of **all** hearts; it speaks of **some** hearts.

That is, as early as the eighth verse the sūra makes a **distinction** — but it does not say between whom the distinction falls. Whose heart is pounding and whose is not: it is not specified.

This is another instance of the principle recorded in the section on al-Qāriʿa: **the sūra gives the criterion, it does not give the list.**

**The heart — a short note.** **قَلْب** in the Qurʾān is not an organ of emotion; it is the centre of **understanding, deciding and turning towards**. Its root is **ق-ل-ب**, meaning "to turn, to overturn" — the heart's bearing this name is explained by its turning from state to state. In this commentary it was treated in the section on al-Baqara in connection with the sealing of the heart.

### وَاجِفَة — throbbing

**Root: و-ج-ف.** And the concrete sense of the root carries the verse somewhere quite different.

**وَجَفَ** — to move quickly and irregularly. It is used especially of **the running of a horse**.

**Its only other use in the Qurʾān is decisive:**

*"As for what God gave His Messenger as spoils from them: you **spurred** neither horse nor camel for it."* (al-Ḥashr 59/6)

The verb there is **أَوْجَفْتُمْ** and it plainly means **to spur a horse.**

**That is, the picture the verse sets up is this: hearts running like horses.**

The English "the heart pounding", "the heart in one's mouth" answer to this. But the picture in the Arabic is more concrete: **an animal running inside the ribcage.**

**The word is an active participle and it is feminine** — the third feminine active participle in the first nine verses of this sūra (*rājifa*, *rādifa*, *wājifa*, *khāshiʿa*).

### خَٰشِعَة — downcast

**Root: خ-ش-ع.** This root was treated at al-Baqara 2/45 and the finding there will be relied on.

What was recorded there was this: the sense of the root is **to soften, to be lowered, to bend**; the Qurʾān uses this root of the earth as well (*"You see the earth dried up — *khāshiʿa* — and when We send down water upon it, it stirs and swells"* — Fuṣṣilat 41/39); *khushūʿ* is **the breaking of hardness, the softening of the shell, becoming able to take in what comes.**

**What is to be added here is this: in this verse the word is used of the eye.**

The Qurʾān mentions the *khushūʿ* of eyes in several places, and all of them are in the same scene:

*"Their eyes downcast, abasement covering them, they come out of the graves."* (al-Maʿārij 70/44)

*"Their eyes downcast, an abasement covering them…"* (al-Qalam 68/43)

*"With eyes downcast, they come out of the graves as though they were scattered locusts."* (al-Qamar 54/7 — this verse was also mentioned in the section on al-Qāriʿa)

**What does the *khushūʿ* of the eye mean?** The bending of the eye is the falling of the gaze: not being able to look across, not being able to lift one's eyes.

And this agrees with the finding in al-Baqara: *khushūʿ* is the breaking of hardness. An upright gaze is a hardness; a fallen gaze shows that the hardness has broken.

**In al-Baqara *khushūʿ* was a praised state; here it is a sign of fear.** The two uses do not conflict: the word names not an **attitude** but a **state** — bentness. Whether the bending is good or bad depends on before whom and why one bends.

This is the same as the principle recorded for *mabthūth* in the section on al-Qāriʿa and for *mihād* in the section on al-Nabaʾ: **a word is not in itself positive or negative.**

### The contrast between the two verses

Now read the two verses together:

| | **Hearts (8)** | **Eyes (9)** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **وَاجِفَة** — running | **خَٰشِعَة** — downcast |
| Position | Inside | Outside |
| Movement | **Excess of movement** | **Motionlessness** |
| Visibility | Unseen | Seen |

**Astir within, frozen without.**

This is the most recognisable description of fear: the storm breaking inside appears outside only as **a gaze that cannot be lifted.**

And the two verses are bound to one another: the ninth verse comes to the eighth without a connective, and the pronoun **أَبْصَٰرُهَا** refers back to **قُلُوبٌ**.

**A grammatical point.** The expression "the eyes of the hearts" is odd in Arabic — eyes belong not to hearts but to persons.

The grammarians' explanation is this: there is here a **synecdoche**; by saying *qulūb* their **owners** are meant. That is, "on that day certain hearts [their owners] will throb; **their** eyes are downcast."

Some grammarians count this a **figurative attribution**. It was a similar case to the one recorded for *ʿīshatin rāḍiya* in the section on al-Qāriʿa.

**And one further reading is possible, and I record it as my own reading:** the expression reduces the person to his heart. On that day a human being is named neither by his name nor by his body but by **his heart**. In this scene the sūra, in describing the human being, counts first an inner organ, then an outer organ belonging to it.

## 79/10-12 — يَقُولُونَ أَءِنَّا لَمَرْدُودُونَ فِى ٱلْحَافِرَةِ · أَءِذَا كُنَّا عِظَٰمًا نَّخِرَةً · قَالُوا۟ تِلْكَ إِذًا كَرَّةٌ خَاسِرَةٌ

*Yaqūlūna a-innā la-mardūdūna fi'l-ḥāfira · A-idhā kunnā ʿiẓāman nakhira · Qālū tilka idhan karratun khāsira*
"They say: 'Are we really to be brought back to our former state? When we have become decayed bones?' They said: 'That, then, would be a losing return.'"

### The scene changes

Up to the ninth verse the Day of Resurrection was being described. In the tenth verse there is a sudden return to **the present tense**: *"they say."*

That is, the sūra sets up the scene of the future and then comes back to this world and reports the one addressed **as he speaks today.**

**This is the same thing Sūrat al-Nabaʾ does.** Al-Nabaʾ too opened by reporting the speech of the one addressed (*"about what are they asking one another?"*). Both neighbouring sūras take the other side's own sentence into the text.

### أَءِنَّا — the interrogative pattern

**أَ** (the interrogative hamza) + **إِنَّ** (emphasis) + **نَا** (we).

And there is a **لَ** in the sentence as well: *la-mardūdūn.*

That is, there are **two emphases** stacked in the sentence: *inna* and the *lām*.

In Arabic the presence of two emphases in a sentence shows that the sentence is said **with great force**. And combined with the interrogative hamza what emerges is a **rhetorical question of denial**: a question that seeks no answer but expresses rejection.

In English it comes out as: *"So we are really to be brought back, are we?"* — the tag at the end is there to show disbelief.

**A note on variant readings.** Differences are transmitted among the imāms of the variant readings in the pronunciation of the two hamzas in this verse (full realisation, easing, separating the second hamza with an alif, and so on). These differences **do not change the meaning**; since I cannot state with certainty which imām held which preference, I give no name.

### مَرْدُودُونَ — those brought back

**Root: ر-د-د.** To turn back, to return. **رَدَّ** — he gave back, he returned. **مَرْدُود** — turned back.

**The word is passive, and this is worth recording.** The objection is not "shall we return?" but **"shall we be brought back?"**

That is, those objecting know that the operation will be done to them. Their objection is not to the **agent** but to **the possibility of the operation.**

### فِى ٱلْحَافِرَة — in which sense?

**This is the most disputed word of the sūra.**

**Root: ح-ف-ر.** To dig, to hollow out, to open a pit. **حُفْرَة** — a pit. **حَافِر** — one who digs; and **a hoof** (the nail of a horse or a donkey).

**The relation between the two derivatives:** a hoof is called *ḥāfir* because the animal **digs** the ground as it walks. A walking horse leaves a mark in the soil at every step.

**Now to the views:**

| View | Explanation | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The former state, the original condition** | In Arabic **رَجَعَ فِى حَافِرَتِهِ / عَلَىٰ حَافِرَتِهِ** — "he returned by the road he came", "he returned to his former state" — is an idiom. Its origin: an animal's returning **by treading in its own hoof-prints**. *Ḥāfira* is the road on which the prints dug on the way out are found | The idiom is established in Arabic; and the context of the verse concerns returning to a former state (resurrection) | The lexicographers are not in full agreement about the origin of the idiom |
| **The grave** | *Ḥāfira* means "that which has been dug" — an active participle in a passive sense. That is, the grave | The root's original sense (to dig) goes straight to this; *ḥufra* (a pit) is from the same root | That the verse is built with the particle *fī* (in) does not make this reading difficult, but "being brought back into the grave" is not the subject of the objection — they already accept entering the grave. Their objection is to **coming out** of the grave |
| **The life of this world** | The first life; "shall we be brought back to our former state" | It is a sub-case of the first view | Hard to count as a separate view |

**The first view is the most widespread and rests on the idiom.**

**The weak side of the second view is especially important and needs to be dwelt on:** their objection is not to **entering the grave** but to **coming out of it**. The next verse confirms this: *"when we have become decayed bones?"* That is, they accept the decay; what they object to is the stage that comes after the decay.

**I prefer the first view; it is not binding.** My ground is this: the existence of the idiom is on record in Arabic and it sits directly on the meaning of the verse; the second view does not agree with the subject of the objection.

**A note on the language.** In Arabic an expression **ٱلنَّقْدُ عِنْدَ ٱلْحَافِرَةِ** is transmitted, and it is said to mean "payment in cash" — the money being paid while the animal has not stirred from its place, at the sale of a horse. Since I cannot give certain information about the currency and the exact sense of this expression, I record it as a note on the language and do not use it as evidence.

**A note on variant readings.** It is transmitted that the word is also read as **ٱلْحَفِرَة**. On this reading the sense comes closer to "pit / grave". I give no imām's name.

### عِظَٰمًا نَّخِرَةً — decayed bones

**عِظَام — bones.** The root is ع-ظ-م; and as recorded at al-Nabaʾ 78/2, this root is also the root of the word *ʿaẓīm* (great) — because the bone is the skeleton of the body.

**The choice of bones is no accident.** Bone is the part of the body that decays last. The flesh goes, the skin goes, the bone remains.

That is, the objection is built **from the furthest point**: after even the most durable part has decayed.

**The Qurʾān records this objection in several places, and always with bone:**

*"When we have become bones and crumbled dust, shall we really be raised in a new creation?"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/49, 98)

*"He has struck for Us a similitude and forgotten his own creation: he said, 'Who will give life to the bones when they are decayed?' Say: He who brought them forth the first time will give them life."* (Yāsīn 36/78-79)

*"Does man suppose that We shall not gather his bones? Indeed — We are able to set in order even his fingertips."* (al-Qiyāma 75/3-4)

**The last verse is striking:** the objection is built on bones, the answer is given by way of **the fingertips** — that is, a still finer detail.

**نَخِرَة — Root: ن-خ-ر.** And this root has two directions:

1. **To decay and become hollow within.** *Nakhira'l-ʿaẓm* — the bone decayed, its inside became a cavity.
2. **A sound issuing from the nose.** **نَخِير** — snoring, a nasal sound. **مَنْخِر** — a nostril.

**The place where the two senses meet:** a bone hollowed out within **gives a sound** when blown upon. A hollow thing whistles when the wind passes through it.

That is, the word describes the furthest stage of decay: not merely brittle but **hollowed out within.**

**A variant reading is transmitted:** the word is also read as **نَاخِرَة** (*nākhira*). The difference of meaning between the two readings is small; some lexicographers distinguish *nakhira* as "decayed" and *nākhira* as "hollow, giving out sound", while some count the two equivalent. Since I cannot state with certainty which imām of the variant readings held which preference, I give no name.

**And an observation — my own reading:** the sound-association of the word joins with the thirteenth verse of the sūra. There the resurrection will come about **by a cry** (*zajra*). A bone hollowed out within is a thing that lets sound through.

I must state that I am making this connection and am not claiming that the verse intends it. But both senses of the word do stand in the dictionary.

### قَالُوا۟ — the change of tense

**In the tenth verse يَقُولُونَ (imperfect — "they say"), in the twelfth verse قَالُوا۟ (perfect — "they said").**

This shift has drawn the attention of the grammarians. The explanations:

| Explanation | Account |
|---|---|
| **Two separate utterances** | The imperfect reports the ongoing objection, the perfect a word said at a particular moment |
| **A future utterance** | *Qālū* here describes the future: "they will say" — a widespread manner in the Qurʾān |
| **A mocking concluding sentence** | The first two verses are questions, the third the conclusion they themselves draw. The perfect shows the conclusion being made definite |

The third explanation best suits the tone of the sentence: two questions are asked and then a verdict is delivered.

### تِلْكَ إِذًا كَرَّةٌ خَاسِرَةٌ — "a losing return"

**كَرَّة — Root: ك-ر-ر.** To return, to repeat, to come back. **كَرَّ** — he turned back, he repeated the charge. *Karra* is also used as a noun of instance — "a turn, one time".

In the Qurʾān *karra* occurs in sentences of regret:

*"If only we had a return, that we might quit them."* (al-Baqara 2/167)

*"If only we had a return, that we might be among the believers."* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/102)

**The same word in both verses, the same wish.** And in this sūra the same word is used for **mockery**.

That is: **the "return" made an object of mockery in this world becomes the thing begged for in the hereafter.**

I record this as an observation; the shared word is a datum that can be verified.

**خَاسِرَة — Root: خ-س-ر.** This root was treated in detail in the section on Sūrat al-ʿAṣr and the finding there will be relied on.

What was recorded there was this: *khusr* is **a term of accounting** — the balance coming out negative, the capital diminishing. And it was compared in the section on al-Shams with *khāba* (failing to find what one hoped for): in *khusr* there is a reckoning, in *khayba* a hope.

**Now let us look at the use here — and there is an irony in it.**

They characterise the resurrection as **a loss-making transaction**. That is, they are making a calculation: "if we are brought back, it is a loss for us."

**And they are right.** The rest of the sūra confirms it: for them the resurrection really is a loss (79/39: *"the Fire, that is the refuge"*).

**But the place where the reckoning is set up is wrong.** They use the resurrection's being harmful as evidence **that it will not take place**. Yet a thing's being harmful does not show that it will not happen.

I record this as my own reading. But the choice of the word and the rest of the sūra are in the text.

**A structural note.** The verse-endings of these three verses — *al-ḥāfira, nakhira, khāsira* — belong to the same family of sound as *wājifa, khāshiʿa* in the eighth and ninth verses. And so do the sixth and seventh verses: *al-rājifa, al-rādifa*.

That is, from the sixth verse to the fourteenth, nine verses are bound by **a single sound**: *-ira / -ifa / -iʿa.*

## 79/13-14 — فَإِنَّمَا هِىَ زَجْرَةٌ وَٰحِدَةٌ · فَإِذَا هُم بِٱلسَّاهِرَةِ

*Fa-innamā hiya zajratun wāḥida · Fa-idhā hum bi'l-sāhira*
"It is but a single cry. And at once they are upon the wakeful plain."

### The brevity of the answer

An objection of three verses is answered with **two verses**. And the answer is not an argument; it is a **description**.

This is the same method as was recorded in the section on al-Qāriʿa: instead of continuing the dispute, the text shows the scene.

The particle **فَ** binds the answer to what precedes: *"[is that what they say?] Then [let them know that] it is but…"*

**إِنَّمَا** — a particle of restriction: "only, nothing but." That is, the matter is not being magnified but **made small.**

They had presented the resurrection as an impossible undertaking. The answer reduces the undertaking to a single sound.

### زَجْرَة — a cry

**Root: ز-ج-ر.** **To drive with the voice, to drive off with a rebuke, to direct by shouting.**

And the concrete use of the root comes from herding: **زَجَرَ ٱلرَّاعِى ٱلْإِبِلَ** — the herdsman drove the camels with his voice. The herdsman does not touch the animal; he **shouts**, and the animal rises and moves.

Its derivatives:

- **زَاجِر** — those who drive, who drive off. In the Qurʾān: *"By those who drive with a driving"* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/2 — *fa'l-zājirāti zajrā*). **This is in the same pattern as al-Nāziʿāt's series of oaths.**
- **اِزْدَجَرَ** — he desisted, he refrained; and he was rebuked. *"They denied and said 'a madman'; and he was rebuked and driven off"* (al-Qamar 54/9 — *wa'zdujira*).
- **مُزْدَجَر** — a deterrent. *"In them are tidings that deter"* (al-Qamar 54/4).

**The word's function here is striking.**

In al-Nabaʾ 78/18 the same event was described with **نَفْخ** (a blast) — a sound produced with an instrument. Here **زَجْرَة** is used — **the herdsman's shout to the flock.**

**That is, the two neighbouring sūras describe the same event with two different kinds of sound:**

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/18** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/13** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **نَفْخ** — a blast | **زَجْرَة** — a cry |
| Source of the sound | An instrument (the trumpet) | An agent who cries out |
| Picture | The sound of a horn | **The herdsman raising the flock** |
| Result | *"You come in throngs"* | *"And at once they are on the plain"* |

**The picture in the second sūra is harder.** *Zajr* is not a courteous call; it is a voice that lifts the flock from its place and admits of no objection.

And the result in al-Nabaʾ and the result here give the same idea: the crowd rises not by its own decision but **because it is made to rise.**

I record this as an observation. The difference between the two words and the neighbouring position of the two sūras are in the text.

### وَٰحِدَة — single

**That the number is specified is worth recording.** The sentence does not content itself with "a cry"; it says **"a single"** one.

The Qurʾān often uses this emphasis in scenes of the Hour:

*"It is but a single shout; and at once they are looking on."* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/19)

*"They await nothing but a single shout."* (Yāsīn 36/49)

*"Our command is but a single act, like the twinkling of an eye."* (al-Qamar 54/50)

**The function of the emphasis:** their objection was about the **difficulty** of the undertaking (how are decayed bones to be gathered?). The answer does not dispute the difficulty of the undertaking; it states **its duration.**

And this is the Qurʾān's general posture on the resurrection. Power is not debated; the scale is changed.

### ٱلسَّاهِرَة — the wakeful plain

**Root: س-ه-ر.** **To stay sleepless, to remain awake at night.** **سَهَر** — sleeplessness. **سَاهِر** — one who stays awake.

**The word is used as the name of a place, and this oddity has drawn the attention of the exegetes.**

| View | Explanation | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Flat, bare ground** (the most widespread) | In Arabic *al-sāhira* means level ground without trees and without unevenness. It takes its name from no one sleeping there, or from its being fit for keeping watch | A usage recorded by the lexicographers |
| **The place where there is no sleeping** | Because on the Day of Resurrection no one will sleep | The direct sense of the root |
| **The face of the earth** | Not the inside of the grave but its outside | The scene describes the coming out of the grave |
| **A particular place-name** | In some reports a specific location | Weak; its ground is uncertain |

**The first three views do not exclude one another** and all arrive at the same place: **the open ground that is come out onto from within the grave.**

### The link with al-Nabaʾ: sleep

**And now we come to one of the most striking connections to be made in this commentary.**

In al-Nabaʾ 78/9 sleep was counted as a favour: **وَجَعَلْنَا نَوْمَكُمْ سُبَاتًا** — *"We made your sleep a cutting-off."* And what was recorded there was this: the root of *subāt* is **to cut**; sleep is the cutting-off of activity. It was also recorded that sleep is spoken of in the Qurʾān as a daily death (al-Zumar 39/42; al-Anʿām 6/60).

**In al-Nāziʿāt 79/14 the ground of the scene of resurrection is named ٱلسَّاهِرَة — "the sleepless one".**

**The two neighbouring sūras take hold of the notion of sleep from its two ends:**

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/9** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/14** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **سُبَاتًا** — a cutting-off (sleep) | **ٱلسَّاهِرَة** — the sleepless one |
| Sense of the root | To cut, to halt | To stay awake |
| Where | In this world | On the plain of resurrection |
| What | A favour given | A place arrived at |

**In this world there was sleep; there there is no sleep.**

And this, joined with the sleep–death connection, says one thing more. If sleep is a daily cutting-off, and if after every cutting-off a waking comes, then the name of the last waking is **al-sāhira** — the place where the cutting-off ends.

**I record this as my own reading.** The neighbouring position of the two sūras and the root senses of the two words are in the text; the connection is mine.

### فَإِذَا هُم — the *idhā* of suddenness

**إِذَا** here is not a particle of condition; it is the **إِذَا ٱلْفُجَائِيَّة** (the *idhā* of surprise).

The function of this particle is this: it announces the **sudden** appearance of something unexpected. In English it answers to "and lo", "all at once", "and there they are".

It is widespread in the Qurʾān:

*"He cast down his staff; and **at once** it was a manifest serpent."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/107; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/32)

*"…**and at once** they are standing, looking on."* (al-Zumar 39/68)

**And the function of the particle here completes the emphasis on "single" in the thirteenth verse.**

They were imagining a long and difficult operation: the gathering of bones, the clothing of them with flesh, the giving of life. The sūra finishes it in two steps: **a sound, and at once.**

Nothing at all is described in between. **There is no process; there are only two ends.**

I record this as my own reading; but the functions of the two particles (*innamā wāḥida* and the *idhā* of suddenness) are on record in grammar.

## 79/15-16 — هَلْ أَتَىٰكَ حَدِيثُ مُوسَىٰٓ · إِذْ نَادَىٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ بِٱلْوَادِ ٱلْمُقَدَّسِ طُوًى

*Hal atāka ḥadīthu Mūsā · Idh nādāhu rabbuhu bi'l-wādi'l-muqaddasi ṭuwā*
"Has the account of Moses (Mūsā) come to you? When his Lord called to him in the holy valley, Ṭuwā."

### The narrative's entry into the context

In the fourteenth verse the scene ended. In the fifteenth verse, with no connecting particle at all, a narrative begins.

**And why the narrative is here will not be said until the twenty-sixth verse:** *"Surely in that there is a lesson for whoever feels awe."*

That is, the sūra first narrates and then states its ground. This is an arrangement often seen in the Qurʾān's use of narrative.

**The function of the narrative in the context is this** — and I record it as a reading I draw from the structure of the sūra:

In verses ten to twelve there was an objection: *"are we to be brought back?"* And in verses thirteen and fourteen an answer was given.

But the answer was **theoretical.** The narrative makes the same matter **historical**: there was once someone who made this objection at the top of his voice, and what happened to him is a known thing.

That is, the narrative is not a proof; it is an **example**. And the sūra will say this in the twenty-sixth verse with the word *ʿibra* (lesson).

### هَلْ أَتَىٰكَ — "has it come to you?"

**هَلْ** — an interrogative particle. **أَتَىٰ** — it came, it reached.

**The kind of question.** This is not a question for information. It is not assumed that the Prophet does not know the narrative of Moses; the narrative is told many times in the Qurʾān.

The explanations the rhetoricians give for this pattern:

| Explanation | Its meaning |
|---|---|
| **Magnification (tafkhīm / taʿẓīm)** | To enlarge the importance of what is about to be told. In English: "Now listen to this!" |
| **Incitement** | To prepare for listening; to gather attention |
| **Confirmation (taqrīr)** | To recall a known thing and have it affirmed |

None of the three excludes the others.

**The pattern occurs in several other places in the Qurʾān and always does the same work:**

*"Has the account of the Overwhelming come to you?"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/1)

*"Has the account of the hosts come to you?"* (al-Burūj 85/17)

*"Has the account of Abraham's honoured guests come to you?"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/24)

**In all of them the pattern stands at the head of a narration.**

### حَدِيث — "account"

**Root: ح-د-ث.** And the original sense of the root is **to be new, to come into being afterwards.**

- **حَادِث** — that which comes into being afterwards, that which is new. In the literature of theology *ḥādith* (that which came into being) is the opposite of *qadīm* (that which has no beginning).
- **أَحْدَثَ** — he brought forth something new. The notions **بِدْعَة / مُحْدَث** come from here.
- **حَدِيث** — speech, narration, news. **The thing newly heard.**

**That is, in Arabic "news" and "new" are the same word.** The Arabic plural *ḥawādith* carries the same sense; and that English *news* is built on *new* is the same logic in another language.

### The difference between حَدِيث and نَبَأ

**And now the two neighbouring sūras have to be compared.**

In the section on al-Nabaʾ the difference between **نَبَأ** and **خَبَر** was treated. What was recorded there was this: *nabaʾ* is news that carries great benefit and produces conviction; *khabar* is any piece of information.

**Now a third word comes: حَدِيث.**

| Word | Sense of the root | What it brings forward |
|---|---|---|
| **خَبَر** | Knowledge, conveying | The content of the news |
| **نَبَأ** | To come from somewhere, to arrive | The **weight** of the news |
| **حَدِيث** | To be new | The **telling** of the news |

**Three words hold three sides of news.**

And the choices of the two neighbouring sūras are meaningful:

- **al-Nabaʾ 78/2:** *al-nabaʾu'l-ʿaẓīm* — **the great tiding.** Its subject is the resurrection; its weight is emphasised.
- **al-Nāziʿāt 79/15:** *ḥadīthu Mūsā* — **the account of Moses.** Its subject is a past event; its telling is emphasised.

**And there is a subtlety here:** the narrative of Moses belongs to thousands of years ago; but the word means **"new"**.

This shows the function of the narrative: an old event becomes **new every time it is told**. What the narrative carries is not history but a pattern that repeats.

I record this as my own reading. But the sense of the root and the word-choices of the two sūras are in the text.

### إِذْ نَادَىٰهُ رَبُّهُۥ — "when his Lord called to him"

**نَادَىٰ — Root: ن-د-و / ن-د-ي.** To call out, to summon — especially **from a distance** and **in a loud voice.**

In Arabic *nidāʾ* is the opposite of a whisper. One does not make *nidāʾ* to someone nearby.

**And the verb will occur twice in this sūra:**

| Verse | Who called | To whom |
|---|---|---|
| **79/16** | **His Lord** | To Moses |
| **79/23** | **Pharaoh** | To the crowd he had gathered |

**The same verb, two agents.** One calls to a single person in the silence of the valley; the other calls to a gathered crowd.

I record this as an observation; the repetition of the word is a datum that can be verified.

**رَبُّهُۥ — "his Lord".** The name Allāh is not used; *Rabb* is used, and it is annexed to Moses.

As recorded in the section on al-Fātiḥa, *Rabb* carries ownership together with **nurture**. That is, the call comes not as a declaration of authority but from within a **relationship**.

**And this word will occur five times in the sūra** — all of them at decisive places:

| Verse | Expression |
|---|---|
| 16 | *nādāhu **rabbuhu*** — his Lord called to him |
| 19 | *wa ahdiyaka ilā **rabbika*** — that I may guide you to your Lord |
| 24 | *anā **rabbukumu'l-aʿlā*** — "I am your most high lord" |
| 40 | *man khāfa maqāma **rabbihi*** — whoever fears the station of his Lord |
| 44 | *ilā **rabbika** muntahāhā* — its final end belongs to your Lord |

**The backbone of the sūra turns around this word:** to whom the call was made, to whom the guiding is, who put himself in that place, of whom there is fear, to whom it belongs.

I record this as my own reading. The repetitions of the word, however, are in the text.

### ٱلْوَادِ ٱلْمُقَدَّس — the holy valley

**مُقَدَّس — Root: ق-د-س.** The sense of the root is **purity, cleanness**. **تَقْدِيس** — hallowing, sanctifying; in origin **purifying.**

- **ٱلْقُدُّوس** — one of the names of God; purified of every deficiency.
- **رُوحُ ٱلْقُدُس** — occurs at al-Baqara 2/87.
- **بَيْتُ ٱلْمَقْدِس** — the purified house.

**The word's place in this verse needs recording:** the valley is not "holy" but **"hallowed / purified"** — a passive participle. That is, the holiness is not in the valley itself; it is a quality given to it.

**And two verses later a word from the same field of meaning will come: تَزَكَّىٰ (to purify oneself).** The valley has been purified; purification is offered to Pharaoh. The two words are from different roots (ق-د-س and ز-ك-و) but they circle the same idea.

I record this as an observation; I claim no kinship of root.

### طُوًى — Ṭuwā

The views as to what the word is:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The proper name of the valley** | A place-name; a valley in Sinai. The most widespread view |
| **"Twice hallowed"** | The root **ط-و-ي** means "to fold, to roll up"; *ṭuwā* is folded — that is, a hallowing repeated |
| **"Traversed by night"** | From the same root, the sense of "folding up the road" (covering it swiftly) |

**The first view is the most widespread.**

The word occurs in one other place in the Qurʾān, in the same scene: *"I am your Lord; take off your sandals, for you are in the holy valley, Ṭuwā"* (Ṭāhā 20/12).

**A note on variant readings.** It is transmitted that the word is read with nunation (**طُوًى**) and without it (**طُوَىٰ**); the difference arises from whether the word is counted fully declinable. There is no essential change in the meaning. I give no imām's name.

## 79/17-19 — ٱذْهَبْ إِلَىٰ فِرْعَوْنَ إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ · فَقُلْ هَل لَّكَ إِلَىٰٓ أَن تَزَكَّىٰ · وَأَهْدِيَكَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ فَتَخْشَىٰ

*Idhhab ilā Firʿawna innahu ṭaghā · Fa-qul hal laka ilā an tazakkā · Wa ahdiyaka ilā rabbika fa-takhshā*
"Go to Pharaoh; he has run beyond all bounds. And say: 'Would you care to purify yourself? And that I should guide you to your Lord, so that you may feel awe?'"

### إِنَّهُۥ طَغَىٰ — the ground for going

**Root: ط-غ-ي.** This root was treated in detail in the section on Sūrat al-ʿAlaq and the finding there will be relied on.

In brief: the concrete sense of the root is **water's overrunning its bed**; *ṭughyān* is a being's passing beyond its own limit; and *"running beyond bounds is not possessing something bad; it is carrying something good outside its own limit."*

In the section on al-ʿAlaq this was also recorded: this root is used in the Qurʾān most often of Pharaoh, and *"the furthest point of ṭughyān is to put oneself in the position of lord."* There the twenty-fourth verse of this sūra was cited as evidence.

**What is to be added here is this: the place of كلم in the sentence.**

The verse says *"Go to Pharaoh, for he has run beyond bounds."* That is, the running beyond bounds is given as the **ground** for going.

This may look inverted: why go to someone who has run wild? What one would expect is to keep away.

**But the sūra makes it a ground for a call.** And the next verse shows how the ground works: the going is not to punish but **to offer purification.**

I record this as my own reading. But the order of the two verses is in the text.

**The verb's coming in the perfect.** *Ṭaghā* — "he has run beyond bounds", past tense. That is, the running beyond bounds is a completed, settled condition. Not something still in the making.

### هَل لَّكَ — "would you care?"

**And one of the most unexpected sentences of the sūra is here.**

**هَلْ لَكَ إِلَىٰ كَذَا** is in Arabic a **pattern of offer**: "would you care for this?", "would you like this?"

Within the pattern a noun is implicit; understood: *"hal laka [raghbatun / maylun] ilā…"* — "have you [any wish / inclination] towards this?"

**That is, the sentence is not a command but a question; not a threat but an invitation.**

And this is worth recording when one considers who is being addressed. Moses goes before a ruler who has declared himself a god and has had the children of the Israelites killed, and his first sentence is an **offer.**

**The Qurʾān describes this scene more explicitly in another sūra:**

*"Go, both of you, to Pharaoh; he has run beyond bounds. Speak to him **a gentle word**; perhaps he will take heed or fear."* (Ṭāhā 20/43-44)

The expression there is **قَوْلًا لَّيِّنًا** — a gentle word. And a ground is given as well: *"perhaps he will take heed or fear."*

**What emerges when the two sūras are read together is this:** even to the most extreme instance of running beyond bounds an **offer** is carried first, and the offer is made in a **gentle** manner.

**And the word "perhaps" (لَعَلَّ) in Ṭāhā is worth recording too.** As was treated in the section on al-Nabaʾ, *laʿalla* is used for what is possible; *layta* for what is impossible. That is, Pharaoh's taking heed is counted **possible.**

This is so despite the verdict at the end of the sūra. We know the outcome of the offer (79/21: *"he denied and disobeyed"*), but the offer itself is made as a real offer.

### تَزَكَّىٰ — to purify oneself

**Root: ز-ك-و.** This root was treated in detail in the section on Sūrat al-Shams and the finding there will be relied on.

What was recorded there was this: beneath the root there are **two senses at once** and they are inseparable — **cleanness/purification** and **growth/increase.** The dictionaries do not divide these into separate entries. And the expression *"he purified his self"* remains half-said when rendered only as "he cleansed": **"he purified his self and thereby made it grow."**

In the section on al-Shams this distinction was also drawn:

| Use | What it means | Ruling |
|---|---|---|
| **Purifying in fact** | Cleansing the self of its filth, actually setting it right | Praised |
| **Declaring oneself pure** | **Proclaiming** oneself clean | Forbidden (al-Najm 53/32; al-Nisāʾ 4/49) |

**Now let us come to this verse and record a grammatical difference.**

| | **al-Shams 91/9** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/18** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **زَكَّىٰهَا** (*zakkāhā*) | **تَزَكَّىٰ** (*tazakkā*) |
| Form | **تَفْعِيل** — transitive | **تَفَعُّل** — reflexive |
| Meaning | "He purified it (the self)" | "He purified himself, he became pure" |
| Object | Present (the pronoun *hā*: the self) | Absent |

**In al-Shams the verb had an object; here it has none.**

The *tafaʿʿul* form announces the act's **returning upon the agent**: the person does the act to himself. And it generally announces **gradualness** as well: not all at once but by degrees.

That is, the offer is: *"would you care to purify yourself?"* — not an operation that happens at a stroke but a process.

**And this joins with the distinction made in the section on al-Shams.** There what was forbidden was **declaring** oneself pure. Here what is offered is **purifying** oneself. The two verbs are from the same root, but one is a claim and the other a work.

**Pharaoh's case sharpens this distinction particularly.** He is the one who will make the greatest claim about himself (79/24: *"I am your most high lord"*). What is offered is not a claim but a work.

I record this as my own reading. But the difference of form between the two verbs and the finding in al-Shams are in the text.

**A note on variant readings.** It is transmitted that the verb is read in an assimilated form (**تَزَّكَّىٰ**); the meaning is the same. I give no imām's name.

### وَأَهْدِيَكَ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ — "that I may guide you to your Lord"

**هَدَىٰ — Root: ه-د-ي.** To show the way, to guide to the right road. This root was treated in detail in the section on al-Fātiḥa.

**The verb comes in the subjunctive** (*ahdiya*), because it is coupled to the **أَن** in the previous verse: *"[would you care] to purify yourself and that I should guide you?"*

**رَبِّكَ — "your Lord".**

**And here is one of the sharpest sentences of the sūra.**

To Pharaoh it is said **"your Lord"**. The man who is going to declare himself lord is reminded that he has a Lord.

And this is directly connected with the finding recorded for **رَبِّ ٱلْعَٰلَمِين** in the section on al-Fātiḥa. What was written there was: *"To say 'Lord of all the worlds' is at the same time to say that no human being is the lord of another human being. The sentence has a political side as much as a theological one."* And there the sequel of this verse (79/24) was cited as evidence.

**Al-Nāziʿāt 79/19 gives the other half of that finding:** the man who makes the claim to lordship **has a Lord himself.**

The claim does not change the fact. Pharaoh may declare himself lord; but this does not mean that he has no Lord.

I record this as my own reading; the words of the two verses (19 and 24) are, however, in the text.

### فَتَخْشَىٰ — "so that you may feel awe"

**Root: خ-ش-ي.** To fear — but of a particular kind.

**The distinction the lexicographers record:**

| Word | What kind of fear |
|---|---|
| **خَوْف** | General fear; alarm at a danger. Its cause is outside |
| **خَشْيَة** | **Fear grounded in knowledge**; the shudder that arises from knowing the greatness of the one feared. Its cause is knowledge |

I record this distinction as a finding made by the lexicographers; I do not claim that it works rigidly everywhere.

**There is a verse in the Qurʾān that supports this distinction:**

*"Of His servants, only the **learned** feel awe of God."* (Fāṭir 35/28)

That is, awe is the result of knowledge.

**And now look at the word order of the verse.**

The sentence runs: *"that I may guide you to your Lord, **so** that you may feel awe."*

**فَ** here announces a result. That is, the order is:

**Guiding → knowing → awe.**

**Awe is positioned not as a beginning but as a result.**

This is a choice worth recording. The expected order could have been the reverse: "first fear, then believe." The verse turns this around: first know, and let the fear be born of that.

And this agrees with the sense of the word itself: *khashya* is fear grounded in knowledge; without knowledge there is no awe.

I record this as my own reading. But the *fāʾ*'s announcing a result, and Fāṭir 35/28, are in the text.

**The word's distribution in the sūra.** The root **خ-ش-ي** occurs **three times** in this sūra:

| Verse | Expression | To whom |
|---|---|---|
| **19** | *fa-**takhshā*** | What is offered to Pharaoh |
| **26** | *li-man **yakhshā*** | To whom the lesson will be of benefit |
| **45** | *mundhiru man **yakhshāhā*** | Whom the warning reaches |

**Three occurrences, in the three parts of the sūra:** in the narrative, in the verdict of the narrative, and at the end of the sūra.

And what the three set up is this: awe is the **measure** of the sūra. The narrative is a lesson to whoever feels awe; the warning reaches whoever feels awe; and what was offered to Pharaoh was to arrive at awe.

I record this as an observation; the repetitions of the word are a datum that can be verified.

## 79/20-21 — فَأَرَىٰهُ ٱلْـَٔايَةَ ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ · فَكَذَّبَ وَعَصَىٰ

*Fa-arāhu'l-āyata'l-kubrā · Fa-kadhdhaba wa ʿaṣā*
"So he showed him the greatest sign. But he denied and disobeyed."

### The speed of the narration

**And now attend to the sūra's technique of narration.**

From the seventeenth verse to the twenty-fifth there are nine verses, and all of them are joined with **فَ**:

*fa-qul (18) · fa-arāhu (20) · fa-kadhdhaba (21) · thumma adbara (22) · fa-ḥashara (23) · fa-qāla (24) · fa-akhadhahu (25)*

**Seven *fāʾ*s, one *thumma*.** And *fāʾ*, as recorded above, announces **succession**: immediately after.

That is, the narrative flows without drawing breath. An offer, a miracle, a denial, a turning away, an assembly, a claim, a seizing.

**And there are things not told in between.** Moses's going to Egypt, his entering the palace, his encounter with the magicians, the parting of the sea — none of them is there. The Qurʾān tells this narrative at length in other sūras (al-Aʿrāf 7/103-137; Ṭāhā 20/9-79; al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/10-68); here it fits it into **nine verses.**

**The scenes chosen are these:** the call, the offer, the miracle, the refusal, the claim, the punishment.

That is, the sūra takes from the narrative **only the course of the running beyond bounds.** It leaves the rest.

I record this as an observation; the connecting particles of the verses and their content are in the text.

### ٱلْـَٔايَةَ ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ — the greatest sign

**Which sign?** The exegetes differ:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The staff** | The rod that turned into a serpent. The first miracle shown in Pharaoh's presence |
| **The white hand** | The hand that came out of his breast shining white |
| **Both together** | The Qurʾān mentions the two together: *"These are two proofs from your Lord"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/32) |
| **All the miracles** | The Qurʾān says nine signs were given to Moses: *"We gave Moses nine clear signs"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/101) |

**I impose no preference.** What is to be recorded is this: the verse says **"the greatest"**; that is, what was shown was the highest of what could be shown.

**The verb is not "he saw" but "he showed".** *Arā* (Form IV) — to show. That is, Pharaoh did not see; **it was shown to him.** It was actively set before him.

This agrees with the finding recorded at al-Baqara 2/6: *"unbelief is not not-knowing; it is covering over what one knows."* And there a verse concerning this narrative was cited: *"You have certainly known that none has sent these down but the Lord of the heavens and the earth"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/102).

**That is, Pharaoh's case is not ignorance.** The sign was shown, he saw it, and he denied.

### فَكَذَّبَ وَعَصَىٰ — he denied and disobeyed

**Two verbs, two distinct levels:**

| Verb | Root | Where it happens |
|---|---|---|
| **كَذَّبَ** | ك-ذ-ب | **Inside** — not to accept, to say "a lie" |
| **عَصَىٰ** | ع-ص-ي | **Outside** — not to comply with a command, to resist |

**عَصَىٰ — Root: ع-ص-ي.** And the concrete sense of the root is worth recording: **عَصَا** (*ʿaṣā*) — **a staff, a stick.**

The connection the lexicographers set up is this: **عَصَى ٱلْقَوْمُ** — "the group parted from one another, dispersed"; and **عَصَا** is the staff that symbolises unity. In Arabic *"to break the staff"* (شَقَّ ٱلْعَصَا) means to part from the community.

That is, the idea beneath *ʿiṣyān* is **parting, breaking away** — going out of the circle of obedience.

**And a coincidence is worth recording:** the view is widespread that the sign shown to Pharaoh was **the staff**. And his response is described with a verb from the root of **staff**: *ʿaṣā* (he disobeyed).

**This is not a claim of wordplay** and I do not claim that the two words are from the same root — *ʿaṣā* (staff) and *ʿaṣā* (he disobeyed) are treated in the classical dictionaries under the same root letters, but the lexicographers are not in agreement as to whether they derive from a single root. **I do not enter this disagreement and I draw no meaning from this coincidence of sound**; I merely record it.

**The order is meaningful.** First the denial (inner), then the disobedience (outer). At al-Nabaʾ 78/27-28 there was the same order: first *"they did not expect a reckoning"* (inner), then *"they denied"* (outer).

Two neighbouring sūras set up the same ordering with two different pairs: **the inner attitude comes first.**

## 79/22-24 — ثُمَّ أَدْبَرَ يَسْعَىٰ · فَحَشَرَ فَنَادَىٰ · فَقَالَ أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ

*Thumma adbara yasʿā · Fa-ḥashara fa-nādā · Fa-qāla ana rabbukumu'l-aʿlā*
"Then he turned his back, hurrying. He gathered and called out. And he said: 'I am your most high lord.'"

### أَدْبَرَ يَسْعَىٰ — "he turned his back, hurrying"

**أَدْبَرَ — Root: د-ب-ر.** And this root occurred in the fifth verse of the sūra as **مُدَبِّرَات**.

What was recorded above was this: the concrete sense of the root is **the back**; *tadbīr* is acting **with a view to the end** of a matter.

**Now let us set the two uses side by side:**

| Verse | Word | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **79/5** | **مُدَبِّرَات** | *tafʿīl* | Those who carry out with the **end** in view |
| **79/22** | **أَدْبَرَ** | *ifʿāl* | He turned his **back** and went |

**The same root, in two opposite directions.** One advances with a view to the end; the other turns his back and goes.

I record this as an observation. The shared root is a linguistic datum that can be verified; I do not claim that there is a deliberate link between the two verses.

**يَسْعَىٰ — Root: س-ع-ي.** To run, to walk quickly, to exert oneself.

The word stands as a **circumstantial accusative**: "running, in a state of running".

**In the Qurʾān this root is used both positively and negatively:**

- *"When the call to prayer is made on the day of congregation, **hasten** to the remembrance of God."* (al-Jumuʿa 62/9)
- *"And a man came **running** from the far side of the city."* (Yāsīn 36/20)
- *"When he turns away, he **strives to spread corruption** in the land."* (al-Baqara 2/205)

**That is, running is not in itself good or bad; it depends on where one runs.**

**And this root will come back later in the sūra:**

*"On that day man remembers **what he strove for**."* (79/35 — *mā saʿā*)

**The link between the two verses is worth recording:**

| Verse | Expression | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **79/22** | *adbara **يَسْعَىٰ*** | Pharaoh — turning his back and running |
| **79/35** | *mā **سَعَىٰ*** | Man — remembering what he ran for |

The sūra first shows one person's running; then it says that everyone's running will be remembered.

I record this as an observation; the repetition of the word is in the text.

**The function of ثُمَّ.** This verse is joined with the only *thumma* in the series — the others are *fāʾ*.

*Thumma*, unlike *fāʾ*, announces **an interval**: not immediately after, but after some time.

That is, a period of time has passed between the denial and the turning of the back. Some of the grammarians read this also as **a difference of rank** (treated in the section on al-Nabaʾ): the second verb is graver than the first.

### فَحَشَرَ فَنَادَىٰ — he gathered and called out

**حَشَرَ — Root: ح-ش-ر.** To gather together, to drive and assemble.

**And this is a word-choice worth recording.**

In the Qurʾān **حَشْر** is almost always used of **the gathering on the Day of Resurrection**:

*"On that day We gather them and leave not one of them."* (al-Kahf 18/47)

*"Warn with it those who fear that they will be gathered before their Lord."* (al-Anʿām 6/51)

*"The day We shall gather them all…"* (Yūnus 10/28)

And the word has become the name of a sūra: **al-Ḥashr** (59).

**Now let us come back to this verse: the agent is Pharaoh.**

Pharaoh **gathers** — that is, he does on his own scale the thing God will do on the Day of Resurrection: he assembles the people, comes out before them, makes his proclamation.

**I record this as my own reading and I must state its limit as well:** the word *ḥashr* is not used in Arabic only of the Resurrection; it means "to gather" generally and occurs in the Qurʾān in other contexts too (for instance at al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/36-53, Pharaoh's gathering of the magicians). That is, the use of the word here is not by itself an allusion.

What I record is this: the thing Pharaoh does — gathering, coming out before them, proclaiming a verdict — is in its structure an imitation of a courtroom scene. And this agrees with the description of *ṭughyān* recorded in the section on al-ʿAlaq: **putting oneself in that place.**

**فَنَادَىٰ — he called out.** As recorded above, the same verb was used in the sixteenth verse of the Lord's calling to Moses.

**The difference between the two callings:**

| | **79/16** | **79/23** |
|---|---|---|
| Who calls | **The Lord** | **Pharaoh** |
| To whom | **A single person** (Moses) | **A crowd** |
| Where | In the holy valley — desolate | In a gathered assembly |
| What follows | A commission | A claim |

**An observation:** the calling that is real is made to a single person and in a solitude; the calling that is a claim is made to a crowd and in a public place.

I record this as my own reading. But the elements of the two verses are in the text.

### أَنَا۠ رَبُّكُمُ ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ — "I am your most high lord"

The most extreme claim the Qurʾān records.

**And the most striking word of the sentence is ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ.**

**Root: ع-ل-و.** Height. **أَعْلَىٰ** — an elative: **higher, highest.**

**And an elative requires a comparison.**

That is, Pharaoh does not say *"I am lord."* He says *"I am your **most high** lord."* This does not **deny** the existence of other lords — it claims to be above them.

**This is the exact description of the structure of pre-Islamic associationism** and it was recorded in the section on al-Fātiḥa: *"The Arab of the age of ignorance was no stranger to the concept of 'lord'; but the lords were divided — every tribe, every oasis, every trade route had its own lord."*

In Egypt the situation was similar: there was a polytheistic system and the pharaoh was positioned at the top of that system.

**That is, Pharaoh's claim is not a claim of "one god"; it is a claim of hierarchy.**

And this explains why the Qurʾān calls him *ṭāghī* (one who runs beyond bounds). According to the description in the section on al-ʿAlaq, *ṭughyān* is **exceeding the limit**. Pharaoh does not invent godhood; he **exceeds his own limit** and enters that category.

**The Qurʾān records this claim elsewhere as well:**

*"Pharaoh said: 'O chiefs! I know of no god for you other than myself.'"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/38)

**The difference between the two sentences is worth recording** and will be taken up in the twenty-fifth verse.

**And the sequel of the finding in the section on al-Fātiḥa is completed here.** What was recorded there was: *"When Moses spoke to him of the 'Lord of all the worlds', Pharaoh's answer is a question: 'And what is the Lord of all the worlds?' (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/23). The question is not a request for information but a rejection — because if the worlds have a Lord, Pharaoh's lordship falls."*

**Al-Nāziʿāt 79/24 and al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/23 are two sentences of the same man and they explain one another:** one proclaims his own position, the other rejects the concept that would abolish that position.

**A note on the sound.** The word **ٱلْأَعْلَىٰ** is used in the Qurʾān of God as well: *"Glorify the name of your Lord, the Most High"* (al-Aʿlā 87/1); *"…except him who seeks the pleasure of his Lord, the Most High"* (al-Layl 92/20 — treated in the section on al-Layl in this commentary).

**That is, the same epithet occurs in the Qurʾān both for God and in Pharaoh's claim.** The difference is that for the one it is an attribute and for the other a claim.

I record this as an observation; the shared word is a datum that can be verified.

## 79/25-26 — فَأَخَذَهُ ٱللَّهُ نَكَالَ ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ وَٱلْأُولَىٰٓ · إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَعِبْرَةً لِّمَن يَخْشَىٰ

*Fa-akhadhahu'llāhu nakāla'l-ākhirati wa'l-ūlā · Inna fī dhālika la-ʿibratan li-man yakhshā*
"So God seized him with the punishment of the last and of the first. Surely in that there is a lesson for whoever feels awe."

### أَخَذَهُ ٱللَّهُ — "God seized him"

**And this is the only place in the sūra where the name Allāh occurs.**

Through forty-six verses the sūra uses *Rabb* (five times), pronouns and verbal subjects; *al-Raḥmān* does not occur. The name **ٱللَّه** occurs in this verse alone.

And precisely **immediately after** the verse in which Pharaoh said *"I am your most high lord."*

**This is an observation and a datum that can be verified.** Its interpretation is this, and it **belongs to me**: the claim is met with a name. Pharaoh claims lordship; in the very next sentence the name **God** appears.

**أَخَذَ — Root: أ-خ-ذ.** To take, to seize, to grasp.

In the Qurʾān this verb is often used in the context of destruction and the word announces **a sudden and inescapable seizing**:

*"I seized them; and how was My retribution!"* (al-Raʿd 13/32; al-Ḥajj 22/44)

*"Such is the seizing of your Lord when He seizes the towns while they are doing wrong. His seizing is painful and severe."* (Hūd 11/102)

### نَكَال — a punishment that is a lesson

**Root: ن-ك-ل.** And the sense of the root separates this word from an ordinary "punishment".

**نَكَلَ عَنْ** — he held back, he shrank, he did not dare.
**نِكْل** — a fetter, a bit, a hobble; the iron that holds an animal or a captive.
**نَكَال** — **a punishment that deters others.**

**That is, *nakāl* is not the punishment itself but the deterrent function of the punishment.**

A punishment given to an offender does two things: the first pays what is owed, the second deters others. *Nakāl* names the second.

**Another verse of the Qurʾān says this explicitly:**

*"We made it a **nakāl** for those before it and those after it, and an admonition for those who guard themselves."* (al-Baqara 2/66)

There the word plainly means "a lesson for others": *"for those before it and those after it."*

**And this prepares the next verse:** *"Surely in that there is a **lesson**."*

Two verses say the same idea with two words: *nakāl* and *ʿibra*. The punishment is not only about Pharaoh; it is about **those who see.**

### ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ وَٱلْأُولَىٰ — "of the last and of the first"

There are two views on this construction:

| View | Explanation | Its ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The punishment of the hereafter and of this world** | He was punished both in this world (drowning) and in the hereafter | *Ākhira* and *ūlā* answer to this pair in the Qurʾān; the order (the hereafter first) may be for the sake of magnifying the terror | That the order is inverted requires explanation |
| **The punishment of his last word and of his first word** | *"I am your most high lord"* (79/24 — the last word) and *"I know of no god for you other than myself"* (al-Qaṣaṣ 28/38 — the first word) | On one view it is transmitted that there were forty years between the two utterances; the punishment is the sum of the two | It rests on a report, and the transmission about the interval is not certain |

**The first view is the most widespread.**

**The second view is striking and deserves recording**, because the Qurʾān really does record two distinct claims of Pharaoh and there is an escalation between them:

- **al-Qaṣaṣ 28/38:** *"I **know** of no god for you other than myself."* — This is a sentence not about himself but about his **knowledge**. An implicit claim.
- **al-Nāziʿāt 79/24:** *"I am your most high lord."* — A direct claim.

**That is, the claim has become open with time.** And this agrees with the description of *ṭughyān* recorded in the section on al-ʿAlaq: the limit is not crossed at one stroke but by degrees.

**I prefer the first view; it is not binding.** My ground: the verse speaks not of an interval of time but of two punishments, and the pair *ākhira/ūlā* is widely used in the Qurʾān for this world and the hereafter.

**The escalation the second view records, however, is separately valuable** and can be verified by comparing the two verses.

### إِنَّ فِى ذَٰلِكَ لَعِبْرَةً — the verdict of the narrative

**عِبْرَة — Root: ع-ب-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root is **to cross over**.

Its derivatives:

- **عَبَرَ ٱلنَّهْرَ** — he crossed the river. **عُبُور** — a crossing.
- **عَابِرُ سَبِيلٍ** — a traveller; one **passing** along a road. In the Qurʾān: *"…except while passing along a way"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/43).
- **تَعْبِيرُ ٱلرُّؤْيَا** — the interpretation of dreams. It occurs in the narrative of Joseph (Yūsuf 12/43: *"if you can interpret dreams"*). **To interpret a dream is to cross from the image to the meaning.**
- **عِبَارَة** — an expression; speech that carries a meaning across.
- **عَبْرَة** — a tear; the drop that **overflows and crosses** from the eye.

**The common core of the root: to cross from one bank to the other.**

**And *ʿibra* is exactly this: to cross from the event to the verdict.**

An event is not by itself a lesson. A lesson arises when one crosses from the **visible** face of the event to its **invisible** face. Pharaoh's drowning is an event; to draw a measure from it is a lesson.

**The Qurʾān uses this word in several places and it always does the same work:**

*"O you who have insight! Take the lesson."* (al-Ḥashr 59/2 — *fa'-ʿtabirū*)

*"Surely in their stories there is a lesson for those who have insight."* (Yūsuf 12/111)

*"And in the cattle there is surely a lesson for you."* (al-Naḥl 16/66; al-Muʾminūn 23/21)

**The last example is worth recording:** the lesson is taken not only from a narrative but from nature as well. And this prepares the next section (79/27-33, the table of creation).

### لِّمَن يَخْشَىٰ — "for whoever feels awe"

**And the verdict of the narrative is limited by a qualification.**

The verse does not say *"in that there is a lesson"*; it says *"in that there is a lesson for whoever feels awe."*

**That is, the lesson is not in the event; it is in the eye of the one looking.**

When the same event is told to two people, it becomes a lesson to one and not to the other. The difference is not in the event but in the one looking.

**This is the same as the finding recorded at al-Baqara 2/26.** There, in the discussion of the example of the gnat, this was treated: *"the same text, two results"* — the same verse was a cause of guidance for one and of straying for another.

**And this prepares the last verses of the sūra:**

*"You are only a warner to whoever feels awe of it."* (79/45)

**The two verses set the same qualification:** the lesson to whoever feels awe, the warning to whoever feels awe.

I record this as an observation; the repetition of the word is in the text.

**A question and an honest answer.** This qualification may look as though it sets up a vicious circle: if awe is needed in order to take a lesson, how is awe to arise?

**The verse answers this question — but elsewhere in the sūra.** In the nineteenth verse the order was: **guiding → knowing → awe.** That is, awe is not a precondition; it is the result of a process.

I record this connection as my own reading. But the words of the two verses (19 and 26) are in the text.

**The "question of the vicious circle" recorded at al-Baqara 2/2-5 holds here too** and the answer given there may be repeated: the text opens to the one who is open to it; it stays closed to the one who is closed. This is not a decree of destiny but a description of reading.

## 79/27-33 — The table of creation

**أَءَنتُمْ أَشَدُّ خَلْقًا أَمِ ٱلسَّمَآءُ ۚ بَنَىٰهَا · رَفَعَ سَمْكَهَا فَسَوَّىٰهَا · وَأَغْطَشَ لَيْلَهَا وَأَخْرَجَ ضُحَىٰهَا · وَٱلْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ دَحَىٰهَآ · أَخْرَجَ مِنْهَا مَآءَهَا وَمَرْعَىٰهَا · وَٱلْجِبَالَ أَرْسَىٰهَا · مَتَٰعًا لَّكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَٰمِكُمْ**

*A-antum ashaddu khalqan ami'l-samāʾ · Banāhā · Rafaʿa samkahā fa-sawwāhā · Wa aghṭasha laylahā wa akhraja ḍuḥāhā · Wa'l-arḍa baʿda dhālika daḥāhā · Akhraja minhā māʾahā wa marʿāhā · Wa'l-jibāla arsāhā · Matāʿan lakum wa li-anʿāmikum*

"Are you harder to create, or the heaven? He built it: He raised its height and set it in order; He darkened its night and brought forth its forenoon. And the earth, after that, He spread out: from it He brought forth its water and its pasture; and the mountains He set firm — as a provision for you and for your cattle."

### The section's place in the context

The narrative is over. And immediately after the word *ʿibra* in the twenty-sixth verse the sūra asks a question.

**And the question returns to the objection in the twelfth verse.** They had said *"when we have become decayed bones?"* The twenty-seventh verse answers: *"Are you harder to create, or the heaven?"*

That is, the sūra has placed **a narrative of twelve verses** between the objection and its answer. And when the narrative is over it returns to the argument where it left off.

**This is the same structure as what Sūrat al-Nabaʾ does:** there too there was an objection, and by way of answer a **table of creation** was set up (78/6-16).

**A comparison of the two tables will be made below in a separate section.**

### أَءَنتُمْ أَشَدُّ خَلْقًا — the argument a fortiori

**The logic of the question:** one who can make the greater can make the lesser. In logic this is called the argument *a fortiori* (from the stronger).

**أَشَدُّ — Root: ش-د-د.** And this root was used of the heaven at al-Nabaʾ 78/12: **سَبْعًا شِدَادًا** — seven firm ones.

What was recorded there was this: the concrete sense of the root is **to bind, to bind tightly**; *shidād* means that which does not slacken.

**And now the two neighbouring sūras side by side:**

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/12** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/27** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *wa **banaynā** fawqakum sabʿan **shidādā*** | *a-antum **ashaddu** khalqan ami'l-samāʾ · **banāhā*** |
| Roots | **ب-ن-ي** + **ش-د-د** | **ش-د-د** + **ب-ن-ي** |
| Order | First the building, then the firmness | First the firmness, then the building |
| Mood | Statement | **Question** |

**The two sūras use the same two roots for the heaven — in reverse order and in a different mood.**

This is one of the most concrete verbal links between the two sūras and it is **a datum that can be verified.** That the order has been reversed is, however, an observation.

**خَلْقًا** — in the accusative as a specifying complement: "in respect of creation".

**The Qurʾān sets up this argument more explicitly elsewhere:**

*"The creation of the heavens and the earth is **greater** than the creation of mankind; but most people do not know."* (Ghāfir 40/57)

*"Is not He who created the heavens and the earth able to create the like of them? Indeed He is."* (Yāsīn 36/81)

### بَنَىٰهَا — the construction of the sentence

**بَنَىٰهَا** is a separate sentence (*istiʾnāf*) and stands as though it were the answer to the question: *"…or the heaven? — He built it."*

Some of the grammarians count it an adjective of *al-samāʾ*; some count it an independent sentence. The second is the more widespread.

**And the series of verbs begins here.** From the twenty-eighth verse to the thirty-second there are **eight verbs**, and all have the same agent, all are in the perfect, all are third person singular:

*banā · rafaʿa · sawwā · aghṭasha · akhraja · daḥā · akhraja · arsā*

**And the object of all of them ends in a pronoun:** *banā**hā**, sawwā**hā**, daḥā**hā**, arsā**hā**…*

This also sets up the verse-ending of the sūra: **-āhā.** With the exception of the thirty-third verse.

### رَفَعَ سَمْكَهَا — "He raised its height"

**سَمْك — Root: س-م-ك.** The **height** of a structure, the distance of its roof from the ground.

The lexicographers distinguish thus: *samk* is the distance measured from above downwards; that is, the "depth" of a building.

**A note.** The word **سَمَك** (fish) is also written with the same letters. The lexicographers treat these separately and **I set up no connection between them.**

**And the construction is worth recording: "He raised its height."** The sentence does not say "He raised the heaven"; it says He raised **"its height"**.

That is, the heaven was already high; its height was increased. Or: the height of the heaven is a measure given to it.

I record this as an observation about the language.

### فَسَوَّىٰهَا — He set it in order

**Root: س-و-ي.** This root was treated in the section on Sūrat al-Shams and what was recorded there is not repeated.

In brief: **سَوَّىٰ** — to set in order, to bring into balance, to make things equal to one another. At al-Shams 91/7 it was used of the self: *"by the self and Him who set it in order"*; and it was recorded there that the verb is repeated at the two ends of that sūra (at 91/14 in the sense of "He levelled it flat").

**What is to be added here is this:** the verb is joined with **فَ** — that is, the setting in order comes **after** the raising.

The order is meaningful: first the measure is given, then the balance is set. The building first rises, then is levelled.

**And the verb is used of the heaven in several places in the Qurʾān:**

*"Then He turned to the heaven and set them in order as seven heavens."* (al-Baqara 2/29 — *fa-sawwāhunna*; this verse was treated in this commentary)

### وَأَغْطَشَ لَيْلَهَا — "He darkened its night"

**Root: غ-ط-ش.** To be dark; **أَغْطَشَ** (Form IV) — **He made dark, He darkened.**

**The root appears to occur only here in the Qurʾān.** I do not offer this finding as the result of an exhaustive search.

**And that the verb is transitive is worth recording.**

The verse does not say "night fell" or "darkness came down". It says **"He darkened"** — that is, the darkness is not an emptiness formed of itself by the withdrawal of light; it is **something done.**

**This coincides with the finding recorded for غَاسِق in the section on Sūrat al-Falaq. What was written there was: *"In the conception carried by this Arabic root, darkness is not something that 'happens' but something that flows**. Not the cutting-off of light, but the pouring of darkness."*

**Al-Nāziʿāt 79/29 gives the same idea with another root and more explicitly:** darkness is **made.**

I record this as an observation; the two roots are different and I claim no kinship between them.

### وَأَخْرَجَ ضُحَىٰهَا — "and brought forth its forenoon"

**ضُحَىٰ — Root: ض-ح-و.** This root was treated in detail in the section on Sūrat al-Ḍuḥā and the finding there will be relied on.

In brief: *ḍuḥā* is the forenoon, when the sun has risen and the light has spread fully.

**The contrast between the two verbs:**

| | **Night** | **Forenoon** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **أَغْطَشَ** — He darkened | **أَخْرَجَ** — He brought forth |
| Movement | Inward / over — covering | Outward — bringing into view |
| Form | *ifʿāl* | *ifʿāl* |

**Both verbs are transitive and both are of the *ifʿāl* form.** That is, both darkness and light are things **done.**

**And a grammatical point: the pronouns.**

*Layla**hā*** — "**its** night". *Ḍuḥā**hā*** — "**its** forenoon".

**The pronouns refer back to the heaven.** That is, night and day are ascribed not to the earth but to **the heaven.**

This is a striking choice. Night and day are experienced in the place where a person lives; but the verse counts them attributes of the heaven.

I record this as an observation. And I add a reading, **stating that it is mine**: night and day are the business of the heaven, not of the earth — that is, they are part of an order not under human control. The same idea was counted as a favour at al-Nabaʾ 78/10-11; here it becomes a question of **ownership.**

### وَٱلْأَرْضَ بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ دَحَىٰهَا — "and the earth, after that, He spread out"

**دَحَىٰ — Root: د-ح-و / د-ح-ي.** To spread, to lay out, to spread flat.

This word was mentioned in the section on al-Shams: there, in treating **طَحَا** (*ṭaḥā* — al-Shams 91/6), it was recorded that *daḥā* is very close to it both in sound and in meaning and that the lexicographers count the two near to one another.

**There is no disagreement about the meaning of the word: to spread, to level out and lay flat.**

### A warning: the reading "the earth is egg-shaped"

**This word is one of the Qurʾānic words most strained in the modern period** and the rule recorded in the method (`USLUP.md`) will be applied here.

**The ground of the straining is this:** in Arabic there is the word **أُدْحِيَّة** (*udḥiyya*) and it names **the place where the ostrich lays its eggs**. Setting out from this, it is argued that the verb *daḥāhā* means "He made it egg-shaped" and that the verse announces the sphericity of the earth.

**I do not accept this reading and I must write out the grounds:**

**One — the word names not the egg but the nest.** *Udḥiyya* is not the ostrich's egg **itself**; it is the **hollow** in which it lays. And the ostrich opens that hollow **by spreading the soil with its foot.** That is, the word confirms the original sense of the root (to spread) — it does not take us away from it.

**Two — the verb itself does not give this sense.** *Daḥā* means "He spread". *Udḥiyya* is a noun derived from this verb and its sense is "a hollow opened by spreading". The road that runs from verb to noun cannot be run backwards from noun to verb.

**Three — it ruins the argument.** As was recorded in the section on al-Nabaʾ, the function of this table is to enumerate things **the one addressed can verify.** Something that cannot be verified cannot be used as evidence.

**Four — the word's context in the sūra does not support it.** The continuation of the verse is this: *"from it He brought forth its water and its pasture."* That is, the verse speaks not of the **shape** of the earth but of its **being made usable.**

**Is there a tension between the earth's being spherical and the Qurʾān?** There is not — and that is a separate question. The Qurʾān issues no ruling about the shape of the earth. What was recorded for *firāsh* (a bed) at al-Baqara 2/22 holds here too: *"this expression does not say that the earth is flat… What the word says is not shape but function."*

**The verse does not state the shape of the earth; neither that it is flat nor that it is round.** To make the text say something in either direction is a strain.

### بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ — the question of order

**And here is one of the old questions of Qurʾānic exegesis.**

This verse describes the creation of the heaven and then says *"and the earth **after that** He spread out"*.

**But the Qurʾān appears to give a different order elsewhere:**

*"It is He who created for you all that is on the earth. **Then** He turned to the heaven and set them in order as seven heavens."* (al-Baqara 2/29)

*"Say: do you really deny Him who created the earth in two days, and set up equals to Him?… **Then** He turned to the heaven while it was smoke."* (Fuṣṣilat 41/9-11)

**In al-Baqara and in Fuṣṣilat the earth is first and the heaven after; in al-Nāziʿāt the heaven is first and the earth after.**

**The exegetes' solutions:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **بَعْدَ = "moreover, in addition"** | In Arabic *baʿda* sometimes announces not order but addition. That is, "and the earth too He spread out" |
| **Creating and spreading out are separate operations** | The earth was **created** (*khalq*) first; then the heaven was made; then the earth was **spread out** (*daḥw*). What is described in al-Nāziʿāt is the spreading, not the creating |
| **No ordering is intended** | The purpose of the verses is not to give a chronology; each brings forward something in its own context |

**The second view is the most widespread** and it has a support in the text: the verb used for the earth in al-Nāziʿāt is **دَحَىٰ** (He spread out), whereas the verb used in al-Baqara is **خَلَقَ** (He created). Two different verbs, two different operations.

**The third view also deserves to be taken seriously** and agrees with a principle recorded several times in this commentary: the Qurʾān is not a textbook of cosmology; the verses set up evidence within their own contexts.

**I prefer the second view; it is not binding.** My ground: the difference of verb stands in the text and the solution comes from the text itself.

**And a warning:** claims of the sort "the Qurʾān announced the order of the universe's formation" should not be built out of this ordering. The verses do not give a timetable; and that the exegetes are not even in agreement about how the ordering is to be understood shows this.

### أَخْرَجَ مِنْهَا مَآءَهَا وَمَرْعَىٰهَا

**أَخْرَجَ** — this verb occurs in these verses for **the second time**: in the twenty-ninth verse *"He brought forth its forenoon"*, here *"He brought forth its water and its pasture."*

**And the verb had occurred at al-Nabaʾ 78/15 as well:** *"that We may **bring forth** with it grain and plants."* What was recorded there was this: this verb is at the very centre of that sūra's argument, because the Qurʾān describes the resurrection with the same verb (Nūḥ 71/17-18; al-Rūm 30/19; Fāṭir 35/9).

**The same note holds here too and is not repeated.**

**مَرْعَىٰ — Root: ر-ع-ي.** And the two branches of this root carry a shared element worth recording:

1. **To pasture, to graze.** **مَرْعَىٰ** — pasture. **رَعِيَّة** — a flock.
2. **To watch over, to keep.** **رَاعٍ** — a shepherd; and **one who watches over.** **رِعَايَة** — watching, tending.

**The connection the lexicographers set up:** the shepherd, while pasturing the flock, at the same time watches over it. The two tasks are inseparable.

And the Qurʾān uses the second sense: *"Those who **keep** their trusts and their covenants"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/8; al-Maʿārij 70/32 — *rāʿūn*).

**This is an observation about the language.** The meaning of the word in this verse is plainly "pasture".

**The ordering of مَاء and مَرْعَىٰ.** First water, then pasture. The order is natural: pasture is the result of water. And at al-Nabaʾ 78/14-16 there was the same order: first water, then grain and plants, then gardens.

### وَٱلْجِبَالَ أَرْسَىٰهَا — "and the mountains He set firm"

**Root: ر-س-و.** To make firm, to fix in place, to cast anchor.

Its derivatives:

- **رَاسِيَات** — those that are fixed. *"…and cauldrons fixed in place"* (Sabaʾ 34/13).
- **مُرْسَىٰ** — the place or the time of anchoring. **In the Qurʾān:** *"Embark in it; its sailing and its anchoring are in the name of God"* (Hūd 11/41 — *majrāhā wa mursāhā*, of Noah's ark).

**The nautical sense of the word is decisive:** *irsāʾ* is a ship's **casting anchor**. The bringing to a halt of something that moves.

**And now we come to the most striking internal link of this sūra.**

**The same root comes back in the forty-second verse of the sūra:**

*"They ask you about the Hour: **when will it cast anchor?**"* (79/42 — *ayyāna **mursāhā***)

| Verse | Expression | What |
|---|---|---|
| **79/32** | *wa'l-jibāla **أَرْسَىٰهَا*** | The mountains were set firm |
| **79/42** | *ayyāna **مُرْسَىٰهَا*** | When will the Hour cast anchor |

**The same root, at the two ends: in the table of creation and in the closing question of the sūra.**

And what the link sets up is this: the mountains **have been set firm** — that is, their time of anchoring is past and is known. The Hour, on the other hand, has not yet cast anchor, and its time is being asked about.

**This is a repetition of a word that can be verified.** The meaning the link sets up is, however, **my own reading.**

**And the image of the ship gives the forty-second verse a flavour:** the question *"when will the Hour cast anchor?"* is like asking when a ship that is under way will enter harbour. The forty-fourth verse will give the answer: *"Its final end belongs to your Lord."*

**A note about the mountains.** The warning recorded for **أَوْتَاد** (pegs) at al-Nabaʾ 78/7 holds here too and is not repeated: the verse names the **function** of the mountains, not their geological structure; and to draw a modern theory out of it is a strain.

**The two verbs the two sūras use for the mountains are worth recording:**

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/7** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/32** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **أَوْتَادًا** — pegs (a noun) | **أَرْسَىٰهَا** — He set firm (a verb) |
| Image | **A tent** | **A ship** |
| What it does | Holds the ground | Brings the ground to rest |

**The two sūras describe the same function with two different images.** One from the tent of the land, the other from seafaring.

### مَتَٰعًا لَّكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَٰمِكُمْ — "for you and for your cattle"

**And the table closes with a ground.**

**مَتَاع — Root: م-ت-ع.** To benefit, to make use of. **مَتَاع** — a thing made use of, a means of livelihood.

**And the word's use in the Qurʾān carries a qualification:**

*"The life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of delusion."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/185 — *matāʿu'l-ghurūr*)

*"This life of the world is only an enjoyment; the hereafter is the abiding home."* (Ghāfir 40/39)

*"…a little enjoyment; then their abode is the Fire."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/197)

**In the Qurʾān the word almost always carries a qualification of transience.** *Matāʿ* is not what is owned but what is **used**; it is the provision a traveller carries with him.

**That is, the closing sentence of the table carries the table's transience in its own word.**

This is the counterpart in this sūra of the reading recorded for *mihād* (a cradle) at al-Nabaʾ 78/6: there the earth was read as "a place not stayed in"; here the same thing is said with the word *matāʿ*.

I record this as an observation; the word's distribution in the Qurʾān is, however, a datum that can be verified.

**لَّكُمْ — "for you".** This expression was treated at al-Baqara 2/29. What was recorded there was this: it can be read in two ways — as a **right of use** and as a **trust**; and the Qurʾān has set the two side by side, because immediately afterwards the human being is called a "successor".

The same note holds here.

**وَلِأَنْعَٰمِكُمْ — "and for your cattle".**

**This addition is worth recording.** The table does not end with the human being; the animals are counted too.

**أَنْعَام — Root: ن-ع-م.** And the root is the root of the word **niʿma** (favour). *Anʿām* is used of the domestic animals (camel, cattle, sheep, goat) — that is, the animals a person lives from.

That the word is from the same root as *niʿma* is the language's own note: these animals have been named **a favour.**

**And the distinction recorded at al-Nabaʾ 78/15 is confirmed here.** There it was written that the distinction between *ḥabb* (grain — what the human being eats) and *nabāt* (plants — what the animal eats) was not certain, but that a later part of the sūra might support it.

**Al-Nāziʿāt 79/33 makes this distinction explicitly:** *"for you and for your cattle."*

### The single break in the verse-endings

**And now a textual observation.**

From the fifteenth verse to the forty-sixth all the verse-endings close with the sound **-ā**: *Mūsā, ṭuwā, ṭaghā, tazakkā, takhshā, al-kubrā, ʿaṣā, yasʿā, nādā, al-aʿlā, al-ūlā, yakhshā, banāhā, sawwāhā, ḍuḥāhā, daḥāhā, marʿāhā, arsāhā…*

**And one single verse stands outside this order: the thirty-third.**

**مَتَٰعًا لَّكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَٰمِكُمْ** — *…li-anʿāmikum.*

**In an unbroken sequence of sound running thirty-two verses, this is the only break.**

I record this as a textual observation and it is a datum that can be verified.

**And I add a reading, stating that it is mine:** the verse that breaks is the verse that states the **ground** of the table. Through eight verbs the works done were enumerated; then a sentence comes and says *"these are for you."*

That sentence steps out of the sound of the series — and addresses the one spoken to directly (*lakum*, *anʿāmikum* — "you").

**I must state that I am the one setting up this relation and that I do not claim the verse intends it.** The sequence of sound is, however, in the text and can be counted.

## 79/34-36 — فَإِذَا جَآءَتِ ٱلطَّآمَّةُ ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ · يَوْمَ يَتَذَكَّرُ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ مَا سَعَىٰ · وَبُرِّزَتِ ٱلْجَحِيمُ لِمَن يَرَىٰ

*Fa-idhā jāʾati'l-ṭāmmatu'l-kubrā · Yawma yatadhakkaru'l-insānu mā saʿā · Wa burrizati'l-jaḥīmu li-man yarā*
"When the great overwhelming comes — on that day man remembers what he strove for; and the Blaze is brought into view for whoever sees."

### ٱلطَّآمَّة — that which overwhelms

**Root: ط-م-م.** **طَمَّ ٱلْمَاءُ** — the water overflowed, covered everything, filled it. **طَمَّ ٱلْبِئْرَ** — he filled the well, closed it to the brim with earth.

**The idea beneath the root: to fill up enough to cover a thing over, to overflow and blanket.**

And in Arabic **طَامَّة** is used idiomatically in the sense of "a calamity that cannot be overcome".

**The word was already recorded in the table of the names of the Hour in the section on al-Qāriʿa** and the aspect it brings forward was written there as: **comprehensiveness.** That table is not repeated here; it is relied on.

**And the grammatical feature recorded there holds here too:** nearly all the names of the Hour are on the **active participle** pattern and are **feminine**. So is *al-Ṭāmma*.

**There are three words of this pattern in this sūra:** *al-rājifa* (6), *al-rādifa* (7), *al-ṭāmma* (34).

### ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ — its second use in the sūra

**And here there is a striking repetition.**

| Verse | Expression | What |
|---|---|---|
| **79/20** | **ٱلْـَٔايَةَ ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ** | The greatest sign shown to Pharaoh |
| **79/34** | **ٱلطَّآمَّةُ ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ** | The greatest calamity that is to come |

**The same adjective, with two different nouns.**

And what the two verses set up is this: to Pharaoh **the greatest sign** was shown and he denied it; now **the greatest calamity** is coming.

**I record this as my own reading.** The repetition of the word is, however, a datum that can be verified.

And this must be added: *al-kubrā* is used in other places in the Qurʾān as well (al-Najm 53/18; Ṭāhā 20/23). That is, the word's occurring twice in this sūra is not by itself proof of intent.

### يَوْمَ يَتَذَكَّرُ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ مَا سَعَىٰ

**تَذَكَّرَ — Root: ذ-ك-ر.** To mention, to remember. The **تَفَعُّل** form: to try to remember, to call to mind.

**And the choice of the verb is worth recording.**

The verse does not say *"it is shown to him"*, does not say *"he sees"*. It says **"he remembers"**.

**And to remember requires having forgotten.**

That is, what the verse says is this: the human being **had forgotten** what he did; on that day he remembers.

**This differs from the other scenes treated in this commentary and is worth comparing:**

| Sūra | Verb | Where the knowledge comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Zalzala 99/6-8** | **يَرَوْا۟ / يَرَهُ** — they see, he sees | **From outside** — the deeds are shown |
| **al-Nabaʾ 78/40** | **يَنظُرُ** — he looks | **From outside** — he looks at what is set before him |
| **al-Nāziʿāt 79/35** | **يَتَذَكَّرُ** — he remembers | **From inside** — from his own memory |

**Three sūras, three different verbs in the same scene.**

And al-Nāziʿāt's is the most inward: the record is not outside but in the person himself. No ledger is opened; **he remembers.**

I record this as an observation; the verbs of the three verses are in the text.

**مَا سَعَىٰ — "what he strove for".**

As recorded above, this root was used of Pharaoh in the twenty-second verse: *"he turned his back, hurrying."*

**And now the same root is used of everyone.**

This is part of what the sūra has been doing from the beginning: **the story of one person turns into an attribute.** The same will happen with the word *ṭaghā* (79/17 → 79/37).

**The word's sense of "running" gives a flavour here.** The verse does not say "what he did"; it says **"what he ran for"**. That is, what is remembered is the thing for whose sake one ran — the thing effort was spent on, the thing pursued.

**And the comparison with al-Nabaʾ 78/40 is worth recording:**

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/40** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/35** |
|---|---|---|
| Expression | *mā **قَدَّمَتْ** yadāh* | *mā **سَعَىٰ*** |
| Word | **What he sent ahead** | **What he ran for** |
| Image | A traveller sending his load on ahead | A running person |
| Organ | **The two hands** | (none — the verb itself) |

**The two neighbouring sūras use two different expressions in the same structure:** *"mā + verb"* — and both make the person's own act the object.

This parallel is a datum that can be verified.

### وَبُرِّزَتِ ٱلْجَحِيمُ — the Blaze is brought into view

**بَرَّزَ — Root: ب-ر-ز.** To come out, to become apparent, to pass from the hidden to the visible.

- **بَارِز** — that which is out in the open, visible. *"On that day you will see the earth laid **bare**"* (al-Kahf 18/47 — *bārizatan*).
- **بَرَاز** — open ground, a desolate plain.
- **بُرُوز** — coming out, appearing.

**The verb is passive and of the *tafʿīl* form:** *burrizat* — "it was brought into view", "it was brought before them". That is, it does not appear of itself; **it is shown.**

**ٱلْجَحِيم — Root: ج-ح-م.** The fierce burning of fire, its blazing up. *Jaḥīm* is one of the names the Qurʾān uses for the Fire — and the list in the section on al-Qāriʿa is not repeated here.

**And the word will occur twice in this sūra:** 79/36 and 79/39.

### لِمَن يَرَىٰ — "for whoever sees"

**And this qualification is strange.**

When the Blaze is brought into view, will not everyone see it? Why is a qualification set?

**The exegetes' explanations:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Everyone sees; the expression is emphasis** | The phrase "for whoever sees" stresses the clarity of the sight: if there is an eye to see, it is seen |
| **Whoever has the capacity of sight** | Whoever is not blind; and figuratively, whoever has insight |
| **The one who looks** | Whoever wants to see; whoever does not turn his eye away |

**And the Qurʾān describes the same scene in another sūra with a different qualification:**

*"And the Blaze was brought into view for those who had gone astray."* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/91 — *burrizati'l-jaḥīmu **li'l-ghāwīn***)

**The two verses use the same verb but set a different limitation:** one *"for those who had gone astray"*, the other *"for whoever sees"*.

This is a difference worth recording, and the two verses stand as though they complete one another: the Blaze is brought into view for those who have gone astray, and it becomes visible for whoever sees.

**A reading and its note.** In the twenty-sixth verse the sūra had said *"a lesson for whoever feels awe"*; in the forty-fifth it will say *"a warner to whoever feels awe"*. Here too there is a qualification: *"for whoever sees."*

**Three times the sūra says that something reaches not everyone but the one who has a particular state.** I record this as an observation; the places of the three qualifications in the text can be counted.

## 79/37-41 — Two groups

**فَأَمَّا مَن طَغَىٰ · وَءَاثَرَ ٱلْحَيَوٰةَ ٱلدُّنْيَا · فَإِنَّ ٱلْجَحِيمَ هِىَ ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ · وَأَمَّا مَنْ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِۦ وَنَهَى ٱلنَّفْسَ عَنِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ · فَإِنَّ ٱلْجَنَّةَ هِىَ ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ**

*Fa-ammā man ṭaghā · Wa āthara'l-ḥayāta'l-dunyā · Fa-inna'l-jaḥīma hiya'l-maʾwā · Wa ammā man khāfa maqāma rabbihi wa naha'l-nafsa ʿani'l-hawā · Fa-inna'l-jannata hiya'l-maʾwā*

"As for him who ran beyond bounds and preferred the life of this world — the Blaze, that is the refuge. And as for him who feared the station of his Lord and restrained the self from craving — the Garden, that is the refuge."

### فَأَمَّا … وَأَمَّا — the pattern of division into two

This pattern was treated in the section on al-Qāriʿa and the finding there is not repeated.

In brief: **أَمَّا** is a particle of *tafṣīl* (setting out in detail); its answer must contain a **فَ**; and the feature of the pattern is this: **the division is definite and leaves no gap between** — there are two categories, there is no third.

**The comparison with al-Qāriʿa is especially fruitful here**, because there the **symmetry** of the pattern was broken and this was treated in detail.

**What was recorded in al-Qāriʿa was this:**

| | **The heavy scale (101/6-7)** | **The light scale (101/8-9)** |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | *man thaqulat mawāzīnuh* | *man khaffat mawāzīnuh* |
| Answer | *fa-huwa fī ʿīshatin rāḍiya* | *fa-**ummuhu** hāwiya* |

And there this was written: *"The conditional clauses are exactly symmetrical… But the answering clauses are not symmetrical."*

**In al-Nāziʿāt the case is exactly the reverse:**

| | **The one who runs beyond bounds (37-39)** | **The one who guards himself (40-41)** |
|---|---|---|
| Condition | *man **ṭaghā** wa **āthara** al-ḥayāta'l-dunyā* | *man **khāfa** maqāma rabbihi wa **naha** al-nafsa ʿani'l-hawā* |
| Answer | *fa-inna'l-**jaḥīma** hiya'l-**maʾwā*** | *fa-inna'l-**jannata** hiya'l-**maʾwā*** |
| Number of verbs | **Two** | **Two** |
| Structure of the answer | *fa-inna + definite noun + hiya + al-maʾwā* | **Exactly the same** |

**The answers are exactly symmetrical.** Only one word changes: *jaḥīm* / *janna*.

**And the conditions are symmetrical too:** on both sides there are **two verbs**.

**I record this as my own reading, but the comparison can be made directly from the text:**

| | **al-Qāriʿa 101/6-9** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/37-41** |
|---|---|---|
| Measure | **The scale** — an operation from outside | **The verbs** — the person's own act |
| Conditions | Passive (*thaqulat mawāzīnuh* — his scales came out heavy) | **Active** (*ṭaghā, āthara, khāfa, nahā*) |
| Symmetry of the answer | **Broken** | **Complete** |

**The two sūras use the same pattern but take the measure from a different place.** In al-Qāriʿa an operation is performed on the person; in al-Nāziʿāt the person's own deeds are counted.

### Four verbs, two pairs

**And now let us look at the four verbs.** This is the densest place in the sūra.

| Group | First verb | Second verb |
|---|---|---|
| **The one who runs beyond bounds** | **طَغَىٰ** — he ran beyond bounds, he passed his limit | **ءَاثَرَ** — he preferred, he put first |
| **The one who guards himself** | **خَافَ** — he feared | **نَهَىٰ** — he held back, he forbade |

**In each pair there is one inner and one outer movement** — and the two run in opposite directions:

| | **The one who runs beyond bounds** | **The one who guards himself** |
|---|---|---|
| First verb | *Ṭaghā* — **overflowing outward** | *Khāfa* — **a state forming within** |
| Second verb | *Āthara* — **a choice made within** | *Nahā* — **an intervention inward** |

**That is, the two groups are each other's mirror:** the one overflows outward and makes a choice within; the other fears within and intervenes within.

I record this as my own reading. The meanings of the four verbs are, however, dictionary data.

### طَغَىٰ — for the third time

**And the backbone of the sūra is completed here.**

| Verse | Expression | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **79/17** | *innahu **طَغَىٰ*** | **Pharaoh** — a particular person |
| **79/37** | *man **طَغَىٰ*** | **Whoever runs beyond bounds** — an attribute |

**The sūra turns a historical figure into a measure.**

In the seventeenth verse running beyond bounds was the state of one person. In the thirty-seventh verse it becomes a **category**: *man* (whoever) — indefinite, open to everyone.

**And this is one of the clearest instances in the text of the principle recorded in the method (`USLUP.md`):** *"what the verse describes are attributes; whoever bears that attribute is included in it."*

The sūra does not recount Pharaoh and then say *"such are the wicked"*. It recounts Pharaoh and gives the **measure**. And the measure holds for everyone whose name is not mentioned.

**I record this as my own reading**, but the repetition of the word and the indefiniteness of *man* are in the text.

**One further observation:** Sūrat al-Nabaʾ had used the same word too — *li'l-ṭāghīna maʾābā* (78/22), "for those who run beyond bounds, a place of return". But there the word came directly as an attribute; there was no narrative.

**The two neighbouring sūras set up the same attribute by two roads:** al-Nabaʾ names it directly, al-Nāziʿāt first shows its example and then names it.

### ءَاثَرَ — to prefer

**Root: أ-ث-ر.** And the concrete sense of the root is **a track**.

- **أَثَر** — a track, a footprint, a remnant. In the Qurʾān: *"upon their tracks…"* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/70; al-Zukhruf 43/22-23).
- **ءَاثَرَ** — he preferred, he put first, he chose.
- **إِيثَار** — to prefer another to oneself.
- **مَآثِر** — the traces left behind, the works.

**This root was treated in the section on al-ʿĀdiyāt** — there **فَأَثَرْنَ بِهِۦ نَقْعًا** (100/4) was taken up and the root's sense of "raising dust" was recorded within that sūra's agricultural weave.

**The connection between "track" and "preference".** The explanation the lexicographers give: to prefer a thing is to follow its track; or the thing preferred is put in front, and its track becomes visible.

**And a derivative of the word is mentioned in the Qurʾān with praise:**

*"They **prefer** them to themselves, even though they are themselves in need."* (al-Ḥashr 59/9 — *wa yuʾthirūna ʿalā anfusihim*)

**Here there is something worth recording.**

**The same verb is a virtue at al-Ḥashr 59/9 and a fault at al-Nāziʿāt 79/38.**

The difference is in the object: in al-Ḥashr **another** is preferred to oneself; in al-Nāziʿāt **this world** is preferred to the hereafter.

**That is, the verb is not in itself good or bad.** Preferring is a structural business of the human being; the question is what is preferred to what.

I record this as an observation about the language. And I add this, **as my own reading**: this is a repetition of a pattern recorded several times in this commentary — the same thing was said in the discussion of *al-Ṣamad* in al-Ikhlāṣ, of *hāwiya* in al-Qāriʿa, and of *ittikhādh* in al-Nabaʾ: **the human being chooses in any case; the question is what he chooses.**

**The Qurʾān describes this preference elsewhere with the same verb:**

*"But you prefer the life of this world; whereas the hereafter is better and more lasting."* (al-Aʿlā 87/16-17)

**This verse is almost identical with al-Nāziʿāt 79/38** and adds a ground as well: *better and more lasting.*

### ٱلْحَيَوٰةَ ٱلدُّنْيَا — the life of this world

**ٱلدُّنْيَا — Root: د-ن-و.** This root was treated at al-Baqara 2/61 and the finding there will be relied on.

What was recorded there was this: the root carries together the senses of **being near** and **being low**; *dunyā* comes from the same root and *"the name of this life is 'the nearer one'"*; and *"the language itself delivers a verdict: what is near and what is low are named with the same word."*

**And this was written there:** *"What can be grasped by hand, reached at once, tasted now, is for that very reason the lower in worth. The name the Qurʾān chooses for 'this world' already carries this assessment within it."*

**What is to be added here is this: the word is an elative.**

**دُنْيَا** is the feminine of **أَدْنَىٰ** — that is, an **elative** (a comparative/superlative): "the nearer", "the nearest".

**And this requires a comparison.** To say "nearer" requires the existence of something "further".

The Qurʾān names that further one: **ٱلْـَٔاخِرَة** — "the later, the one at the end".

**That is, both lives have been named with relative words:**

| | **ٱلدُّنْيَا** | **ٱلْـَٔاخِرَة** |
|---|---|---|
| Kind of word | Elative | Active participle (that which is at the end) |
| Its meaning | The **nearer** one | The one that comes **after** |
| Relative to what | Relative to the hereafter | Relative to this world |

**Neither of them carries a name of its own; they are named relative to one another.**

I record this as an observation about the language.

**And I add a conclusion, as my own reading:** this naming makes it impossible, at the level of the language itself, **to think of the two lives independently of one another.** To say "this world" is already to presuppose a "hereafter" — because the expression "the nearer" is meaningless on its own.

And the fault in the thirty-eighth verse is exactly this: the person prefers *"the nearer one"* — that is, he makes a comparison and counts nearness as superiority.

### خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِۦ — "he feared the station of his Lord"

**خَافَ — Root: خ-و-ف.** To fear.

**And a word-choice here is worth recording.** In verses nineteen, twenty-six and forty-five the sūra was using the root **خَشْيَة**. Here **خَوْف** is used.

The distinction recorded above should be recalled: the lexicographers count *khawf* general fear and *khashya* fear grounded in knowledge.

**That the two words are found together in this sūra deserves to be recorded as an observation**, but I do not claim that the difference between them is being deliberately used here — because the distinction is not a rule the lexicographers apply rigidly everywhere.

**مَقَام — Root: ق-و-م.** To stand. **مَقَام** — the place of standing, the position of standing, a station. And as a mīmic verbal noun: standing.

**There are three views on the meaning of the construction:**

| View | Explanation | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The presence of God** | The place where a person will stand before his Lord. *"The station of his Lord"* = the station at which one will stand in His presence | The most widespread view; agrees with the scene of the reckoning |
| **God's watching over** | *Qāʾim* — the one who watches, who stands over. *"He who **stands** over every soul with what it has earned"* (al-Raʿd 13/33 — *qāʾimun ʿalā kulli nafsin*) | The Qurʾān's own usage |
| **The person's own station** | The servant's position before his Lord | Few adherents |

**The first and second views do not exclude one another** and both arrive at the same thing: the person's knowing that he is under a watching and a reckoning.

**The Qurʾān uses the same construction in one other place:**

*"For him who fears the station of his Lord there are two gardens."* (al-Raḥmān 55/46)

**The two verses carry exactly the same expression** and both give the same result: the Garden.

### وَنَهَى ٱلنَّفْسَ عَنِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ

**The most quoted sentence of the sūra.**

**نَهَىٰ — Root: ن-ه-ي.** To forbid, to hold back, to set an end.

**And the idea beneath the root is worth recording:** **نِهَايَة** — an end, a finish. **مُنْتَهَىٰ** — the final point that is reached.

That is, *nahy* is **to set a final limit** to something. To forbid is to say "this far".

**And this connects with the forty-fourth verse of the sūra:**

*"Its **final end** belongs to your Lord."* (79/44 — *ilā rabbika **مُنتَهَىٰهَا***)

| Verse | Word | What |
|---|---|---|
| **79/40** | **نَهَى** | The person sets a final limit to his self |
| **79/44** | **مُنتَهَىٰ** | The final limit of the Hour belongs to the Lord |

**The same root, two distinct limits.** One the limit a person sets for himself; the other the limit no one knows.

**This is a repetition of a root that can be verified.** The meaning the link sets up is **my own reading.**

**ٱلنَّفْس — the self.** This word was treated in detail in the section on Sūrat al-Shams and the findings there are not repeated.

**But a grammatical point should be recorded:** the verse does not say *"his self"*, it says **"the self"** — **ٱلنَّفْسَ**, with the definite article, without annexation.

This choice is explained in two ways:

| Explanation | Its meaning |
|---|---|
| **The article stands in place of the annexation** | Meaning "his own self"; a widespread usage in Arabic |
| **It announces the genus** | "The thing called self" — that is, the general structure of the self is meant |

The first is the more widespread. The second is meaningful too and adds something: what is forbidden is not a fault peculiar to the person; it is **the general tendency of the self.**

**ٱلْهَوَىٰ — craving.**

**Root: ه-و-ي.** And this root was treated in detail in the section on al-Qāriʿa. The finding there will not be repeated here, but it needs recalling, because it carries the whole weight of this verse.

What was recorded in al-Qāriʿa was this: **هَوَىٰ** — to fall from a height; **هَاوِيَة** — the place fallen into, one of the names of the Fire; and:

> *"In Arabic 'desire' and 'to fall' are from the same root. What the lexicographers say about this connection is: hawā is what pulls a person downward; the word itself contains the fall. That is: whoever follows his craving arrives at hāwiya."*

**Now let us apply this to the present verse.**

*"He restrained the self from craving"* — that is, **he restrained the self from the fall.** The word itself states the direction of the thing that must be restrained.

**And the comparison of the two sūras is fruitful here:**

| | **al-Qāriʿa 101/9** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/40-41** |
|---|---|---|
| Root | **ه-و-ي** — *hāwiya* | **ه-و-ي** — *hawā* |
| What | **The place arrived at** | **The thing pursued** |
| Result | Falling | Being held back |

**Al-Qāriʿa uses the root as a point of arrival; al-Nāziʿāt as a point of departure.** The two sūras take hold of the two ends of the same root.

**This is a shared root that can be verified.** The connection had already been set up in the section on al-Qāriʿa; here it is completed.

**Craving in the Qurʾān.** The word is almost always in a negative context in the Qurʾān:

*"Have you seen him who takes his craving as his god?"* (al-Jāthiya 45/23; al-Furqān 25/43)

*"Do not follow craving, or it will lead you astray from the way of God."* (Ṣād 38/26)

*"They follow only conjecture and what their selves crave."* (al-Najm 53/23)

### Two refuges

**ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ — Root: أ-و-ي.** To take shelter, to take refuge. **مَأْوَىٰ** — the place taken refuge in, a refuge.

In the Qurʾān this verb occurs in scenes of taking shelter: *"when they took refuge in the cave"* (al-Kahf 18/10, 16); *"I shall take refuge on a mountain that will protect me"* (Hūd 11/43 — Noah's son).

**And that the word comes with the definite article in both verses is worth recording.**

The verse does not say *"the Blaze is his refuge"*; it says **"the Blaze, that is the refuge"** — *hiya'l-maʾwā.*

**The meeting of two definite elements announces restriction in Arabic** — this was recorded for *Allāhu'l-Ṣamad* in the section on al-Ikhlāṣ.

That is, the meaning is: **there is no other refuge.** That is the refuge.

**And this connects with the section on al-Qāriʿa.** There, in treating the expression *"his mother is Hāwiya"*, this was recorded:

> *"To say 'his mother is Hāwiya' is to say 'that is the place he can go back to'. The person is not merely thrown somewhere; he arrives at the place he belongs to."*

**Al-Nāziʿāt sets up the same idea on both sides at once:** both the Blaze and the Garden are **al-maʾwā**. In al-Qāriʿa it had been used for one side only.

**And it connects with al-Nabaʾ as well.** There the word **مَـَٔاب** (a place of return) was used twice, for two different addresses (78/22 and 78/39).

**Three sūras, three words, the same idea:**

| Sūra | Word | Sense of the root |
|---|---|---|
| **al-Qāriʿa 101/9** | **أُمّ** — mother | The origin taken refuge in |
| **al-Nabaʾ 78/22, 39** | **مَـَٔاب** — place of return | To return |
| **al-Nāziʿāt 79/39, 41** | **ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ** — refuge | To take shelter |

**All three say the same thing: a person arrives somewhere, and the place he arrives at is the place he belongs to.**

I record this table as my own reading; the root senses of the three words are, however, dictionary data.

## 79/42-44 — يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلسَّاعَةِ أَيَّانَ مُرْسَىٰهَا · فِيمَ أَنتَ مِن ذِكْرَىٰهَآ · إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ مُنتَهَىٰهَآ

*Yasʾalūnaka ʿani'l-sāʿati ayyāna mursāhā · Fīma anta min dhikrāhā · Ilā rabbika muntahāhā*
"They ask you about the Hour: 'When will it cast anchor?' Where are you in the mention of it? Its final end belongs to your Lord."

### يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ — "they ask you"

**And now we come to the clearest link between the two neighbouring sūras.**

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/1** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/42** |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | **يَتَسَآءَلُونَ** | **يَسْـَٔلُونَكَ** |
| Root | **س-أ-ل** | **س-أ-ل** |
| Form | *tafāʿul* — **reciprocal** | *faʿala* — **one-directional** |
| To whom | **To one another** | **To you** (the Prophet) |
| Subject | *Al-nabaʾu'l-ʿaẓīm* | *Al-sāʿa* |
| Place in the sūra | **The first verse** | **The fifth verse from the end** |

**The same root, in two sūras, in two different forms.**

Al-Nabaʾ opened with *"they ask one another"*; al-Nāziʿāt closes with *"they ask you"*.

**And the answers the two sūras give are different too:**

- **al-Nabaʾ:** it cuts the question off (*kallā*) and sets up a table. No answer is given, but a **scene** is given.
- **al-Nāziʿāt:** it says *"you cannot know"*. No answer is given, and it is said that **none can be given.**

**I record this parallel as my own reading.** But the shared root, the difference of form and the positions in the two sūras are data that can be verified.

**And this must be added:** the two questions are not the same question. What is asked in al-Nabaʾ is **"what?"**; what is asked in al-Nāziʿāt is **"when?"**

The first is about the content of the tiding; the second about its time. And the Qurʾān answers the two in two different ways: the content is described, the time is not disclosed.

### أَيَّانَ — "when"

**In Arabic there are two particles for a question of time: مَتَىٰ and أَيَّانَ.**

**The difference the lexicographers record:** *matā* is the general question of time; *ayyāna* is used when the time of a **great, fearful or distant** event is being asked about.

**And the distribution in the Qurʾān supports this distinction.** Everywhere *ayyāna* occurs in the Qurʾān it is about the Hour or the resurrection:

*"They ask you about the Hour: when will it cast anchor?"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/187 — **almost exactly the same as the verse in this sūra**)

*"When is the Day of Judgement?"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/12 — *ayyāna yawmu'l-dīn*)

*"They have no awareness of when they will be raised."* (al-Naḥl 16/21; al-Naml 27/65 — *ayyāna yubʿathūn*)

**I draw attention to this distribution**; since I cannot say with certainty whether there is an exception, I do not offer it as a rule. But if the distribution is correct, then the particle itself announces the kind of thing being asked about.

### مُرْسَىٰهَا — "its casting anchor"

**Root: ر-س-و.** And as recorded above at the thirty-second verse, the same root was used of the mountains: **أَرْسَىٰهَا.**

**مُرْسَىٰ** may be a mīmic verbal noun or a noun of time/place:

| Analysis | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mīmic verbal noun | The casting of anchor, coming to rest |
| Noun of time | The **time** of casting anchor |
| Noun of place | The **place** of casting anchor |

**Since the question is "when", the reading as a noun of time best suits the context.**

**And the nautical sense of the word sets up an image here.**

As recorded above, *irsāʾ* is a ship's casting of anchor. And the Qurʾān had used this word of Noah's ark: *"Its sailing and its anchoring (**مُرْسَىٰهَا**) are in the name of God"* (Hūd 11/41).

**That is, the question resembles this:** *"When will this ship enter harbour?"*

And the answer that comes two verses later is the same as the answer of Hūd 11/41: **its coming to rest is God's affair.**

I record this as an observation; the shared word is, however, a datum that can be verified.

### فِيمَ أَنتَ مِن ذِكْرَىٰهَا — the shortened question

**And here there is a link of sound with Sūrat al-Nabaʾ.**

**فِيمَ** — **فِي + مَا**, with the alif of *mā* dropped.

**This is the same grammatical event as عَمَّ at al-Nabaʾ 78/1.** There the rule was given in the form of a table and is not repeated: interrogative *mā*, when it takes a preposition before it, loses its alif.

**The two neighbouring sūras use the shortened interrogative at the two ends:**

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/1** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/43** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **عَمَّ** | **فِيمَ** |
| Original | *ʿan mā* | *fī mā* |
| To whom the question is put | About their asking one another | **To the Prophet** |
| Place in the sūra | **The first word** | The fourth verse from the end |

**This is a datum that can be verified.** I do not claim that the two sūras' use of this pattern at the two ends is deliberate; what I record is that two neighbouring sūras carry the same rare pattern.

### The meaning of the verse — the disagreement

**The sentence is obscure in Arabic too, and the exegetes have differed.**

| View | Explanation | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **"You are not in a position to announce it"** | The Prophet has nothing to say on this matter; the knowledge has not been given to him | The next verse says this explicitly: *"its final end belongs to your Lord"* |
| **"Why are you occupied with this?"** | A caution to the Prophet: do not busy yourself with this question | Drawing the question *fīma* towards the sense of "why" |
| **"What have you to do with mentioning it?"** | An answer to the questioners: the Prophet has no concern with this matter | The direct sense of the sentence |
| **"You are at the last stage of its mention"** | On one view the sentence is read positively: the Prophet is a sign of the nearness of the Hour | It is rested on a report; **it stands weak linguistically** |

**The first and third views are close to one another and are the most widespread.** Both arrive at the same result: **the Prophet does not know the time of the Hour.**

**The fourth view does not fit the wording of the sentence** and I record it as a transmitted report; I do not prefer it.

**I prefer the line of the first and third views; it is not binding.** My ground: the next verse gives the sense of the sentence explicitly.

### The Qurʾān's posture on this matter

**This verse does not stand alone.** The Qurʾān says in several places and explicitly that the time of the Hour is not known:

*"They ask you about the Hour: when will it cast anchor? Say: knowledge of it is only with my Lord; none but He will disclose it at its time… Say: knowledge of it is only with God; but most people do not know."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/187)

*"People ask you about the Hour. Say: knowledge of it is only with God."* (al-Aḥzāb 33/63)

*"The knowledge of the Hour is with Him."* (Luqmān 31/34; al-Zukhruf 43/85; Fuṣṣilat 41/47)

**This is a consistency worth recording.** The Qurʾān writes into the text something its own prophet does not know.

And this has a consequence that looks directly at the present: **claims to compute the date of the Hour conflict with the Qurʾān's own text.** In this commentary, per the method (`USLUP.md`), claims of the numerical and alphabetical-computation sort are not used; this verse is one of the supports in the text for that rule.

### إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ مُنتَهَىٰهَا

**And the answer is given.**

**مُنتَهَىٰ — Root: ن-ه-ي.** As recorded above at the fortieth verse, this root carries both the sense of "to forbid" and the sense of "end". *Muntahā* — the final point that is reached.

**The Qurʾān uses this word in several places:**

*"And that the final end is to your Lord."* (al-Najm 53/42 — *wa anna ilā rabbika'l-muntahā*)

*"By the lote-tree of the utmost boundary."* (al-Najm 53/14)

**The word order of the sentence is worth recording.** *"Ilā rabbika"* (to your Lord) has been brought forward; *muntahāhā* (its final end) has been left to the end.

**In Arabic an element brought forward from its normal place announces restriction.** That is, the meaning is: **its final end belongs to your Lord alone** — to no one else.

**And "end" has two senses, and both may hold here:**

| Sense | What it means |
|---|---|
| **The end of knowledge** | Knowledge of it ends with Him; no one can pass beyond |
| **The end of the event** | Its coming to pass belongs to Him |

The exegetes record both and they do not exclude one another.

## 79/45-46 — إِنَّمَآ أَنتَ مُنذِرُ مَن يَخْشَىٰهَا · كَأَنَّهُمْ يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَهَا لَمْ يَلْبَثُوٓا۟ إِلَّا عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَىٰهَا

*Innamā anta mundhiru man yakhshāhā · Ka-annahum yawma yarawnahā lam yalbathū illā ʿashiyyatan aw ḍuḥāhā*
"You are only a warner to whoever feels awe of it. On the day they see it, it will be as though they had tarried only an evening, or its forenoon."

### إِنَّمَآ أَنتَ — the limit of the task

**إِنَّمَا** — a particle of restriction: "only, nothing but."

**And the particle sets a limitation.** The forty-third verse had said what the Prophet **does not know**; this verse says **what he does.**

**That is, the two verses form a pair:** one describes what falls outside the task, the other the task itself.

**مُنذِر — Root: ن-ذ-ر.** A warner. This root was treated at al-Baqara 2/6; the finding there is not repeated: **إِنْذَار** is to give notice of a danger to come; a warning does not remove the danger, it **makes it visible.**

**And the same root occurred in the last verse of Sūrat al-Nabaʾ:** *"We have **warned** you of a punishment near at hand"* (78/40 — *andharnākum*).

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/40** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/45** |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **أَنذَرْنَٰكُمْ** — a verb, perfect | **مُنذِر** — a noun, active participle |
| Agent | **We** (God) | **You** (the Prophet) |
| Addressee | **You** — general | **Whoever feels awe** — limited |
| Position | **The last verse** | The second verse from the end |

**The two neighbouring sūras use the same root in their closing verses.** This is a datum that can be verified.

### مَن يَخْشَىٰهَا — "whoever feels awe of it"

**And here a question arises: is the warning only to the one who feels awe?**

The Qurʾān elsewhere keeps the scope of the warning wide:

*"…that you may warn a heedless people."* (Yāsīn 36/6)

*"It is a warner to mankind."* (al-Muddaththir 74/36)

*"…that he may be a warner to all the worlds."* (al-Furqān 25/1)

**The exegetes' solution:**

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **The warning is to everyone; the benefit is in the one who feels awe** | The warner's task is to reach everyone; but the **effect** of the warning appears only in the one who feels awe. So the verse would mean "your warning takes effect in him" |
| **The real addressee of the warning** | The warning counts as made for the one whom it will benefit |
| **Consolation** | The verse gives the Prophet a consolation: you are not responsible for everyone's not believing |

**The first explanation is the most widespread and removes the conflict with the other verses.**

**And the link within the sūra.** As recorded above, the root **خ-ش-ي** occurs three times in this sūra (19, 26, 45). And in all three it sets a **qualification**:

- **19:** what is offered to Pharaoh as a result is awe.
- **26:** the lesson is for whoever feels awe.
- **45:** the warning reaches whoever feels awe.

**Three times the sūra states the condition of its own working.**

I record this as an observation.

And note the pronoun: يَخْشَىٰ**هَا** — "whoever feels awe **of it**". The pronoun refers back to the Hour.

That is, the object of the awe here is not God but **the Hour**. This is a little different from the word's widespread use in the Qurʾān (awe of God) and is explained by the context: the last part of the sūra is about the Hour.

### كَأَنَّهُمْ يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَهَا — the last sentence of the sūra

**كَأَنَّ** — a particle of comparison: "as though".

**And what the sentence sets up is an illusion of time.**

When they see the Hour, they will feel that the period they spent in the world was **very short.**

**The Qurʾān repeats this idea in many places and the measures vary:**

*"How long did you tarry on the earth, in number of years? They will say: 'We tarried a day or part of a day.'"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/112-113)

*"On the day He gathers them, it will be as though they had tarried only an hour of the day."* (Yūnus 10/45)

*"The guilty will swear that they tarried no more than an hour."* (al-Rūm 30/55)

*"On the day they see it, they will have tarried only an evening or a forenoon."* (al-Nāziʿāt 79/46)

**What is common is this: the measure is always a part of a day.** Not a year, not a month, not a day — **a part of a day.**

### لَمْ يَلْبَثُوٓا۟ — "they did not tarry"

**Root: ل-ب-ث.** To stay, to remain, to linger.

**And this root had occurred in Sūrat al-Nabaʾ:**

*"They will **tarry** there for ages."* (78/23 — *lābithīna fīhā aḥqābā*)

**This link was already recorded in the section on al-Nabaʾ and the table was given.** It is not repeated here; in summary:

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/23** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/46** |
|---|---|---|
| Where | In the Fire | In this world |
| How long | *Aḥqāb* — for ages | Half a day |
| Verb | **لَابِثِينَ** — those who tarry | **لَمْ يَلْبَثُوا۟** — they did not tarry |

**The two neighbouring sūras use the same root in exactly opposite directions.**

And what the link sets up is this: **the proportion of the two durations is reversed.** A lifetime looks long while it is being lived; looked at from there it is half a day. Punishment looks abstract when looked at from a distance; there it is ages.

### عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَىٰهَا — "an evening, or its forenoon"

**عَشِيَّة — Root: ع-ش-و.** The evening, the last part of the day. **عِشَاء** — the time of the night prayer.

**ضُحَىٰ** — the forenoon; treated in the section on Sūrat al-Ḍuḥā.

**And there is a grammatical point here which is needed in order to understand the expression.**

**ضُحَىٰهَا** — "**its** forenoon". What does the pronoun refer to?

To **عَشِيَّة**. That is, the expression is: *"an evening, or the forenoon of that evening."*

**This may look odd** — what is the forenoon of an evening?

**In Arabic the day is counted from evening to evening** (unlike the ordinary English reckoning, in which the day runs from midnight). That is, a "day" begins with an evening and lasts until the following evening. The forenoon of that day is the forenoon of the morning that comes after that evening.

**That is, the expression says this: one of the two halves of a day.**

| Expression | Which part of the day |
|---|---|
| **عَشِيَّة** | The second half of the day — afternoon and evening |
| **ضُحَىٰهَا** | The first half of the day — morning and forenoon |

**Some of the exegetes explain it thus; some read the expression generally as "either an evening or a morning — that is, a very short time."**

**Both readings arrive at the same result: less than half a day.**

**And in this commentary this verse was already mentioned in the section on al-Ḍuḥā.** What was recorded there was: *"The shortness of a lifetime is measured by the two ends of a day."*

### The last word of the sūra

**ضُحَىٰهَا.**

**And this word occurs in the sūra for the second time.** In the twenty-ninth verse: *"He darkened [the heaven's] night and brought forth its **forenoon**"* — **وَأَخْرَجَ ضُحَىٰهَا.**

| Verse | Expression | Whose forenoon |
|---|---|---|
| **79/29** | *wa akhraja **ضُحَىٰهَا*** | **The heaven's** forenoon — in the creation |
| **79/46** | *aw **ضُحَىٰهَا*** | **An evening's** forenoon — the measure of a lifetime |

**The same word, at the two ends.**

In the first the forenoon is a thing **brought forth** — the object of an act of creation. In the second the forenoon is a **unit of measure** — and what it measures is a lifetime.

**I record this as my own reading.** The repetition of the word is, however, a datum that can be verified.

**And the sūra closes with the verse-ending sound it has carried through thirty-two verses:** *-āhā.*

## The sūra as a whole

### The symmetry of the structure

The sūra divides into two great halves, and the two halves mirror one another:

| | **First half (1-26)** | **Second half (27-46)** |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Five oaths — unanswered | A question — the argument a fortiori |
| Scene | Two quakings (6-7) | Al-Ṭāmma (34) |
| The human being | Hearts throb, eyes fall (8-9) | Man remembers what he ran for (35) |
| Objection / verdict | *"Are we to be brought back?"* (10-12) | Two groups are separated (37-41) |
| Example | **The narrative of Moses and Pharaoh** (15-25) | **The table of creation** (27-33) |
| Close | *"A lesson for whoever feels awe"* (26) | *"A warner to whoever feels awe"* (45) |

**Both halves close with the same word: يَخْشَىٰ.**

I record this symmetry as my own reading. But that the two closing verses carry the same root is a datum that can be verified.

### طَغَىٰ — the backbone of the sūra

Let us see together what was recorded separately above:

| Verse | Expression | Its position |
|---|---|---|
| **79/17** | *innahu **طَغَىٰ*** | A particular person: **Pharaoh** |
| **79/37** | *man **طَغَىٰ*** | An indefinite attribute: **whoever runs beyond bounds** |

**And what is in between?** The **course** of Pharaoh's running beyond bounds: the offer, the miracle, the refusal, the turning of the back, the gathering of the crowd, the claim, the punishment.

**That is, the sūra does this:** it shows step by step on a single example how an attribute works, then detaches the attribute from the example and sets it out.

**And in the thirty-seventh verse the example is not mentioned.** It does not say *"those who are like Pharaoh"*; it says *"whoever runs beyond bounds"*. The example turns into a measure that goes beyond itself.

**This is one of the clearest instances in the text of the principle recorded in the method (`USLUP.md`)** and it has a practical consequence: the sūra recounts a person but does not teach the passing of judgement on a person. It gives the measure.

### The verse-endings — the three voices of the sūra

The sūra divides into three regions of sound:

| Verses | Verse-ending sound | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1-5** | **-ā + nunation** | *gharqā · nashṭā · sabḥā · sabqā · amrā* |
| **6-14** | **-ira / -ifa / -iʿa** | *al-rājifa · al-rādifa · wājifa · khāshiʿa · al-ḥāfira · nakhira · khāsira · wāḥida · al-sāhira* |
| **15-46** | **-ā** | *Mūsā · ṭuwā · ṭaghā · tazakkā · takhshā · al-kubrā · ʿaṣā · yasʿā · nādā · al-aʿlā · al-ūlā · yakhshā · banāhā · sawwāhā · ḍuḥāhā · daḥāhā · marʿāhā · arsāhā · al-kubrā · saʿā · yarā · ṭaghā · al-dunyā · al-maʾwā · al-hawā · al-maʾwā · mursāhā · dhikrāhā · muntahāhā · yakhshāhā · ḍuḥāhā* |

**And in the third region there is a single break: verse 33** (*li-anʿāmikum*).

**The changes of sound coincide with the changes of content:**

- **1-5:** the series of oaths.
- **6-14:** the scene of the Hour and the objection.
- **15-46:** the narrative, the table, the verdict and the close.

This is the form in this sūra of the fact recorded in the section on al-Nabaʾ and earlier in the sections on al-Takāthur and al-Qāriʿa: in the short Meccan sūras sound and content frequently move together.

**I do not offer this as a claim of miracle**; I record it as an instance of the sūra's weave of sound.

### The verbal links within the sūra

There are **eight places** in the sūra where the same root is used in two different places in two different functions:

| Root | First occurrence | Second occurrence |
|---|---|---|
| **د-ب-ر** | *mudabbirāt* (5) — those that direct the affair | *adbara* (22) — he turned his back |
| **ن-د-و** | *nādāhu rabbuhu* (16) — the Lord called | *fa-nādā* (23) — Pharaoh called |
| **ط-غ-ي** | *innahu ṭaghā* (17) — Pharaoh | *man ṭaghā* (37) — an attribute |
| **س-ع-ي** | *yasʿā* (22) — Pharaoh running | *mā saʿā* (35) — man remembers what he ran for |
| **ك-ب-ر** | *al-āyata'l-kubrā* (20) | *al-ṭāmmata'l-kubrā* (34) |
| **ر-س-و** | *arsāhā* (32) — the mountains were set firm | *mursāhā* (42) — when will the Hour cast anchor |
| **ن-ه-ي** | *naha'l-nafsa* (40) — he restrained the self | *muntahāhā* (44) — its final end |
| **ض-ح-و** | *ḍuḥāhā* (29) — the heaven's forenoon | *ḍuḥāhā* (46) — an evening's forenoon |

**All the shared roots in this table are linguistic data that can be verified.** What is interpretation is the claim that they form an order — and I offer that claim as an observation, not as a ruling.

## Al-Nabaʾ and al-Nāziʿāt — two neighbouring sūras

In the arrangement of the codex sūras 78 and 79 come one after the other, and both treat the same matter: **the resurrection.**

**First a note:** this is **an observation about the arrangement of the codex, not about the order of revelation.** The relation of sūras to one another (*tanāsub al-suwar*) is a recognised subject in exegesis, but some of the scholars have preferred to be cautious about the straining of such links. In this commentary the same note was set down for the trio al-Zalzala–al-ʿĀdiyāt–al-Qāriʿa and for the trio al-Takāthur–al-ʿAṣr–al-Humaza.

### A comparison of the two sūras

| | **al-Nabaʾ (78)** | **al-Nāziʿāt (79)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening** | A question: *"About what are they asking one another?"* | Five oaths — unanswered |
| **The state of the one addressed** | Speaking, disputing | Objecting, mocking |
| **The evidential section** | **A table of the earth** (6-16) | **A table of heaven and earth** (27-33) |
| **Place of the table** | At the beginning of the sūra | In the middle of the sūra |
| **Order of the table** | Earth → heaven | **Heaven → earth** |
| **Is there a narrative** | **No** | **Yes** — Moses and Pharaoh |
| **The sound of the Hour** | **نَفْخ** — a blast (18) | **زَجْرَة** — a cry (13) |
| **Number of quakings** | One blast is mentioned | **Two** — *rājifa* and *rādifa* |
| **The pattern of the two groups** | *inna li'l-ṭāghīna / inna li'l-muttaqīna* | *fa-ammā man … wa ammā man* |
| **The place arrived at** | **مَـَٔاب** — place of return | **ٱلْمَأْوَىٰ** — refuge |
| **The human being's account** | *mā **قَدَّمَتْ** yadāh* — what he sent ahead | *mā **سَعَىٰ*** — what he ran for |
| **Close** | The denier's wish: *"Would that I were dust"* | The measure of a lifetime: *"As though an evening"* |
| **Verse-endings** | A single sound: **-ābā** (6-40) | Three regions of sound |

### Shared words and roots

There are **verifiable** verbal links between the two sūras:

| Root | **al-Nabaʾ** | **al-Nāziʿāt** | Relation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **س-أ-ل** | *yatasāʾalūn* (1) — to one another | *yasʾalūnaka* (42) — to you | **Reciprocal / one-directional** |
| **مَا + a preposition** | *ʿamma* (1) | *fīma* (43) | The same rare pattern, at the two ends |
| **ب-ن-ي** | *banaynā* (12) — the heaven | *banāhā* (27) — the heaven | The same verb, the same object |
| **ش-د-د** | *shidādā* (12) — the heaven | *ashaddu* (27) — a comparison | The same root, the same context |
| **ط-غ-ي** | *al-ṭāghīn* (22) | *ṭaghā* (17, 37) | Attribute / person + attribute |
| **ل-ب-ث** | *lābithīna … aḥqābā* (23) | *lam yalbathū … ʿashiyya* (46) | **Opposite directions** |
| **ن-ذ-ر** | *andharnākum* (40) | *mundhiru* (45) | Verb / noun, at the ends |
| **س-ب-ت / س-ه-ر** | *subātā* (9) — sleep | *al-sāhira* (14) — the sleepless | **Opposite notions** |
| **خ-ر-ج** | *li-nukhrija* (15) | *akhraja* (29, 31) | The same verb, the same function |

**Two of the last rows deserve to be recorded separately:**

**One — sleep.** Al-Nabaʾ counts sleep as a favour (*subāt* = a cutting-off); al-Nāziʿāt names the plain of resurrection *al-sāhira* (the sleepless one). **In this world there is sleep; there there is none.**

**Two — the duration of stay.** Al-Nabaʾ speaks of tarrying *for ages* in the Fire; al-Nāziʿāt of having tarried *less than half a day* in this world. **Two durations, at the ends of two sūras, each the reverse of the other.**

### A comparison of the two tables

The tables of creation in the two sūras do the same work but are built differently:

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/6-16** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/27-33** |
|---|---|---|
| Length | **Eleven verses** | **Seven verses** |
| Mood | **Question** — *"did We not make…?"* | **Statement** — eight perfect verbs |
| Person | **We** — *jaʿalnā, banaynā, anzalnā* | **He** — *banāhā, rafaʿa, daḥāhā* |
| Order | **Earth → heaven → water → plants** | **Heaven → earth → water → pasture → mountains** |
| Is the human being counted | **Yes** (8: *azwājā*) | No — the human being is the one addressed |
| Close | *"Gardens dense with growth"* | *"A provision for you and for your cattle"* |
| Image | **Tent / dwelling** | **Building and ship** |

**The most striking difference is in the order:** al-Nabaʾ starts from the earth and rises to the heaven; al-Nāziʿāt starts from the heaven and descends to the earth.

**And this agrees with the difference between the arguments of the two sūras:**

- **Al-Nabaʾ** begins from what is **under the feet** of the one addressed. It builds its argument from the near towards the far.
- **Al-Nāziʿāt** puts a direct **comparison** to the one addressed: *"are you harder to create, or the heaven?"* It builds its argument from the great to the small.

I record this as my own reading; the orders of the two tables are, however, in the text.

### A comparison of the two closings

**Al-Nabaʾ:** *"…and the denier will say, 'Would that I were dust!'"*

**Al-Nāziʿāt:** *"On the day they see it, it will be as though they had tarried only an evening, or its forenoon."*

| | **al-Nabaʾ 78/40** | **al-Nāziʿāt 79/46** |
|---|---|---|
| Who speaks | **The denier** — his own sentence | **No one** — the narrator describes |
| What is said | A **wish** | A **perception** |
| Mood | *Yā layta* — an impossible wish | *Ka-anna* — a comparison |
| Direction | **To the past** — not to have existed | **To the past** — to have lived a short time |

**Both closings look to the past** — but the one in order to erase it, the other in order to measure it.

I record this as an observation.

## What this says to the present

### First: two forms of the same work

The first two verses of the sūra — *nāziʿāt* and *nāshiṭāt* — give a principle, and this principle works throughout the sūra.

**The same operation takes two different forms according to whom it is done to.**

Both verbs separate a thing from the place it is in. One tears it loose, the other unties it. The result is the same; the **experience** differs.

**The side of this that looks to the present is:** what is met with and how it is met are separate things. A loss, a separation, the coming of an end — these are the same for everyone. How they come differs.

And the sūra says where the difference comes from in verses thirty-seven to forty-one: **from what the person has done.**

### Second: the description of running beyond bounds

The word at the centre of the sūra — *ṭughyān* — was described in the section on al-ʿAlaq: **to pass one's own limit.**

And the sūra shows how this attribute works on a single example. Look at Pharaoh's course:

| Verse | What he does |
|---|---|
| 21 | He denied and disobeyed |
| 22 | **He turned his back, hurrying** |
| 23 | **He gathered** and called out |
| 24 | He made his claim |

**The most notable step is the second: turning one's back and running.**

Running beyond bounds is described here not as a stance but as a **movement**. The person does not stand where he is and refuse; he turns and goes, and **runs.**

**And the third step is important too: gathering a crowd.** The claim is not proclaimed alone; a community is first assembled.

The side of this that looks to the present is as follows, and I record it **as my own reading**: what is needed for a claim to be accepted is not the truth of the claim but **the assembling of a crowd that will confirm it.** Pharaoh does not bring evidence; he gathers a community and proclaims it there.

The Qurʾān records this separately: *"He made light of his people, and they obeyed him"* (al-Zukhruf 43/54 — this verse was also mentioned in the section on al-Qāriʿa).

### Third: the priority of the offer

The pattern in the eighteenth verse — **هَل لَّكَ** ("would you care?") — is the most easily passed over place in the sūra.

The first sentence of the man sent to the most extreme instance of running beyond bounds is an **offer**. Not a command, not a threat, not a proclamation.

And in Ṭāhā 20/44 this is explicitly commanded: *"Speak to him a gentle word."*

**The side of this that looks to the present is a question of method:** however hard the position of the other side, the first move can be made as an offer. And knowing the outcome of the offer does not make the offer unnecessary — the sūra knows that Pharaoh will refuse and records the offer all the same.

### Fourth: awe does not begin with fear

The ordering in the nineteenth verse is worth recording: **guiding → knowing → awe.**

The verse does not say *"fear and then believe"*; it says *"let me guide you to your Lord, **so** that you may feel awe"*. Awe is the result.

And Fāṭir 35/28 confirms this: *"Of His servants, only the learned feel awe of God."*

**The practical consequence is this:** a language that tries to produce fear puts itself in the place of the thing it describes. The sūra's method is the reverse: **it makes known, and leaves the fear to be the result of knowing.**

A rule recorded in this commentary's method (`USLUP.md`) agrees with this too: *"No ornate, preaching, emotionally exploitative language."*

### Fifth: remembering

The verb in the thirty-fifth verse — **يَتَذَكَّرُ** (he remembers) — is this sūra's finest diagnosis.

The verse does not say that a ledger will be shown to the human being on that day. It says he will **remember**. And to remember requires having forgotten.

**That is, the knowledge is already in the person; it is not accessible.**

The side of this that looks to the present is direct: a person's knowledge about his own deeds is more complete than knowledge coming from outside — but it is at the same time the most easily suppressed. We are far better at forgetting what we know about ourselves than at forgetting what we know about others.

**And the object of the verb is worth recording: مَا سَعَىٰ — "what he ran for".**

What will be remembered is not the work done; it is **the thing for whose sake one ran.** That is, what effort was spent on, what was pursued, what time was given to.

This gives a practical measure for today: what a person runs for is read not from what he says but from **where he spends his time.**

### Sixth: "the nearer one"

The fault in the thirty-eighth verse — *"preferring the life of this world"* — is described in the language itself.

As recorded at al-Baqara 2/61, **دُنْيَا** means "the nearer one". And as recorded above, this is an elative — that is, it requires a comparison.

**That is, "to prefer this world" is exactly this: to count nearness as superiority.**

And the side of this that looks to the present is very wide, because this preference is not confined to the question of the hereafter. The same structure works at every scale:

- Preferring what looks good in the near term to what is good in the long term.
- Preferring what can be got now to what will be got later.
- Preferring the result seen at once to the result seen late.

**And note: the verse does not say "taking this world", it says "preferring".** The fault is not in possessing but in the **comparison.** The person sets two things side by side and chooses the nearer.

This arrives at the same place as the finding recorded at al-Baqara 2/61: *"A person may prefer an order he knows to be bad to an order he does not know to be good."*

### Seventh: setting a limit to the self

The fortieth verse is the most concrete command of the sūra — and it is not a command but a description.

**وَنَهَى ٱلنَّفْسَ عَنِ ٱلْهَوَىٰ** — "he restrained the self from craving."

**Three things are worth recording:**

**One — the act is directed at the person himself.** He restrains not another but himself. The sūra makes the measure not intervention in another but intervention in oneself.

**Two — the sense of the root is to set a limit.** As recorded above, *nahy* means "this far". That is, what is done is not an annihilation but a **limitation**. Craving is not abolished; it is limited.

And this is the exact opposite of *ṭughyān*: *ṭughyān* is to pass the limit, *nahy* is to set the limit.

**Three — the word "craving" states the direction.** As recorded in the section on al-Qāriʿa, *hawā* and *hāwiya* are from the same root and the root means **to fall**. That is, what is restrained is the thing that pulls downward.

**The practical consequence is this:** setting a limit to oneself is not a deprivation but a correction of direction. And where the limit is to be set is determined by looking at where the thing limited pulls.

### Eighth: trying to know the unknown

Verses forty-two to forty-four are the support in the text for a rule recorded in this commentary's method (`USLUP.md`).

**The Qurʾān writes into the text that its own prophet does not know the time of the Hour.**

And this has two consequences:

**One — attempts to compute the date of the Hour conflict with the text.** This is something that has been done many times through history and has proved wrong many times. The Qurʾān's own statement closes this door.

**Two — and this is the wider consequence:** to accept that knowledge has a limit is not ignorance. The sūra does not say *"we do not know"*; it says *"its final end belongs to your Lord"* — that is, it **knows** where knowledge stops.

An attitude recorded several times in this commentary comes here from the text itself: to say that an unknown thing is unknown is not a defect of exegesis but a condition of it.

### Ninth: the measure of a lifetime

The last verse of the sūra gives a description of time: **half a day.**

And this is not a belittling; it is a statement of **proportion**. It does not say a lifetime is short; it says that a lifetime will look short **when seen from there.**

**Two things must be kept apart:**

- A lifetime is long while it is being lived. The time one is inside is wide; every day has its own weight.
- A lifetime is short when seen from its end. This is something nearly everyone experiences — one who looks back says how quickly the time went.

**The verse carries the second a step further:** seen from the final point, the measure comes down to **half a day.**

And the picture that emerges when it is read together with al-Nabaʾ 78/23 is this: **half a day on one side, ages on the other.** The two sūras set the two durations side by side and show the proportion.

**The practical consequence of this is not a matter of fear but of measure:** how much weight is to be given to a thing is determined by looking at how long that thing will last. And an estimate of duration made from within the present moment goes wrong.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

- **What the five oaths answer to** (angels / stars / souls / warriors and their horses / the angel of death) is one of the most open disagreements in Qurʾānic exegesis. Five views were given in the form of a table, with their grounds and their weak sides, and **no preference was imposed.** The ground for not imposing a preference was written in three points. That the fifth verse is an indication in favour of the angels and the third in favour of the stars was recorded separately — and it was stated that the two indications point in two different directions. That the attributes may have been **deliberately left open** was offered as a possibility, not as a preference.
- The difference of connecting particle in the five oaths (*wāw* / *fāʾ*) and the distribution of the absolute objects are **verifiable grammatical data**; that they form a "frame" is an order I see.
- The idiom **أَغْرَقَ ٱلنَّازِعُ فِى ٱلْقَوْسِ** was given as a usage recorded by the lexicographers; **it was not ascribed to any particular exegete.**
- The findings that the roots **ن-ش-ط** and **غ-ط-ش** occur in the Qurʾān only in this sūra **were not offered as the result of an exhaustive search.**
- Reading verses 1 and 2 as "the soul of the denier / of the believer" **requires accepting the angels view**, and since that view is not certain no preference was imposed. The reading that the two verbs are "two forms of the same work" holds whichever view is accepted and **belongs to me.**
- That the answer of the oath has been elided is a datum the grammarians accept unanimously; three views were given as to what the answer is. That the elision functions to leave the tension undischarged is **my own reading.**
- What the two quakings are (the two blasts / earth and heaven / death and resurrection) is disputed; the majority's preference was given with its ground.
- The sense of **إِرْجَاف** (to shake with rumour) was recorded as a linguistic datum; **it was not claimed that the use in the verse alludes to this.**
- The grammarians' explanations were given concerning the pronoun in verse 9 (*abṣāruhā*) referring back to the hearts. The reading "the person is reduced to his heart" **belongs to me.**
- Three views were given in the form of a table on the meaning of the word **ٱلْحَافِرَة**. **The first view was preferred, its ground was written, and it was recorded that it is not binding.** It was stated that I cannot give certain information about the expression *al-naqdu ʿinda'l-ḥāfira*, and **it was not used as evidence.** The reading *al-ḥafira* was transmitted, **with no imām's name given.**
- Since the differences of variant reading in the pronunciation of the hamzas in verse 10 **do not change the meaning**, no detail was entered into and **no imām's name was given.**
- The variant **نَخِرَة / نَاخِرَة** was transmitted; **no imām's name was given.** The link set up between the word's sense of sound and verse 13 **belongs to me**, and it **was not claimed** that the verse intends it.
- Three explanations were given for the change of tense between verses 10 and 12 (*yaqūlūna* / *qālū*).
- The ironic reading concerning *khāsira* in verse 12 ("the place where the reckoning is set up is wrong") **belongs to me.**
- Four views were given on the meaning of the word **ٱلسَّاهِرَة**; it was stated that the first three do not exclude one another. The link set up with *subāt* at al-Nabaʾ 78/9 is **my own reading.**
- The three-way table **حَدِيث / نَبَأ / خَبَر** is a linguistic comparison. The reading that "an old event becomes new every time it is told" **belongs to me.**
- Three views were given on the meaning of the word **طُوًى**; its reading with and without nunation was transmitted, **with no imām's name given.**
- The assimilated reading **تَزَّكَّىٰ** was transmitted; **no imām's name was given.**
- The reading resting on the difference of form between *zakkā* (al-Shams 91/9) and *tazakkā* (al-Nāziʿāt 79/18) **belongs to me**; the difference of form is, however, grammatical data.
- The distinction **خَوْف / خَشْيَة** was recorded as a finding made by the lexicographers; **it was not claimed that it works rigidly everywhere.** It **was not claimed** that the presence of the two words together in the sūra is deliberate.
- The reading of the ordering "guiding → knowing → awe" in verse 19 **belongs to me**; the *fāʾ*'s announcing a result, and Fāṭir 35/28, are in the text.
- Which miracle **ٱلْـَٔايَةَ ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ** was is disputed; four views were given and **no preference was imposed.**
- It was stated that the lexicographers differ about the shared sound of **عَصَا** (staff) and **عَصَىٰ** (he disobeyed); **no meaning was drawn from this sharing.**
- The reading concerning the use of the verb **حَشَرَ** of Pharaoh **belongs to me** and its limit was written: the word is not used in Arabic only of the Resurrection and occurs in the Qurʾān in other contexts as well; that is, it is not by itself an allusion.
- The reading resting on the comparison of the two callings (16 and 23) **belongs to me**; the repetition of the word is, however, in the text.
- Two views were given on the construction **نَكَال ٱلْـَٔاخِرَةِ وَٱلْأُولَىٰ**. **The first view was preferred, its ground was written, and it was recorded that it is not binding.** The report of "forty years" on which the second view rests **was not confirmed, since it is not certain**; but the escalation between al-Qaṣaṣ 28/38 and al-Nāziʿāt 79/24 was recorded separately and it was stated that it can be verified by comparing the two verses.
- That the name **ٱللَّه** occurs in the sūra only in verse 25 is **a datum that can be verified**; its interpretation (the claim being met with a name) **belongs to me.**
- The reading **"the earth is egg-shaped"** **was not accepted** and was argued against in four points. That the verse says nothing about the shape of the earth — neither flat nor round — was written plainly; it was stated that **to make the text say something in either direction is a strain.**
- Three views were given on the question of ordering in **بَعْدَ ذَٰلِكَ**. **The second view was preferred, its ground was written, and it was recorded that it is not binding.** It was also stated that claims of the sort "the Qurʾān announced the order of the universe's formation" should not be built out of this ordering.
- On the **function of the mountains** the warning recorded at al-Nabaʾ 78/7 was repeated here: the verse names the function, says nothing about geological structure, and **no hunting for scientific miracles was done.**
- **No connection was set up** between **سَمْك** and **سَمَك** (fish).
- The reading concerning the ascription of night and day to the heaven **belongs to me**; that the pronouns refer back to the heaven is, however, grammatical data.
- **That verse 33 is the only break in the sequence of verse-endings is a datum that can be verified**; the meaning laid upon it **belongs to me**, and it **was not claimed** that the verse intends it.
- That the word **ٱلْكُبْرَىٰ** occurs twice in the sūra is a datum that can be verified; but it was stated that the word is used in other places in the Qurʾān as well and that **the repetition is not by itself proof of intent.**
- Three explanations were given concerning the qualification **لِمَن يَرَىٰ**; the different limitation at al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/91 was recorded separately.
- The comparison of symmetry between al-Qāriʿa 101/6-9 and al-Nāziʿāt 79/37-41 is **my own reading**; the structures of the two texts can, however, be compared directly.
- The reading that the four verbs (*ṭaghā / āthara / khāfa / nahā*) form an "inner-outer" mirror structure **belongs to me.**
- That the verb **ءَاثَرَ** is a virtue at al-Ḥashr 59/9 and a fault at al-Nāziʿāt 79/38 was recorded as an observation about the language.
- Three views were given on the construction **مَقَامَ رَبِّهِ**; it was stated that the first two do not exclude one another.
- Two explanations were given for **ٱلنَّفْسَ**'s coming with the definite article.
- The shared root between **هَاوِيَة** in al-Qāriʿa and **ٱلْهَوَىٰ** in this sūra is **a verifiable linguistic datum**; it had already been set up in the section on al-Qāriʿa.
- The observation about the distribution of the particle **أَيَّانَ** in the Qurʾān **was not offered as a rule**; it was stated plainly that I could not verify whether there is an exception.
- Four views were given on the meaning of the verse **فِيمَ أَنتَ مِن ذِكْرَىٰهَا**. The line of the first and third views was preferred, its ground was written, and **it was recorded that it is not binding.** The fourth view **was recorded as a transmitted report and was not preferred.**
- The two senses of **مُنتَهَىٰ** (the end of knowledge / the end of the event) were given; it was stated that the exegetes record both and that they do not exclude one another.
- Three explanations were given about the relation of the verse **إِنَّمَآ أَنتَ مُنذِرُ مَن يَخْشَىٰهَا** to the other verses (Yāsīn 36/6; al-Muddaththir 74/36; al-Furqān 25/1).
- Two readings were given for the resolution of the expression **عَشِيَّةً أَوْ ضُحَىٰهَا**; it was stated that both arrive at the same result.
- The **table of eight repeated roots within the sūra consists of verifiable linguistic data**; the claim that they form an order was offered as an observation, not as a ruling.
- **All the links set up between al-Nabaʾ and al-Nāziʿāt are observations about the arrangement of the codex**; they are not a claim about the order of revelation. The shared roots in the table of verbal links are verifiable data; the claim that they form an order is a reading.
- The reading that the difference in the order of the two tables (earth→heaven / heaven→earth) agrees with the argumentative structure of the two sūras **belongs to me.**
- The observation about the sūra's arrangement of verse-endings was recorded as a finding about a weave of sound, **not offered as a claim of miracle.**
