# 96. Sūrat al-ʿAlaq

Nineteen verses. It is counted the first sūra of the Qurʾān in the order of revelation — or more exactly, its first five verses. Its place in the codex, however, is nineteenth from the end. That is: the text has put its first words somewhere near the end.

This oddity is worth thinking about in itself. The Qurʾān has not reflected its own history in its own order. The verses that came down first were not placed at the head of the book; al-Fātiḥa and al-Baqara were placed at the head of the book. That the order of revelation and the order of the codex are separate shows that the Qurʾān does not present itself as a diary or a chronicle.

## The name of the sūra

**al-ʿAlaq**, from the word in the second verse. In the sources the sūra is also called **Iqraʾ** and **Iqraʾ bi-smi rabbik** — from its opening word. In the early period sūras were referred to not by official names but by some striking word inside them; we saw the same thing in Sūrat al-Māʿūn.

The two names point to two different places: "Iqraʾ" to the sūra's command, "al-ʿAlaq" to what the human being is made of. The sūra as a whole is built exactly between these two.

## The structure of the sūra: two parts, one argument

The sūra divides into two sections in content and in style:

| Section | Verses | Subject | Sound of the verse-ending |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | 1-5 | The Lord who creates and teaches; the command to read | *-aq*, then *-am/-em* |
| Second | 6-19 | The human being who sees himself as needing nothing; transgression, threat, the command to prostrate | *-ā*, then *-iyah*, at the very end *-ib* |

It is widely said in the tradition that the first five verses were the first revelation and that the remaining fourteen verses came down later. The style of the second section (the address turning into polemic, the language of threat, the presence of a concrete addressee) supports this division.

But the sūra stands in the codex as a single piece, and this joining is not meaningless. The first section tells what was given to the human being: existence and knowledge. The second section tells what the human being did with these two once he had them. The word كَلَّا (*kallā* — no!) between them is the hinge that binds the two sections together.

To see the backbone of the sūra it is enough to look at the places where a single word occurs. **الإنسان** (*al-insān*) occurs three times in the sūra:

- **Verse 2:** *Khalaqa'l-insāna min ʿalaq* — He created the human being from something that hangs on and clings.
- **Verse 5:** *ʿAllama'l-insāna mā lam yaʿlam* — He taught the human being what he did not know.
- **Verse 6:** *Inna'l-insāna la-yaṭghā* — The human being does indeed transgress.

Set the three sentences side by side and the whole claim of the sūra comes out: **the one who has received both his existence and his knowledge from another supposes himself to be in need of no one.** The rest of the sūra is the unfolding of this finding.

## The basmala

The word-by-word analysis of the basmala was done in Sūrat al-Fātiḥa; I do not repeat it here. Only this note is needed: the basmala begins with *ism Allāh*, and the first verse of this sūra with *bi-smi rabbik*. That is, the first sentence of the revelation and the formula at the head of every sūra carry the same structure. The debate over the functions of the letter *bāʾ* was also treated there and holds here word for word; below I shall touch only on the side of it that is particular to this verse.

## Historical framework: the first encounter

In speaking about the first verses of this sūra, one has to be honest about what we have in hand.

**The transmitted framework is this.** Muḥammad, in his fortieth year or thereabouts, would withdraw to the cave of Ḥirāʾ on Mount Nūr to the northeast of Mecca and stay there for days at a time (*taḥannuth*). One night an angel comes to him and says "Iqraʾ". He answers, "I am not one who reads" (*mā anā bi-qāriʾ*). The angel presses him, lets him go, says "Iqraʾ" again; this is repeated three times. On the third the first five verses of the sūra are recited. Muḥammad returns home trembling and says to his wife Khadīja, "Cover me." Khadīja calms him and takes him to her cousin Waraqa b. Nawfal, an old man who had embraced Christianity and knew the Hebrew script. After hearing what is recounted, Waraqa says that this is the *nāmūs* that came to Moses (Mūsā), and gives notice that his people will drive him out. After a while the revelation ceases (*fatrat al-waḥy*), and then begins again. This pause was treated in the discussion of Sūrat al-Ḍuḥā (`093-duha.md`); that the pause occurred is widely accepted, while its length and its details rest on report.

**The standing of this report.** The core of the account is among the first hadiths of al-Bukhārī's *Ṣaḥīḥ* and is transmitted in Muslim, from ʿĀʾisha. In respect of its chain, then, it stands in the soundest part of the hadith corpus. But several points need stating:

1. **ʿĀʾisha had not yet been born at the time of the event.** She did not see directly what she recounts; it was recounted to her. In the science of hadith this case, called the *mursal* of a Companion, is generally accepted, because it is known from whom the Companion took it; but one has to know that it is not a report coming from an eyewitness to the event.
2. **There are variants in the details.** How many times the pressing occurred, where the angel appeared, what happened in the days that followed, Waraqa's exact words — these come down differently in different lines of transmission.
3. **The expression "*mā anā bi-qāriʾ*" is not univocal.** It has two readings, and both are usages the lexicographers accept: (a) *mā* as the particle of negation → "I am not one who reads / who can read." (b) *mā* as the interrogative particle → "What shall I read?" The second reading bears directly on the matter of "the command has no object" that we shall see in a moment.

So: that the event took place and that the first verses came with an encounter of this kind may be counted established by sound transmission. The details of the scene, however, are report, and are to be read as report. To make this distinction is not to diminish the transmitted material; it is to say correctly what it is.

**The dispute over the order of revelation.** In the widespread lists the first sūra to come down is al-ʿAlaq. But other views on "the first to come down" are also transmitted:

| View | Ground | Attempt at reconciliation |
|---|---|---|
| al-ʿAlaq 1-5 | The Ḥirāʾ report in al-Bukhārī and Muslim | — |
| al-Muddaththir | The report transmitted from Jābir b. ʿAbd Allāh | al-Muddaththir is the first sūra to come down **after** the period of the pause; al-ʿAlaq is first absolutely |
| al-Fātiḥa | Certain *mursal* reports | al-Fātiḥa may be the first sūra to come down **in its entirety**; al-ʿAlaq did not come down all at once |

Attempts at reconciliation are made in the classical sources and are reasonable; but the reconciliation is itself an inference, not a transmitted report. The lists of the order of revelation likewise rest on reports whose chains vary. I transmit this not as a certainty but as the most widespread acceptance.

## 96/1 — ٱقْرَأْ بِٱسْمِ رَبِّكَ ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ

*Iqraʾ bi-smi rabbika'lladhī khalaq*
"Read in the name of your Lord who created."

### ٱقْرَأْ — the two wings of the root

The first word of the revelation is a command, and the sense of this command is not as one-directional as is supposed.

**Root: ق ر أ.** The Arabic philologists record two basic senses in this root:

**1. To gather, to bring together, to collect.** This is what is given as the root's original (concrete) sense. In the classical dictionaries the proof offered for it is the word *qarʾ/qurʾ*: a woman's period of menstruation or of purity. The Qurʾān uses this word: *"Divorced women wait three qurʾ"* (al-Baqara 2/228). This sense of the word is explained by the womb's gathering something in itself and then releasing it. There is also an Arab expression transmitted in the dictionaries: *mā qaraʾati'n-nāqatu salan qaṭṭu* — "this she-camel has never gathered a young", that is, has never conceived. There is nothing here of reading; there is only gathering.

**2. To read, to voice aloud.** The sense of the word that we know. Most of the lexicographers derive this from the first: to read is to bring letters and words together in the mouth. One who gives voice to a written text gathers scattered marks and makes of them a meaningful whole.

The two senses are not cut off from one another; the second is a particular case of the first. But there is a difference in respect of the weight of the command: if you say "gather", the material is outside and the work is the gatherer's; if you say "read", the material already exists as a text.

**The name "Qurʾān" also comes from this root** — and this puts the word at the centre of the discussion. The chief views enumerated about this name in the works on the sciences of the Qurʾān are these:

| View | Explanation |
|---|---|
| A verbal noun from the root ق ر أ | In the sense of "reading / what is read". The view of the majority. |
| From the root ق ر أ, with the sense "to gather" | That which gathers in itself the sūras, the verses, the core of the earlier books. |
| An underived proper name, without hamza | Ascribed to al-Shāfiʿī; on his account it is read *Qurān* and is not derived. |
| From the root ق ر ن | Ascribed to Abū'l-Ḥasan al-Ashʿarī: the joining of verses to one another. |
| From the word قرائن | Ascribed to al-Farrāʾ: the verses' confirming one another, each standing as an indication of the other. |

These views are enumerated together in the classical sources; I issue no decisive ruling as to which is correct. But this much can be established: the root of the first command and the root of the book's name are the same. **The book took its name from the first command given to it.** The command came first, the name formed afterwards.

### Saying "read" when there is no text to read

The real matter here is this: at the moment the command is given there is no text to read. The Qurʾān does not yet exist — this is its first sentence. There is no page held in the hand, no tablet memorised, no book given beforehand.

This situation is handled along different paths in the classical commentaries, and several readings stand out:

**First reading — the command is for what is about to be said.** What is meant by "read" is the words the angel will recite immediately afterwards. That is, there is an object, but it comes into being at the same moment as the command. The threefold repetition in the report fits this reading: the command is repeated, the resistance is broken, and then the text is given.

**Second reading — the command is the opening of a capacity.** The answer *"I am not one who reads"* is an excuse; the command voids that excuse. Accordingly the third verse will say *"Your Lord is the most generous"*, and the fifth *"He taught the human being what he did not know."* That is, the answer is: if you cannot read, there is One who makes you read.

**Third reading — the "gathering" sense of the root is taken as basic.** On this reading the command is not "give voice to a text placed before you" but **"gather it in yourself, commit it to memory, take it in."** The absence of an object then ceases to be a lack: what is to be gathered has not yet come, it will come; the command has been given at the outset.

I do not present these three as alternatives to one another. The text itself does not state the object, and its not stating it is a choice. The dropping of the object (*ḥadhf*, the suppression of an element) is a known technique in Arabic, and its function is **generality**: when the object is not stated, the verb stays open in such a way as to take in every object that suits the subject. We handled the absence of an object for the verb *yurāʾūna* in the sixth verse of Sūrat al-Māʿūn within the same frame.

One more point: the command is a **verb**, not a noun. It did not begin with "book" or "revelation"; it began with an act. The first word of the revelation asks the human being to do something. Religion opens not as a package of information but as a command — and the first command is not worship but reading.

### بِٱسْمِ — "in the name of"

The debate over the functions of the letter *bāʾ* was conducted in al-Fātiḥa in the discussion of the basmala; the three possibilities there (adhesion, asking for help, accompaniment) hold here as well. I do not repeat it here. There are two points particular to this verse:

**First, the possibility that the *bāʾ* is pleonastic (*zāʾida*).** Some lexicographers say that the *bāʾ* here comes without adding a sense, and that the original of the sentence is *iqra'sma rabbik* — "read the name of your Lord". On this reading the command has been found an object: what is to be read is the name of the Lord. The sentence then turns into a command to remembrance.

| Reading | Sense | Its consequence |
|---|---|---|
| *Bāʾ* of **asking for help / accompaniment** | "Read in the name of your Lord" | The object is not stated; the act of reading is bound to His name |
| *Bāʾ* **pleonastic** | "Read the name of your Lord" | The object has been found: what is to be read is the name |

The majority prefer the first reading; the second is also linguistically defensible and is mentioned in the classical sources. To my mind the first suits the context better, because the sūra goes on to treat reading as a matter of knowledge, not as a formula of remembrance. This is a preference, and it is not binding.

**Second, that it has been brought forward.** The sentence is built as *iqraʾ bi-smi rabbik*; that is, the phrase "in the name of" comes immediately adjacent to the verb. In Arabic a fronted element conveys restriction. In the discussion of the basmala we noted that this is a sentence set against the practice in the Age of Ignorance of slaughtering and beginning "in the name of the idols". The same restriction is at work here: no work at all, reading included, can be done under another name.

The modern counterpart of this is not immediately visible, but it exists. Reading — acquiring knowledge — is today counted a "neutral" work that answers to itself alone. The verse does not reject this; it does not forbid reading, it refuses to leave it nameless. An activity of knowledge always has a name: for what, for whom, by what measure. When it is not stated, it does not disappear; it merely becomes invisible.

### رَبِّكَ — why "Lord" and not "God"

The divine name occurring in the first sentence of the revelation is not **God** but **Lord**. This is not an accident.

The root analysis of the word was done in al-Fātiḥa: the root **ر ب ب** carries both the sense of *owning* and the sense of *nurturing*; ownership here is not a right of use but a responsibility to bring up. The Lord is the one who makes what He owns grow, who feeds it, who brings it to completion. I rely on that treatment; I do not repeat it here.

The question here is this: why was this name chosen?

**First, in respect of the one addressed.** "God" is a proper name, and the one addressed need not have a relationship with it established beforehand. "Lord", however, is a name that conveys a relationship and is always attached to some party. Accordingly it comes here too not bare but in a construct: *rabbi-ka* — **your** Lord. What is established in the first sentence is not a definition but a bond.

**Second, its fit with the command.** The command is "read"; that is, a command bearing on learning, on being brought up, on the opening of a capacity. And the name that gives it is the name of the one who nurtures. The Lord is the one who does not leave what He owns where it is — and reading is exactly the human being's not being left where he is. The command and the root of the name are doing the same work.

**Third, the ordering.** The sūra sets up this arrangement: first *Lord* (verse 1), then *al-Akram* (verse 3), then *God* (verse 14). That is, the name moves from relationship towards the proper name. There was a similar ordering in al-Fātiḥa: there the names followed the order of an introduction. So too here: first the one who brings you up, then the highest degree of generosity, then — when transgression is described — God. That the last name comes where the threat is, is meaningful.

### ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ — a verb whose object is not stated

The first verse of the sūra ends thus: *"…of your Lord who created."* What He created is not stated.

In Arabic this is the technique of *ḥadhf* we touched on under the previous heading, and its function here is very plain: **when a verb is left absolute it takes in all objects.** Had it said "He created such-and-such", creation would have been limited to that object. When it is not stated the limit falls away: the one who created — whatever He created, whatever there is.

Then the second verse comes and, repeating the verb, gives the object: *khalaqa'l-insāna min ʿalaq.* So the ordering is this:

1. **Absolute creation** — the scope unlimited.
2. **Particular creation** — the scope narrowing to a single being: the human.

This narrowing shows where the sūra's attention is being turned. The whole cosmos is passed over in one sentence, and then the camera comes down onto the human being. And the rest of the sūra is about the human being from beginning to end: where he came from, what he knows, what he does, where he will return.

One further nicety: in the first verse the attribute "who created" is named together with the Lord. When the two names come side by side, this is completed: the one who **brought you into being** and the one who **brings you up** are the same. Bringing into being is the work of an instant; bringing up is continuous. The sentence binds the two to a single subject.

### The verse-ending and the sound

The first two verses end with the sound **qāf**: *khalaq / ʿalaq*. This is a hard, clipped sound produced at the back of the throat. The next three verses end with **mīm**: *akram / qalam / yaʿlam* — a soft sound closing at the lips.

The change of sound corresponds to the change of subject: creation with hard sounds, teaching with soft ones. I record this not as a proof but as an observation about the texture of the text.

## 96/2 — خَلَقَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مِنْ عَلَقٍ

*Khalaqa'l-insāna min ʿalaq*
"He created the human being from *ʿalaq*."

### ٱلْإِنسَٰن — the human being

There are two old views about its root: **أ ن س** (familiarity, growing used to, forming closeness) or **ن س ي** (to forget). Both are defended in the classical sources.

On the first derivation the human being is "the one who grows used, who forms closeness" — the being who cannot live alone, who forms bonds. On the second he is "the one who forgets". The second derivation is the more strained grammatically, but in respect of sense it fits this sūra strangely well: the sūra is about to describe a human being who forgets what has been given him and supposes himself self-sufficient.

The word comes here **with the definite article** (*al-insān*). This conveys the genus: not a particular person but the being called human. The sūra is describing not a person but a kind.

### عَلَق — the senses of the root

The word that gives the sūra its name, and the place where the greatest care is required.

**Root: ع ل ق.** The central sense of the root is this: **to hang on to something, to hold fast, to stick, to dangle from it.** Its derivatives display this sense on every side:

- **علق (*ʿaliqa*)** — he held on, he caught on, he stayed suspended.
- **علاقة (*ʿalāqa*)** — bond, relation. English covers the same ground with *attachment*: one word for a physical fastening and for the tie between two parties — exactly this holding-on established between two things.
- **تعلّق (*taʿalluq*)** — to be attached to something, to be caught on it.
- **علاّقة (*ʿallāqa*)** — a hanger, the hook on which a thing is hung.
- **علق (*ʿalaq*)** — the leech. The creature that lives in water, sticks to the skin and does not let go. It takes its name from its sticking.
- **علقة (*ʿalaqa*)** — thick, clotted blood that has stuck together.
- **علق (*ʿilq*)** — something precious, the thing a person's heart is caught on.

As is plain, all of them turn about a single image: **what hangs, what holds on, what sticks.** This is why both the leech and the clot take the name — the leech sticks, and the clot, instead of flowing, gathers and sticks.

The word in the verse is a noun conveying **plurality/genus** (its singular is *ʿalaqa*) and comes **indefinite**: *min ʿalaq* — "from an *ʿalaq*", "from something of the kind *ʿalaq*". The indefiniteness here conveys diminution and unimportance: a thing not even named, not even defined.

### The work the verse does: a contrast

The verse's function in the context must not be missed. The verse is there not to give a piece of biological information but to set up a **contrast**.

The preceding verse said "Read in the name of your Lord" — that is, it commanded the human being to the highest of works, knowledge and reading. Immediately afterwards this verse comes and states the human being's point of departure: something sticky, hanging, nameless.

The Qurʾān sets up this contrast elsewhere too. *al-Ṭāriq 86/5-7*: *"Let the human being look at what he was created from."* *ʿAbasa 80/17-19*: *"Perish the human being, how ungrateful he is! From what thing did He create him? He created him from a drop (*nuṭfa*) and gave him measure."* The pattern is always the same: the human being who makes himself great is reminded of the material he came from.

So the verse is saying: **the being who is to read was once a thing that could do nothing but hold on.** The diagnosis in the sixth verse of the sūra (the human being supposes himself self-sufficient) sits exactly on top of this verse. The first answer to be given to one who supposes himself self-sufficient is where he came from.

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

The same word occurs in the places where the Qurʾān enumerates the stages of creation:

- *al-Muʾminūn 23/12-14*: *"We created the human being from an extract of clay. Then We made him a drop in a secure place. Then We made the drop an ʿalaqa, and We made the ʿalaqa a lump of chewed flesh (mudgha)…"*
- *al-Ḥajj 22/5*: *"We created you from dust, then from a drop, then from an ʿalaqa, then from a lump of flesh, formed and unformed…"*
- The same sequence occurs in *Ghāfir 40/67* and in *al-Qiyāma 75/37-38*.

In these verses *ʿalaqa* is the name of a stage. In Sūrat al-ʿAlaq there is no sequence of stages; the raw material of the human being is named in a single word. The context is different: there a process is being recounted, here a contrast is being set up.

### This word and modern embryology — a careful section

This word has been much discussed over the last century, and most of the discussion has not gone soundly. Here one has to speak plainly.

**The claim is this:** *ʿalaqa* describes the phase in which the fertilised ovum attaches itself to the wall of the womb; the word's sense of "hanging / sticking" fits that attachment exactly; therefore the verse announced in advance a fact that can be seen under a microscope.

**What has to be said about this claim:**

**1. The sense of the word really is "sticking / hanging".** This is not a modern imputation; it is the root's original sense in Arabic, and the classical dictionaries record it together with pre-Islamic usages. To reduce this sense of the word would also be a distortion. The verse names the human being's material of origin "something that holds on"; this is the plain sense of the word.

**2. But the overlap of a word's semantic field with a scientific description is not proof.** "Sticking" is a wide notion. The leech sticks, the clot sticks, mud sticks. This width allows the word to fit many things — and the more things an expression fits, the less its fitting one particular thing proves.

**3. What did the first addressees understand from this word?** In all likelihood the leech and clotted blood. That is what was seen in cases of miscarriage, and that was the everyday counterpart of the word. To suppose that a text said something unintelligible to its first addressees runs against the logic of address itself.

**4. A matter of method.** To tie the truth of a verse to a current scientific description is to make that verse a partner in the fate of that description. Scientific descriptions change, become more detailed, are sometimes abandoned. If you nail a verse to today's embryology textbook, then when the textbook changes the question arises where you are to put the verse. This is to make the text fragile in the very act of wishing to defend it.

**5. The verse's own purpose is not to give information.** The context is plain: the sūra is reminding the human being who sees himself as self-sufficient what he came from. The verse is a sentence of lowering, not a description. To turn the verse into a lesson in embryology renders its real work invisible.

**To sum up:** the word's sense of "hanging, holding on, sticking" is given and is not reduced; the reader who notices that this calls to mind the phase of attachment in modern embryology may count that association, for his own part, an occasion for thought; but this cannot be presented as the verse's claim and cannot be turned into a proof of miracle. The rule of the method (`USLUP.md`) that "no hunting for scientific miracles is done" exists precisely to preserve this distinction.

### What this says to the present

At the centre of the word is **holding on**, and this is a notion with a side that looks out over the whole sūra.

The human being's first state is holding on to something. This holding-on later changes form but does not end: the infant holds on to the mother, the child to the family, the adult to work, to a relationship, to a group, to a name. And the transgression to be described in the sixth verse of the sūra is exactly the state of **supposing oneself not to be holding on**. It will say: *"He saw himself as needing nothing."*

So at the outset the sūra puts a name to the human being: the one who holds on. Then it puts a name to the illness: to suppose one is not holding on. The distance between the two is the whole sūra.

## 96/3 — ٱقْرَأْ وَرَبُّكَ ٱلْأَكْرَمُ

*Iqraʾ wa rabbuka'l-akram*
"Read! Your Lord is the most generous."

### The repetition of the command

The command comes a second time. Several explanations of the reason for the repetition are given in the classical commentaries:

**1. Emphasis (*taʾkīd*).** The repetition of the same command strengthens the command in Arabic and conveys urgency.

**2. A pointer to two distinct readings.** The first for learning, the second for conveying. That is: first you receive, then you pass on.

**3. Corresponding to the threefold repetition in the report.** The angel said "iqraʾ" three times and the Prophet resisted twice; the command's occurring twice in the text is connected with that scene.

**4. An answer to the excuse.** The first command was given, and "I cannot read" was said; the second command comes on top of that answer, and immediately afterwards the ground is mentioned: *your Lord is al-Akram.*

The fourth explanation is the one most in accord with the verse's own word order, and, as we shall see below, the structure of the sentence supports it.

### Word order — the nominal sentence after the command

The verse consists of two parts, and the bond between them is striking: *Iqraʾ* (a verbal sentence, a command) — *wa rabbuka'l-akram* (a nominal sentence, a statement).

The **wāw** here is mostly read by the grammarians as the *wāw* of circumstance or the *wāw* of resumption. Read as the *wāw* of circumstance, the sense is: *"Read — your Lord being the most generous"*, that is, "read while the generosity that will enable your reading stands in place."

On this reading the sentence sets a command and a guarantee side by side. The command asks something of the human being; the nominal sentence that comes right after it says from where that thing will be supplied. And that it is a nominal sentence matters — as we treated in the discussions of al-Fātiḥa and al-Ikhlāṣ, a nominal sentence in Arabic conveys **fixity and permanence**. It is not that your Lord *became* or *becomes* al-Akram; He is so, and always.

### ٱلْأَكْرَم — al-akram

**Root: ك ر م.** The semantic field of the root is wide, and no single English word carries it:

- **كرم (*karam*)** — generosity, but not only generosity with goods: nobility, loftiness, distance from what is base.
- **كريم (*karīm*)** — at once generous, honourable and precious. The Qurʾān gives this attribute to very different things: *"qurʾānun karīm"* (al-Wāqiʿa 56/77), *"al-ʿarshi'l-karīm"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/116), *"rizqun karīm"* (al-Anfāl 8/74).
- **كرم (*karm*)** — the vine, the grape-arbour. The Arabs gave it this name because they counted it the most generous of plants; what it gives is abundant and without return.
- **مكرمة (*makruma*)** — an honourable deed, an ennobling act.

At the centre of the word lies this: **to give abundantly, without expecting a return, without keeping account.** The one who is *karīm* does not throw his gift in your face and does not expect gratitude for what he gives. Accordingly the Qurʾān says, in the mouth of Solomon: *"My Lord is Ghanī, Karīm"* (al-Naml 27/40) — the one who is in need of nothing and who gives; the two side by side.

**The pattern: the elative (*ism al-tafḍīl*).** *Akram* is on the *afʿal* pattern, and this pattern in Arabic normally conveys comparison: "more generous". From this a question arises: **more generous than whom?**

The lexicographers' answer is this: in Arabic the elative does not always convey comparison. When the second party is not stated, the pattern conveys **absolute superiority** — "the highest point of generosity", with no comparable second. There are parallels to this in the Qurʾān: *al-Aʿlā* (the Most High — al-Aʿlā 87/1), *aḥkam al-ḥākimīn* (al-Tīn 95/8), *khayr al-fāṣilīn*. In these too the question "than whom" is not asked.

In Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ it was said *"and there is none equal to Him"*. The absolute elative here comes out at the same place: no comparison is being made, because there is no party to compare with.

**Why this name, exactly here?** Because the command is a hard command and the one addressed has said "I cannot." The answer is not a compulsion but an introduction: *He is the one who gives, and He keeps no account in giving.* If you cannot read, this is no obstacle; because the capacity to read is itself among the things He will give. Accordingly the next verse will say exactly this.

### The sūra's inner bond: al-Akram and *istighnāʾ*

Here there is a contrast that binds the two halves of the sūra together, and it must be noted already.

**Al-Akram** is the giving party. **Istighnāʾ** (verse 7) means saying "I have need of no one." That is, in the first half of the sūra a Lord who gives without end is described; in the second half a human being who is fed by what is given and yet does not reckon with the giver.

When generosity and *istighnāʾ* are set face to face, the picture that emerges is this: **the person who does not see himself as needy is in fact the person who does not see the generosity.** One who supposes he did not receive a thing as a gift does not look to the one who gave it. The sūra describes the source of transgression here: supposing it was deserved.

## 96/4 — ٱلَّذِى عَلَّمَ بِٱلْقَلَمِ

*Alladhī ʿallama bi'l-qalam*
"He who taught by the pen."

### عَلَّمَ — He taught

**Root: ع ل م.** To know. *ʿIlm*, *ʿālim*, *maʿlūm*, *ʿalāma* (a mark — that by which a thing is made known) all come from this root. In treating the word *ʿālamīn* in al-Fātiḥa we touched on this root's sense of "that by which something is known".

The verb is in **Form II** (*tafʿīl*): *ʿallama*. This form conveys transitivity and acting upon — "he brought it about that he knew, he caused him to acquire knowledge." That is, knowledge is presented here not as something the human being produced on his own but as **something done to him**.

This is the verse's most basic claim, and it will be stated openly in the fifth verse. For the moment let us note the weight the pattern carries: the subject of the verb is God, its object the human being. The human being stands here in the passive, not the active position.

### ٱلْقَلَم — the pen

**Root: ق ل م.** The root's original sense is **to cut, to whittle, to take off the ends**. Its derivatives:

- **قلامة (*qulāma*)** — a piece cut off and thrown away from something, a paring.
- **تقليم (*taqlīm*)** — the cutting of nails. It is still used today.
- **قلم (*qalam*)** — a reed whose tip has been cut to make it ready for writing.

So the pen is not "the thing that writes"; it is **"the thing that has been whittled"**. It takes its name not from its function but from its making. A piece of reed becomes a pen when its tip is cut at a slanting angle. The word carries in itself how the instrument came to be.

This detail may look unimportant, but it says something: the pen is not found in nature, it is made. Writing is not something the human being possesses of himself; it is a technology he has produced. And the verse says that the one who taught this technology too is God.

**The pen in the Qurʾān.** The word occurs in several places, and not each time within the same frame:

- *al-Qalam 68/1*: *"Nūn. By the pen and what they write."* — the Qurʾān swears by an instrument. In the widespread lists of the order of revelation this sūra comes immediately after al-ʿAlaq. That is, both of the first two texts of revelation speak of the pen. This is too narrow an interval to be counted a coincidence.
- *Āl ʿImrān 3/44*: *"When they cast their pens as to who should take charge of Mary…"* — here the pen is the rod used in casting lots. The root's sense of "a cut rod" appears here in its bare state.
- *Luqmān 31/27*: *"If the trees on earth were pens and the sea, with seven seas added after it, were ink, the words of God would not run out."* — here the pen is used as a measure of inexhaustibility.

### بِٱلْقَلَمِ — "by the pen" — two readings

The letter *bāʾ* here conveys instrument. But the instrument of what?

| Reading | Sense | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **What is taught is writing** | "He taught the human being the pen (writing)" | The subject of the teaching is writing itself |
| **The means of teaching is the pen** | "He taught the human being by means of the pen" | Knowledge reaches the human being by way of writing |

Both are mentioned in the classical sources. The second reading looks the better suited to the context, because the next verse will leave the subject of the teaching general: *"He taught him what he did not know."* That is, verse 4 states the **means**, verse 5 the **scope**. This is a preference, and it is not binding.

### That the first revelation to come down to an unlettered society speaks of the pen

This is one of the most striking places in the sūra and is worth dwelling on.

**The historical picture.** At the beginning of the seventh century there was writing in Mecca. The Arabic alphabet was in use; commercial records were kept and agreements were written down — the page of the boycott, which is transmitted to have hung in the Kaʿba, is cited as one example of this. But literacy was not widespread. Knowledge was transmitted predominantly **by word of mouth**: poetry was memorised, genealogies were drilled in, tribal history was recounted. Memory was the library.

The Qurʾān says openly that the Prophet came from within this order: *"You were not reciting any book before this, nor writing it with your right hand"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/48). And it names him *al-nabī al-ummī*, the unlettered prophet (al-Aʿrāf 7/157-158).

**Now see the oddity.** Someone who does not know how to read is told "read"; and as the ground for it, the fact that writing was taught to the human being is adduced.

This does several things at once:

**1. It takes the ground of the command out of personal capacity.** It does not say "you can read"; it says "He is the one who teaches." The capacity is sought not in the one addressed but in the one who teaches.

**2. It gives notice at the outset that what is coming will be lasting.** In an oral culture a saying lives as long as those who have memorised it live. Writing changes this: knowledge becomes independent of the lifespan of the one who knows. That the first revelation speaks of the pen shows that the text coming down was conceived not as an oral tradition but as **a text that would be put on record**. And so it was.

**3. It takes knowledge out of the monopoly of a single person.** Memorisation produces dependence on the one who memorises; writing loosens that dependence. A written text can be copied, compared, checked.

**A connection with the social sciences.** How the passage from oral to written culture changes the manner of thinking became a separate field of study in the twentieth century (the work of Jack Goody and Walter Ong on this subject is widely known). The common finding of these studies is this: writing is not merely a means of record; it makes **accumulating, looking back, comparing and criticising** possible. In oral transmission a saying changes a little with each repetition and the change is not noticed, because there is no fixed text to compare it with. Writing establishes that fixity.

I do not tie this to the verse as a miracle. I say only this: in the first sentences of a text that takes knowledge as its subject, the mention of the technology by which knowledge is transmitted is an apt choice.

**4. It is consistent with the Qurʾān's own practice.** The Qurʾān devotes its longest verse (al-Baqara 2/282) to the recording of debts in writing. The command there is plain: *"Write it down."* That is, the pen is necessary not only for revelation but for everyday justice. The writing down of a debt is a precaution taken against the fallibility of memory — and it is a precaution that protects the weaker party, because in an oral dispute the one who loses is generally the one with the smaller voice.

### What this says to the present

The verse speaks of an instrument and counts the instrument a favour. This is a frame worth dwelling on.

The means of transmitting knowledge have changed from that day to this: the reed pen, the printing press, the telegraph, the archive, the screen, the network. Each time the same thing happened — the portability and storability of knowledge increased, and each time this increase enlarged both the possibility and the risk. Writing makes both the true and the false lasting. The record carries both justice and slander.

What the verse says is not to sanctify these instruments but **to recall their source.** The existence of the instrument can be recounted as a success story ("the human being invented writing") or as a trust ("it was taught to the human being"). The difference between the two comes out in what is done with the instrument: one who counts it his own achievement gives no account; one who counts it a trust does.

## 96/5 — عَلَّمَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ مَا لَمْ يَعْلَمْ

*ʿAllama'l-insāna mā lam yaʿlam*
"He taught the human being what he did not know."

### The construction of the sentence

The verse uses the same verb (*ʿallama*) once more, but this time not with a *bāʾ*, and instead with two direct objects: *al-insān* (to whom) and *mā lam yaʿlam* (what).

And the second object is a negative clause: **"the thing he did not know."** That is, the content of what is taught is not stated; only that the human being **did not previously know** it.

This is the same technique as the leaving of the verb "created" without an object in the first verse. When the object is left indefinite the scope becomes unlimited: whatever the human being knows, all of it. Language, name, number, art, agriculture, law, writing — all are inside this sentence.

That the verb comes in the **perfect** is meaningful too: the teaching is presented as a finished, completed work. But because its content is left open, the scope keeps widening.

### That knowledge is something taught

This verse is the conceptual centre of the sūra, and it claims this: **the human being's knowledge is not from himself.**

At first sight this claim may seem odd. The human being learns; he works, researches, tries, errs, corrects. Knowledge looks like the return on effort spent.

The verse does not deny this. What it says stands at a more basic level: **the capacity for learning is itself given.** For a person to be able to learn he must first have memory, attention, language, curiosity, the faculty of inference. He acquired none of these himself. If knowledge is a building, the human being raises the storeys; but he did not pour the foundation.

The Qurʾān sets this up elsewhere too:

- *al-Naḥl 16/78*: *"God brought you out of your mothers' wombs knowing nothing; and He gave you hearing, sight and hearts."* The ordering is plain: first the not-knowing, then the giving of the instruments.
- *al-Raḥmān 55/1-4*: *"al-Raḥmān. He taught the Qurʾān. He created the human being. He taught him clear speech."* The order here is striking: teaching is mentioned **before** creating. In Sūrat al-ʿAlaq the order is the reverse — first creation (1-2), then teaching (4-5). The two sūras give the same two verbs in different orders; both are apt in their own context.

### The link with al-Baqara 2/31-32

The nearest relative of this verse is the story of Adam in Sūrat al-Baqara, and that discussion was treated in `002-bakara.md`. Here I establish the link and rely on that treatment.

What was there is this: God taught Adam all the names; the angels could not know them and said: *"Glory be to You, we have no knowledge except what You have taught us"* (al-Baqara 2/32).

This sentence says two things at once:

**1. Knowledge is something given.** The angels say it by way of confession. And note that the confession comes not as a declaration of deficiency but inside a sentence of **tasbīḥ** (declaring God free of imperfection). That is, to say "our knowledge is given" is for them no humiliation; it is a correct finding.

**2. The human being's distinction too lies in knowledge.** Adam's standing over against the angels was established not by worship but by knowing the names.

Now set the two texts side by side:

| | al-Baqara 2/31-32 | al-ʿAlaq 96/5 |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *ʿallama* (he taught) | *ʿallama* (he taught) |
| The one taught | Adam | the human being |
| Subject matter | all the names | what he did not know |
| Outcome | the human being's standing is established by knowledge | knowledge comes to be given to the human being |

The same verb, the same structure. The Qurʾān describes the human being's place in two separate places with the same word.

But here there is a critical point, and the rest of the sūra is built on it: **knowledge is a privilege, and like every privilege it is open to being misunderstood.** The angels said "we have no knowledge except what we have been taught" — that is, they kept the source of their knowledge in the right place. The human being may not do this. He may count knowledge his own property, count it his own achievement, and once he does, he begins to see himself as self-sufficient.

The sixth verse of the sūra begins exactly here.

### The balance sheet of the sūra's first half

Let us gather what the five verses say:

1. Read — in the name of your Lord; He is the one who created.
2. He created you from something that hangs on and clings.
3. Read — your Lord is the highest degree of generosity.
4. He taught by the pen.
5. He taught the human being what he did not know.

That is, everything the human being has in hand has been enumerated: his **existence** (he was created), his **material** (*ʿalaq*), his **knowledge** (it was taught), his **instruments** (the pen). All four came from outside. The first revelation shows the human being nothing at all that belongs to him.

And in spite of this the tone is soft: there is a command, but no threat; there is a lowering (*ʿalaq*), but no humiliation; the Lord is named *al-Akram*. In five verses there is not one mention of hell, of punishment, or of the reckoning.

This picture will change all at once in the sixth verse.

## 96/6 — كَلَّآ إِنَّ ٱلْإِنسَٰنَ لَيَطْغَىٰٓ

*Kallā inna'l-insāna la-yaṭghā*
"No! The human being does indeed transgress."

### كَلَّا — kallā

The hinge word of the sūra. It occurs three times: in verses 6, 15 and 19. The second half of the sūra opens with this word, the threat begins with this word, and the last verse comes again with this word.

**Its function.** The lexicographers differ over the sense of this word:

| View | Explanation | To whom it is ascribed |
|---|---|---|
| Only **rejection and deterrence** (*radd* and *zajr*) | "No, it is not so!" — it rejects a prior thought or attitude | Sībawayh and the Basran lexicographers |
| Sometimes **affirmation** (*taḥqīq*: certainly, indeed) | It is also used as an opening particle reinforcing a sentence | al-Kisāʾī and some Kufan lexicographers |

The first view is the widespread one. In this verse too the sense of rejection suits the context — but the question of what is being rejected remains open, because there is no preceding statement to reject.

One of the classical explanations is this: what is rejected has not been stated, but it has been described. Through five verses it was recounted that everything was given to the human being; *kallā* rejects the path the human being takes in spite of that picture. That is, the "no" is said not to a sentence but to a **conduct**.

**Its distribution within the Qurʾān.** This word occurs in more than thirty places in the Qurʾān and has a striking feature: all the places where it occurs are in the sūras of the second half of the codex, and all of them are Meccan sūras. This observation is recorded in the classical sources. The reason given for it is this: the word is a sharp particle of rejection and belongs to the polemical atmosphere of Mecca. I do not give the exact count; but that the distribution runs in this direction is correct.

### إِنَّ … لَ — double emphasis

The sentence begins with *inna* and a *lām* comes at the head of the predicate: *inna'l-insāna **la**-yaṭghā*. In Arabic this is one of the strongest structures of emphasis. Two particles of emphasis side by side.

Emphasis is not used idly in Arabic; it presupposes that the one addressed is in a state of denial or hesitation. That is, the sentence implies: **this is a finding that will meet with objection.** No one says of himself "I am a transgressor." The sūra speaks knowing this.

The verb is in the **imperfect**: *yaṭghā*. It conveys continuity and renewal. Not "he transgressed once"; the tendency to transgress is something continuously at work.

### طَغَىٰ — *ṭughyān*

**Root: ط غ ي / ط غ و.** The root's original, concrete sense is **water's exceeding its bounds, overflowing**.

There is a very clean proof of this from within the Qurʾān: *"When the water overflowed (ṭagha'l-māʾ), We carried you in the running ship"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/11). Here the word describes the water in the flood of Noah and carries no moral sense at all; it is simply water's exceeding its limit.

The root's moral use comes from this concrete image. **Transgression (*ṭughyān*) is a thing's exceeding its own limit.** When water exceeds its bed it becomes a flood; flood water is no longer water that nourishes but water that destroys. The same water, in the same quantity, is life when it stays inside its bed.

This image explains the whole weight of the word: transgression is not possessing something bad; it is carrying something good outside its own limit. Power is not bad; unlimited power is transgression. Knowledge is not bad; knowledge that gives no account is transgression.

**The root's other derivatives:**

- **طاغوت (*ṭāghūt*)** — an authority that has exceeded its limit; in the Qurʾān it is used for every seat of appeal put in the place of God (al-Baqara 2/256, al-Nisāʾ 4/60).
- **طاغية (*ṭāghiya*)** — that which exceeds all bounds; in al-Ḥāqqa 69/5 the thing that destroyed Thamūd is named with this word.

**The emblem of transgression in the Qurʾān.** This root is used in the Qurʾān most of all for Pharaoh: *"Go to Pharaoh; he has transgressed (innahu ṭaghā)"* (Ṭāhā 20/24; al-Nāziʿāt 79/17). And the content of Pharaoh's transgression is given in al-Nāziʿāt: *"He said: I am your lord, the most high"* (79/24).

That is, the furthest point of transgression is to put oneself in the position of a lord. And note: Sūrat al-ʿAlaq had begun with the word *Lord*. In the first verse the human being is introduced to his Lord; in the sixth verse the human being's tendency to reject that introduction and put himself in that place is recounted. This bond within the sūra stands in the words themselves.

### ٱلْإِنسَٰن — who is meant?

The word comes again with the definite article: *al-insān*. That is, not a particular person but the genus.

This is a familiar pattern in the Qurʾān. The word *"the human being"* occurs many times together with a diagnosis:

- *"The human being is indeed in loss"* (al-ʿAṣr 103/2)
- *"The human being was created anxious and impatient"* (al-Maʿārij 70/19)
- *"The human being is very ungrateful to his Lord"* (al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/6)
- *"He is indeed very unjust and very ignorant"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/72)

The common feature of these diagnoses is this: almost all of them are followed by an **exception**. In al-ʿAṣr: *"except those who believe and do good works."* In al-Maʿārij: *"except those who pray."* That is, the Qurʾān describes the human being not as a fate but as **what he becomes when left untended**.

In Sūrat al-ʿAlaq this exception is not written openly; but the last verse of the sūra stands in its place: *"prostrate and draw near."* The diagnosis has been made, and the treatment has been put at the very end.

One more thing must be noted, per the method (`USLUP.md`): the verse does not describe a group, a class, a section of people. It describes a **tendency**. Whoever goes after that tendency is the one the verse is speaking of.

## 96/7 — أَن رَّءَاهُ ٱسْتَغْنَىٰٓ

*An raʾāhu'staghnā*
"Because he sees himself as needing nothing."

### The sūra's diagnosis

This verse is the ground of the judgment in the preceding verse and is the sūra's most important sentence. Because it states the **cause** of transgression — and the cause it states is not the expected one.

The following could have been counted as causes of transgression: pride, greed, anger, ignorance, ill will. The verse says none of these. What it says is: **seeing oneself as needing nothing.**

The subtlety of this diagnosis lies here: the supposal of self-sufficiency is not a wickedness; it is a **miscalculation**. The person does not decide to be bad; he simply reads the situation wrongly. And the wrong reading determines the whole of his conduct.

Its logic works like this: one who has no need gives no account. To give an account is to admit that you need something — approval, permission, pardon, help, a return. If you need none of these, you owe no one an explanation either. And one who owes no explanation draws for himself the limit of what he does. And one who draws his own limit has, by definition, exceeded it.

So the bond between *istighnāʾ* and *ṭughyān* is not psychological but **logical**. The one produces the other.

### رَّءَاهُ — "he saw himself"

**Root: ر أ ي.** To see. In Sūrat al-Māʿūn we treated how this root covers two kinds of seeing (seeing with the eye and seeing with the heart); here the second holds: *raʾā* here means "he counted, he accepted, he saw it so".

**A grammatical nicety.** *Raʾā-hu* — "he saw him". The subject of the verb and its object are the same person. In Arabic a reflexive pronoun is normally used in this case: *raʾā nafsahu* (he saw himself). But for the **verbs of the heart** (*afʿāl al-qulūb*: to suppose, to count, to know, to see) the lexicographers record an exception: with these verbs the subject and the object may be covered by the same pronoun.

This is not a dry piece of grammar; it shows what the sentence is recounting. **The man passes judgment on himself by himself.** There is no confirmation from outside. He looks at himself and finds himself sufficient. The structure of the sentence carries this closed circuit: the seer is he, and the seen is he.

**"He saw", not "he became".** The choice of word here is exact. The verse does not say *istaghnā* (he became free of need); it says *raʾāhu'staghnā* (he saw himself as free of need). That is, **the claim works not because it is true but because it is supposed to be true.**

This distinction carries the whole sūra. Because it is not possible for a human being really to be free of need: the sūra's first five verses had already shown this — his existence given, his material given, his knowledge given, his instrument given. Freedom from need is a **delusion**. And the sūra ties the human being's transgression not to a power but to a delusion.

### ٱسْتَغْنَىٰ — *istighnāʾ*

**Root: غ ن ي.** *Ghanī* — one who is not in need, self-sufficient, rich. *Ghinā* — wealth, freedom from need.

**Pattern: Form X (*istifʿāl*).** One of this form's chief functions in Arabic is **"to count a thing so / to see it so"**: *istakbara* (he counted himself great), *istaskhara* (he mocked, he saw as small), *istabʿada* (he saw as remote). Another of its functions is *seeking*: *istaghfara* (he sought forgiveness).

The sense of the pattern here, read together with *raʾāhu*, is a repeated emphasis: both "he saw" and "he counted". Two words say the same thing twice — because this is exactly the matter: this is not a state but a conviction.

**In respect of what does he count himself free of need?** The verse does not say. The absence of an object again opens the scope. In the classical commentaries property and wealth are mentioned most; the reports cited as the occasion of revelation for the sūra also point to the rich of Mecca. But the word is far wider than property: health, power, position, standing, knowledge, the men behind him. Everything that makes a person say "I no longer need anything."

### The Qurʾān interpreted by the Qurʾān

The opposite pole of this verse is the concept of *al-Ṣamad*, which we treated in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ: the one who is not in need but who is needed. There we gave this verse:

> *"O people! You are the ones in need of God (**al-fuqarāʾ**); God is al-Ghanī, al-Ḥamīd"* (Fāṭir 35/15).

The word used in the verse is from exactly this root: *al-ghanī*. That is, the Qurʾān assigns the attribute "ghanī" to God and places the human being openly on the opposite side: *faqīr*, that is, the one in need.

In that case what the transgressor of al-ʿAlaq 96/7 does is this: **to take upon himself an attribute that does not belong to him.** Freedom from need is God's attribute; when the human being ascribes it to himself, a displacement occurs. There is no description showing more clearly what transgression is: to exceed the limit is to pass over into a place that is not one's own.

**The link with Sūrat al-Layl.** This root was touched on earlier in this commentary: in al-Layl 92/8 it says *"as for the one who is miserly and counts himself free of need (**wa-staghnā**)"*, and in the eleventh verse of the same sūra comes *"his property will not avail him (**mā yughnī**) when he falls"* — the same root, once for the human being's supposal, once for the real capacity of property. That discussion was treated in `092-leyl.md`; the finding there holds here too: **the origin of transgression is not wealth itself but the supposal of freedom from need.** al-ʿAlaq binds this supposal to *ṭughyān*, al-Layl to *bukhl* (miserliness) and *takdhīb*.

The Qurʾān establishes this bond by way of property in Sūrat al-Takāthur and Sūrat al-Humaza — those sūras were treated in this commentary; the diagnosis there, that "property gives the human being the delusion of permanence", is the same mechanism as the *istighnāʾ* here. In al-Humaza it says *"he supposes that his property will make him immortal"* (al-Humaza 104/3); here, without property being mentioned, the supposal itself is directly targeted.

### What this says to the present

*Istighnāʾ* is today the name not of a defect but of a goal.

One of the most widespread ideals of modern life is **to be in need of no one**: to stand on one's own feet, to ask nothing of anybody, to suffice alone. This ideal comes largely from a reasonable place — being left to another's mercy really is bad, and independence really is a value.

The verse does not target this value. What it targets is the **supposing that independence is real**. Because no one is really independent. Even the person who stands on his own feet has not grown the food he eats, has not built the language he speaks, has not constructed the order he relies on. Independence is always partial and always on somebody's back.

The practical symptom of *istighnāʾ* is for that reason a measurable thing: **the disappearance of the wish to give an account.** To the degree that a person sees himself as sufficient he finds explaining unnecessary. The sentence "I owe nothing to anyone" is most often said as a declaration of pride; the verse reads it as a diagnosis.

And this is the source of the conduct that will be displayed in the second half of the sūra: the man who sees himself as sufficient sees himself as entitled enough to interfere in another man's prayer. *Istighnāʾ* begins inside and shows itself outside.

## 96/8 — إِنَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكَ ٱلرُّجْعَىٰ

*Inna ilā rabbika'r-rujʿā*
"Truly, to your Lord is the return."

### Word order — the fronted predicate

The grammatical structure of the sentence is striking. After the particle *inna* the noun (the subject) normally comes first and the predicate after: *inna'r-rujʿā ilā rabbik*. The verse breaks this order and brings the predicate forward: *inna **ilā rabbika** r-rujʿā*.

In Arabic a fronted element conveys **restriction** (*ḥaṣr*). We saw the same technique in the last verse of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ: there *lahu* had been fronted, and the sense "to Him in particular there is no equal" arose.

Here the sense becomes: the return is **only** to your Lord. There is no other place of return, no other seat of appeal. And this is a direct answer to the preceding verse: the man was seeing himself as sufficient; the verse does not say "you are not sufficient", it says "where you are going is settled". The claim of *istighnāʾ* is refuted by a reminder of the destination.

### ٱلرُّجْعَىٰ — *rujʿā*

**Root: ر ج ع.** To return, to go back. *Rujūʿ*, *murājaʿa*, *irjiʿī* all come from this root.

**Pattern: *fuʿlā*.** This is a verbal-noun pattern rarely seen in Arabic and is generally used for verbal nouns that have become the **name** of a thing, that have turned into concepts: *bushrā* (good news), *dhikrā* (reminder), *ḥusnā* (the most beautiful), *ʿuqbā* (the outcome).

That is, the verse does not say "you will return"; it says **"the return"**. A noun is used in place of a verb, and the noun turns the event into a concept. A verb recounts an event; a noun establishes a fact.

The word also comes **with the definite article**: *al-rujʿā*. That is, not "a return" but "the return" — the known, the awaited, the unavoidable one.

### "Your Lord" — to whom the address is made

There is a nicety in the verse: it says *"to **your** Lord"*, not *"to **your** Lord"*. That is, the second person singular: **your** Lord. The one addressed is not the human being whose transgression is being recounted; it is the addressee from the beginning of the sūra — the Prophet.

This is an instance of a shift of address (*iltifāt* — the changing of the direction of address). In the middle of a picture recounted in the third person, there is a sudden turn to the second person.

What is its function? Two readings are possible, and both fit the text:

**1. Consolation.** While the depiction of the transgressor continues, a fixed point is given to the one addressed: do not mind his state, the return is to your Lord. The same name (*Lord*) had also occurred in the first verse; the name that gives the command "read" in the first verse is here the name of which it is said "the return is there". Beginning and destination are in the same place.

**2. Connection.** The first half of the sūra had begun "in the name of your Lord"; this verse of the second half says "the return to your Lord". The two halves are stitched to each other with the same word.

One more note: the verse does not say "the return to God", it says "the return to your Lord". The place to be returned to is not a foreign seat of appeal; it is the one who brought you up. In the discussion of al-Fātiḥa we treated this root's sense of "the one who nurtures, who brings to completion". To return to the one who brought you up carries a different resonance from being brought before a court — and the sūra uses this name at the very head of the section of threat.

## 96/9-10 — أَرَءَيْتَ ٱلَّذِى يَنْهَىٰ • عَبْدًا إِذَا صَلَّىٰ

*A-raʾayta'lladhī yanhā • ʿabdan idhā ṣallā*
"Have you seen the one who restrains — a servant when he prays?"

### أَرَءَيْتَ — the same door as al-Māʿūn

The analysis of this pattern was done on the first verse of Sūrat al-Māʿūn: the interrogative *hamza* + *raʾayta*; a pattern that has frozen in Arabic into the sense "tell me, what do you say"; its function is to draw the one addressed into the matter and then to say something startling. I rely on that treatment; I do not repeat it here.

What has to be added here is that the pattern is **repeated three times**: in verses 9, 11 and 13. In al-Māʿūn it occurred once; here it comes three times over and sets up a chain. I shall take up the structure of this chain separately at the end of verse 13.

And this too: the sūra uses the root ر أ ي heavily. Let us count:

- Verse 7: *raʾāhu* — he saw **himself** (a wrong seeing)
- Verses 9, 11, 13: *a-raʾayta* — **have you seen** (a call to attention)
- Verse 14: *yarā* — **God sees** (absolute seeing)

Three levels: the man's seeing of himself is wrong; the one addressed is asked to see rightly; and above both there is the one who really sees. The sūra sets up a hierarchy of seeing, and does it with a single root. This is a weave standing in the text's own texture; it is not forced.

### يَنْهَىٰ — the one who restrains

**Root: ن ه ي.** To forbid, to restrain, to hold back. *Nahy* (a prohibition), *nihāya* (an end — the point at which a thing is kept from going further), *muntahā*, *intihāʾ* come from the same root.

At the centre of the root is **stopping**. Not allowing a thing to go further.

**The verb has no object.** Note: the verse does not say "the one who restrains him", only "the one who restrains". The object will come in the next verse. The gap between them is deliberate — the same technique as the gap between the first two verses of al-Māʿūn. The question is left hanging in the air: what is he restraining? And as the answer is delayed it grows heavier.

**A contrast within the Qurʾān.** The most famous use of this root in the Qurʾān is this: *"Truly prayer **restrains** (tanhā) from indecency and wrong"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/45).

Now set the two sentences side by side:

| | Verb | Who | What he restrains |
|---|---|---|---|
| al-ʿAnkabūt 29/45 | *tanhā* | prayer | evil |
| al-ʿAlaq 96/9-10 | *yanhā* | the man | prayer |

The same verb, in opposite directions. Prayer restrains evil; the man here restrains prayer. That is, the person the sūra describes has let go of what he ought to be restraining and laid hands on what ought not to be restrained.

I give this link not as a transmitted piece of exegesis but as a verbal contrast established between two verses. The same root, the same pattern, the opposite object.

### عَبْدًا — "a servant"

**Root: ع ب د.** The analysis of this root was done in al-Fātiḥa in the discussion of *iyyāka naʿbudu*: servanthood, absolute submission and surrender; in the concrete semantic field of the root there are resonances such as a "road"'s being levelled by being trodden underfoot.

**Its coming indefinite.** *ʿAbdan* — "a servant", indefinite. Not definite. This choice needs dwelling on.

The verse does not say "the one who restrains Muḥammad" or "the one who restrains the Prophet". The name does not occur. Not even the title occurs. It says only "a servant".

This has two effects:

**1. It widens the scope.** If the person restrained is not a particular someone, the verse is describing not a particular event but an **attitude**. Whoever restrains someone who is praying, the verse is speaking of him.

**2. It puts the one addressed in the right place.** That the Prophet is called "servant" when he is spoken of is a consistent choice of the Qurʾān's; in the discussion of al-Baqara we dwelt on the word *ʿabdinā* (our servant): even where the highest ranks are mentioned, the title is servant. So too here. And this sharpens the scene: the man who restrains was seeing himself as sufficient (verse 7); the person he restrains does exactly the opposite — he shows that he is a servant.

That is, the two things brought face to face are in fact two stances: **the one who supposes himself ghanī** and **the one who knows himself to be ʿabd.** The sūra sets them against each other not by name but by attribute.

### إِذَا صَلَّىٰ — "when he prays"

**صلّى** — the analysis of the root (ص ل و; the sense of supplication, the explanation of *ṣalawān*, the calling of the horse that comes second in a race *muṣallī*) was done in Sūrat al-Māʿūn. I rely on that treatment.

The particle **إِذَا (*idhā*)** is used for a time whose occurrence is certain — it does not convey doubt as *in* (if) does. That is, the sentence does not say "if he prays"; it says "at the time when he prays". The prayer is being performed; the restraining is set against that.

**Why prayer?** Because prayer is the **visible** form of servanthood. A person may believe inwardly and no one know it. But when he prostrates he has made his declaration with his body. In al-Māʿūn, in the discussion of ostentation, we said that prayer's being a "visible act of worship" made it apt for ostentation; here the same visibility produces another consequence: prayer is the only act of worship that can be prevented, because it is the only act of worship that can be seen.

And this is exactly the target of the restraining: not belief itself but **belief's becoming visible.** No one can forbid the belief in the heart. What can be forbidden is its coming out in the body, in a place, in the public space.

### Historical background and the report of the occasion of revelation

In the classical commentaries it is said that these verses came down concerning Abū Jahl. The transmitted framework is this: Abū Jahl forbade the Prophet to pray in the vicinity of the Kaʿba and said that if he persisted he would tread on his neck; then he approached and drew back, and when he was asked the reason for his drawing back, he said that he had seen a trench of fire and wings in between.

**Concerning this report.** This story occurs in the hadith sources and is stated to be transmitted from Abū Hurayra; it is stated to be found in the collections of sound hadith. But I make no definite citation here to a particular book and chapter, because the report has different lines of transmission and different variants. The story itself is very widespread in the exegetical tradition.

**A note on method.** Even if the report is correct, it is plain that the ruling is not restricted to Abū Jahl. The rule we mentioned in the discussion of al-Māʿūn holds here too: *al-ʿibra bi-ʿumūm al-lafẓ lā bi-khuṣūṣ al-sabab* — the ruling goes by the generality of the wording. Accordingly the verse itself names no one.

One more observation: the sūra leaves both parties **without a name**. Neither the one who restrains nor the one restrained has a name. Both are described by what they do. This displays in the text itself the principle the method (`USLUP.md`) underlines: what is described is not persons but attributes.

## 96/11-12 — أَرَءَيْتَ إِن كَانَ عَلَى ٱلْهُدَىٰٓ • أَوْ أَمَرَ بِٱلتَّقْوَىٰٓ

*A-raʾayta in kāna ʿala'l-hudā • aw amara bi't-taqwā*
"Tell me — what if he (that servant) is upon guidance, or is enjoining godfearing?"

### The sentence left hanging

These two verses are grammatically an **incomplete** conditional sentence. "If he is such-and-such…" is said, and its answer is not stated.

The classical grammarians explain this gap as *ḥadhf* (suppression) and say that the answer is left to be completed in the mind of the one addressed. The suppressed answer is roughly this: *"…what a great thing it would be to restrain that!"* or *"…what will become of the one who restrains him?"*

The effect of the gap is this: a judgment left unsaid stands heavier than a judgment said. Because it forces the reader to construct the answer himself, and no one can reject an answer he has constructed himself. We saw that the gap between the first two verses of al-Māʿūn does the same work.

### هُدًى — guidance

The analysis of the root was done in al-Fātiḥa in the discussion of *ihdinā*: **ه د ي**, to show the way; within the word there is both the road itself and the direction of the journey. I rely on that treatment.

What is to be attended to here is the particle **عَلَى (*ʿalā*)**: *ʿala'l-hudā* — "**upon** guidance". In the discussions of al-Fātiḥa and al-Baqara we dwelt on this particle: in al-Baqara 2/5 it says of the believers *ʿalā hudan min rabbihim* (upon a guidance from their Lord), while of those who go astray it says *fī ḍalālin* (**in** error). *ʿAlā* conveys being on top, having the mastery, standing firm; *fī* conveys being buried inside.

Here the same particle: the man is restraining someone who stands **upon** guidance.

### تَقْوَىٰ — *taqwā*

**Root: و ق ي.** To protect, to be protected, to guard oneself. *Wiqāya* (protection), *ittiqāʾ*, *muttaqī* come from the same root.

The concrete sense of the root matters: **to put a barrier in front of something, to make a shelter.** The Arabs used this root for a shield and for the covering that protects an animal from the cold. That is, *taqwā* is first of all a concept of **protection**; not of fear.

In English *taqwā* is most often rendered "fear of God", and that rendering is deficient. What the root says is not to fear but **to put oneself under protection.** Fear is a feeling; *taqwā* is a conduct — a person's placing a distance between himself and what will harm him.

The verse uses this word together with the verb **to enjoin**: *amara bi't-taqwā* — he enjoined godfearing. That is, the person being restrained is not only performing his own prayer; he is also saying something to others.

### The sense of the two alternatives

Verses 11 and 12 present two possibilities, with the conjunction *aw* (or) between them:

1. **Being upon guidance** — concerning himself, turned inward.
2. **Enjoining godfearing** — concerning others, turned outward.

That is, the two possibilities cover the two directions of religious life: your own state and what you say to another. Whichever of these two the person restrained is doing, the restraining is out of place.

I think the ordering is meaningful: first his own state, then what he says to another. This is the Qurʾān's general arrangement; in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr too, faith and righteous work come first and the counselling of truth after. This is an observation, not a transmitted report.

### The rhetorical force of the sentence

What these two verses do is this: instead of defending the person restrained, they make **the restraining itself impossible.**

The verse does not say "that servant is on the right path" — it builds a conditional sentence: "what if he is on the right path?" This pulls the one addressed out of a defensive position and forces him to think. The man who restrains may not know what it is he is restraining. The verse sets that possibility in front of him.

And it gives rise to this question: on what reckoning can one restrain a human being from turning to his Lord? If what is restrained is right, the restrainer stands against the right. And even if it is wrong, who gave the restrainer this authority? The sūra does not ask this second question, but the gap in the sentence takes it in as well.

## 96/13 — أَرَءَيْتَ إِن كَذَّبَ وَتَوَلَّىٰٓ

*A-raʾayta in kadhdhaba wa tawallā*
"Tell me — what if he (the one who restrains) has denied and turned away?"

### The change of subject

Who is the subject of this verse? There are two possibilities, and both are mentioned in the classical commentaries:

| Reading | Subject | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| **The man who restrains** | The one who forbids | "What if he himself is denying and turning away?" |
| **The servant who is restrained** | The one who prays | (As a hypothetical possibility) "What if he has denied?" |

The first reading is by far the stronger and is the preference of the majority. Its ground is both the context and the sentence itself: verses 11-12 were supposing the good state of the servant restrained; verse 13 sets the state of the restrainer over against it. The contrast is completed in this way.

In that case the three occurrences of *a-raʾayta* set up a picture like this:

| Verse | Who | What he does |
|---|---|---|
| 9-10 | The one who restrains | Restrains a servant who is praying |
| 11-12 | The one restrained | Is upon guidance, enjoins godfearing |
| 13 | The one who restrains | Denies, turns away |

Three questions place the two persons opposite one another. And the answer to the chain will come in verse 14 — but not the expected answer.

### كَذَّبَ — he denied

The analysis of the root was done on the first verse of Sūrat al-Māʿūn: **ك ذ ب**, in Form II, "to call another's true word a lie"; its difference from inward unbelief (*kufr*) is that the attitude is made outward. I rely on that treatment.

Here the verb comes in the **perfect**, not the imperfect as in al-Māʿūn. *In kadhdhaba* — "if he denied". A conditional sentence requires the perfect; this is a requirement of the grammar, and no separate sense need be loaded onto it.

### تَوَلَّىٰ — he turned away

**Root: و ل ي.** This root is one of the most interesting in Arabic, because it carries opposite senses.

The central sense of the root is **nearness, adjacency, succession**: *walī* (friend, near one, protector), *walāya* (nearness and authority), *tawallī*.

But in Form V (*tawallā*) the verb can go in both directions at once:

- **تولّى + مفعول** (with an object): "he took charge of, he took possession of, he took as a friend."
- **تولّى + عن** or used absolutely: **"he turned his back, he turned away, he withdrew."**

The usage here is the second. That the same root carries both "to draw near" and "to draw away" is no contradiction: the root in fact conveys **turning towards**, and turning has two sides — to turn to one thing is at the same time to turn one's back on another. The verb describes the movement of showing the back.

This is an image in accord with the whole sūra. The sūra will end with prostration — with bringing the body down to the ground. *Tawallā*, on the other hand, is turning the body in the opposite direction. Two movements of the body, the counterparts of two attitudes.

### The order of the two verbs

*Kadhdhaba* first, *tawallā* after. That is, first the denial, then the turning away.

The ordering is logical: first the judgment is passed inside, then the distance is taken outside. Denial is a conviction, turning away a conduct. The Qurʾān uses this pair together elsewhere as well (al-Qiyāma 75/32: *wa lākin kadhdhaba wa tawallā*).

And this sets up a complete symmetry with the picture in the preceding verse:

| The servant restrained | The man who restrains |
|---|---|
| *ʿala'l-hudā* — upon guidance | *kadhdhaba* — he denies |
| *amara bi't-taqwā* — he enjoins godfearing | *tawallā* — he turns away |

Two attributes each, matched one to one. One is in guidance, the other in denial; one enjoins, the other turns his back. And the party that finds in itself the authority to restrain someone praying is the second.

## 96/14 — أَلَمْ يَعْلَم بِأَنَّ ٱللَّهَ يَرَىٰ

*A-lam yaʿlam bi-anna'llāha yarā*
"Does he not know that God sees?"

### The answer to the chain

The answer to the three *a-raʾayta* questions is here. And one has to attend to what the answer is.

The answer could have been:
- "Whoever does this will be punished."
- "He belongs to the fire."
- "What he does is a great wrong."

The verse says none of these. What it says is: **God sees.**

That is, not a threat but a **reminder of a piece of knowledge** is being made. The accusation is built not upon the man's wickedness but upon his **ignorance**: does he not know?

This choice is directly connected with the first half of the sūra, and it is one of the sūra's tightest inner bonds.

### أَلَمْ يَعْلَم — the sūra's own root

The verb is from the root **ع ل م**. This root had occurred twice earlier in the sūra:

- Verse 4: *ʿallama bi'l-qalam* — He **taught** by the pen
- Verse 5: *ʿallama'l-insāna mā lam **yaʿlam*** — He taught the human being **what he did not know**

And now verse 14: *a-lam **yaʿlam*** — **does he not know?**

Note the pattern in the fifth verse: *mā lam yaʿlam*. And in the fourteenth: *a-lam yaʿlam*. Almost the same expression.

The sūra is saying this: **the being who was taught what he did not know does not know the most basic thing.** He was taught reading, he was taught writing, he was taught the things he did not know — and the man who received all this does not know that he is being watched. Or he knows, but his conduct is as though he did not.

This bond stands in the wording of the text itself; it does not rest on an interpretation. It is the soundest bridge between the two halves of the sūra.

**The form of the question.** *A-lam yaʿlam* is a **rhetorical interrogative of denial**: it is in the form of a question but expects no answer; it conveys reproach and astonishment. The English "Does he not know?" is its exact counterpart — no question is being asked, astonishment is being expressed.

### بِأَنَّ — the letter *bāʾ*

The verb *yaʿlam* takes its object here with a **bāʾ**: *yaʿlam **bi**-anna'llāha yarā*. In Arabic the construction *ʿalima bi-* carries the sense of the knowledge touching its object — the *bāʾ*'s sense of adhesion (*ilṣāq*). We dwelt on this letter in al-Fātiḥa and in al-Māʿūn.

That is, what the sentence intends is not a dry piece of information: the real grasping of the knowledge, the touching of it.

### ٱللَّه — the name's single occurrence in the sūra

**The name "God" occurs in the sūra only here.** In a sūra of nineteen verses, *Lord* occurs three times as the divine name (*rabbika*, *rabbuka*, *rabbika*…) and *God* once.

And that once is exactly here: at the place where it is said that the transgressor is being watched.

This shows how careful the sūra is in its choice of names. *Lord* conveyed relationship and nurture; it was said to the party who is the addressee of the discourse. *God*, on the other hand, is the absolute name; it recalls to one who refuses to establish a relationship the one who exists independently of relationship.

To say "your Lord" to the man who sees himself as sufficient means nothing — he does not accept that he has a Lord in the first place. For that reason the name said to him is the name that is independent of any relationship with him.

### يَرَىٰ — He sees

**Root: ر أ ي.** The root woven through the sūra from the beginning, here at its last and highest level.

**Its object is not stated.** The verse does not say "God sees him"; it says only "God sees". The absence of an object here makes the scope unlimited: him, and what he does, and what he thinks, and everyone, and everything.

**The verb is in the imperfect:** continuous and uninterrupted. Not a single glance but an unbroken seeing.

**The sūra's chain of seeing is completed:**

| Verse | Verb | Who sees | What he sees | Its correctness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | *raʾāhu* | the man | himself | wrong |
| 9, 11, 13 | *a-raʾayta* | the one addressed | the man's state | right but limited |
| 14 | *yarā* | God | everything | absolute |

The man's whole problem is in the first row: he looked at himself and saw wrongly. The sūra first teaches the one addressed to look rightly, and then recalls the gaze that stands above them both.

### What this says to the present

The verse presents as the corrector of conduct not punishment but **being seen**. This is a distinction worth dwelling on.

Punishment is external and can be calculated: the likelihood of being caught, the weight of the penalty, the ways of escape. A person makes this calculation and most often comes out of it in his own favour. Being seen, however, cannot be calculated; it leaves no place to escape to.

In Sūrat al-Māʿūn we said this: the orphan is the live examination paper of whether or not you believe in the reckoning, because what is done to him carries no worldly cost at all. The verse here is the other half of the same mechanism: if there is no cost, the only thing that holds a person back is the belief that he is seen.

An observation in today's language: modern life has produced surveillance in abundance — cameras, records, traces. But what this surveillance produces is not morality but **an imitation of morality**: people behave properly where the camera sees and freely where it does not. The being-seen the verse speaks of is different from this, because it has no blind spot; and a surveillance with no blind spot ceases to be an external check and turns into a measure from within.

## 96/15-16 — كَلَّا لَئِن لَّمْ يَنتَهِ لَنَسْفَعًۢا بِٱلنَّاصِيَةِ • نَاصِيَةٍ كَٰذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ

*Kallā la-in lam yantahi la-nasfaʿan bi'n-nāṣiya • nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭiʾa*
"No! If he does not desist, We shall seize him by the forelock — a lying, sinning forelock."

### The second كَلَّا

The sūra's second *kallā*. The first (verse 6) had opened the second half of the sūra; this second one marks the place where the threat begins.

The structure comes out like this: *kallā* → diagnosis (6-14) → *kallā* → threat (15-18) → *kallā* → command (19). Three *kallā*s, three sections. The skeleton of the sūra's second half is built with these three words.

### يَنتَهِ — to desist

**Root: ن ه ي** — the same root as *yanhā* in verse 9. In Form VIII: *intahā*, "he stopped himself, he desisted, he put an end to it."

This is a striking choice of word. The man was **restraining** (stopping someone else); now he is asked to **desist** (to stop himself). The same root, the same family of verbs, but the direction has changed: the one who stops another is called to stop himself.

This is another example of the sūra's verbal weave. The text announces the punishment not with a new word but with the transformed shape of the man's own act.

**The sense of the condition.** *La-in lam yantahi* — "if he does not desist". That is, the threat is conditional. The door is not shut; the possibility of desisting has been left open. The sūra is not proclaiming a judgment; it is giving a warning.

### لَنَسْفَعًا — the nicety of the *nūn* of emphasis

**Root: س ف ع.** Two senses of the root are recorded, and both can be at work here:

1. **To seize violently and pull, to drag.** To grasp something and pull it hard towards oneself.
2. **To blacken, to brand.** *Sufʿa* is a darkening on the face, a mark. It is also used for the trace fire leaves where it has licked.

The first sense suits the context better (to take hold of the forelock). The second is not entirely excluded either; the explanation "We shall blacken his face" is also mentioned in the classical commentaries. It is possible for it to carry both: to seize and drag, and to leave a mark.

**A grammatical nicety: نون التوكيد الخفيفة.** The `ـًا` at the end of the word looks in the codex like an *alif* with *tanwīn*, but is in fact the **light *nūn* of emphasis** — in pausal form it is read as an *alif*, in connected form it gives the sound of *nūn*.

This particle occurs in only **two places** in the Qurʾān:

- al-ʿAlaq 96/15: *la-nasfaʿan*
- Yūsuf 12/32: *wa la-yakūnan mina'ṣ-ṣāghirīn* (وَلَيَكُونًا مِّنَ ٱلصَّٰغِرِينَ)

In both places there is a threat. In Yūsuf, the threat of the wife of the ʿAzīz against Joseph; here, the threat made against the transgressor.

This is not a dry statistic; it says something about the particle's function. The heavy *nūn* of emphasis (*nūn thaqīla*) occurs frequently in the Qurʾān. That the light one is used this rarely makes it striking in the places where it occurs. In sound too it is short and clipped — it suits the abruptly cut tone of the threat.

**A plural subject: "We shall seize".** The verb is in the first person plural. This is the widespread usage in the Qurʾān conveying majesty (the plural of majesty, *taʿẓīm*). We shall see it in Sūrat al-Qadr too: *innā anzalnāhu*.

### ٱلنَّاصِيَة — the forelock

**Root: ن ص و.** *Nāṣiya* is the hair at the top of the brow, the **forelock**. In the root's semantic field there is also "the front or tip of a thing".

**Taking by the forelock among the Arabs.** This is not a detail of language but a cultural gesture, and the whole weight of the verse is here.

A person taken captive in war was held by the forelock; this showed complete surrender and that disposal over him had passed to the other party. To release a captive without ransom was expressed by the phrase "to cut / to let go his forelock". That is, the forelock was the emblem of mastery over a person.

The reason is plain: the forelock is the foremost and highest part of the head. A person who grows proud raises his head; when he raises it, the part that comes forward is the forelock. And when it is held, the head is compelled to go wherever the one holding it wishes.

**The *nāṣiya* in the Qurʾān:**

- *Hūd 11/56*: *"There is no living creature but He holds it by its forelock."* — This verse establishes the matter at the root: the mastery is already in fact in His hand. The threat in al-ʿAlaq announces not the setting up of a new mastery but the **making visible of what already exists**.
- *al-Raḥmān 55/41*: *"The guilty will be known by their marks, and will be seized by the forelocks and the feet."*

Read together with these two verses, the threat of al-ʿAlaq comes to this: a man who has forgotten that he is held by the forelock is reminded that he will be held by the forelock.

### كَٰذِبَةٍ خَاطِئَةٍ — the attribute shifting to the place

The sixteenth verse repeats the *nāṣiya* of the preceding verse — but this time as an **indefinite** and with two attributes: *nāṣiyatin kādhibatin khāṭiʾa* — "a lying, sinning forelock."

**Grammar:** This is an **appositive substitute** (*badal*, an explanatory repetition) for the earlier *al-nāṣiya*. The passage from definite to indefinite is in order to load on the attributes.

**The real nicety: a forelock does not lie.** It is the man who lies, the man who errs. But the attributes are given not to the man but to his hair.

Arabic has a name for this: the ascription of an attribute not to its bearer but to **the place where it is found**. English does the same: we call a thief *light-fingered*, and it is not the fingers that steal. This usage makes the attribute concrete and renders it heavier.

But there is a further choice here: why is the attribute given to the forelock rather than to some other limb? Because the forelock is where pride shows itself. A lie is told with the tongue, an error is committed with the hand; but the stance behind them both — the holding of the head erect — shows itself in the forelock. The sūra brands not the acts but the stance at the source of the acts.

**The difference between the two attributes:**

- **كاذبة (*kādhiba*)** — lying. The analysis of the root was done in al-Māʿūn. A falsehood spoken knowingly; there is intent.
- **خاطئة (*khāṭiʾa*)** — its root is **خ ط أ**. To err, to miss what is right, to fall into wrong. There is a distinction the lexicographers make between *khaṭaʾ* and *khiṭʾ*: *khaṭaʾ* is unintentional error, *khiṭʾ* deliberate straying from the way. The *khāṭiʾa* here comes from the second pattern and conveys intent.

The two attributes together say this: both what he says is wrong (*kādhiba*) and what he does is wrong (*khāṭiʾa*). Word and deed, both corrupted.

### The forelock and prostration

The last verse of the sūra will say *"prostrate"*. And the place that touches the ground in prostration is exactly the brow — the area immediately below the forelock.

That is, the sūra sets up this contrast:

| The transgressor | The believer |
|---|---|
| His forelock erect | His brow on the ground |
| He is dragged by his forelock | He goes down of his own will |
| *Tawallā* — he turns his back | *Iqtarib* — he draws near |

The same region, in two different positions. The head that does not go down of its own will is seized and pulled.

I give this link not as a transmitted piece of exegesis but as a contrast standing between the sūra's own words. The text says *nāṣiya* and then says *usjud*; what binds the two together is the same point of the same body.

## 96/17-18 — فَلْيَدْعُ نَادِيَهُۥ • سَنَدْعُ ٱلزَّبَانِيَةَ

*Fa'l-yadʿu nādiyah • sa-nadʿu'z-zabāniya*
"Then let him call his council! We too shall call the *zabāniya*."

### Two callings

These two verses are the most dramatic place in the sūra, and the technique is simple: **the same verb, two parties.**

*Yadʿu* (let him call) — *sa-nadʿu* (We shall call). The same root (**د ع و**), the same verb, set against each other. One is in the imperative and mocking; the other in the future and certain.

### فَلْيَدْعُ — "then let him call"

**A command built with the *lām* of command** (*lām al-amr*): "let him call". But this is not a real command; in Arabic the imperative is sometimes used for **challenge** (*taḥaddī*) or for **threat**.

English has the same construction: "Let him do his worst", "Let him call them, then, and we shall see." In the form of a command, but nothing is being commanded; the helplessness of the other party is being displayed.

The ground for this reading is in the next verse: the call that will answer it is already at hand. That is, the one addressed is told "use every means you have", because the insufficiency of the means he will use is what is meant to be shown.

### ٱلنَّادِى — *nādī*

**Root: ن د و / ن د ي.** At the centre of the root lie **calling and gathering**. *Nidāʾ* (a call), *munādī* (one who calls), *nadwa* (an assembly) all come from here.

**نادي (*nādī*)** means "council" — but not merely a room: **the company that sits together, takes counsel and makes decisions, and the place of that company.** The word covers both the people and the place at once.

In Arabic today this word is still used for a sports club and for an association (*al-nādī*). The word's vitality comes from the continuity of its function.

**The historical counterpart: Dār al-Nadwa.** In Mecca, near the Kaʿba, the building in which the leading men of Quraysh gathered and took decisions bore a name from this root: *Dār al-Nadwa* — "the house of assembly". The decision for war, alliances, marriages, the affairs of the caravan were discussed there.

So the phrase "let him call his council" in the verse is not an abstract threat; it is a direct reference to the centre of the power structure in Mecca.

**What was a man's power in Mecca?** In treating the orphan in Sūrat al-Māʿūn we dealt with this in detail: there was no state, no police, no court. What protected a person was **the men behind him**. A person's safety depended on the existence of somebody who would call to account if he were harmed.

In this picture the *nādī* is a life insurance. The more crowded and the more respected a man's council, the less he is touched. And the concrete support for the transgressor's seeing himself as sufficient (*istighnāʾ*) is in all likelihood this too: he has men behind him.

The verse targets this support. What is meant by "let him call" is: **call the thing you rely on, and let us see whether it will be of use.**

**The *nādī* in the Qurʾān.** The word occurs once more: *"…and you commit abominations in your assembly"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/29) — the words of Lot to his people. There too the matter is that wickedness has ceased to be individual and is done **in assembly, together**. In both places the word calls to mind the courage a crowd gives.

### ٱلزَّبَانِيَة — the *zabāniya*

**The formation of the word is disputed.** It is a plural, but the lexicographers differ over its singular: forms such as *zibniyy*, *zabānī*, *zābin* are proposed. There is no agreement on a definite singular form.

**Its root** is generally given as **ز ب ن**. The sense of this root is: **to push violently, to repel, to drive off with a kick.** The Arabs called a camel that kicked while being milked *zabūn*. That is, the word means "one who pushes, who thrusts away".

Here a link can be made with Sūrat al-Māʿūn. There were two verbs there: *yaduʿʿu* (the one who violently pushes the orphan) and *yudaʿʿūna* in al-Ṭūr 52/13 (they are thrust into hell). The same logic works here too: the one who pushes is pushed. *Zabāniya* is the name of those who do the pushing.

**Who are they?** In the exegetical tradition they are understood as the officers of hell, the angels of punishment. The Qurʾān describes these angels elsewhere:

- *al-Taḥrīm 66/6*: *"Over it are angels harsh and severe, who do not disobey God in what He commands them."*
- *al-Muddaththir 74/30-31*: *"Over it are nineteen"*, and then *"We have made the keepers of the fire none but angels."*

Sūrat al-ʿAlaq does not explain the word; the name alone is given. This falls into the category of words left unexplained — like *māʿūn* in al-Māʿūn. Its not being explained preserves the terror: an unknown name is more effective than a described punishment.

### A comparison of the two callings

The force of these verses lies in the asymmetry they set up:

| | The man's call | The counter-call |
|---|---|---|
| Mood of the verb | Imperative (*fa'l-yadʿu*) — mocking | Future (*sa-nadʿu*) — certain |
| What is called | *nādī* — his own council | *zabāniya* — unknown officers |
| Possession | *nādiya-hu* — **his** council | definite, but ascribed to no one |
| Its character | people, countable, familiar | undescribed |

And note: the man's council is **ascribed to him** (*nādiyahu* — his council), while the other party is not ascribed; it is said only *al-zabāniya*. Even this difference says something: the power of the one rests on a relation of ownership, that of the other does not.

**The letter *sīn*.** The *sīn* at the head of the verb *sa-nadʿu* is the particle of the future, and in Arabic it is said to convey the near future (for the distant future, *sawfa*). That is, the threat is not a threat cast far off.

### What this says to the present

The mechanism behind the verse does not belong to history. **The confidence a crowd gives** is one of the oldest of human calculations, and it persists by changing form.

The question "who is behind him" is asked today too, and its answer still determines conduct. One with no one behind him holds back; one with a crowd takes courage. This does not have to be a bad thing — solidarity is a real need, and the sūra does not target solidarity.

What is targeted is the crowd's **standing in the place of the measure.** When the council is crowded a person does not question what he himself does; because there are many doing the same thing, and multitude feels like rightness. The wrongness of a conduct does not decrease with the number of those who share it — but the **felt** wrongness does. The verse speaks exactly about this feeling: call them, then, and see how many come, and what will change when they do.

## 96/19 — كَلَّا لَا تُطِعْهُ وَٱسْجُدْ وَٱقْتَرِب

*Kallā lā tuṭiʿhu wa'sjud wa'qtarib*
"No! Do not obey him; prostrate and draw near."

### The third كَلَّا and the closing of the sūra

The sūra's last *kallā*. And this time what is rejected is plain: the scene of threat in the preceding verse has ended, and the address turns back to the Prophet — and in his person to the reader.

The sūra began with a depiction (creating, teaching), continued with a diagnosis (transgression), went on with a threat (the forelock, the *zabāniya*), and now closes with **three commands**: do not obey, prostrate, draw near.

What is to be noticed is this: throughout the whole sūra nothing had been asked of the one addressed — apart from the "read" of the first two verses. Now at the end three commands come at once. And the order of the commands carries a logic.

### لَا تُطِعْهُ — "do not obey him"

**Root: ط و ع.** To obey, to submit, to comply. There is an interesting side to the root: *ṭawʿ* also means **willingness**. *Ṭawʿan aw karhan* — "willingly or unwillingly" (Āl ʿImrān 3/83, Fuṣṣilat 41/11). That is, *ṭāʿa* is not something done under compulsion; it is complying by accepting.

This refines the sense of the prohibition. To say "do not obey him" is more than to say "do not do what he says": **do not take him as your measure, do not count his word as admissible.**

**The form of the prohibition.** The prohibition is directed not at the threat but at the person: "do not obey him". That is, the matter is not the not carrying out of an order but the cutting of the relationship established with the one who gives the order.

And it also does not do this: it does not command retaliation, fighting, reprisal. What is commanded is not a conflict but an **independence**: do not listen to what he says, get on with your work.

The attitude treated in Sūrat al-Kāfirūn is at work here too: to draw a line, but not to attack. This is in accord with the sūra's context as well; in the first years of Mecca the Muslims had neither the power nor the means.

### وَٱسْجُدْ — prostrate

**Root: س ج د.** The root's original sense is **to bend, to lower oneself, to bow down**. The lexicographers record as concrete uses of the word things that bend and come close to the ground: the camel kneeling to take on its load, palm trees bent in the wind.

That is, in the root there is first a **physical lowering**; the sense of worship comes from that movement. Prostration expresses not a feeling but a **position**.

**Why prostration?** Look at what has been recounted throughout the sūra: a man who sees himself as sufficient, who holds his head erect, who restrains another's prayer. And the sūra's answer: you, prostrate.

This answer is not a debate. The verse does not say "argue with him", "answer him", "convince him". It does not set a thesis against a thesis; it sets a stance against a stance.

**The architecture of the sūra:** the sūra began with **read** and ends with **prostrate**. That is, it opens with knowledge and closes with prostration.

This is the summary of the sūra's whole argument. Because in the middle of the sūra there was this diagnosis: the human being who is given knowledge can transgress; the one who is taught can forget that he was taught. In that case knowledge is not by itself a sufficient destination. Knowledge can lead to transgression as well as to prostration.

The difference between the two extremes is not in the quantity of the knowledge but in whether or not the source of the knowledge is acknowledged. The one who acknowledges it prostrates; the one who does not sees himself, with the same knowledge, as sufficient.

**The sūra thus closes a circle:**

- Verse 1: *read in the name of your Lord* — knowledge, in dependence upon Him.
- Verse 5: *He taught him what he did not know* — the source of knowledge.
- Verse 7: *he saw himself as sufficient* — the forgetting of the source.
- Verse 19: *prostrate* — the acknowledgment of the source with the body.

### وَٱقْتَرِب — draw near

**Root: ق ر ب.** Nearness. *Qurb*, *qarīb*, *qurba*, *taqarrub* come from the same root.

**Pattern: Form VIII.** *Iqtarib*. This form generally conveys that the verb is done **with care, with effort, taking it upon oneself**. That is, not "be near" but **"strive to draw near, take the drawing near upon yourself."**

**The bond between the two commands.** The verse says "prostrate and draw near". The two commands are joined by a *wāw*. But the relation between them is not an ordinary sequence: the second is the consequence of the first.

And the paradox here is the sūra's last word: **the body's lowest position is its nearest position.** The human being draws near not by rising but by going down.

This is in accord with everything the sūra has built from the beginning. The transgressor was rising — he saw himself as sufficient, he restrained others, he held his head erect. And the more he rose, the further off he got. The believer goes down, and the more he goes down, the nearer he draws.

In the hadith corpus a report is transmitted that expresses this relation directly: it is announced that the moment at which the servant is nearest to his Lord is the state of prostration (the report is stated to be found in Muslim; I do not quote its wording exactly). The report shows how the link the verse establishes was understood in the early period.

**An observation about sound.** The sūra begins with *iqraʾ* and ends with *iqtarib*. Both open with the sound ق ر and both are in the imperative.

Here one has to be careful: **the roots are different.** *Iqraʾ* is from the root ق ر أ, *iqtarib* from the root ق ر ب. It would be wrong to present these as though they were the same root. But the similarity of the sound, and the two commands' standing at the two ends of the sūra, set up a frame in the ear. I record this as an observation about sound; not as a proof of meaning.

### That it is a verse of prostration

This verse is one of the verses of the Qurʾān that require the **prostration of recitation** (*sajdat al-tilāwa*). It is established in the tradition that a prostration is made when it is recited or heard.

The number of the verses of prostration is **fourteen or fifteen** according to the schools of law. The disagreement is chiefly over two places: whether the prostration in Ṣād 38/24 is a prostration of recitation or a prostration of thanksgiving, and whether the second prostration in Sūrat al-Ḥajj (22/77) is to be counted. I do not go into the legal detail; I confine myself to transmitting this difference between the schools.

Two points are important:

**1. There is no disagreement over al-ʿAlaq 96/19.** On all views it is counted a verse of prostration.

**2. It is the last verse of prostration in the order of the codex.** For one who reads the Qurʾān from beginning to end, this is the last place at which he prostrates. And this verse is the last verse of the first sūra to come down.

This is an arrangement established by the ordering of the codex, and it is striking: the sūra that carries the text's first words closes with the last of the prostrations. I record this not as a ruling but as a correspondence produced by the ordering.

## The sūra as a whole

### The verse-endings and the sound structure

The sūra divides into three distinct sound regions, and the boundaries of the regions coincide with the boundaries of the subject matter:

| Verses | Verse-ending | Character of the sound |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | *khalaq / ʿalaq* | **qāf** — coming from the throat, hard, clipped |
| 3-5 | *akram / qalam / yaʿlam* | **mīm** — closing at the lips, soft |
| 6-14 | *yaṭghā / istaghnā / rujʿā / yanhā / ṣallā / hudā / taqwā / tawallā / yarā* | **alif maqṣūra** — a long, open *ā*; unbroken through nine verses |
| 15-18 | *nāṣiya / khāṭiʾa / nādiyah / zabāniya* | **-iyah** — shortening, tightening |
| 19 | *iqtarib* | **-ib** — a closed consonant; the rhyme breaks |

Three observations:

**1. The region of the long *ā* is exactly the region of diagnosis and polemic.** Nine verses end with the same open sound. This allows the speech to quicken and to run in a single breath.

**2. When the threat begins the sound shortens.** *Nāṣiya*, *khāṭiʾa*, *nādiyah*, *zabāniya* — four verses, a closed and constricted sound. This sound, taking the place of the open *ā*, corresponds to the narrowing of the scene.

**3. The last verse breaks the rhyme.** *Iqtarib* agrees in sound with no other verse in the sūra. The sūra ends without rhyme — with a hard consonant, with a closing.

This may be a coincidence; but its effect is plain. The ear grows used to a rhythm through eighteen verses, and in the last verse the rhythm stops. The sūra takes the listener out of the flow and leaves him where he is. And as it leaves him there, the last word it says is "draw near".

### The weave of words — in a single table

The most striking side of the sūra is the use of the same roots in different places in different directions. These are not interpretation but verbal data:

| Root | Occurrences in the sūra | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **ع ل م** | *ʿallama* (4), *ʿallama / yaʿlam* (5), *a-lam yaʿlam* (14) | The human being who was taught does not know what he ought to know |
| **ر أ ي** | *raʾāhu* (7), *a-raʾayta* (9, 11, 13), *yarā* (14) | Three levels of seeing: wrong, limited, absolute |
| **ن ه ي** | *yanhā* (9), *yantahi* (15) | The one who stops another is called to stop himself |
| **ر ب ب** | *rabbika* (1), *rabbuka* (3), *rabbika* (8) | Beginning, guarantee, destination — always the same name |
| **د ع و** | *yadʿu* (17), *nadʿu* (18) | Two answering calls |
| **خ ل ق** | *khalaqa* (1), *khalaqa* (2) | The narrowing from absolute to particular |
| **إنسان** | Verses 2, 5, 6 | His material, his knowledge, his illness |

This density is extraordinary for a text of nineteen verses. The sūra advances by using almost no new words, changing instead the direction of the same roots.

### The argument of the sūra — step by step

1. **The command:** Read — but in the name of your Lord. (1)
2. **Ground 1:** Because He created you; and from something that hangs on and clings, at that. (1-2)
3. **The command again + a guarantee:** Read — your Lord is the highest degree of generosity. (3)
4. **Ground 2:** Because your knowledge too is from Him; and even the instrument that carries knowledge. (4-5)
5. **The diagnosis:** In spite of this the human being transgresses. (6)
6. **The cause of the cause:** Because he sees himself as needing nothing. (7)
7. **The refutation:** The return is to Him alone. (8)
8. **A concrete example:** What does the supposal of self-sufficiency do? It interferes with another man's prostration. (9-13)
9. **The answer:** What the one who does this does not know: that he is being seen. (14)
10. **The warning:** If he does not desist, he will be seized by the very place he holds erect. (15-16)
11. **The refutation of his support:** The crowd behind him will be of no use. (17-18)
12. **The conclusion:** Do not follow him; prostrate and draw near. (19)

Note that the ordering cannot be reversed. Without the diagnosis (6) the cause (7) is not understood; without the cause being known the example (9-13) does not fall into place; without the example being seen the solution (19) remains like a formula.

### What the first revelation says

The first sentences of a text show what that text wants to be. For the first five verses of Sūrat al-ʿAlaq this can be summed up thus:

**What is not said is striking.** In the first revelation there is none of the following: an article of belief (it does not say "God is one"), a command to worship (there is no prayer, no fasting, no pilgrimage), a list of the lawful and the forbidden, a depiction of paradise and hell, a declaration of prophethood (it does not say "you are a messenger").

**What is said:** a command (read), a name (Lord), a verb (He created), a material (*ʿalaq*), an attribute (al-Akram), an instrument (the pen), a gift (He taught what he did not know).

That is, the first revelation does not describe a religion; it establishes a **relationship**. The one addressed is first told who he is (created, taught) and who stands before him (the one who creates, the one who teaches). Everything else will come on top of this.

And note: the first command is not an act of worship. To read — that is, to receive knowledge, to grasp, to gather. This diminishes nothing of what came afterwards; but it shows the ordering.

### What this says to the present

**One. *Istighnāʾ* as an illness of the age.** The sūra's diagnosis — that the source of transgression is the supposal of freedom from need — is especially serviceable for today. Because modern life can technically produce the feeling of needing nothing: in a great many fields, from shopping to health, from transport to knowledge, one need not humble oneself before anybody. This is a real convenience and is not to be belittled.

But the feeling produced and the real situation are not the same thing. A person who orders food at the press of a button is in fact bound to a chain produced by hundreds of people and by an enormous infrastructure — he simply does not see the chain. A dependence that is not seen feels like a dependence that does not exist. The sūra's word *raʾāhu* (he saw himself) describes exactly this: the matter is not the situation but the seeing.

**Two. The disconnection between knowledge and humility.** The sūra praises knowledge (the first five verses) and says that the one who knows can transgress (the sixth verse). What binds the two together is this: if knowledge displays its own source it lowers; if it does not, it inflates.

The present-day counterpart of this is plainly visible. Access to knowledge is easier than at any time in history; but access also changes the knower's conviction about himself. Having read a few things about a subject can feel like the authority to pass judgment on it. The verse's question *a-lam yaʿlam* stands here: the most-known thing can cover over the least-known.

**Three. Interfering with another's worship.** The sūra chooses a very particular conduct: preventing someone's prayer. The psychology of this conduct is worth dwelling on, because it looks irrational — whom does another man's prostration harm?

The answer is in the sūra's own logic: for someone who sees himself as sufficient, another person's surrender is a **source of disturbance.** Because that surrender shows that the claim of sufficiency may be false. To stand erect where no one is bowing is ordinary; to stand erect where some are bowing becomes a position and has to be defended.

This mechanism is not confined to a religious context. The unease that arises within a community when someone begins to live by a different measure comes most often not from anger at that person but from the questioning of one's own measure.

What the sūra says on this is measured: *"Do not obey him."* Not a counter-attack, but independence. And this measure holds in the other direction too — the sūra does not legitimise anyone's imposing worship on anyone either; what it describes is a worship prevented, not a worship imposed.

**Four. The crowd supposed to be the measure.** The present-day counterpart of the *nādī* verse is the human being's supposing the support behind him to be rightness. The number of those defending a conduct does not make that conduct defensible — it only makes defending it easier. The sūra separates these two.

**Five. Prostration as a form of answer.** The end of the sūra is not a debate but a conduct, and this is the place in the commentary that will most easily be passed over and is the most important. The problem of seeing oneself as sufficient is not a problem of knowledge; it is not solved with knowledge — because the problem itself arises from a surplus of knowledge.

What is set down as the solution is a movement of the body. The human being comes out of seeing himself as great not by **telling** himself that he is not great but by **entering** a position in which he is not great. This also explains why worship is repeated: a conviction is learned once, a stance is continually renewed.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

- The core of the report of the first revelation is found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim; but ʿĀʾisha had not yet been born at the time of the event, and in this respect the transmission is the *mursal* of a Companion. The details of the scene (the number of the pressings, the exact wording of the sayings) admit of variants and were conveyed as report.
- The differing views on "the first sūra to come down" and the attempts at reconciliation were conveyed; the reconciliations are themselves inference, not transmitted report.
- The readings concerning the object of the command *Iqraʾ* were given in a table; none was presented as certainly correct.
- The views about the derivation of the name "Qurʾān" are the views enumerated in the classical sources; the ascriptions to particular persons (such as "ascribed to al-Shāfiʿī") were given in that language because they occur so in the sources, and carry no claim of definite attribution.
- No claim of a definite correspondence was set up regarding the relation between the word *ʿalaq* and modern embryology; the sense of the root was given, no claim of miracle was set up, and the grounds for this were written out separately.
- The story of Abū Jahl's preventing the prayer is found in the hadith sources; no definite citation was made to a particular book and chapter, and it was stated that it has variants.
- The wording of the report that the state of prostration is the moment of greatest nearness was not quoted in full; its source was given with a note of caution.
- There is no agreement among the lexicographers about the singular form and the root of the word *zabāniya*; the most widespread derivation was given.
- The number of the verses of prostration and the points of disagreement were conveyed; no preference was adopted among the views of the schools.
- The observations about sound and the verse-endings (the rhyme regions, the sound resemblance of *iqraʾ* and *iqtarib*) are observations about the texture of the text; they were not presented as proofs of meaning, and the difference of root was stated openly.
