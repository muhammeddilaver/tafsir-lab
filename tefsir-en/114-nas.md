# 114. Sūrat al-Nās

Six verses, twenty words. The last sūra of the codex and the last text of the Qurʾān.

The sūra opens with the same sentence as the one before it (al-Falaq): *"Say: I take refuge…"* But from there on it does something entirely different. Where al-Falaq counts four separate evils in four verses and points to a single refuge, al-Nās counts three separate names in three verses and points to **a single evil**.

This inverse symmetry is the most striking feature of the sūra, and it cannot be seen unless the two sūras are read together. We shall set aside a section of its own for it at the end of the text.

The second marked feature of the sūra is that it repeats a single word five times: **al-nās** — people. The sūra opens with this word and closes with this word. And the Qurʾān ends with this word.

### The name of the sūra

The name comes from the word that occurs five times in the text and that forms the verse-ending. In short sūras this is the commonest way of naming.

Together with the sūra before it, it is called **al-Muʿawwidhatān** ("the two sūras that give refuge"); the explanation of this term was given at the head of the section on al-Falaq and will not be repeated here.

### Meccan or Medinan

The same debate as with Sūrat al-Falaq. The majority say it is Meccan; the view that it is Medinan rests on the report that the Prophet was bewitched. The standing of that report was taken up in detail in the section on al-Falaq. I do not issue a definite ruling.

---

## 114/1 — قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ ٱلنَّاسِ

*Qul aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi'n-nās*
"Say: I take refuge with the Lord of the people."

### قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِ — the repeated opening

The analysis of these three elements (the imperative *qul*; the verb *aʿūdhu* being imperfect and first person singular; the *bāʾ al-ilṣāq*) was made in the section on al-Falaq; it will not be repeated here.

But the repetition itself calls for attention. The two sūras open with the same four words: *qul aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi*. Then one says *al-falaq*, the other *al-nās*.

This is a structure that locks the two texts to one another. The shared opening makes the difference visible: one enters by the same door but comes out into two different rooms. And precisely for this reason, every difference between the two sūras must be read as **deliberate** — because the text has marked the difference by building it on a common ground.

### ٱلنَّاس — al-nās

The key word of the sūra; it occurs five times and is the last word of five of the six verses.

**The question of the root.** The lexicographers are divided in two about the origin of the word, and both views are recorded in the classical sources:

**1. From the root أ-ن-س.** Its original form is *unās* (أُنَاس); the hamza at its head was dropped and, with the article *al-* prefixed, it became *al-nās*. From this root: *uns* (closeness, familiarity, being at ease with another), *ins* (the human kind), *insān*, *anīs* (a familiar friend), *mustaʾnis* (one who grows used to something). At the centre of the root stand **visibility, being known, being familiar** — beings who see and know one another.

**2. From the root ن-و-س.** *Naws* means to swing, to sway, to move from one side to the other. It has been said that people may have taken this name because they are constantly in motion.

The first view is the more widespread. The second is interesting as an association but has been counted weak.

There is also this: a view is transmitted that the word *insān* derives from the root **نسي** (to forget) — the human being is the being that forgets. This view is mentioned and repeated in the classical sources, but it is disputed in point of language and is not accepted by the majority. I record it; I do not make it a ground.

**The function of the word in this sūra.** If we take the first view as our basis, the root of *ins/nās* carries **visibility**. This will set up an exact opposition with the word *jinn*, which we shall meet in the sixth verse — because the root of *jinn* carries **coveredness**. Between its first word and its last, the sūra is preparing this opposition.

### بِرَبِّ ٱلنَّاسِ — "the Lord of the people"

**Why not "the Lord of all the worlds"?**

al-Fātiḥa, the first sūra of the Qurʾān, opens with *"the Lord of all the worlds"* — the widest circle possible. The last sūra of the Qurʾān says *"the Lord of the people"* — the circle has narrowed.

This narrowing is not without meaning. Taking refuge is a personal act, and a being who is being addressed is expected to be called out to with the attribute that bears directly on him. What the frightened person needs is not to know the general workings of the universe but to know **in whose hand he himself is**.

Moreover the threat of the sūra too is directed at the human being: the whispering is put "into the breasts of people", and part of its source is again the human being. The subject of the taking of refuge and the stage of the threat are thus the same field: the human being. The sūra builds its whole terminology on this single field.

That the Qurʾān opens in al-Fātiḥa with the widest circle and closes in al-Nās with the narrowest is an observation about the arrangement of the codex and a frame I have constructed myself; I do not present it as a classical transmitted report.

**The analysis of the word *Rabb*** was made in the section on al-Fātiḥa; why this name is chosen in the context of taking refuge was treated in the section on al-Falaq. In sum: *Rabb* means the one who stands in a relation, who looks after and watches over, and who has disposal over. And refuge is taken with whoever owns the thing that is dangerous.

## 114/2 — مَلِكِ ٱلنَّاسِ

*Maliki'n-nās*
"To the Malik of the people (their absolute sovereign)."

## 114/3 — إِلَٰهِ ٱلنَّاسِ

*Ilāhi'n-nās*
"To the Ilāh of the people (the one who alone is served)."

I take these two verses together, because together with the first verse they form a single structure.

### Word order — three names, zero conjunctions

Between the three verses there is **no conjunction at all.** It could have been said "Rabbi'n-nāsi **wa** Maliki'n-nāsi **wa** Ilāhi'n-nās". It was not.

In Arabic a second noun that comes after a noun without a conjunction is called in grammar **badal** (appositive substitute) or **ʿaṭf al-bayān** (explanatory apposition): the second is an element that stands in place of the first or that unfolds it. Had the conjunction (*wāw*) been placed there, the impression would have arisen that three separate things were being spoken of.

The dropping of the conjunction (*faṣl*) says this: **these are not three separate beings; they are three separate names of a single being.** We had seen the same structure between verses 1 and 2 of Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ, and there too the function was the same: the absence of the conjunction shows that the second expression is the continuation and the unfolding of the first.

This grammatical point is not trivial. That not even a trace in the language is left for the idea of three separate gods is part of the sūra's emphasis on *tawḥīd*.

### The order of the three names

Why Rabb → Malik → Ilāh? Could the order have been reversed? What does the order itself say?

**The meanings of the words:**

| Name | Root | Meaning | Kind of relation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **الرب** | ر-ب-ب | The one who nurtures, who raises, who looks after and brings up, who owns | Care and nurture |
| **الملك** | م-ل-ك | The owner and governor of dominion; the one who judges, who commands | Authority and judgement |
| **الإله** | أ-ل-ه | The one who is served, to whom service is directed | Service and attachment |

**On الملك.** Root م-ل-ك: to own a thing and to have disposal over it. *Mulk*, *mālik*, *malik*, *malakūt*, *mamlūk* are from the same root. The difference between *mālik* and *malik* is fine but real: *mālik* carries the emphasis of ownership, *malik* that of **government and judgement**. In al-Fātiḥa there is a famous variant reading between these two words (*māliki yawmi'd-dīn* / *maliki yawmi'd-dīn*); it was treated there. Here the word is beyond dispute *malik*.

The Qurʾān uses this name of God in several places: *"Exalted is God, the true Sovereign (al-Malik al-Ḥaqq)"* (Ṭāhā 20/114); *"Say: O God, Owner of all dominion!"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/26); and within a sequence of names: *"He is God; there is no god but He. He is al-Malik, al-Quddūs, al-Salām…"* (al-Ḥashr 59/23).

**On الإله.** Root أ-ل-ه. *Ilāh* means the one who is served; its plural is *āliha*. Its relation to the name "Allāh" was taken up in the section on al-Fātiḥa.

Among the meanings the lexicographers record for this root are "to serve", "to be bound in wonder, to stand before a greatness the mind cannot reach", and — interestingly in this context — **"to turn to someone and take refuge with him"**. This last sense is transmitted; I do not present it as a settled etymology. But if it is correct, it means that an echo is set up between the verb of the sūra (*aʿūdhu* — I take refuge) and its third name.

**The logic of the order.** Several explanations can be given for the three names being set out in this order, and they do not exclude one another:

**1. The order in which they answer to a human life.** A person is first **nurtured** (he is born, is raised, is fed — Rabb), then becomes aware that he lives under an **order and a judgement** (Malik), and in the end asks **to whom he is to attach himself** (Ilāh). This is the order of experience.

**2. From the near to the far, from the concrete to the absolute.** *Rabb* is the nearest and the most felt — the relation of care is lived directly. *Malik* is a higher and more abstract authority. *Ilāh* is the most absolute; it enters into no comparison at all. The order thus **rises** from what is felt towards what is transcendent.

**3. From what is accepted to what is disputed.** This explanation takes account of the first addressees of the sūra. The mushrikūn of Mecca accepted that God was the creator and the giver of provision — that is, in a sense, that He was *Rabb*; the Qurʾān records this in several places (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/61-63; al-Zumar 39/3). What they did not accept was **divinity**: that service be rendered to Him alone. The order begins from where the addressee agrees and advances towards where he objects. This is an arrangement for persuasion.

**4. The layers of the capacity to protect.** It is as though the things needed for protection against the whispering were being named in order: the one who raised you (and therefore knows your weak points), the one who has the authority to command you (and therefore has the authority to command your enemy too), and the one who is the sole address of service (and therefore stands over against the direction the whispering tries to pull you in). This reading is my own.

**The irreversibility of the order** is not as strict here as the chain of reasoning we saw in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ; the order here is a gradation rather than a logical necessity. This needs to be stated, because looking for a necessary logic in every sequence produces forced readings.

### The threefold repetition of "nās"

In Arabic these three verses could also have been built thus: *"Rabbi'n-nāsi, malikihim, ilāhihim"* — "the Lord of the people, their Malik, their Ilāh". A pronoun would have been used, there would have been no repetition, and it would have been shorter.

It was not so. The word is repeated three times, in its full form. This has several functions:

**1. Fixing and reinforcement.** In Arabic the repetition of a noun where a pronoun would have served underlines that noun. We had seen the same thing in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ: instead of saying "huwa'ṣ-Ṣamad", the second verse repeated the name by saying "Allāhu'ṣ-Ṣamad".

**2. Each name being bound to the human being independently.** Had a pronoun been used, the second and third names would have hung from the first. Thanks to the repetition each name establishes a complete relation with the human being **on its own**: He is separately Rabb and Malik and Ilāh — and in all three the one addressed is the same: the human being.

**3. Forcing the addressee to listen.** The word rings in the ear. "People, people, people" — the sūra does not let one forget whom it is speaking of. And this will gain its meaning after the fourth verse: because the threat will settle exactly inside the human being.

**4. Building the verse-ending.** We shall return to this in the section on the sound structure.

### Why are there three names here when in al-Falaq there was one?

The answer to this question cannot be given without comparing the two sūras as wholes. I leave it to the section of comparison at the end of the text. But let me set down one marker already: **the further the threat moves inside, the deeper the refuge grows.**

## 114/4 — مِن شَرِّ ٱلْوَسْوَاسِ ٱلْخَنَّاسِ

*Min sharri'l-waswāsi'l-khannās*
"From the evil of the whisperer who stealthily draws back."

### الوَسْوَاس — al-waswās

**The sound itself.** The most striking thing about this word, before its meaning, is **its sound**.

In Arabic there is a class of words: four-lettered, built by the repetition of the first two letters, and **imitating a sound that is heard**. What in English is called onomatopoeia. Examples: *washwasha* (a whisper), *khashkhasha* (a rustle), *hamhama*, *damdama*, *zalzala*.

**وسوسة** belongs to this class. The word itself is the sound of a whisper: *was-wa-sa*. A twice-repeated, fricative, soft, low sound. The moment you say it, it does the thing it imitates.

In the books of the language the original sense of the word is recorded as **"a hidden, faint sound"**; it is transmitted that it was used of the fine sound made by ornaments and pieces of jewellery. The word thus first names **a sound at the edge of hearing**, and afterwards acquires the sense "a word whispered inward".

This places the definition of the concept inside the word itself. The whispering does not shout. Against something that shouts a defence is taken up; something that whispers, on the other hand, is most often taken for your own thought. **The power of the whispering lies in the lowness of its volume.**

**A verbal noun or a noun?** It is transmitted that in Arabic a distinction is drawn among words of this pattern according to their vowelling: *al-waswās* (with *fatḥa*) is a **noun** — that is, the whisperer himself; *al-wiswās* (with *kasra*) is a **verbal noun** — that is, the act of whispering. The reading in the Qurʾān is with *fatḥa*.

If this is accepted, what is named in the verse is **not the act but the agent**: the one who whispers. Indeed the next verse confirms this by saying "who whispers" — the relative pronoun (*alladhī*) qualifies an agent, not an act.

Even so, some exegetes have counted the word a verbal noun and understood it as "from the evil of whispering". The wording of the following verse supports the first reading.

**As a verb in the Qurʾān.** The root occurs in several places in the Qurʾān and **its prepositions change** — this is a detail worth pausing over:

| Verse | Expression | Preposition |
|---|---|---|
| al-Aʿrāf 7/20 | *fa-waswasa **lahumā** al-shayṭān* | **لـ** — "for them / against them" |
| Ṭāhā 20/120 | *fa-waswasa **ilayhi** al-shayṭān* | **إلى** — "towards him" |
| al-Nās 114/5 | *yuwaswisu **fī** ṣudūri'n-nās* | **في** — "inside…" |

Three prepositions, three distinct distances: **for / towards / inside.** The first is from outside and directed at a target; the second shows an approach; the third is within. The last sūra of the Qurʾān uses the innermost preposition.

I present this finding as a reading; the differences of preposition are dwelt on in the classical commentaries, but this threefold arrangement is a frame I have constructed myself.

**And there is one critical verse:**

> *"We created the human being, and We know what his own soul whispers to him (mā tuwaswisu bihi nafsuhu). We are nearer to him than his jugular vein."* (Qāf 50/16)

Here the one who whispers is **not the devil but the person's own soul**. This verse shows that the source of the whispering is not single, and when it is read together with the last verse of Sūrat al-Nās the whole of the matter comes out. We shall return to this at verse 6.

### الخَنَّاس — al-khannās

**Root: خ-ن-س.** Its concrete sense: **to draw back, to go backwards, to cower, to hide, to withdraw and disappear.**

The most concrete use of the root is of the face: *khanas* is the state of the nose being drawn back into the face, of its being flat. *Akhnas* — flat-nosed. The name of the pre-Islamic poet **al-Khansāʾ** is from this root as well. At the centre of the root, then, stands the movement of **drawing back, of slipping inward**.

**Its most important use in the Qurʾān:**

> *"No! I swear by those that draw back (al-khunnas), those that run their course and slip into their lairs (al-jawāri'l-kunnas)…"* (al-Takwīr 81/15-16)

In the classical commentaries *al-khunnas* is mostly explained as **the stars, the planets**: they are visible by night and disappear by day; or, as they advance across the sky, they appear to be going backwards and then advance again. And the word *kunnas* calls up the covert in which a gazelle hides (*kinās*) — the image of an animal that shows itself and then goes into its lair.

The link between the two verses is very apt: **khunnas** are beings that come out and then disappear. **Khannās**, for its part, is the intensive form of the same root — the *faʿʿāl* pattern conveys the one who does a thing much, who has become identified with that work (the same logic of pattern as *naffāthāt* in Sūrat al-Falaq).

So *al-khannās*: **the one who cowers constantly, who draws back again and again, who has made vanishing his habit.**

### The description of the whisperer: he both comes and flees

These two sides of the word are strange taken together. The whisperer both draws near (to whisper one must be close) and withdraws. In the classical commentaries this duality is explained in two ways, and the two complete one another:

**1. He withdraws when God is remembered.** This is the widespread explanation: when the servant remembers God the whisperer cowers, and when the servant sinks into heedlessness he comes back. Being *khannās* thus describes a **cycle** — withdrawal and return.

**2. He strikes and runs; he does not show himself.** He leaves the whisper and vanishes. The word that remains is now of uncertain ownership — and precisely because of that uncertainty the person takes it for his own thought.

The second explanation, to my mind, better accounts for the force of the word, and this is my preference, and it is not binding. For the matter is this: **if the whisperer stayed in view, the whispering would be ineffective.** If the source of a thought is known, that thought can be argued with. A thought whose source is unknown leaves one defenceless — because whoever objects to it has objected to himself.

**This overlaps with the fundamental thing the Qurʾān says about the devil:**

> *"I had no power (sulṭān) over you; I only called you, and you answered me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves."* (Ibrāhīm 14/22)

> *"Over My servants you have no power…"* (al-Ḥijr 15/42; similarly al-Isrāʾ 17/65)

The whisperer, then, holds no compelling power. The only thing in his hand is a **call** — an invitation, a word, a whisper. Because there is no compulsion, responsibility stays with the person. And because there is no compulsion, his weapon consists of nothing but concealment.

**The meaning of the cowering follows from this: it is the tactic of an enemy who has no power.** One who is strong does not hide. To be *khannās* is to be unable to risk open conflict.

### Who is the whisperer? — a debate about definition

A question stands here: is *al-waswās al-khannās* a name of the devil, or an attribute?

The verse uses an **attribute**: "the one who whispers, who cowers". It gives no proper name. And the sixth verse will say that this attribute can be found **in two kinds at once**. The sūra thus binds the threat not to a being but to a **function**.

This accords with the general method of the Qurʾān: the attribute is described, and whoever bears that attribute falls within the ruling.

## 114/5 — ٱلَّذِى يُوَسْوِسُ فِى صُدُورِ ٱلنَّاسِ

*Alladhī yuwaswisu fī ṣudūri'n-nās*
"Who whispers into the breasts of the people."

### The function of the verse: description

This verse adds no new evil; it **defines** the one before it. *Alladhī* ("who") is a relative pronoun and qualifies the noun that precedes it.

In the sūra the side with whom refuge is taken is set out in three verses, and the side from which refuge is taken in three verses as well (4, 5, 6). The structure is symmetrical: three verses of presentation, three verses of description.

### فِى صُدُورِ — "in the breasts"

**Why not "in the hearts"?**

The Qurʾān uses the word "heart" in hundreds of places. Here it does not use it. This choice calls for attention.

**The word ṣadr.** Root **ص-د-ر**: to come out from a place, to return, to come forward. *Ṣadara* — he came out, he returned. In the Qurʾān: *"That day people will come forth in scattered groups"* (al-Zilzāl 99/6). From the same root come *maṣdar* (source, place of issue), *ṣudūr* (issuings) and *ṣadr* — **breast, the front side, the head of a thing**. The head place of an assembly is called *ṣadr al-majlis*; the word carries the sense of "front, first, head".

**And the Qurʾān establishes the relation between the two explicitly in one verse:**

> *"It is not the eyes that go blind; it is the hearts in the breasts that go blind."* (al-Ḥajj 22/46)

*"al-qulūbu'llatī fi'ṣ-ṣudūr"* — **the hearts that are inside the breasts.** In the Qurʾān's own language, then, the *ṣadr* is what holds the heart within it. The heart is what is inside; the *ṣadr* is the space that surrounds it.

**The reading that follows from this is:** the whispering enters the breast; whether it reaches the heart is a separate matter. The *ṣadr* is the outer courtyard — the entrance. The heart is the inner room — the place of decision, of faith, of intention.

The verse, that is, describes how far the whispering comes: **as far as the courtyard.** Whether it goes inside is another thing.

There are several things that support this reading:
- The Qurʾān speaks of a seal being set on the heart, of the heart falling sick, of the heart being darkened — the heart is where a person's real condition is located.
- The breast, on the other hand, is spoken of in the Qurʾān as a place that widens and narrows: *"Whomever God wishes to guide, He opens his breast to Islam"* (al-Anʿām 6/125); *"Did We not open up and expand your breast?"* (al-Inshirāḥ 94/1); *"We certainly know that your breast is straitened by what they say"* (al-Ḥijr 15/97). The *ṣadr*, that is, is a **volume**; a place into which and out of which things pass, which widens and narrows.

I know that this distinction is treated in the classical commentaries; but I do not ascribe the frame above, in exactly this form, to any one exegete — I present it as a reading resting on al-Ḥajj 22/46.

**Its moral consequence is important.** If the whispering stays in the breast, you are not responsible for everything that comes into your mind. A thought's appearing in your mind and your adopting it are not the same thing. This distinction is one of those that most relieve people who have a religious scrupulousness, and it has often been voiced in the tradition as well.

**The breast has a bodily side too.** Fear, distress, palpitation, shortness of breath — these are felt in the body, in the chest. This is why the Qurʾān uses the expression "the breast being straitened". The *ṣadr*, then, is both where thought passes and where feeling is **felt**. The whispering is described not as an abstract event of the mind but as a state that has a counterpart in the person's body.

### صُدُورِ ٱلنَّاسِ — "the breasts of the people"

Plural. And general.

The verse does not say "the breasts of the unbelievers" or "the breasts of the heedless". The breasts of **people**. The whispering, that is, comes to everyone. To have faith does not mean not to be addressed by the whispering.

This is one of the most relieving sentences of the sūra and at the same time one of those that must be taken most seriously. Relieving, because the coming of the whispering is not a sign of a defect. Serious, because no one is exempt.

And this too is worth noting: the one taking refuge is **singular** (*aʿūdhu* — I take refuge), the field of the threat is **plural** (*ṣudūri'n-nās* — the breasts of the people). The danger is shared, the taking of refuge is personal. No one can take refuge on another's behalf.

## 114/6 — مِنَ ٱلْجِنَّةِ وَٱلنَّاسِ

*Mina'l-jinnati wa'n-nās*
"Of the jinn and of people."

The last verse of the Qurʾān. And the verse that makes the whole structure of the sūra be read again.

### Word order — where does "min" attach?

There is disagreement about which element the preposition *min* in this verse attaches to, and the disagreement changes the meaning:

| View | Reading | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. An explanation (*bayān*) of "al-waswās al-khannās"** | "The whisperer who cowers … who is of the jinn and of people" | **The whisperer is of two kinds: he may be of the jinn, and he may be a human being.** The view of the majority |
| **2. An explanation of the "nās" in "ṣudūri'n-nās"** | "…into the breasts of the people, and these people are of the jinn and of human beings" | Rests on the assumption that the jinn too may be called "nās". Counted weak |
| **3. Attached directly to "aʿūdhu"** | "I take refuge … from the evil of the whisperer, from the jinn and from people" | The result is close to the first |

**The first view is both the most widespread and the strongest,** because another verse of the Qurʾān supports it directly:

> *"Thus We made for every prophet an enemy: the devils of humankind and of the jinn. They whisper gilded words to one another, to deceive."* (al-Anʿām 6/112)

In this verse **"shayāṭīna'l-insi wa'l-jinn"** — the devils of humankind and the devils of the jinn — are named explicitly side by side. And the work they do is described not with the same word but with the same logic: *"yūḥī baʿḍuhum ilā baʿḍin zukhrufa'l-qawl"* — they whisper ornamented speech to one another.

In the Qurʾān, that is, "devil" is not only the name of an unseen being; it is an **attribute**, and it can be found in a human being too.

### ٱلْجِنَّة — al-jinna

**Root: ج-ن-ن.** And the meaning of this root explains the force of the sūra's last sentence: **to cover, to conceal, to hide.**

This meaning is present in all that derives from the same root:

| Word | Meaning | What is covered? |
|---|---|---|
| **jinn** | Beings that are not seen | They themselves are covered from sight |
| **janīn** | The child in the womb | Covered by the womb |
| **janna** | Garden | Its ground covered over by trees |
| **majnūn** | One whose mind is covered | The mind is covered |
| **junna** | Shield | The thing that covers, that protects |
| **janna (verb)** | It covered | *"When the night covered him…"* (al-Anʿām 6/76) |

*Al-jinna* is the collective name of the jinn.

**Here, then, is the fine point at the close of the sūra:** *jinn* = **what is covered**, *nās* = (in its root, on the first view) **what is seen, what is known, what one becomes familiar with**. The last two words of the sūra set up an exact opposition: **those who are hidden and those who are in the open.**

The whispering can come from either.

### The weight of the last word: "wa'n-nās"

The sūra began by saying *"the Lord of the people"*. And it ends thus: *"…and from people."*

The same word. In the first as the party to whom the taking of refuge belongs, in the last as the source of the threat.

**The meaning of this is heavy and should not be softened.** The sūra places the human being both as the being to be protected and as the being to be protected against. The human being is both the target of the whispering and — sometimes — its source.

And more important still, the order is this: **the jinn first, then human beings.** The human being, that is, is named **last**. In rhetoric what is named last is most often where the emphasis falls; what is heard last is what stays longest in the mind.

Why should this be so? Several explanations can be thought of, and these are my own reading:

**1. Because it is not expected.** Throughout the sūra a hidden, stealthy, unseen enemy is described. The reader naturally expects an unseen being. The last word breaks that expectation: a human being does the same work.

**2. Because it is more dangerous.** Against a whispering that comes from a jinnī a person is already on his guard; he knows he is to protect himself, and he takes refuge. Against a whispering that comes from a human being he takes up no defensive position — because the one before him is familiar, is legitimate, may be someone he loves, may be someone in authority. **Being visible secures being taken for trustworthy.**

**3. Because it brings responsibility back.** To lay evil entirely upon an unseen being is the easiest way of acquitting the human being. The last word of the sūra closes that door.

**The Qurʾān records this matter in another place as well:**

> *"And there were men among human beings who took refuge with men among the jinn, and so they increased them in insolence."* (al-Jinn 72/6)

In this verse the root **ع-و-ذ**, the backbone verb of al-Falaq and al-Nās, is used (*yaʿūdhūna* — they used to take refuge). The Qurʾān, that is, has also given a name to the taking of refuge that is made to the wrong address. That al-Muʿawwidhatān begin with "qul aʿūdhu bi-Rabbi" is a correction that puts that wrong address right.

### The last word of the Qurʾān

The codex opens with the name "Allāh" in the basmala and closes with the word **"al-nās"**.

This is an observation, and I do not present it as a theological claim; but it deserves to be recorded. From beginning to end the Qurʾān speaks of God and of the human being. In the first sūra it says *"the Lord of all the worlds"* — the human being is somewhere inside the worlds. In the last sūra it says *"the Lord of the people"* — the circle has narrowed and only the human being is left inside it. And the last word is that human being himself.

At the close of the Qurʾān the human being appears twice: as the one whose Lord the one with whom refuge is taken is (verse 1), and as the place from which the evil taken refuge from may come (verse 6). The distance between the two is the whole field of the sūra.

### The texture of the sound

All six verses of the sūra end in **س**: *nās — nās — nās — khannās — nās — nās*.

The letter *sīn* is in Arabic a **fricative** sound: the air comes out by leaking continuously between the tongue and the teeth. It is not cut off; it lengthens; it hisses.

This needs to be compared with the sound texture of Sūrat al-Falaq. All five verses of al-Falaq end in letters of **qalqala** (ق ق ب د د) — plosive, cut-off, striking sounds.

| | **al-Falaq** | **al-Nās** |
|---|---|---|
| Verse-ending letters | ق ق ب د د | س س س س س س |
| Kind of sound | Plosive, cut off (*qalqala*) | Fricative, drawn out |
| Effect on the ear | A stroke, a blow | A hiss, a whisper |

And this overlaps with the two kinds of threat the two sūras describe: **al-Falaq describes the blows that come from outside and ends with the sound of a blow; al-Nās describes the whisper that comes from inside and ends with the sound of a whisper.**

I do not present this as a numerical claim or as a "miracle". It is an observation about the sound weave of the text, and one of the known examples of the accord between sound and meaning in the short sūras of the Qurʾān.

Moreover the word *waswasa* itself carries the *sīn* sound. The sūra makes, again and again, the very sound it describes.

## The two sūras together: an inverse symmetry

What really gives al-Muʿawwidhatān their meaning is that the two texts are built **as the inverse of one another**.

### The basic opposition

| | **al-Falaq (113)** | **al-Nās (114)** |
|---|---|---|
| **The one with whom refuge is taken** | **One** name: Rabb | **Three** names: Rabb, Malik, Ilāh |
| **The evil refuge is taken from** | **Three** particular evils (+ one general) | **One** evil: *al-waswās* |
| Number of verses | 5 | 6 |
| Direction of the evil | From outside | From inside |
| Target of the evil | Body, property, life, relationships | Heart, intention, faith |
| Character of the evil | Concrete, something that happens, noticeable | Hidden, continuous, unnoticed |
| Restriction of time | **Present:** *idhā waqab*, *idhā ḥasad* | **Absent** |
| Agent | Darkness, those who blow, the envier | A single attribute: the one who whispers and cowers |
| Sound | Cut off, plosive | Continuous, hissing |

### What does this inverse symmetry say?

**First: the outer threats are many but the refuge is one.**

In al-Falaq the evils are multiplied because the outer threats really are many and various — things as unlike one another as darkness and envy. But against all of them a single name suffices: *Rabb al-falaq*. Because they are all of one category: **the evil of what has been created.** To take refuge with the owner of what has been created covers them all at once.

**Second: the inner threat is one but the refuge is multiplied.**

In al-Nās the threat is single: the whisper. But the one with whom refuge is taken is named by three names. The reason for this is not that the threat is **greater** but that it is **deeper**.

The outer threat targets the body. Harm that comes to the body is temporary and is outside. The inner threat targets the person himself — what he believes, what he will want, who he is. And in this field there is no defence; because the one who is to defend is that same person.

Hence the three names: **Rabb** (the one who raised you, and therefore knows your weak points), **Malik** (the one who has judgement over you, and therefore has judgement over your enemy too), **Ilāh** (the sole address of service, and therefore standing over against all the directions the whispering tries to pull you in). The whispering tries to tear the person away from each of these three relations — from the Rabb (ingratitude and despair), from the Malik (objection to the judgement), from the Ilāh (the turning sliding towards another). The three layers of the taking of refuge answer to the three faces of the threat.

I present this reading as my own inference; it is not a classical transmitted report.

**Third: the outer evil is restricted, the inner evil unrestricted.**

In al-Falaq two of the evils are bounded by a restriction of time: darkness **when it settles**, the envier **when he envies**. These evils, that is, are not continuous; they appear in particular states. There are times when there is no darkness; there are times when the envier does not stir.

In al-Nās there is no such restriction. It is not said of the whisperer "when he whispers at such and such a time". The verb is in the imperfect: *yuwaswisu* — he whispers. Continuous, uninterrupted, unconditional.

**This is the sharpest difference between the two sūras.** Outer threats come and pass. The inner threat neither comes nor goes; it is there.

**Fourth: the point of transition between the two sūras is envy.**

The last item of al-Falaq was envy — the **nearest** evil that can come from outside. Coming from an acquaintance, from a brother, from a friend.

The single item of al-Nās, for its part, is the whispering — which does not come from outside, which speaks inside the breast.

al-Falaq, that is, starts the threat at a distance, brings it step by step nearer, and ends at the closest range. al-Nās takes over at exactly that point and crosses the last boundary as well: **inside.** The two sūras together draw a complete map of the danger — a map that begins at the horizon and ends inside the breast.

### A caution

In presenting these symmetries one thing needs to be stated: the structural features that really are present in the text (the number of names, the number of evils, the presence or absence of restrictions of time, the verse-ending letters) and the meanings I have drawn from them are on different levels. The first are objective findings; the second are interpretation, and they are not binding.

There is a limit to drawing theological conclusions from numerical patterns, and that limit is easily overstepped. What is attempted here is to make the structure of the text visible; not to produce hidden meanings out of the structure.

## The place of the sūras in practice

al-Muʿawwidhatān are among the most heavily used texts in the daily practice of Muslim society. In the ḥadīth sources reports are transmitted that these two sūras (sometimes all three, together with al-Ikhlāṣ) were recited in the context of taking refuge:

- In a report coming from ʿUqba b. ʿĀmir and stated to be found in Muslim, it is transmitted that the Prophet said of these two sūras: *"Have you seen the verses sent down this night? The like of them has never been seen."*
- In a report transmitted from ʿĀʾisha in al-Bukhārī, it is related that on going to his bed the Prophet would blow into his palms, recite al-Muʿawwidhāt, and pass his hands over his body.
- Reports are also transmitted that the recitation of these sūras after every prayer was recommended.

I relate these reports naming the source but in language that carries no claim to certainty. This is the widespread opinion about the source of the first two; I have not verified the details.

Beyond the reports, there is a practical reason for this prevalence of the sūras as well: they are short, they are easy to memorise, and they answer directly to what a person most often needs — the need for protection.

## What this says to the present

**First: one does not argue with the whispering; one takes refuge from it.**

The only movement the sūra teaches is to take refuge. It does not teach refuting the whispering, answering it, settling accounts with it.

The reason for this can be understood. The whispering is not a claim; it is a **whisper**. A claim has evidence, is argued over, is refuted. A whisper has no evidence; it is merely repeated. And to answer a thing that is repeated means setting it up in the mind once more.

Anyone who has wrestled with a doubt in his own mind knows this: the moment you find the answer, the question changes shape. Because the matter is not a lack of knowledge. The movement the sūra proposes is to go in another direction — to get up from the table of debate.

**Second: *khannās* — naming it.**

The whole power of the whisperer lies in his not being noticed. The whisper is effective for as long as it is taken for the person's own voice. The difference between "this is my thought" and "this is a thought that has come to me" is the whole of the defence.

This is why the sūra gives the enemy a **name**: the one who whispers, who cowers. To name is to separate. The moment you can separate a thought from yourself, you can look at it; as long as you cannot, it looks out through your eyes.

This is one of the most practical techniques of the inner life, and it is known outside the religious traditions as well: not to identify with the voice that passes through you.

**Third: the whispering and a clinical condition are not the same thing.**

In English, "scrupulosity" carries the same doubling: it names both a religious condition of conscience and, in clinical usage, a pattern of obsessive doubt. These two things must not be confused.

In the tradition of *fiqh*, *waswasa* figures as a topic in its own right — constantly doubting whether one's ablution has been broken, how many cycles of the prayer have been performed, whether the cleaning was complete. The general attitude of the tradition on this matter is worth noting: **no regard is paid to waswasa.** The doubt, that is, is not magnified, the act is not made to be repeated, the matter is not pressed. To feed a doubt strengthens it.

But there is a limit to this. Obsessive thoughts and repetitions that a person cannot control, that make his life unworkable, that take up hours of his day, may be the symptom of a medical condition. What is to be done in that case is not more service and not more taking of refuge but **to consult a physician.** To confuse the two keeps the person who really needs help away from help.

The sūra speaks of a state of the heart; it does not promise the cure of an illness.

**Fourth: "from people" — the most dangerous of all.**

The face the last word of the sūra turns to the present is perhaps the sharpest of all.

The whispering that comes from a human being is dangerous because it is familiar. A word out of a friend's mouth, a word said by an authority, a word implied by an expert, a word passed on with concern by someone you love — none of these is heard as "the voice of an enemy". And precisely for that reason it enters undefended.

The forms of whispering carried out by human hands existed in earlier times too: incitement, needling, the constant instilling of anxiety, the slow erosion of trust, sentences that begin "I only want what is good for you". Today one thing more has been added to these: **scale.** Continuous, uninterrupted, targeted suggestion whose source is most often invisible has now become a technical possibility. The quantity of "words said in secret" a person is exposed to in the course of a day has in no period of history been as high as this.

I do not present this as a prediction made by the verse — the verse says nothing of technology. The **structure** the verse describes serves to recognise a present-day phenomenon: concealment, repetition, a low voice, and the source not showing itself. The measure of the sūra is not technical; it looks at **how the word arrives**.

And one further mirror comes out of this: when the sūra says "from people", one of those people may be the reader himself. What we whisper into whose ear is the question the close of the sūra leaves with a person.

**Fifth: three names, three empty places.**

*Rabb, Malik, Ilāh* — these three names answer to three relations in a human life: **who is bringing me up and watching over me, whose judgement am I under, to what am I attached.**

These three empty places do not stay empty. A person cannot live without taking something as *rabb*, something as *malik*, something as *ilāh* — there is no possibility of deciding about that; the possibility lies in **what** is taken.

A rung of a career can be a *rabb* (you suppose it is raising you, that it is looking after you). Public opinion can be a *malik* (you await judgement from it, you decide according to its approval). A desire can be an *ilāh* (the thing you turn to, for whose sake you give things up, in whose name you change your measure).

By counting these three names one after another the sūra does this: **it fills all the empty places at once.** The whispering targets exactly these empty places; and the taking of refuge is done exactly by closing them.

**Sixth: the Qurʾān ends with the human being.**

The last word of the codex is "people". The Book closes with the name of the being that reads it.

This may be a coincidence; it is a consequence of the arrangement of the codex, and it would not be right to draw a ruling from it. But it brings out a scene that deserves to be recorded: the Qurʾān opens with the name of God and closes with the name of the human being. Everything set out in between — creation, the prophets, the rulings, the narratives, the promises and the warnings — establishes the relation between these two words.

And that the last sentence places the human being both on the side of the one taking refuge and on the side of the threat shows how full of tension this relation is. The Book leaves the human being neither exalted nor condemned. **It leaves him between two possibilities.**

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

- Whether the sūra is Meccan or Medinan is disputed; no preference was stated.
- Two views were given about the origin of the word *nās* (أ-ن-س and ن-و-س); it was stated that the first is the more widespread, but no definite ruling was issued. The view that the word *insān* comes from the root meaning "to forget" was transmitted, and it was stated that it is disputed.
- It was transmitted that "to take refuge" is counted among the meanings of the root of *ilāh*; it was not presented as a settled etymology.
- The noun–verbal noun difference of the word *al-waswās*, resting on the distinction of *fatḥa* and *kasra*, was related as a transmitted report; it was not presented as a settled rule of the language.
- The threefold arrangement built on the differences of preposition of the verb *waswasa* (لـ / إلى / في) is my own frame; it was not presented as a classical transmitted report.
- The "outer courtyard / inner room" distinction between *ṣadr* and *qalb* is a reading resting on al-Ḥajj 22/46. It was stated that this distinction is treated in the classical commentaries, but no particular sentence was ascribed to any particular exegete.
- Two of the four explanations given for the ordering of the three names (the layers of protection and the three faces of the whispering) are my own reading and are not transmitted reports.
- The disagreement about what the preposition *min* in the last verse attaches to was given in a table; the view of the majority was stated, but the others were recorded as well.
- The sources of the reports about the recitation of al-Muʿawwidhatān (Muslim, al-Bukhārī) were stated according to the widespread opinion; their details were not verified and they carry no claim to certainty.
- The comparison of sound built on the verse-ending letters (*qalqala* and *sīn*) is an observation; it has not been presented as a theological or numerical claim.
- The observation made about the first and last words of the Qurʾān is a finding about the arrangement of the codex; no ruling has been drawn from it.
