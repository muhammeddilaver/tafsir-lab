# 71. Sūrat Nūḥ

Twenty-eight verses. It is among the Meccan sūras.

This sūra, which carries the name of Noah (Nūḥ) in the Qurʾān, steps outside the Qurʾān's usual manner of telling a story. The Qurʾān generally tells the story of a prophet **from the outside**: he was sent, he said, they said, this was the end of it. Here, however, the body of the sūra is made up of **a report given in the prophet's own words, addressed to his Lord**.

This determines from the outset how the sūra is to be read. What we have is not a story but **a text of accounting**. Noah enumerates what he did, which methods he tried, what he said, what response he received. It is hard to find a second place in the Qurʾān where a messenger's work is set out in this much detail.

## What is not in the sūra

To see what the sūra does, one must first look at **what it does not do**. Almost none of the best-known elements of the story of Noah are in this sūra:

- **There is no ark.** Its building, its measurements, what was taken into it — none of it is mentioned.
- **There are no animals.**
- **There is no scene of the flood.** The rain, the boiling oven, the mountainous waves — none of it occurs.
- **There is no son of Noah.** One of the most affecting scenes in the Qurʾān (Hūd 11/42-47) is not mentioned here.
- **There is no wife of Noah** (she occurs at al-Taḥrīm 66/10).
- **There are no survivors.** How many believed, what became of them — it is not said.
- **There is no span of time.** The figure of nine hundred and fifty years is not here but at al-ʿAnkabūt 29/14.

All of this exists elsewhere in the Qurʾān, and in detail. So it is not an omission but a **choice**. This sūra does not take the event side of the story; it takes **the effort side**.

And the flood itself occurs in the sūra in **a single word**: *ughriqū* — "they were drowned" (verse 25). The outcome of a struggle spread over centuries is fitted into a single verb in the passive. Twenty verses are given to the effort, one word to the outcome. This proportion says what the sūra is about.

## The structure of the sūra

| Section | Verses | Voice | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The commissioning** | 1 | The narrator (God) | Noah was sent; his task: to warn |
| **The first address** | 2-4 | Noah → his people | I am a clear warner to you; serve, guard yourselves, obey me |
| **Report I: method** | 5-9 | Noah → his Lord | I called night and day; they fled. I tried in secret, in the open, by every route |
| **Report II: the words spoken** | 10-20 | Noah → his Lord (relating what he said to his people) | Ask forgiveness — the return is abundance. Then the proofs: creation, the heaven, the moon, the sun, the plant, the earth |
| **Report III: the outcome** | 21-24 | Noah → his Lord | They opposed me, they followed the rich, they laid a snare, they clung to their idols |
| **The verdict** | 25 | The narrator (God) | They were drowned, they were put into a fire |
| **Two prayers** | 26-28 | Noah → his Lord | Leave no one / forgive me, my parents and the believers |

What is striking in this table is **the changes of voice**. The sūra opens in God's voice, passes at once to Noah, stays with Noah for twenty-three verses, returns to God for a single verse in order to deliver the verdict, and then leaves the last three verses to Noah again.

That is, **the sentence of verdict does not come from Noah's mouth.** Noah relates, asks, prays; but he is not the one who frames the sentence "they were drowned". The boundary between the messenger and the verdict is cut into the grammar of the sūra.

## The backbone of the sūra: "to increase"

The most striking pattern of the sūra is the return, four times and always in the same construction, of the root **ز-ي-د** (to increase): *lā yazīdu … illā* — "it increased / increase nothing but…".

| Verse | Who/what increases | What increases |
|---|---|---|
| **6** | My call, for them | *firār* — flight |
| **21** | His wealth and children, for him | *khasār* — loss |
| **24** | (prayer) For the wrongdoers | *ḍalāl* — straying |
| **28** | (prayer) For the wrongdoers | *tabār* — ruin |

In all four verses the same structure: **something increases, but in the wrong direction.** As the call increases, the flight increases; as the wealth increases, the loss increases.

Against this, in the middle of the sūra, at verse 12, there is an increase God promises, and it comes with a different root: **yumdidkum** (م-د-د — to extend, to multiply, to reinforce). That is, the increase offered and the increase that actually takes place are named with two different words.

I record this as my own reading, not as a transmitted report: the sūra says that *increase in itself* is a neutral thing. There is always something increasing. The question is what it is that increases.

## The second backbone: "to call"

The root **د-ع-و** occurs four times, in verses 5, 6, 7 and 8. And in Arabic this root does two things at once:

- **daʿwa** — to call a person to something
- **duʿāʾ** — to plead with God

What the sūra does is exactly this: **the call (*daʿwa*) Noah makes to his people turns into the call (*duʿāʾ*) he makes to his Lord.** In verse 5 he says "I called my people"; in verse 26 he says "My Lord, do not leave". The same root, two directions. When the horizontal call closes, the vertical call opens.

This may be taken as the sūra summed up in one sentence.

## The historical and textual frame: what we know, and from where

The story of Noah is one of the most frequently mentioned in the Qurʾān; and around it there is a very wide body of narrative outside the Qurʾān. For this reason the boundary must be drawn from the start.

### What the Qurʾān says about Noah

Besides devoting a sūra of twenty-eight verses directly to Noah, the Qurʾān mentions him in many places. The main passages:

- **Hūd 11/25-49** — the most detailed account: the preaching, the people's answer, the building of the ark, the flood, the drowning of his son, Noah's objection and the warning given to him.
- **al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/105-122** — a short and schematic account; the people's objection "it is the rabble who follow you".
- **al-Muʾminūn 23/23-30** — the preaching and the deliverance.
- **Yūnus 10/71-73** — Noah's challenge.
- **al-Ṣāffāt 37/75-82** — a short summary and praise.
- **al-Qamar 54/9-16** — the most concentrated description of the flood.
- **al-ʿAnkabūt 29/14-15** — the information about the span: *"a thousand years less fifty"*.
- **al-Anbiyāʾ 21/76-77**, **al-Aʿrāf 7/59-64**, **Nūḥ 71** (this sūra).

Noah is also mentioned in the Qurʾān **at the head of the chain of prophethood**. al-Nisāʾ 4/163 says that revelation was given to him; al-Shūrā 42/13 says "He has prescribed for you the religion He enjoined upon Noah" — that is, what was given to Noah and what was given to Muḥammad are tied to the same core. At al-Aḥzāb 33/7 he is among those named in the covenant taken from the prophets. al-Isrāʾ 17/3 describes him as *"a much-thankful servant"*.

### What the Qurʾān does not say

Many details found in the popular telling of the story of Noah are not in the Qurʾān:

- Whether the flood covered the whole earth or a particular region **is not stated openly.**
- The measurements of the ark, how many decks it had, how long it took to build, are not there.
- How many believed is not given as a number; Hūd 11/40 says only *"very few"*.
- The name of the place where the people lived does not occur.
- The date of the event is not given.
- Noah's age is not given; the figure given at al-ʿAnkabūt 29/14 is **the time he stayed among his people**, not his lifespan. This distinction is recorded in the classical commentaries too.
- Mount Jūdī is mentioned at Hūd 11/44; but which mountain this is today is not stated in the Qurʾān.

### On the material of report and of history

The wide body of narrative around the story of Noah has two sources: the first is the pre-Islamic Near Eastern narratives and the flood story in the Torah; the second is the forms in which these entered Islamic literature (the material known as *isrāʾīliyyāt*). This material is valuable for historical research; but when it is used in exegesis, which piece of information comes from where has to be kept separate.

The rule I apply in this text is this: **I do not transmit any detail that is not in the Qurʾān as though the Qurʾān said it.** Where I transmit a report, I state it openly.

**On the matter of the flood.** Very ancient texts describing a great flood have been found in Mesopotamian literature, and these bear marked resemblances to the account in the Torah. How this resemblance is to be explained — the trace of a common memory, a literary transmission, different records of the same event — is one of the long disputes of both the history of religions and Assyriology, and no side will be taken here. What must be recorded is this: **there are important structural differences between what the Qurʾān narrates and what these texts narrate.** In the Qurʾān the flood is not the result of a disagreement among gods or of the noise of human beings; it is the result of **the rejection of a long-sustained preaching**. This sūra describes exactly that preaching.

Geologically, the claim of "a single, universal flood" has no counterpart in present-day earth sciences; against this, it is known that great regional inundations have occurred many times in history. I record both of these pieces of information as they stand, and I do not attach the Qurʾānic text to either of them — because the Qurʾān says nothing about the geographical extent of the flood. To speak where the text is silent would be addition, not exegesis.

## 71/1 — إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا نُوحًا إِلَىٰ قَوْمِهِ أَنْ أَنذِرْ قَوْمَكَ مِن قَبْلِ أَن يَأْتِيَهُمْ عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ

*Innā arsalnā Nūḥan ilā qawmihī an andhir qawmaka min qabli an yaʾtiyahum ʿadhābun alīm*

"We sent Noah to his people: 'Warn your people before a painful punishment comes upon them.'"

### إِنَّا أَرْسَلْنَا — "We sent"

The sūra opens with **a particle of emphasis** (*inna*). In Arabic *inna* reinforces the sentence: "truly, indeed". The placing of *inna* at the head of a statement shows that the statement is disputed, or that there is a possibility of the addressee taking it lightly.

What is disputed here? Most probably the institution of messengership itself. At the centre of the objections raised against the Prophet in Mecca stood the question "does God send a human being as a messenger?" The sūra opens by stating in a sentence of emphasis that a human being was sent.

**That the verb is plural** (*arsalnā* — we sent) is the Qurʾān's plural of majesty; it conveys no partner. This is a usage settled in the Qurʾān's narrative language.

The root *r-s-l*: to let loose, to send, to make flow. The analysis of this root was made in Sūrat al-Fīl (105/3); I do not repeat the observation made there. What is to be recorded here is this: the same root will be used again in verse 11 for **the sending of rain** (*yursili'l-samāʾa*). So there are two sendings within the sūra: **the sending of the messenger and the sending of water.** The second is a promise; when it is rejected, it gives way to another water.

### أَنْ أَنذِرْ — "that he warn"

The *an* here is counted in grammar as **the explicative *an***: it opens up the content of the verb before it. "We sent" — to say what? — "that he warn". That is, the content of the task is placed inside the sentence of commissioning.

**Root ن-ذ-ر.** This root was taken up in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/6 and summarised in Sūrat al-Layl (in connection with 92/15); I rely on the observations made there. Briefly: *indhār* is **to give notice of a coming danger before that danger arrives**. There are two conditions inside the word: the danger will be real, and the notice will be given in time.

The verse writes both these conditions **openly within itself**: the danger is specified (*ʿadhābun alīm*), and the timing is specified (*min qabli an yaʾtiyahum* — before it comes upon them). So the lexical definition of the word *indhār* is repeated as a sentence in the rest of the verse.

When the same root was treated in Sūrat al-Nabaʾ (78/40) this observation was recorded: **a warning does not remove the danger — it makes it visible.** This observation summarises the whole of Sūrat Nūḥ in advance. What the sūra describes is a danger made visible and not seen for twenty verses. There is also a difference of word order between the two sūras, and it is meaningful: in al-Nabaʾ *"We have warned you"* (*andharnākum*) — perfect, the work is done; here *"warn"* (*andhir*) — imperative, the work is beginning. One stands at the end of the process, the other at its start.

A note: the verb **لا تذر** (*lā tadhar* — do not leave) that occurs in verses 26 and 27 sounds close to *andhir* but **is not from the same root.** *Andhir* is from the root ن-ذ-ر, *tadhar* from the root و-ذ-ر. I draw no meaning from the resemblance of sound; I record the distinction because it is liable to be confused.

### قَوْمِهِ / قَوْمَكَ — "his people" / "your people"

The same word occurs twice with two different pronouns: first in the narrator's words *qawmihī* (his people), then inside the command *qawmaka* (your people).

This repetition may look unnecessary; it could have said "to them". That it did not does something: **it defines the field of responsibility.** The group Noah is to warn is not a crowd he does not know but his own people — his kin, his neighbours, the people among whom he grew up.

The prophets of the Qurʾān are as a rule sent to their own communities; the Qurʾān gives a ground for this separately: the messenger is someone who speaks the language of the one he addresses and is known by him (Ibrāhīm 14/4). There is a burdening side to this — the hardest people to persuade are those who have known a person since childhood.

**Root ق-و-م**: to stand up, to rise to one's feet, to stand. *Qawm* means originally "those who stand together behind an undertaking". The wide analysis of this root was made in the discussions of al-Fātiḥa (1/6, *mustaqīm*) and al-Bayyina (98/5, *qayyima*).

### عَذَابٌ أَلِيمٌ — "a painful punishment"

**عذاب**, root ع-ذ-ب. The word has an interesting side: from the same root comes *ʿadhb*, which means **sweet water** (in the Qurʾān: al-Furqān 25/53, Fāṭir 35/12 — *ʿadhbun furāt*). The lexicographers explain this relation in different ways; the most widespread explanation is that the root's primary sense is **to cut something off and hold it back**, punishment being "what cuts a person off from life or from ease", and sweet water "the water that cuts off thirst". I give this explanation as a transmitted report, not with a claim of certainty.

**أليم**, root أ-ل-م: to feel pain. *Alīm* is on the *faʿīl* pattern. In Arabic this pattern can carry both the active sense (causing pain) and the passive (suffering pain); here it is the active: a punishment that hurts.

And the word comes **indefinite**: *ʿadhābun alīm* — "**a** painful punishment". The indefiniteness here conveys magnification (*taʿẓīm*): a punishment whose description is not given, whose limit is not drawn. Part of the effect of the warning comes from this indefiniteness; a threat whose nature is not stated stands heavier than one described.

### Context — the first sentence of the sūra sets up a time

The last part of the verse — *min qabli an yaʾtiyahum* — frames the whole sūra. Everything that will be narrated through the sūra takes place inside this "before".

And in verse 25 the sūra will state the moment that "before" ended, in one word: *ughriqū*. That is, the *ʿadhābun alīm* of the first verse and the drowning of verse 25 are the same thing standing at the two ends of the sūra. The twenty-three verses in between are **the content of the term granted.**

## 71/2-4 — Noah's first address

**2.** قَالَ يَا قَوْمِ إِنِّي لَكُمْ نَذِيرٌ مُّبِينٌ
*Qāla yā qawmi innī lakum nadhīrun mubīn*
"He said: O my people! I am to you a clear warner."

**3.** أَنِ اعْبُدُوا اللَّهَ وَاتَّقُوهُ وَأَطِيعُونِ
*Ani'budu'llāha wa'ttaqūhu wa aṭīʿūn*
"Serve God, guard yourselves against Him, and obey me."

**4.** يَغْفِرْ لَكُم مِّن ذُنُوبِكُمْ وَيُؤَخِّرْكُمْ إِلَىٰ أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى ۚ إِنَّ أَجَلَ اللَّهِ إِذَا جَاءَ لَا يُؤَخَّرُ ۖ لَوْ كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ
*Yaghfir lakum min dhunūbikum wa yuʾakhkhirkum ilā ajalin musammā; inna ajala'llāhi idhā jāʾa lā yuʾakhkhar; law kuntum taʿlamūn*
"That He may forgive you some of your sins and defer you to an appointed term. Surely when God's appointed term comes it is not deferred — if only you knew."

### يَا قَوْمِ — "O my people"

The address begins with a declaration of belonging. Not "O mankind" but "O my people". And the possessive ending (*yā qawmī* → *yā qawmi*) is shortened; this is a common lightening in Arabic address and lends intimacy to the speech.

That Noah does not place himself outside the people is a stance kept up to the end of the sūra. Even in verse 28 — when his people have been destroyed — he will ask forgiveness for his own parents.

### نَذِيرٌ مُّبِينٌ — "a clear warner"

*Nadhīr* is from the same root as *andhir* in verse 1. The command has become an attribute: the man commanded to warn introduces himself as "a warner". The task has turned into an identity.

**مبين**, root ب-ي-ن: to open the space between two things, to separate. The wide analysis of this root was made in Sūrat al-Bayyina (*bayyina* — the proof that distinguishes). *Mubīn* is the active participle of Form IV, and in Arabic this pattern carries both a **transitive** sense (making clear, distinguishing) and an **intransitive** one (being itself clear).

Both senses work here:

- **A warner who makes clear**: one who does not hide what he warns of, who does not speak obliquely.
- **A clear warner**: one whose position is evident, whose identity is in the open.

The rest of the sūra supports the first: in verse 8 Noah will say *jihār* (openly), in verse 9 *aʿlantu* (I announced). So "mubīn" is like a promise, and its carrying out is described in the verses that follow.

### Three commands: service, guarding, obedience

Verse 3 lists three verbs, and the order is not idle:

| Command | Root | Its direction |
|---|---|---|
| **uʿbudū'llāh** | ع-ب-د | To God — a positive bond |
| **ittaqūh** | و-ق-ي | To God — a protective distance |
| **aṭīʿūn** | ط-و-ع | To the messenger — the intermediate link |

The root of **عبادة** is ع-ب-د: a road's being levelled by being trodden over and over (*ṭarīqun muʿabbad*). The root's primary picture is **to walk with the neck bent**. Its analysis was made at al-Fātiḥa 1/5.

The root of **تقوى** is و-ق-ي: to protect, to take shelter. *Taqwā* is not fearing but **guarding oneself**; there is a shield inside the word. This distinction was treated in the discussion of al-Baqara.

The root of **طاعة** is ط-و-ع: to do willingly, to comply from the heart. Its opposite is *karh* (unwillingly). Inside the word there is **the absence of compulsion** — in Arabic what is made to be done by force is not called *ṭāʿa*.

The presence of the third command is striking and has been discussed in the classical commentaries: why "obey me"? The answer given is reasonable and fits the text: obedience to the messenger is directed not to the messenger's person but to **the message he brings**; because there is no other way of reaching the message. And indeed Noah asks nothing here for himself — no property, no precedence, no reward. It is recorded that elsewhere in the Qurʾān Noah says openly that he asks no wage (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/109; Yūnus 10/72; Hūd 11/29).

### مِّن ذُنُوبِكُمْ — "of your sins"

There is a grammatical subtlety here and the classical sources have dwelt on it. The verse does not say "that He forgive you your sins"; it says **"that He forgive you of your sins"**. A *min* has come in between.

In Arabic there are three readings of the *min* here:

| Reading | Its meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **Baʿḍiyya** (partitive) | "some of your sins" | *Min* most commonly conveys a part in Arabic |
| **Zāʾida** (redundant) | "your sins" — the *min* adds nothing to the sense | In negative and conditional contexts *min* can be redundant |
| **Bayāniyya** (explicative) | "the things that are of the kind of sin" | It specifies the kind of what is forgiven |

Those who defend the first reading give this explanation: sins involving the rights of others are a category separate from sins committed against God alone; believing removes the second, it does not by itself drop the first. This is an explanation transmitted in the classical commentaries and it is reasonable; but it does not follow necessarily from the wording of the verse.

A comparison worth recording: the Qurʾān does not use *min* in a similar place when addressing Muḥammad. Whether this difference is meaningful has been disputed; I issue no definite ruling.

### أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى — "an appointed term"

**أجل**, root أ-ج-ل: the term granted for a matter, a due date. In commerce it is used for the term of a debt. **مسمى**, root س-م-و/س-م-ي: named, appointed.

The verse here appears to draw a distinction between **two kinds of appointed term**, and this is the subject of a long dispute in the history of theology:

- What Noah promises: *"He will defer you to an appointed term"* — that is, if you are not destroyed you will live out your normal span.
- The note that follows: *"when God's appointed term comes it is not deferred"* — that is, the final limit does not change.

The relation between the two sentences has been read in the classical sources in two principal ways:

**The first reading:** There are two separate terms — one conditional (the term of destruction), one absolute (the natural term). If the warning is heeded the first does not come about, and one lives until the second.

**The second reading:** The term is single and unchanging; the expression "deferral" is a language taken from the human point of view. Those who defend this reading say that the term is single in God's knowledge.

The theological depth of this dispute (destiny, the appointed term, divine knowledge) exceeds the limits of this text. What the verse itself does is this: **it presents an offer and says in the same sentence that the offer's term is limited.** That is, the promise is not open-ended.

### لَوْ كُنتُمْ تَعْلَمُونَ — "if only you knew"

The verse ends with a sentence left unfinished. In Arabic *law* (if) is a conditional particle and normally requires an apodosis: "if you knew… you would do such and such." The apodosis **is not said.**

In rhetoric this is a deliberate ellipsis (*ḥadhf*): the withholding of the apodosis leaves the addressee to complete it himself. And every answer that could be completed is wider than one that has been said.

English does the same thing: we say "If you only knew…" and fall silent. The silence is part of the sentence.

### What this says to the present

Verses 2-4 give **the complete structure** of an offer: who is speaking (a warner), what is asked (three verbs), what the return is (forgiveness and time), and what the limit is (it ends when the term comes).

What is striking is that the offer is built **not on fear but on gain**. After the punishment is mentioned in verse 1, Noah's first sentence is not a threat but an offer. This will become clearer still in the rest of the sūra: what is promised in verses 10-12 will be directly **worldly abundance**.

## 71/5-6 — قَالَ رَبِّ إِنِّي دَعَوْتُ قَوْمِي لَيْلًا وَنَهَارًا · فَلَمْ يَزِدْهُمْ دُعَائِي إِلَّا فِرَارًا

*Qāla Rabbi innī daʿawtu qawmī laylan wa nahārā · Fa-lam yazidhum duʿāʾī illā firārā*

"He said: My Lord! I called my people night and day. But my call served only to increase their flight."

### The scene changes

Something happens at verse 5 and the whole character of the sūra changes: **the addressee changes.** In verses 2-4 Noah was speaking to his people; here he turns to his Lord.

And this turn takes place without any narrative bridge in between. There is no sentence like "long years passed", "at last he gave up hope". Only *qāla* (he said) is repeated and the address changes direction.

In rhetoric a change in the direction of address is called **iltifāt**. What the *iltifāt* does here is this: all the time in between is erased. The reader jumps straight from the beginning of the preaching to its report. How long it lasted — nine hundred and fifty years — is never said in this sūra; it is said in another sūra (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/14).

### دَعَوْتُ — "I called"

**Root د-ع-و**: to call out, to summon, to invite. The concrete picture of the root is **trying to reach someone far off with the voice**.

This root has two great branches in Arabic and both are used in this sūra:

- **daʿwa / daʿwat** — to call a person to something. It is in this sense in this verse and in verse 8.
- **duʿāʾ** — to plead with God. It is in this sense in the last three verses of the sūra.

And these are not two separate words; they are **two directions of the same word.** That Noah uses the same verb throughout his work may be taken as a silent observation of the sūra: the messenger is the one who calls continuously; his direction changes, his verb does not.

A detail: *daʿawtu* is first person singular and **in the perfect**. Noah is reporting a finished work. This determines the character of the sūra — this is not a prayer but first of all **a declaration**. The prayer will come in verse 26.

### لَيْلًا وَنَهَارًا — "night and day"

Both words are **indefinite** and **in the accusative** (as adverbials); that is, not "a particular night and day" but "by night and by day" — a usage conveying continuity.

Note the order: **night first.** This order is widespread in Arabic and in the Qurʾān (*"in the succession of night and day"*, al-Baqara 2/164; Āl ʿImrān 3/190). But it is meaningful here in addition: the night is the hard time for a call. The time when people are asleep, when no one is open to listening. Noah counts the hard time first, not the easy one.

I record this as my own reading. In the classical commentaries there is agreement that the phrase means "without a break, continuously"; drawing a further meaning from the order is not widespread.

### فَلَمْ يَزِدْهُمْ دُعَائِي إِلَّا فِرَارًا — "nothing but their flight"

One of the most striking sentences of the sūra and the first "increase" sentence.

**Word order first.** The normal order of the sentence is *fa-lam yazidhum duʿāʾī* — verb, object, subject. The subject (*duʿāʾī* — my call) has been **thrown to the end**. In Arabic postponement (*taʾkhīr*) most often conveys emphasis; and the place the emphasis falls here is the **cause** of the increase: what made them flee was not a pressure from outside but **the call itself.**

The construction **لم يزدهم إلا** — "it did not increase, except this" — sets up *ḥaṣr* (restriction) in Arabic. That is, the **only** effect of the call was flight. Not even a partial success is stated.

**فرار**, root ف-ر-ر: to flee. There is an interesting detail the lexicographers transmit about the concrete sense of the root: from the same root comes *farra'l-dābba* — **to open and look at an animal's teeth** to determine its age. From here the sense *farr* (to bring into the open) is derived, and it is said that fleeing too is a kind of "coming out / separating". I give this explanation as a transmitted report; I make no claim of certainty.

The best-known use of this root in the Qurʾān is in the description of the dog in the story of the Cave (*"you would have turned from them in flight"*, al-Kahf 18/18) and in the context of war. Here the flight is **not physical** — the people do not disappear, they go on living in the same city. The flight is a flight **from hearing**. And indeed the next verse will describe its technique: fingers in the ears.

### The preaching having the opposite effect

What these two verses set out is a phenomenon generally not taken seriously enough in religious literature: **a well-intentioned and correct call can drive the one addressed away.**

The Qurʾān records this in many places and treats it as a mechanism. One of the clearest expressions is this: *"We send down of the Qurʾān what is a healing and a mercy for the believers; but it increases the wrongdoers only in loss"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/82). The same structure: *lā yazīdu … illā*.

The point here is this: the same text produces two different results in two people. The difference is not in the text but **in the party who encounters it.**

The consequence of this for the messenger is heavy: the outcome itself cannot be the measure of the effort. If it were, Noah's report would be a report of failure. The sūra does not present it that way — Noah gives his account, he is not shamed.

### What this says to the present

These two verses describe a phenomenon familiar to anyone who has thought about persuasion. The insistent repetition of a view most often produces in the other party not reconsideration but **defence**. A person protects not the idea that is objected to but **himself**, the one objected to; and when he does that, the first thing he does is to stop hearing.

The sūra sets this down not as an excuse but as **an observation of reality**. Noah does not stop calling; he only names correctly what is happening. And in the next verse he changes his method.

## 71/7 — وَإِنِّي كُلَّمَا دَعَوْتُهُمْ لِتَغْفِرَ لَهُمْ جَعَلُوا أَصَابِعَهُمْ فِي آذَانِهِمْ وَاسْتَغْشَوْا ثِيَابَهُمْ وَأَصَرُّوا وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا اسْتِكْبَارًا

*Wa innī kullamā daʿawtuhum li-taghfira lahum jaʿalū aṣābiʿahum fī ādhānihim wa'staghshaw thiyābahum wa aṣarrū wa'stakbarū'stikbārā*

"Every time I called them, so that You might forgive them, they put their fingers in their ears, wrapped themselves in their garments, persisted, and grew proud beyond all pride."

This is the densest verse in the sūra. In a single sentence **four separate acts of defence** are enumerated; two bodily, two mental.

### كُلَّمَا — "every time I called them"

**كلما** conveys repetition: every time, whenever. That is, what is described is not an event but **a settled reflex**. Noah does not call once and receive this response once; an automatic link has been established between call and response.

This is the ground for **the change of method** that will be described in the next two verses: one who cannot break a fixed response changes his method.

### لِتَغْفِرَ لَهُمْ — "so that You might forgive them"

This parenthetical clause is easily missed but it is the moral centre of the verse.

Noah reminds his Lord of **the purpose** of his call: I called them so that You would forgive them. That is, the call was not an attempt to establish superiority, to gather a following, to gain something; it was a work **in their favour**.

Note: the verb is second person singular — *li-taghfira*, "so that You might forgive". Noah ascribes the work of forgiving neither to himself nor to something the people would do afterwards, but directly to God. Forgiving is not in the messenger's hands; only calling is in his hands.

The analysis of the root *gh-f-r* was made in Sūrat al-Naṣr (110/3): **to cover, to close over**; the Turkish *miğfer* ("helmet") is a survival of the same root. I rely on the observation made there: forgiveness is not counting what happened as not having happened, but covering it.

This root returns three times in the sūra and each time in a different position:

| Verse | Construction | Who does it |
|---|---|---|
| **7** | *li-taghfira lahum* | Noah's intention: that God forgive them |
| **10** | *istaghfirū rabbakum* | A command to the people: you ask |
| **28** | *rabbi'ghfir lī* | Noah asks for himself |

So forgiveness is first hoped for on another's behalf, then others are taught to ask for it, and last of all the person asks for himself. The sūra closes on that sequence.

### The four defences

Now let us look at the four acts one by one. The order itself sets up a line of ascent.

#### 1. جَعَلُوا أَصَابِعَهُمْ فِي آذَانِهِمْ — they put their fingers in their ears

The most primitive, the most bodily defence. The gesture children make.

**اصبع** (*iṣbaʿ* / *uṣbuʿ*), finger. Its plural is *aṣābiʿ*. **أذن** (*udhun*), ear; its plural is *ādhān*. From this root comes *idhn* (permission) — according to the lexicographers the link is that to give permission is to "lend an ear".

The verb is **جعل**: they put, they made. It does not say "they closed" or "they stopped up"; it says **they put**. The neutrality of the verb makes the deliberateness of the act still clearer: this is not a flinch but a placing.

**This gesture occurs once more in the Qurʾān, in a very familiar scene.** At al-Baqara 2/19, in the second likeness of the hypocrites: people caught in a downpour bursting from the sky put their fingers in their ears against the thunderclaps, in fear of death. In the discussion of al-Baqara this observation was made about that gesture: *"This act, done to protect against the thunder, is useless — shutting out the sound does not stop the lightning. Not hearing the warning does not remove the danger warned of."*

That observation holds exactly here, but there is a difference and the difference matters:

| | **al-Baqara 2/19** | **Nūḥ 71/7** |
|---|---|---|
| The sound shut out | The noise of thunder | The messenger's call |
| The motive | Fear | Rejection |
| The situation | The danger has begun | The danger has not yet come |

The people in al-Baqara stop their ears **in the midst of the punishment**; Noah's people stop theirs **before the punishment**, so as not to hear the warning. The second is heavier, because what is shut out is not the sound of the danger but the sound of the way out of it.

**And the exact opposite of this gesture is in another sūra where the same event is narrated.** Sūrat al-Ḥāqqa mentions the flood in two verses and ties it thus: *"that We might make it a reminder for you and that a retaining ear might retain it"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/12). In the discussion of al-Ḥāqqa the phrase *udhunun wāʿiya* (a retaining ear) was dwelt on and this observation was made: everyone has the organ, not everyone has the function.

When the two verses are set side by side the two ends of the story of Noah become visible: inside the story there are **ears stopped with fingers**, and at the story's target **a retaining ear**. The same event is narrated between two ears, one closing and one opening.

And I record this as an observation — as my own reading: the scene of the likeness in al-Baqara is **a downpour**, and what comes upon Noah's people is likewise **water**. The picture set up as a comparison in al-Baqara stands here in its realised form.

#### 2. وَاسْتَغْشَوْا ثِيَابَهُمْ — they wrapped themselves in their garments

The second act is an extension of the first. The ear was not enough; the whole body has been covered.

**Root غ-ش-ي.** The analysis of this root was made in Sūrat al-Ghāshiya (88/1) and in the discussion of al-Shams (91/4); I do not repeat the observations made there. In summary: the concrete sense of the root is **to cover something over, to wrap it, to come over it and envelop it**; *ghishāwa* is the veil over the eye (al-Baqara 2/7).

The verb here is in **Form X**: *istaghshā*. In Arabic this form conveys "to seek" or "to do for oneself". So *istaghshaw thiyābahum* does not mean "their garments covered them"; it means **"they made their garments a covering for themselves"**. The verb is a covering performed by the subject's own hand.

In the classical commentaries this expression is seen to be explained in two ways, and both fit the language:

**The first: to cover the face.** To pull the garment over the head and cover the face — both so as not to see and so as not to be recognised. Not to see Noah, and not to appear to him.

**The second: to wrap the head so as to cover the ears too.** This reading is taken as a continuation of the first act: when the finger is not enough, cloth.

A third explanation is also transmitted: the covering is the symbolic expression of **shame or of turning away in obstinacy**. This is a reading drawn not from the wording of the word but from the context.

**Another use of the same root within the Qurʾān illuminates this verse:** at Hūd 11/5, of those who wish to hide from the Prophet, the expression *"they wrap themselves in their garments"* (*yastaghshūna thiyābahum*) occurs — almost exactly the same construction. That is, the Qurʾān records this gesture not as an oddity peculiar to Noah's people but as **a recurring human behaviour**.

**ثياب**, root ث-و-ب: to return. *Thawb* (garment), *thawāb* (recompense), *mathāba* (a place returned to) are all from this root. The explanation the lexicographers give: cloth took this name because in being woven the thread has "returned" to its first state. I give this explanation as a transmitted report.

#### 3. وَأَصَرُّوا — they persisted

With the third act the body is left behind and the mind is entered.

**Root ص-ر-ر.** Its concrete sense: **to tie, to knot tightly**. *Ṣurra* means a money purse tied at the mouth. From the same root come *ṣarīr* (a creak, the sound of friction) and *ṣarṣar* (a howling, freezing wind — Fuṣṣilat 41/16, al-Qamar 54/19).

**إصرار**, in Form IV: to bind oneself to a course, not to turn back from it, to persist in it. The Turkish "ısrar" ("insistence") is directly this word.

The root's picture of "tying a purse" describes the word very well: the one who persists has put his decision into a purse and tied its mouth. Nothing new gets in.

The Qurʾān uses this word in one other place and there its sense becomes clear: *"and they do not knowingly persist in what they have done"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/135). That is, *iṣrār* is not the fault itself but **the stance taken after the fault.** A fault is an event; persistence is a position.

**Note:** This verb (*aṣarrū*, ص-ر-ر) and the *asrartu* to come in verse 9 (أسررت, س-ر-ر — to conceal) **are not from the same root.** The resemblance in sound is close and they are confused; I record the distinction here because they do two different works in two different places of the sūra.

#### 4. وَاسْتَكْبَرُوا اسْتِكْبَارًا — they grew proud beyond all pride

The fourth and last act. The top of the series.

**Root ك-ب-ر**, in **Form X**. The analysis of this pattern was made at al-Baqara 2/34 — in the discussion of Iblīs's refusal to prostrate. What was established there was this: Form X conveys "to seek a thing or to reckon it so"; therefore *istikbār* is not **being** great but **reckoning oneself** great; and pride is born of a comparison.

I do not repeat that analysis. Two things need adding here:

**First: the cognate accusative.** The verse does not say *wa'stakbarū* and leave it; it adds after it the verbal noun from the same root: *istikbārā*. In Arabic the coming of a verb's own verbal noun after the verb is called the **cognate accusative** and its function is **emphasis**: "they grew proud — and how they grew proud."

English has no exact counterpart; the nearest is an intensifying repetition — "they grew proud, and how proud they grew". This pattern of the language conveys not the degree of the verb but **its certainty and its force**.

**This construction is a marked feature of this sūra.** It occurs in five places:

| Verse | Verb + verbal noun |
|---|---|
| **7** | *wa'stakbarū'stikbārā* — they grew proud beyond all pride |
| **9** | *wa asrartu lahum isrārā* — I spoke to them in deepest secret |
| **17** | *anbatakum mina'l-arḍi nabātā* — He made you grow from the earth as a growing thing |
| **18** | *wa yukhrijukum ikhrājā* — He will bring you out, and how He will bring you out |
| **22** | *wa makarū makran kubbārā* — they laid a snare, a snare of the greatest |

Five cognate accusatives in a sūra of twenty-eight verses is above the ordinary. Throughout the language of the sūra there is an emphasis on **force and certainty**; both in the people's resistance and in God's act. I record this as an observation.

**Second: the logic of the series.** The four acts mount one upon another and draw an ascent:

1. **The finger** — block the sound (momentary, bodily, from outside)
2. **The garment** — cut off all contact (comprehensive, still bodily)
3. **Persistence** — lock the decision (mental, from inside)
4. **Pride** — fix the position (at the level of identity)

As one goes from the bottom upward, the act becomes more lasting, more inward and less reversible. The first two can be done in a moment and then dropped; the last two are a state of character.

And that the series closes with **pride** is no accident. The other three are the **applications** of pride: the man who counts himself superior does not listen, does not make contact, does not reconsider his opinion. By putting the cause last, the sūra explains the preceding three.

### What this says to the present

These four acts are like a complete inventory of closing oneself to a piece of knowledge, and none of them has grown old. Their present-day counterparts differ technically and are identical in structure: silencing the source, narrowing the field of contact, repeating one's position, and turning the discussion into a matter of status.

The distinguishing side of the verse is that it names these not as **ignorance** but as **defence**. The person who puts his finger in his ear knows there is a sound there. The one who covers himself knows there is someone before him. The one who persists knows there is a contrary view. All four show not **the absence** of the knowledge but that it **is not wanted**.

This distinction is the diagnosis of the whole sūra and it coincides with the Qurʾān's general diagnosis: the matter is not not-knowing but covering what is known (treated in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/6).

## 71/8-9 — ثُمَّ إِنِّي دَعَوْتُهُمْ جِهَارًا · ثُمَّ إِنِّي أَعْلَنتُ لَهُمْ وَأَسْرَرْتُ لَهُمْ إِسْرَارًا

*Thumma innī daʿawtuhum jihārā · Thumma innī aʿlantu lahum wa asrartu lahum isrārā*

"Then I called them openly. Then I both proclaimed to them aloud and spoke to them in deepest secret."

### Variation of method

These two verses are the most practical part of the sūra. Noah reports that he did not persist in a single method.

In the classical commentaries **a three-stage sequence of preaching** is drawn from these verses, and the order of the sequence is disputed. The clearest table that emerges from the wording of the verses is this:

| Stage | Expression | Form |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Verse 5: *daʿawtu qawmī laylan wa nahārā* | A general call — emphasis on duration |
| **2** | Verse 8: *daʿawtuhum jihārā* | Openly, before everyone |
| **3** | Verse 9: *aʿlantu … wa asrartu* | Both open and secret — the two together |

That is, the third stage is not a new method but **the combining of two methods.** It is possible to read it thus: the messenger first makes a general call; when no response comes he makes it public; when that too fails he opens **two channels at once** — both address to the group and one-to-one speech.

On the order there are also different readings in the classical sources. Some exegetes hold that the secret call came **first** and the open call afterwards, and count the ordering in verse 9 as one of scope rather than of time. The ground supporting this view is that the Qurʾān records a similar order in Muḥammad's preaching too (*"And warn your nearest kin"*, al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/214 — from the narrow circle to the wide). I make no definite preference; the wording of the verse is open to both readings.

### ثُمَّ — "then"

Both verses begin with *thumma*. In Arabic *thumma*, unlike *fa*, is a conjunction that **leaves an interval**: "then, after some time has passed".

Had it been *fa*, the acts would have followed one another directly; *thumma* puts time between them. That is, Noah does not change his method at once; he tries each method at length, then passes to the next. The two *thumma*s are the only trace in the text of those nine hundred and fifty years the sūra never states.

### جِهَارًا — openly

**Root ج-ه-ر**: to raise the voice, to bring into the open, to say without concealing. From the same root come *jahr* (recitation aloud — the "audible" recitation in prayer is this word) and *jahīr* (loud-voiced).

The root's primary field is **sound**, but in Arabic it is used for "making visible to the eye" as well. Both uses occur in the Qurʾān: *"Though you speak the word aloud…"* (Ṭāhā 20/7) and *"until we see God plainly"* (al-Baqara 2/55; al-Nisāʾ 4/153).

The word here is **accusative and indefinite**; it can be read as a circumstantial accusative or as a cognate accusative. The sense is close on either reading: "I called them out in the open".

### أَعْلَنتُ — I proclaimed

**Root ع-ل-ن**: the coming into the open of what was hidden. *ʿAlāniya* is the opposite of secrecy. The Qurʾān uses these two words as a pair a great deal: *"they spend in secret and in the open"* (al-Baqara 2/274; Ibrāhīm 14/31; al-Raʿd 13/22).

The difference between *jihār* and *iʿlān* is fine: *jihār* looks more to **loudness of voice**, *iʿlān* more to **the announcing itself**. It is like the difference in English between "I shouted it out" and "I announced it".

### وَأَسْرَرْتُ لَهُمْ إِسْرَارًا — I spoke to them in deepest secret

**Root س-ر-ر**: to conceal. *Sirr* (a secret), *sarīra* (what is kept within), *isrār* (keeping secret) are all from this root.

A note on the language: from the same root comes *surūr* (joy) as well, and the lexicographers explain this relation by "joy's touching the hidden place of the heart". I give this explanation as a transmitted report.

Again there is a **cognate accusative**: *asrartu … isrārā*. That is, the secrecy is not casual but deliberate and intense.

### What these verses really say: why the secret call is necessary

When the two verses are read together a principle of method emerges, and this principle is concrete for the present too.

**The limit of the open call is this:** the person spoken to before a crowd becomes the representative of his own group. His answer is now shaped not by himself but by those watching. To accept means to take a step back in front of everyone — and most people shrink less from changing an opinion than from changing it before an audience.

**This is what the secret call does:** it separates the person from the audience. A person spoken to one-to-one can think on his own behalf. The four defences described in verse 7 — pride above all — largely presuppose an **audience**. When the audience is removed, the cost of the defence falls.

I present this as a reading I draw from the verse, not as a transmitted report. But the arrangement of the sūra supports this reading: pride is mentioned in verse 7, the secret call comes in verse 9.

Note: the "secrecy" here is not hiding or covering the message. In the same verse Noah says that he **proclaimed** as well. What is hidden is not the message but **the setting of the conversation.**

## 71/10-12 — Asking forgiveness and its return

**10.** فَقُلْتُ اسْتَغْفِرُوا رَبَّكُمْ إِنَّهُ كَانَ غَفَّارًا
*Fa-qultu'staghfirū rabbakum innahū kāna ghaffārā*
"So I said: ask forgiveness of your Lord; He is much-forgiving."

**11.** يُرْسِلِ السَّمَاءَ عَلَيْكُم مِّدْرَارًا
*Yursili'l-samāʾa ʿalaykum midrārā*
"He will send the heaven upon you in abundant rain."

**12.** وَيُمْدِدْكُم بِأَمْوَالٍ وَبَنِينَ وَيَجْعَل لَّكُمْ جَنَّاتٍ وَيَجْعَل لَّكُمْ أَنْهَارًا
*Wa yumdidkum bi-amwālin wa banīna wa yajʿal lakum jannātin wa yajʿal lakum anhārā*
"And He will reinforce you with wealth and sons, and make gardens for you, and make rivers for you."

### The position of the verses

With verse 10 the sūra enters a new layer. Until now Noah was relating **what he did**; from here on he relates **what he said**. That is, the report turns into direct quotation.

This is the most technical side of the sūra and it must not be missed: verses 10-20 are **speech within a report**. Noah repeats to his Lord what he said to his people. There are three layers: the Qurʾān narrates → Noah gives his report → what Noah said to his people.

### اسْتَغْفِرُوا — "ask forgiveness"

The analysis of *istighfār* was made in Sūrat al-Naṣr (110/3); I rely on the observations made there. Briefly: the root *gh-f-r* is **to cover**; Form X conveys **seeking**; therefore *istighfār* is **to request being covered** — the servant does not do something here, he asks that something be done.

What needs adding here is the **position** of *istighfār* in this sūra.

In Sūrat al-Naṣr *istighfār* was an act of **ending**: after the work was completed, the covering of the shortfalls during that work was asked for. Here, by contrast, *istighfār* is an act of **beginning**: it is proposed to a community that has not yet believed, as the first thing to be done.

This difference is important. *Istighfār* here is not an act of worship for the devout but **the way of coming in at the door.** Noah does not first give his people a list of articles of belief; he says "ask that your past be covered". That is, the offer begins with the lifting of the burden of the past.

### إِنَّهُ كَانَ غَفَّارًا — "He is much-forgiving"

**غفار** is on the *faʿʿāl* pattern. In Arabic this pattern conveys **intensification** and **continuity**: one who does a thing much, who is a master of it. On the same pattern: *razzāq* (giver of much provision), *tawwāb* (much accepting of repentance), *qahhār*.

The classical sources distinguish *Ghafūr* from *Ghaffār* thus: *ghafūr* (*faʿūl*) conveys **depth** — it covers even a very great sin; *ghaffār* (*faʿʿāl*) conveys **repetition** — it covers the same sin of the same person again and again. This distinction is widely made in the traditional books on language; there are also views that count both patterns as absolute.

The choice of *ghaffār* in this context is meaningful: those addressed are a community that has been repeating the same refusal for centuries. They are told not "He forgives once" but **"He forgives again and again"**.

The function of the verb **كان** here must be recorded separately. It could have said *innahū ghafūrun*; it says *innahū kāna ghaffārā*. In Arabic *kāna* used of God does not convey past time; it conveys **a continuing, unchanging attribute**. To render it with "was" in English is misleading. Its meaning is this: this is not a stance He took up later; it has been so from the beginning.

### يُرْسِلِ — the apodosis

Verse 11 is in the **jussive** (with a silent ending): *yursil*, *yumdid*, *yajʿal*. This is the state taken in Arabic by verbs that are the answer to a command.

So the grammar sets it up thus: **"Ask forgiveness — and if you do, these things follow."** No conditional particle is written, but the jussive following the command has already set up the condition. It is the same thing English does in "come, let us talk": the second verb is the result of the first.

This grammatical detail determines how the verses are to be read. The abundance enumerated is **the return** for asking forgiveness — not an independent promise added alongside it.

### السَّمَاء — "the heaven"

The verse does not say "may He send rain"; it says **"may He send the heaven"**. This is a widespread figure in Arabic: the name of the vessel is given to what is in it (in the language of rhetoric, *majāz mursal*, by the relation of place).

But the choice is not idle. Had it said "rain", a single event would have been spoken of; when it says "heaven", **the whole of the source** is named. Abundance comes not from one fall of rain but from the continuous working of the heaven.

Root **س-م-و**: to rise. *Samāʾ* means "that which is above"; in Arabic anything above a thing can be called its *samāʾ* — the ceiling of a house too. From the same root come *ism* (a name: that which raises a thing and makes it distinct), *sumuww* (loftiness).

### مِّدْرَارًا — "in abundance"

One of the finest choices of word in the sūra.

**Root د-ر-ر.** The concrete and primary sense of the root is **the flowing of milk abundantly and without interruption**. *Darrat-i nāqa* — the camel's milk flowed abundantly. *Dirra* is the flow of milk; *dārr* is an animal rich in milk. The Arabs spoke of a fruitful camel with this word: one that gives the more it is milked, that does not run dry.

**مدرار** is on the *mifʿāl* pattern. In Arabic this pattern does two things: it forms the **noun of instrument** (*miftāḥ* — key, *mīzān* — scales) and it conveys **intensification** (one who does a thing continuously and intensely). Here it is in the second sense: "pouring without cease".

So the picture the verse sets up is this: **the heaven, like an animal being milked.** Rain is named not as water poured from the sky but as *milk flowing* from the sky.

One must see how concrete this comparison was for the one addressed. For a community living in a desert and semi-arid region there are two things that keep life standing: rain and the milk-giving animal. The verse joins the two in a single word.

The word occurs in three places in the Qurʾān and in all three in the same context — abundance promised in return for asking forgiveness or for faith: here, at al-Anʿām 6/6 (of the peoples destroyed: "We sent down upon them abundant rain from the sky") and at Hūd 11/52.

### An exact parallel with Hūd 11/52

The most striking side of these three verses is that they are repeated in almost the same sentences in another place in the Qurʾān. In Sūrat Hūd, the prophet Hūd says to his own people:

> *"O my people! Ask forgiveness of your Lord, then turn to Him in repentance; He will send the heaven upon you in abundant rain and will add strength to your strength."* (Hūd 11/52)

The words are shared: *istaghfirū rabbakum*, *yursili'l-samāʾa ʿalaykum midrārā*, and a verb of "increase". Two different prophets, to two different peoples, speaking with the same formula.

The Qurʾān makes the same link a third time, with its own addressee:

> *"Ask forgiveness of your Lord, then turn to Him in repentance; He will let you enjoy a good provision for an appointed term."* (Hūd 11/3)

And as a general rule:

> *"Had the people of those towns believed and guarded themselves, We would have opened upon them blessings from the heaven and the earth."* (al-Aʿrāf 7/96)

This repetition says something: **the link between asking forgiveness and worldly abundance is not a motif peculiar to this sūra; it is a principle the Qurʾān repeats.**

### وَيُمْدِدْكُم بِأَمْوَالٍ وَبَنِينَ — wealth and sons

**Root م-د-د**: to extend, to draw out, to sustain, to reinforce. From the same root come *madad* (aid), *imdād* (sending reinforcement), *mudda* (a span of time).

The picture of the word is **extending** — drawing a thing out and continuing it. That is, wealth and children are mentioned not as something "given" but as something "extended": like the continuation of a line.

This is a fine choice in the context of the sūra. The verb of "increase" (*zāda*) that occurs in four places in the sūra always conveyed a negative outcome. Here, in God's promise, a different root is used. I record this as an observation.

**أموال** — the plural of *māl*. Root م-و-ل. **بنين** — the plural of *ibn*; root ب-ن-ي (to build, to construct). That a word meaning "what is built/constructed" is used in Arabic for a child is striking; a generation is the continuation of the structure built.

**"Wealth and sons" is a standard pair in the Qurʾān** and almost everywhere it comes together with a warning:

- *"Your wealth and your children are only a trial"* (al-Anfāl 8/28; al-Taghābun 64/15)
- *"Neither your wealth nor your children are what will bring you near to Us"* (Sabaʾ 34/37)
- *"Neither their wealth nor their children will avail them anything"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/10; al-Mujādala 58/17)

And this sūra itself will come back to the same pair nine verses later: *"and they followed one whose wealth and children increased him in nothing but loss"* (71/21).

**Within the same sūra, the same two things occur nine verses apart in two different statuses:** in verse 12 a **promise**, in verse 21 a **cause of loss.** This is not a contradiction; it is the most important distinction in the sūra and I shall take it up below.

### جَنَّاتٍ … أَنْهَارًا — gardens and rivers

**جنة**, root ج-ن-ن (to cover, to conceal). The full analysis of this root was made at al-Baqara 2/25 (*janīn*, *jinn*, *majnūn*, *mijann*, *janna*); I rely on that. *Janna* is the garden that covers the ground with its trees.

**نهر**, root ن-ه-ر: to cleave something open widely and make a way. *Nahr* is, before the running water itself, **its bed**; the water flows through the channel it has cut for itself. From the same root comes *nahār* (day — light's cleaving of the darkness).

The best-known use of the pair in the Qurʾān is in the descriptions of the hereafter: *jannātun tajrī min taḥtihā'l-anhār*. Here, though, it is promised **in this world**, in this life. That is, the sūra uses the words of the description of the hereafter for a worldly promise.

An observation: both words have to do with **cleaving/covering**. The garden covers, the river cleaves. The two together are the exact description of a worked and watered land — that is, **agriculture**.

### The four promises and their order

What is promised in verses 10-12, in order: **rain → wealth and offspring → gardens → rivers.**

This order is like a chain of production: the water comes, wealth and population increase, the land turns into a garden, a system of irrigation is set up. That is, what is promised is not a random abundance but **a working economy**.

And note: none of it is a miracle. No gold rains from the sky. Everything promised is something that could come about within the ordinary working of nature. The sūra promises not the extraordinary but **the turning of the order in one's favour**.

### The hard question: does asking forgiveness bring wealth?

These verses touch on one of the most exploited areas of religious discourse. One must be honest.

**What the verse says for certain:** the Qurʾān makes a link between a society's putting right its relation with God and that society's material condition. This link is repeated in more than one place (al-Aʿrāf 7/96; Hūd 11/3, 52; al-Ṭalāq 65/2-3) and cannot be denied.

**What the verse does not say is this:**

**First: the one addressed is a community, not an individual.** In all these verses the address is plural: *istaghfirū*, *ʿalaykum*, *yumdidkum*. Noah does not say to one person "ask forgiveness and you will become rich"; he says to a society "set your direction right and your order will be set right". This is not a formula at the level of the individual but a principle at the level of society.

**Second: this is not a payment but a consequence.** What the verse sets up is not a mechanical exchange. The Qurʾān itself says openly that abundance does not always come from goodness and that good people are not always in abundance. This is treated exactly in Sūrat al-Fajr (89/15-16): abundance and straitness are two faces of the same act — testing — and the Qurʾān rejects the inference "abundance is honouring" with *kallā*. That observation from the discussion of al-Fajr holds here too.

**Third: the sūra itself draws the limit of this promise.** In verse 21 Noah will say that his people followed those whose **wealth and children were many**, and that such a person's wealth increased him in nothing but loss. That is, the sūra records that what it promised in verse 12 is found in verse 21 **already obtained and with exactly the opposite outcome.**

Where is the difference? **In the source and in the direction.** In verse 12 wealth is something that comes as the result of turning to God and that is shared with the community. In verse 21 wealth is the support of a person's own position and an instrument for making others subject to him. The object is the same, the relation different.

I draw this distinction from the sūra's own internal structure; I do not present it as a transmitted report. But that the sūra uses the same two words (*amwāl*, *banīn* / *māl*, *walad*) in two opposite contexts nine verses apart supports this reading.

**Fourth:** the Qurʾān records the reverse openly as well — abundance can also be given before a society's destruction. *"When they forgot what they had been reminded of, We opened upon them the doors of all things; until, just as they were exulting in what they had been given, We seized them suddenly"* (al-Anʿām 6/44). That is, abundance has no single meaning.

**Fifth — and this the next sūra says.** Sūrat al-Jinn sets up the same equation in almost the same words: *"Had they gone straight upon that path, We would have given them abundant water to drink"* (al-Jinn 72/16). But immediately afterwards, in a single sentence, it names what that water is: *"that We might test them by it"* (al-Jinn 72/17). The word is *fitna* — as was treated in the discussion of al-Burūj, the concrete sense of the root is the melting of gold in fire to bring out its purity.

When the two sūras are set side by side the picture is complete: **Nūḥ gives the promise, al-Jinn names it.** The abundance is genuinely given; but at the moment it is given it turns into an instrument of testing. This also explains how the person Noah will describe in verse 21 — the man whose wealth increases him in nothing but loss — comes about: the one who fails the test loses by what he has been given.

In conclusion: to draw from these verses a rule like "whoever asks forgiveness becomes rich" would be to disregard the verse's plural address and the Qurʾān's other statements. The link the verse makes is real, but it is **social, conditional and not guaranteed.**

### What this says to the present

The striking side of these verses is that a religious call opens with **a worldly offer**. Noah does not first speak to those he addresses of the hereafter; he speaks of water, crops, offspring, land.

There are two readings of this and both fit the text:

**The first — pedagogical.** One speaks in the language the addressee understands. An argument from the hereafter cannot be built with a person who does not believe in the hereafter; the common ground is this world.

**The second — a matter of principle.** The Qurʾān does not set up an enmity between this world and the hereafter. To say that turning rightly has a return in this world too is part of an understanding of religion that does not belittle the world. The rest of the sūra (verses 15-20) strengthens this: all the proofs are brought **from this world** — the heaven, the moon, the sun, the plant, the earth, the roads.

## 71/13-14 — مَّا لَكُمْ لَا تَرْجُونَ لِلَّهِ وَقَارًا · وَقَدْ خَلَقَكُمْ أَطْوَارًا

*Mā lakum lā tarjūna lillāhi waqārā · Wa qad khalaqakum aṭwārā*

"What is the matter with you, that you do not credit God with any weight? It is He who created you stage by stage."

### مَّا لَكُمْ — "what is the matter with you"

The tone changes in Noah's speech. Until now there was an offer; here a **question** comes, and it is not a question whose answer is expected.

In Arabic the construction *mā lakum* marks an oddity in the addressee's behaviour. Its English equivalent is exactly "what is the matter with you", "what has come over you". It conveys astonishment and reproach. The Qurʾān uses this construction in several places: *"What is the matter with you, that you do not spend in the way of God?"* (al-Ḥadīd 57/10); *"What is the matter with you? How do you judge?"* (al-Qalam 68/36).

Its name in rhetoric is *istifhām inkārī* — a question that seeks no answer but conveys objection. What is asked for is not information; what is asked of the addressee is that he look at his own behaviour from the outside.

### لَا تَرْجُونَ — a problem of translation

There is a genuine dispute about the sense here and it must not be hidden.

**Root ر-ج-و**: to hope, to expect. From the same root comes *rajāʾ* (hope). This is the standard sense of the word and it occurs often in the Qurʾān in this sense: *"whoever hopes for the meeting with God"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/5; al-Kahf 18/110).

But in this verse the sense "to hope" is strained: the sentence "you do not hope for a weight for God" stands oddly in English as it does in Arabic. For this reason three readings are transmitted in the classical sources:

| Reading | Its meaning | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **To fear** | "You do not fear God's greatness" | It is transmitted that in the speech of some Arab tribes *rajāʾ* was used in the sense of "to fear"; it is said that this sense comes to the fore in negative sentences |
| **To expect, to reckon with** | "You do not expect, do not reckon with, a weight for God" | The one closest to the root's primary sense; the difference between "hoping" and "expecting" |
| **To hope (to look for)** | "You do not hope for any dignity/honour from God" | The choice of those who read *waqār* as "the value God will give you" |

The first reading is the one most widely transmitted in the classical commentaries. The second interferes least with the language and in my judgement sits better with the context of the verse: the sūra's complaint is not fearlessness but **not taking seriously.** But this preference is not binding; none of the three readings runs against the text.

This is why I render it "do not credit Him with": the work of the word here is to convey the **failure to ascribe** an attribute to God.

### وَقَارًا — *waqār*

The densest word in the sūra and — thanks to a link in the language — the word that does the most work within it.

**Root و-ق-ر.** Its concrete sense: **weight.** A thing's being heavy, not easily moved from its place.

The root's derivatives give different faces of this weight:

- **waqār** — gravity, seriousness, standing. What is heavy is not blown about; this is why the word "heavy" conveys standing in Arabic as it does in English ("weighty", "a person of gravity").
- **wiqr** — a load, a weight carried.
- **waqr** — **a heaviness in the ear, deafness.** In the Qurʾān: *"in their ears there is a heaviness"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/44; also al-Anʿām 6/25, Luqmān 31/7, al-Isrāʾ 17/46).
- **tawqīr** — to give someone weight, to show respect. In the Qurʾān: *"…that you may support him and honour him"* (al-Fatḥ 48/9).

**Now look inside the sūra.** Six verses earlier, in verse 7, this people's first defence was this: *"they put their fingers in their ears"* — that is, they put a **waqr** (heaviness) into their own ears.

And in verse 13 this is said: you do not credit God with any **waqār** (weight).

The same root, in two places, in two opposite works: **those who did not give the weight to God put it into their own ears.** I record this link as a reading I draw from the text of the sūra; I do not know of such a link being made between these two verses in the classical commentaries. The unity of the root, on the other hand, is a datum of the language and is not disputed.

**That the word comes indefinite** is worth recording too: *waqāran* — "a weight". That is, not "God's weight" but "any weight at all for God". The sentence thus voices the most minimal demand: at least **some measure** of taking seriously.

### وَقَدْ خَلَقَكُمْ أَطْوَارًا — "yet He created you stage by stage"

**The *wāw* at the head of the sentence** is the *wāw* of circumstance: "…whereas, and yet". That is, verse 14 gives the ground of the question in verse 13: your not taking it seriously is strange, **because** your very existence is His work.

**أطوار** is the plural of *ṭawr*. **Root ط-و-ر.**

*Ṭawr* means the **state, condition, stage** a thing is in. Further, according to what the lexicographers transmit, the root carries the sense of **limit, boundary**: usages such as *mā ṭāra bi-hādhā'l-makān* — "he did not come near the bounds of this place" — are related. The two senses join: every stage has a limit, every limit a stage.

The word occurs in the Qurʾān **only here**. This solitariness gives it a weight of its own.

**What does it point to?** Three main explanations are transmitted in the classical commentaries, and the three do not exclude one another:

**1. The stages in the womb.** The Qurʾān enumerates this openly elsewhere: *"We created the human being from an extract of clay. Then We made him a drop of fluid in a secure place. Then We turned the drop into a clinging thing; and the clinging thing into a lump of flesh, and the lump of flesh into bones; and We clothed the bones with flesh; then We brought him into being as another creation"* (al-Muʾminūn 23/12-14). Similarly: al-Ḥajj 22/5.

**2. The phases of life.** Infancy, childhood, youth, maturity, old age. The Qurʾān enumerates this separately too: *"It is He who created you from dust, then from a drop of fluid, then from a clinging thing. Then He brings you forth as an infant, then that you may reach your full strength, then that you may grow old…"* (al-Ghāfir 40/67; also al-Rūm 30/54).

**3. Difference among human beings.** Being created differing in colour, language, temperament, capacity. This reading rests on the word's sense of "state, condition".

The verse chooses none of the three, and that it does not is meaningful: **the word has been left too wide to be tied to a single process.**

**A limit has to be drawn here.** I do not draw from this verse the conclusion that modern embryological knowledge is being announced. The verse says that a human being **is not always in the same state**, that he passes through stages — and this was something known by observation. Birth, growth and ageing are facts every society sees. The verse's argument is not the disclosure of a hidden piece of knowledge; it is a demand for **the correct reading of something everyone sees**.

### The logic of the argument

Verses 13-14 build an inference, and the force of the inference lies in its simplicity:

*You did not make yourselves. You were not always in the state you are in now; you were passed through a series of stages and had no say in any of them. That being so, what is the explanation of your giving no weight at all to the party that passed you through them?*

The verse's reproach is, before a demand for belief, a demand for **consistency**. Someone who makes light of what he owes his own existence to is inconsistent.

### What this says to the present

The sense of *waqār* as "giving weight" describes a present-day behaviour well. People most often do not reject the idea of God; they **lighten** it. It is an acceptance that is accepted but has no consequence: it touches no decision of life, changes no choice, weighs nowhere.

This is exactly the sūra's complaint. Noah's people too were not denying God altogether — as will be seen in verse 23, their problem was not God's existence but the things they set beside Him. The sūra says that in practice the difference between denial and indifference is small: an acceptance with no weight is not counted an acceptance.

## 71/15-16 — أَلَمْ تَرَوْا كَيْفَ خَلَقَ اللَّهُ سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ طِبَاقًا · وَجَعَلَ الْقَمَرَ فِيهِنَّ نُورًا وَجَعَلَ الشَّمْسَ سِرَاجًا

*A lam taraw kayfa khalaqa'llāhu sabʿa samāwātin ṭibāqā · Wa jaʿala'l-qamara fīhinna nūran wa jaʿala'l-shamsa sirājā*

"Have you not seen how God created seven heavens in layers? And He made the moon in them a light, and made the sun a lamp."

### أَلَمْ تَرَوْا — "have you not seen"

The analysis of this construction was made in Sūrat al-Fīl (105/1): the verb *raʾā* conveys knowing and grasping as well as seeing with the eye; the construction *a lam tara* is a formula the Qurʾān uses to say "look at this thing everyone knows". I rely on the observation made there.

The difference here: in al-Fīl the construction was **singular** (*a lam tara* — addressed to the Prophet), here it is **plural** (*a lam taraw* — addressed to the people). And here something genuinely visible to the eye is spoken of. So the construction works in both its figurative and its literal sense.

Note that Noah changes his argument. In verses 13-14 the proof was **the human being himself**; in verses 15-20 the proof will be **what is outside**: the heaven, the moon, the sun, the plant, the earth, the roads. From near to far, from inside to outside.

### سَبْعَ سَمَاوَاتٍ — seven heavens

On **the number "seven"** there have been two readings since the classical period:

**The first reading — the number is in its literal sense.** There are seven separate heavens; their nature has not been made known to us. This view is the majority one and is the reading that stands closest to the Qurʾān's expression. The Qurʾān repeats the phrase "seven heavens" in many places (al-Baqara 2/29; al-Isrāʾ 17/44; al-Muʾminūn 23/86; Fuṣṣilat 41/12; al-Mulk 67/3; al-Nabaʾ 78/12) — this repetition suggests that the number is not arbitrary.

**The second reading — "seven" conveys multiplicity.** That certain numbers in Arabic (seven, seventy, seven hundred) convey "many" rather than a definite quantity is a fact recorded in the classical sources on language. On this reading "seven heavens" means "layer upon layer, countless heavens".

I make no definite preference. **One thing, though, must be said plainly:** matching this expression with any layering in modern astronomy (the layers of the atmosphere, planetary orbits, galactic structures) is a work the text does not do. Such matchings became widespread in the twentieth century; but that they have had to be rebuilt with every new astronomical model is the proof of their own weakness. The verse offers no model of the structure of the heaven; it says **that the heaven has been made**.

### طِبَاقًا — in layers

**Root ط-ب-ق**: one thing's sitting exactly upon another, fitting it, matching it.

Its derivatives:
- **ṭabaqa** — a storey, a layer. (It has passed into Turkish as well.)
- **muṭābaqa** — two things' fitting each other, matching. It is used as a term in logic and rhetoric too.
- **ṭabaq** — a vessel that closes over; one whose lid sits exactly on its body.
- **aṭbaqa** — to close, to shut over.

So *ṭibāq* does not mean simply "one above another"; it means **one above another in mutual fit.** Like lid and vessel, in a way that completes each other.

The word occurs once more in the Qurʾān, in the same construct: *"It is He who created seven heavens in layers"* (al-Mulk 67/3). And that verse adds immediately after: *"You will see no disharmony in the creation of the Merciful. Turn your gaze again: do you see any crack?"* That is, Sūrat al-Mulk openly relates the word *ṭibāq* to **harmony**. This illuminates the sense of the word here too.

### وَجَعَلَ الْقَمَرَ فِيهِنَّ نُورًا — "He made the moon in them a light"

**فِيهِنَّ — "in them".** There is a matter here that the classical commentaries have dwelt on: how can the moon be *in* the seven heavens? Elsewhere the Qurʾān places the lights in the nearest heaven: *"We adorned the nearest heaven with stars"* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/6); *"We furnished the nearest heaven with lamps"* (Fuṣṣilat 41/12; similarly al-Mulk 67/5).

The explanations transmitted in the classical sources:

- ***Fī* means not "inside" but "among / within the whole of".** If a thing is in a part of a whole, it is counted as within that whole. In English too, when we say "I live in the city" we do not mean the whole of it.
- ***Fī* means "together with" (*maʿa*).** The use of one particle in place of another is not rare in Arabic.
- **The light reaches all the layers**; though the moon be in one layer, its light passes through them all.

All of these explanations are in the classical sources; I do not present one of them as definitely correct. The verse's real work is not to give a scheme of cosmology.

### The difference between نُور and سِرَاج

This is the most discussed detail in the sūra and it needs careful handling.

**نور**, root ن-و-ر: **light.** The word conveys light itself — what illuminates, what removes darkness. The Qurʾān uses this word for guidance too and always leaves it singular (this was treated in the discussion of al-Baqara 2/17: *nūr* singular, *ẓulumāt* plural).

**سراج**, root س-ر-ج: **a lamp.** The concrete referent of the word is an instrument that gives light by burning oil. From the same root come *sirāj* (lamp), *masraja* (the place the lamp is set).

**The difference between the two words at the lexical level is this:** *nūr* is light itself; *sirāj* is the body that **produces** light. A lamp is an object that gives light by something burning inside it.

And the Qurʾān distributes these two words between the moon and the sun **consistently**:

| Verse | Sun | Moon |
|---|---|---|
| **Nūḥ 71/16** | *sirāj* (lamp) | *nūr* (light) |
| **Yūnus 10/5** | *ḍiyāʾ* (brightness) | *nūr* (light) |
| **al-Furqān 25/61** | *sirāj* (lamp) | *munīr* (illuminating) |
| **al-Nabaʾ 78/13** | *sirājan wahhājā* (a blazing lamp) | — |

That is, the Qurʾān always chooses a word from the field of **burning/blazing** for the sun, and always one from the field of **light** for the moon. This is a choice repeated in more than one place; it becomes hard to call it coincidence.

**The classical exegetes saw this difference.** The different choice of words was dwelt on; explanations to the effect that the sun's light is from itself and the moon's light otherwise appear in the classical exegetical literature. Moreover, in medieval Islamic astronomy it was a known assumption that the moon's light is taken from the sun, and this knowledge filtered into the exegetical literature too.

**Now let us draw the limit.** I do **not** draw from this verse the conclusion that "the Qurʾān announced that the moon's light is reflected, and this is a scientific miracle", and the method of this text does not permit it. The reasons:

1. That the moon's light is not from itself **was known in Greek astronomy** long before the age in which the Qurʾān came down. It was not a new piece of knowledge.
2. The word *nūr* is used in Arabic for things that have their own light too; it has no technical sense such as "reflected light".
3. **And this is what is decisive:** the Qurʾān uses the word *sirāj* of the Prophet as well — *"an illuminating lamp"* (*sirājan munīrā*, al-Aḥzāb 33/46). There, of course, no burning body is meant. The word conveys not a physical mechanism but **the function of being a source**. That the same word is used of the sun conveys primarily this: the light comes from it.

**What can be said is this, and it is not a small thing:** the Qurʾān chooses different words for the sun and the moon and is consistent in that choice. The sun is named a **source** (a lamp), the moon a **light**. This distinction stands within the text itself; there is no need to translate it into modern physics, and translating it does not increase the text's force — it decreases it, because it leaves the text having to be defended anew with every new scientific model.

### The verse's place in the argument

Why does Noah speak of the heaven? The structure of the proof is this: the people live inside an order and **they did not set that order up themselves.** The heaven, the moon and the sun are things a human being cannot intervene in, that work without interruption, and that everyone sees.

And that the moon and the sun in particular are chosen is no accident: these two are humanity's oldest **instruments of the calendar.** Sowing, harvest, migration, pilgrimage, trade — all of it is set by them. That is, Noah points to the two things that actually order his people's life and asks "who put these here?"

The Qurʾān makes this link openly elsewhere: *"It is He who made the sun a brightness and the moon a light, and determined for it stations, that you might know the number of the years and the reckoning"* (Yūnus 10/5).

## 71/17-18 — وَاللَّهُ أَنبَتَكُم مِّنَ الْأَرْضِ نَبَاتًا · ثُمَّ يُعِيدُكُمْ فِيهَا وَيُخْرِجُكُمْ إِخْرَاجًا

*Wa'llāhu anbatakum mina'l-arḍi nabātā · Thumma yuʿīdukum fīhā wa yukhrijukum ikhrājā*

"And God made you grow from the earth as a growing thing. Then He will return you into it, and He will bring you out, and how He will bring you out."

### One of the most striking choices of verb in the Qurʾān

The verse does not say "He created you from the earth". It says **"He made you grow from the earth"**.

**Root ن-ب-ت**: a plant's coming up out of the ground, its sprouting. *Nabāt*, a plant. *Anbata* (Form IV), to make grow — that is, to bring about a thing's coming up out of the ground. In Arabic this verb belongs to the field of **agriculture and vegetation**; the Qurʾān uses it regularly in the context of rain and crops (al-Anʿām 6/99; al-Naḥl 16/11; al-Ḥajj 22/5; ʿAbasa 80/27).

Here the object of that verb is **the human being.**

This is one of the most organic images the Qurʾān uses in describing the human being, and it says several things at once:

**First: origin.** The human being comes from the earth. The Qurʾān says this elsewhere too (*"He created you from dust"*, al-Rūm 30/20; Fāṭir 35/11), but the verb "created" suggests a manufacture. The verb "made grow" suggests something else: **growth.** A plant is not cast in a mould; it comes out slowly, from within itself.

**Second: passivity.** A plant does not grow of itself and does not decide that it has grown. It does not determine what is in the soil, when it will come up, how big it will grow. The subject of the verb is **God**, the human being is the object. The argument of the preceding pair of verses (13-14) attains an image here: the human being is not the agent of his own beginning.

**Third: transience.** A plant is seasonal. This will be said openly in the next verse: "then He will return you into it."

### نَبَاتًا — the subtlety in the grammar

The verse says *anbatakum … nabātā*. This is a **cognate accusative** (a verbal noun reinforcing the verb). But there is a technical oddity and the classical grammarians have dwelt on it:

The verb *anbata* is in **Form IV**; its verbal noun should be *inbāt*. In the verse, however, *nabāt* occurs — this is the verbal noun of the base three-letter verb (*nabata*).

The grammarians explain it thus: in place of a verb's own verbal noun, another verbal noun from the same root can be used as a cognate accusative; this is called "an expression indicating the sense of the verbal noun". There are examples of it in Arabic and it is not counted an irregularity.

But a difference of sense arises and it is a beautiful one: had it said *inbātā*, the emphasis would have fallen on **the act of making grow**. When it says *nabātā*, the emphasis falls on **the plant itself**. That is, the sentence comes close to this: "He made you grow from the earth — as a growing thing grows." Not the act but **the image** is being reinforced.

### A second use of the same verb for a human being

The Qurʾān uses this verb once more for a human being, and that place is striking:

> *"So her Lord accepted her with a beautiful acceptance and made her grow as a beautiful growing thing."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/37)

This is the verse that speaks of Mary's birth and her upbringing; the expression used is *wa anbatahā nabātan ḥasanā* — the same construction as Nūḥ 71/17, with an adjective added.

When the two verses are read together the scope of the root becomes visible: *inbāt* covers both coming into being and **growing to maturity**. In the verse about Mary, the qualification "as a beautiful growing thing" describes nurture itself.

### ثُمَّ يُعِيدُكُمْ فِيهَا — "then He will return you into it"

**Root ع-و-د**: to return, to repeat. From the same root come *ʿāda* (a repeated behaviour, a custom), *iʿāda* (bringing back), *maʿād* (the place returned to), *ʿīd* (the day that comes round again).

**The tense of the verb changes.** Verse 17 is perfect (*anbata* — He made grow), verse 18 imperfect (*yuʿīdu*, *yukhriju* — He returns, He brings out). One has happened, two are to happen.

**فِيهَا — "into it".** The pronoun refers to *arḍ* (earth/soil). That is, death is here named "a return to the earth"; the plant comparison continues. A plant comes out of the earth and, when its season is over, mixes back into the earth.

### وَيُخْرِجُكُمْ إِخْرَاجًا — "He will bring you out, and how He will bring you out"

The third stage: **bringing out again.**

**Root خ-ر-ج**: to come out. *Ikhrāj* (the Form IV verbal noun), to bring out. Again a **cognate accusative**: *yukhrijukum ikhrājā*. The fifth and last cognate accusative of the sūra.

The function of the emphasis here is clear. Those addressed in the sūra did not accept the resurrection; the repetition of the word conveys **the certainty of the act**: "He will bring out — and how He will bring out."

**Three verbs, three stages:**

| Verb | Tense | Movement |
|---|---|---|
| *anbata* | Past | **Brought out** of the earth |
| *yuʿīdu* | Future | Will **return** to the earth |
| *yukhriju* | Future | Will **bring out** of the earth |

The first and the third are in the same direction: **up out of the earth.** That is, the third stage is a repetition of the first. The argument is this: what has been done once can be done a second time. And the first doing is something the people witnessed in their own bodies.

The Qurʾān builds this argument by way of the plant many times; its clearest expression is this:

> *"We gave life to the dead earth and brought out grain from it… Thus too is the resurrection."* (a summary of Yāsīn 36/33-34; also the end of al-Aʿrāf 7/57: *"thus do We bring out the dead"*, and al-Zukhruf 43/11: *"and thus you too will be brought out"*.)

Another verse gives this threefold chain in almost the same order:

> *"From it We created you, and to it We shall return you, and from it We shall bring you out one more time."* (Ṭāhā 20/55)

When Nūḥ 71/17-18 is compared with Ṭāhā 20/55 the difference becomes visible: in Ṭāhā the first verb is *khalaqnā* (We created), here *anbata* (He made grow). The same chain said in the language of plants.

### What this says to the present

The measure the plant comparison gives makes a correction in the human being's view of himself. A plant:

- **does not sow its own seed** — you did not choose your beginning;
- **is subject to its season** — your span is not in your hands;
- **feeds from the soil** — you are not independent;
- **and the place it returns to is the place it came out of.**

It is possible to read this as a lowering, but that would be incomplete. The same comparison says one thing more: **a plant is a thing that grows, not a thing that rots.** The verse likens the human being not to stone, to clay, to mud, but to something living and growing. And the end of the chain is not the earth but a coming out again.

## 71/19-20 — وَاللَّهُ جَعَلَ لَكُمُ الْأَرْضَ بِسَاطًا · لِّتَسْلُكُوا مِنْهَا سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا

*Wa'llāhu jaʿala lakumu'l-arḍa bisāṭā · Li-taslukū minhā subulan fijājā*

"And God made the earth a spread-out carpet for you; that you might take wide roads out of it and travel."

### بِسَاطًا — a spread-out carpet

**Root ب-س-ط**: to spread, to lay out, to open, to widen.

Its derivatives show the field of the root:
- **bisāṭ** — a carpet spread on the ground, a rug, a cloth laid for a meal.
- **basṭ** — to open, to widen. Its opposite is *qabḍ* (to gather, to close). The Qurʾān uses this pair: *"God straitens and widens"* (al-Baqara 2/245).
- **mabsūṭ** — spread out, open. In the Qurʾān for open-handedness: *"Both His hands are outspread"* (al-Māʾida 5/64).
- **basīṭ** — spread out, plain, uncomplicated. The Turkish "basit" ("simple") is this word; its sense has narrowed over time.

### It does not mean "flat"

The note set down for the word *firāsh* (bedding) at al-Baqara 2/22 must be repeated here, because the same objection is raised about this verse: "is the Qurʾān saying the earth is flat?"

The observation in the discussion of al-Baqara was this: *"This expression does not say the earth is flat. Bedding is not flat either — it is spread, it folds, it conforms to the ground beneath it. What the word says is not shape but function."*

That observation holds for *bisāṭ* in exactly the same way, and indeed more strongly. **A spread carpet is a thing that conforms to the ground it is laid on.** A rug is a rug not because it is flat but because one can stand on it. The word conveys no geometry; it conveys a **usability**.

The same note was set down in Sūrat al-Ghāshiya (88/20) for the word *suṭiḥat* (it was spread out). The Qurʾān does the same thing with all the words it uses for the earth, and all of them convey function:

| Word | Image | Place |
|---|---|---|
| **firāsh** | Bedding | al-Baqara 2/22 |
| **mihād** | A cradle | al-Nabaʾ 78/6 |
| **mahd** | Cradle / a spread-out place | al-Zukhruf 43/10; Ṭāhā 20/53 |
| **bisāṭ** | A spread-out carpet | Nūḥ 71/19 |
| **dhalūl** | A docile mount | al-Mulk 67/15 |
| **qarār** | A place to settle | al-Naml 27/61; al-Ghāfir 40/64 |

Six separate words, a single message: **this ground has been set so that life can be lived on it.** None of them describes a shape. That a text conveys the same function with six different words is the clearest indication that the text is not concerned with shape.

Moreover the word *bisāṭ* is itself an image from tent and nomadic culture: the rug spread on the ground, sat upon, gathered up and carried. The object most familiar to the one addressed has been chosen.

### لِّتَسْلُكُوا — "that you might travel"

**Root س-ل-ك**: to enter into something, to take to a road, to pass through. The Turkish "sülûk" ("entering upon a path") and "meslek" ("a profession"; literally the path entered) are from this same root.

The concrete picture of the word is **passing through a narrow place**, like a thread passed through the eye of a needle. The Qurʾān uses this verb in the sense of "to insert one thing into another" as well (al-Zumar 39/21: *"He inserted it into the springs in the earth"*).

**لام** at the head: *li-taslukū* — "**that** you might travel". This is the *lām* of purpose (*taʿlīl*). That is, the verse is in two steps: the earth was made a spread carpet — **why?** — so that one might travel over it.

That the ground of the favour is specified is important. The earth is given not as a field for contemplation but as a **field of use**. The human being is described here not as one who sits but as one who walks.

### مِنْهَا — "out of it"

A small particle, dwelt upon: the roads open not *upon* the earth but **"out of it"**.

Two readings are transmitted in the classical sources: the *min* is either **inceptive** (the roads' point of origin is the earth) or **partitive** (the roads are a part of the earth). Both arrive at the same place: a road is not something brought in from outside the earth; it is something drawn out of the earth itself.

This has a concrete counterpart. Roads are made — they are dug, levelled, opened. The verse says that the earth has been made **fit** for road-making; not that ready-made roads were laid. The possibility has been given, and the work left to the human being.

### سُبُلًا فِجَاجًا — wide roads

**سبل** is the plural of *sabīl*. The distinction between the words for road was made in the discussion of al-Fātiḥa (1/6): *ṣirāṭ* is the wide single main road, *sabīl* a particular route, *ṭarīq* the track followed. I rely on the observation made there.

That the **plural** is used here is striking: *subul*. In al-Fātiḥa *ṣirāṭ* was singular because the right way is one. Here the roads are plural because **geographical roads** are spoken of and these are countless. The Qurʾān preserves the distinction by using the singular for the spiritual road and the plural for the physical.

**فجاج** is the plural of *fajj*. **Root ف-ج-ج**: the space between two things being open. *Fajj* means in particular **a wide pass between two mountains**. So not an ordinary road; a wide, deep pass opened between mountains.

The word occurs in two other places in the Qurʾān:

- *"And We made wide roads in it, that they might find their way"* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/31) — here the construct is reversed: *fijājan subulā*.
- *"They will come to you on foot and on every lean camel, coming from every deep road"* (al-Ḥajj 22/27) — in the context of the call to pilgrimage, *min kulli fajjin ʿamīq*.

The second occurrence is meaningful: *fajj* is the road of coming from afar. The word carries a suggestion of distance and travel.

### The completion of the argument

Across verses 15-20 Noah has set out four proofs, and the arrangement runs downward:

1. **The heaven** (15) — the furthest, wholly untouchable
2. **The moon and the sun** (16) — far, but ordering life
3. **The human being's own body** (17-18) — the nearest
4. **The ground his foot treads** (19-20) — what he stands on

From above downward, from far to near. And the last proof is **directly under the addressee's foot.** The man looking for proof is directed to the place he would look at last.

And there is this too: none of the four proofs is extraordinary. None of them is a miracle. All of them are things that people saw every day. Noah shows nothing unseen; he asks for **another way of looking at what is seen**. The whole proof section of the sūra rests on this principle.

## 71/21 — قَالَ نُوحٌ رَّبِّ إِنَّهُمْ عَصَوْنِي وَاتَّبَعُوا مَن لَّمْ يَزِدْهُ مَالُهُ وَوَلَدُهُ إِلَّا خَسَارًا

*Qāla Nūḥun Rabbi innahum ʿaṣawnī wa'ttabaʿū man lam yazidhu māluhū wa waladuhū illā khasārā*

"Noah said: My Lord! They disobeyed me and followed one whose wealth and children increased him in nothing but loss."

### قَالَ نُوحٌ — the repetition of the name

Something happens here: **Noah's name is mentioned again.**

Throughout the sūra Noah was speaking, and his speech was always given with *qāla* (he said) or directly in the first person singular. At verse 21 the narrator steps in and says **"Noah said"**. The same thing will happen once more at verse 26.

The three places where the name occurs are the three joints of the sūra:

| Verse | Expression | Its function |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | *arsalnā Nūḥan* | The commissioning |
| **21** | *qāla Nūḥun* | The concluding part of the report begins |
| **26** | *wa qāla Nūḥun* | The prayer begins |

That is, the name marks the section headings of the sūra. With verse 21 Noah stops describing method and begins to make a **diagnosis**.

### عَصَوْنِي — "they disobeyed me"

**Root ع-ص-ي.** According to what the lexicographers transmit, this root is related to the word *ʿaṣā* (staff, rod). The place where the relation is made is this expression: *shaqqa'l-ʿaṣā* — "he split the staff", that is, he separated from the community, he broke its unity. From here *maʿṣiya* is tied to the sense of **separating from the whole, going outside the direction given**. I give this explanation as a report from the classical lexicons; I make no claim of certainty.

The verb comes strikingly with **"me"**: *ʿaṣawnī* — they disobeyed me. Not God, but Noah. This is the counterpart of the third command in verse 3 (*aṭīʿūn* — obey me). Noah is reporting the outcome of the command given to him: the demand for obedience was refused.

But the Qurʾān says openly elsewhere what this means: obedience to the messenger is not directed to the messenger's person (*"Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed God"*, al-Nisāʾ 4/80). Noah is not stating a personal grievance here; he is stating **the outcome of the task**.

### وَاتَّبَعُوا — "they followed"

**Root ت-ب-ع**: to go after, to follow, to fall in behind. *Ittibāʿ* (Form VIII), to fall in behind something.

A contrast is set up in the sentence, and this is the verse's main diagnosis:

**They disobeyed me — they followed another.**

That is, the matter is not disobedience; it is **the change of direction of obedience**. A human being cannot go without following something. Noah's observation is that his people did not become "independent people who follow no one"; they only **changed the address they followed**.

### مَن لَّمْ يَزِدْهُ — "one whom it increased in nothing"

**"Man" is singular and indefinite.** No name is given. The leaders of the people could have been named; they are not. Instead a **type** is described: one who has wealth and children, but for whom these bring nothing but loss.

This is the Qurʾān's method of naming, and it is recorded in the method of this text too: what the verse describes are **attributes**; whoever carries that attribute is included in it. To describe a position instead of targeting a person saves the ruling from being imprisoned in history.

### مَالُهُ وَوَلَدُهُ — "his wealth and his children"

Now we come to the most important internal link in the sūra.

In verse 12 Noah had promised his people this: *"He will reinforce you with wealth and sons"* (*bi-amwālin wa banīn*). In verse 21 he says this: the wealth and children (*māluhū wa waladuhū*) of the man they followed increased him in nothing but loss.

The same two things — wealth and offspring — occur nine verses apart as a **promise** and as a **cause of loss**.

A detail: in verse 12 both are **plural** (*amwāl*, *banīn*), in verse 21 both are **singular** (*māl*, *walad*). Plurality suggests an abundance shared and distributed, singularity an accumulation gathered into one hand. I record this as an observation; I do not know of this distinction being treated in the classical commentaries.

The real matter is this: **the same object gives two different results within two different relations.**

The Qurʾān's stance on this is consistent and has been taken up in other parts of this text:

- **al-Humaza 104/2-3** — the man who gathers wealth and counts it and supposes his wealth will make him immortal. The observation in the discussion of al-Humaza: the criticism is directed not at the source of the wealth but at **the relation formed with it**.
- **al-Fajr 89/15-16** — the man who reads abundance as "my Lord has honoured me". The observation in the discussion of al-Fajr: abundance and straitness are two faces of the same act; both inferences are rejected.
- **al-Anfāl 8/28** — *"Your wealth and your children are only a trial."*

What this sūra adds is a third face: **the case where wealth makes its owner a model for others.** In al-Humaza the owner of wealth was deceiving himself; in al-Fajr he was measuring himself wrongly; here he **deceives others.**

### خَسَارًا — loss

**Root خ-س-ر**: to diminish, to lose, to suffer loss. It is a word of the language of commerce — the shrinking of capital.

The same root occurred in Sūrat al-Muṭaffifīn as the verb of giving short measure (*yukhsirūn* — they give less, 83/3) and in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr as the human being's general condition (*la-fī khusr* — surely in loss, 103/2).

The fine side of the use here is this: **something that increases produces a diminishing.** *Lam yazidhu … illā khasārā* — the sentence is built on an increase and ends on a diminishing. As the wealth increases, the capital shrinks. The language of commerce has been turned inside out.

### The social structure of the people

This verse is the sūra's only social analysis, and combined with the Qurʾān's records about the story of Noah elsewhere it gives a clear picture.

The Qurʾān records separately that those who opposed Noah were **the notables of the people**: *"The chiefs of those who disbelieved among his people said…"* (Hūd 11/27). And the objection they put to Noah is this: *"We see that only the rabble follow you"* (Hūd 11/27); the same objection at al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/111: *"Shall we believe you when it is the lowest who follow you?"*

So the picture the Qurʾān records is two-sided:

- **The rich** look down on those who follow Noah ("the rabble").
- **The people** follow the rich (this verse).

The sūra marks the second side. And note: Noah does not accuse his people here of "following the rabble"; he says **"they followed the winners"**. That is, what is followed is not rightness but **visible success**.

This is a structure that recurs in the Qurʾān's stories of the prophets. The same logic is formulated openly elsewhere: *"When We wish to destroy a town, We command those of its people who live in ease; and they commit corruption in it…"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/16).

### What this says to the present

The mechanism this verse describes — **material success producing authority on the question of truth** — is observable and still works.

There is no logical link between the truth of a view and the position of the one who defends it; but in practice people make this link constantly. The winner is listened to outside the field in which he won as well. Wealth is read as a kind of general certificate of competence.

The question the verse asks is plain: **how does that person's own account stand?** Noah's answer is that the man his people followed is himself at a loss. That is, the model being followed stands on the minus side of his own scales — but because his scales are not visible he goes on being a model.

## 71/22 — وَمَكَرُوا مَكْرًا كُبَّارًا

*Wa makarū makran kubbārā*

"And they laid a snare, a snare of the greatest."

### مَكْر — a snare

**Root م-ك-ر**: to try to bring someone to harm by a route he does not notice. The definition given in the classical lexicons: **to direct the target towards harm in such a way that he supposes he is being done a kindness.**

The word *kayd* was treated in Sūrat al-Fīl (105/2). The two words are close but not the same; the distinction made in the classical sources can be summarised thus:

| Word | Its emphasis |
|---|---|
| **kayd** | Setting up a **contrivance** the other party cannot see; the secrecy of the plan |
| **makr** | **Steering** the other party without his noticing; the stealth of the method |
| **ḥīla** | Getting round a difficulty by an indirect route; it is not always negative |

These distinctions are not absolute and the words are used at times in place of one another.

In the Qurʾān *makr* is ascribed both to human beings and — as a response — to God: *"They laid a snare, and God laid a snare; and God is the best of those who lay snares"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/54). This usage is the same principle treated in the analysis of *kayd* in Sūrat al-Fīl: the Qurʾān makes the subject of discussion not the plan itself but **in whose hand the plan will end.**

The Qurʾān's sharpest sentence on this matter is: *"An evil snare closes only on its own author"* (Fāṭir 35/43).

### كُبَّارًا — "of the greatest"

The word itself is a separate matter.

**Root ك-ب-ر.** But the pattern is out of the ordinary: ***fuʿʿāl*** (كُبَّار). This pattern conveys **extreme intensification** in Arabic and is rare. A gradation of the form *kabīr* (great) — *kubār* (very great) — *kubbār* (excessively great) is given in the classical sources on language.

The word occurs in the Qurʾān **only here**. That is, the Qurʾān has used the highest-degree form it has for saying "great" once, and that once is here.

It is transmitted that in some variant readings the word is read without the doubling — *kubāran*. This difference has no consequence beyond a lightening of the sense; intensification is present on both readings. I do not give the names of particular imāms of the readings, because I am not certain of them.

**And again a cognate accusative:** *makarū makran*. One of the sūra's five cognate accusatives, and the only one with an adjective. That is, two reinforcements are laid one on the other here: the repetition of the verbal noun + the pattern of excess.

### What was the snare?

The verse does not say what the snare was. The next verse says it.

This is a work the word order does: verse 22 gives a verdict ("they laid a great snare"), verse 23 opens its content ("they said: do not leave your gods"). The relation between them is the structure called in rhetoric **summary then detail** (*ijmāl–tafṣīl*): first the collective verdict, then the particulars.

This structure, by giving the verdict before the detail, makes the reader read the detail with a particular eye. What will be described in verse 23 — a slogan — might not have looked like a "snare" on its own. Verse 22 names it in advance.

## 71/23 — وَقَالُوا لَا تَذَرُنَّ آلِهَتَكُمْ وَلَا تَذَرُنَّ وَدًّا وَلَا سُوَاعًا وَلَا يَغُوثَ وَيَعُوقَ وَنَسْرًا

*Wa qālū lā tadharunna ālihatakum wa lā tadharunna Waddan wa lā Suwāʿan wa lā Yaghūtha wa Yaʿūqa wa Nasrā*

"And they said: On no account leave your gods; on no account leave Wadd, or Suwāʿ, or Yaghūth and Yaʿūq and Nasr."

### The content of the snare: a slogan

What was called "a snare of the greatest" in verse 22 is this: **a sentence.**

No army, no torture, no exile. The notables of the people say something to the people: *"Do not leave your gods."*

And note: the sentence is a sentence of **defence**, not of attack. No argument is put forward against Noah; what he has brought is not even discussed. Only holding on to what already exists is commanded.

I record this as an observation: the Qurʾān gives the description "the greatest snare" not to an act of violence but to **a call to conserve**. Because what the call does is to close the discussion from the start.

### لَا تَذَرُنَّ — "on no account leave"

**Root و-ذ-ر**: to abandon something, to leave it. This root has an interesting property: in Arabic its perfect form has fallen out of use; only the imperfect and imperative forms survive (*yadharu*, *dhar*). It is among the defective verbs.

**The doubled شدّة at the end of the verb** — *tadharunna* — is the **heavy *nūn* of emphasis**. In Arabic this suffix reinforces a command or a prohibition to the highest degree. Its English equivalent is "on no account", "never".

**And now we come to the finest internal link in the sūra.**

The same verb — the root و-ذ-ر — occurs three times in the sūra:

| Verse | Who says it | The sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **23** | The notables of the people | *lā tadharunna ālihatakum* — "on no account **leave** your gods" |
| **26** | Noah | *rabbi lā tadhar ʿalā'l-arḍi mina'l-kāfirīna dayyārā* — "My Lord, do not **leave** a single one of the disbelievers on the earth" |
| **27** | Noah | *innaka in tadharhum* — "if You **leave** them" |

That is, the people's last word was "do not leave"; Noah's first prayer was "do not leave". The same verb, in two opposite directions.

I draw this link from the text of the sūra. The unity of the root is beyond dispute; that there is a deliberate echo between the two verses is my own reading.

**A caution:** This verb (*tadhar*, و-ذ-ر) and the *andhir* of the sūra's first verse (ن-ذ-ر) **are not from the same root.** The resemblance in sound is close. I record this distinction again because it is liable to be confused.

### آلِهَتَكُمْ — "your gods"

The plural of **إله**. The analysis of the word was made in the discussion of al-Fātiḥa (in connection with the basmala).

Note the structure of the sentence: first *"your gods"* is said generally, then five names are enumerated one by one. In rhetoric this is the structure called **passing from the general to the particular** (*dhikru'l-khāṣṣ baʿda'l-ʿāmm*), and it shows that those enumerated have a particular weight: these are the most held to among the gods.

### The five names

Now we come to the section of the sūra about which there is most curiosity and in which the greatest care is needed. Let us write first **what the Qurʾān says**, then **what the transmitted report says**, then **what is known from outside.** Not confusing these three is more important than anything else in this discussion.

#### What the Qurʾān says

The Qurʾān says the following about these five names and **says nothing else**:

1. These are the things Noah's people were called on not to leave.
2. These are enumerated within the expression "your gods".
3. All five are made the subject, in the next verse (24), of the verdict "they led many astray" — either they themselves or those who called to them.

**What the Qurʾān does not say:** what they were (statues, animals, heavenly bodies), whose names they were, how they arose, how they were worshipped, and how these names passed from Noah's people to the Arabian peninsula. None of this is in the verse.

#### A detail in the grammar

The inflection of the five names is not uniform, and this gives information grammatically:

| Name | Inflection | Its reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Wadd** (وَدًّا) | With tanwīn (declinable) | Normal noun inflection |
| **Suwāʿ** (سُوَاعًا) | With tanwīn | Normal noun inflection |
| **Yaghūth** (يَغُوثَ) | Without tanwīn (diptote) | A proper name + being **on a verbal pattern** |
| **Yaʿūq** (يَعُوقَ) | Without tanwīn | The same reason |
| **Nasr** (نَسْرًا) | With tanwīn | Normal noun inflection |

*Yaghūth* and *Yaʿūq* resemble in form Arabic imperfect verbs (*yaghūthu* — he helps; *yaʿūqu* — he hinders / holds back). In Arabic a proper name on a verbal pattern does not take tanwīn. This is the grammatical explanation of the difference in the text of the verse and it is not disputed.

It is striking, moreover, that these two names are meaningful verbal roots in Arabic:
- **Yaghūth**, from the root غ-و-ث: to rush to help, to come to the rescue. (*Istighātha* — asking for help, from the same root.)
- **Yaʿūq**, from the root ع-و-ق: to hold back, to hinder. (*ʿAwāʾiq* — obstacles.)

That is, the two names resemble verbs meaning **"he helps" and "he hinders"**: the one who brings benefit and the one who wards off harm. These are the two basic functions expected of a god. Whether this is the meaning of the names is not certain — but that they sound so to an Arabic ear is clear.

**Wadd**, for its part, comes from the root و-د-د: to love, to incline towards. From the same root come *mawadda* (love) and the divine name **Wadūd**.

This point was taken up in Sūrat al-Burūj (85/14) and a note was set down there: that the two words are from the same root is a **datum of the language**; but no ruling was issued as to whether this is coincidence or the trace within the language of an older idea of a "god of love". I preserve that note here too and do not repeat it.

**Nasr**, from the root ن-س-ر: an eagle, a bird of prey of the vulture kind. The word is a known bird name in Arabic.

For **Suwāʿ** it is hard to draw a clear meaning from an Arabic root; the root س-و-ع is related to "hour/time" but the connection is forced. I say nothing definite about the meaning of this name.

#### What the transmitted report says

The most widely transmitted explanation of these names is this: **they were the names of righteous men who lived among Noah's people.** When these men died, statues of them were set up in the places where people gathered, in order to keep their memory alive; at the beginning these were not worshipped. But when that generation passed and the knowledge was forgotten, later generations began to worship the statues.

This explanation rests on **a report transmitted, with an ascription to Ibn ʿAbbās, in al-Bukhārī's section of exegesis on Sūrat Nūḥ**. The report is very widely relayed in the exegetical literature.

**One must be honest about this report:**

- The source of the report is sound and it is early.
- But the report is **the word of a Companion** (*mawqūf*); it is not transmitted as a saying ascribed to the Prophet. This does not make it worthless, but neither does it place it at the same degree of certainty as the Qurʾān.
- There is a wide body of detail circulating around the report (which man belonged to which tribe, how the statues were made, which tribe worshipped which idol) and these details diverge from one another. The further one descends to the level of detail, the more the reliability falls.

**The mechanism the report describes, however, is worth attention and is important in itself:**

1. Men worthy of respect live and die.
2. Monuments are set up for their memory — **at this stage there is no worship.**
3. A generation passes.
4. The next generation does not know why the monument was set up; it only sees that respect is shown.
5. Respect turns into worship.

The most important link in this series is **the fourth: the loss of knowledge.** *Shirk* is described not as a deviation that appears all at once but as **the result of a loss of memory**. The first generation knows what it is doing; the third does not.

I record this as a process observable within history. The forgetting of how a practice began and the sacralising of the practice itself is a recurring phenomenon in the history of religions — and it is not confined to a single religion or society.

#### What is known from outside

There are records that some of these names were genuinely in use in pre-Islamic Arabia, and these are not dependent on Islamic sources:

- It has been recorded by researchers that the name **Wadd** occurs as a divine name in South Arabian inscriptions.
- The names **Yaghūth** and **Yaʿūq** are found within pre-Islamic Arab **personal names** — names on the pattern "ʿabd + divine name", such as *ʿAbd Yaghūth*, are an indication that the name belonged to an object of worship. Such names are found in inscriptions and in Arabic poetry and genealogical records alike.
- It is transmitted that the name **Nasr** is associated with the Yemen region.

I relay these data in broad outline; the reading and dating of each inscription is a matter for specialists, and in the details researchers may reach differing conclusions. **What is certain is this:** these names are not names the Qurʾān invented; they are names its addressees knew.

#### The unanswered question

The Qurʾān mentions these names as the gods of **Noah's people**. The transmitted report and the historical data, on the other hand, present them as the idols of **Arabia**. How is the distance between the two closed?

The explanation given for this in the classical sources is that the names were carried from generation to generation after the flood and finally reappeared among the Arab tribes. This is an explanation resting on transmitted report.

Another reading is also possible and, while not classical, does not run against the text: the Qurʾān may here be describing **worship of the same type** by using names the Arabs knew — that is, the emphasis is not on the historical continuity of the names but on **the continuity of the behaviour**.

I issue no definite ruling. The verse's own work is not to establish a genealogy; it is to name a stance.

### What this says to the present

The most enduring side of this verse is how *shirk* is presented as **a conservative reflex**.

The notables of the people do not say "our gods are true". They say "do not leave them". What is put forward is not an argument but **continuity**: this came down to us from our forefathers, let us preserve it.

The Qurʾān names this reflex elsewhere: *"We found our forefathers upon a religion, and we are following in their footsteps"* (al-Zukhruf 43/22-23). And it gives this answer: *"Even if their forefathers understood nothing and were not rightly guided?"* (al-Baqara 2/170).

The measure here is this — and this measure favours no side: **that a thing is old does not show it to be right; nor does its being new.** What the verse criticises is not attachment to tradition but **putting tradition in the place of discussion**. The notables of the people did not even examine what Noah brought; they only closed the door.

## 71/24 — وَقَدْ أَضَلُّوا كَثِيرًا ۖ وَلَا تَزِدِ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا ضَلَالًا

*Wa qad aḍallū kathīrā · Wa lā tazidi'l-ẓālimīna illā ḍalālā*

"They have led many astray. And do not increase the wrongdoers in anything but straying."

### A break in the middle of the verse

This verse is divided in two and its two halves are of different kinds:

- **The first half:** a statement. Noah is reporting what has happened.
- **The second half:** a prayer. Noah is asking something of his Lord.

That is, the report section turns into a prayer in the middle of a verse. This is the real point of transition in the structure of the sūra; the long prayer of verse 26 is the continuation of a movement that began here.

### أَضَلُّوا — "they led astray": who?

The verb is plural and its subject is not written openly. There are two readings in the classical commentaries:

| Reading | Subject | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The idols** | The five names | The nearest plural mentioned is the idols in verse 23. In Arabic the plural of non-rational beings can take a verb in this way. The Qurʾān says elsewhere too that idols "led astray": *"My Lord! They have led many of mankind astray"* (Ibrāhīm 14/36 — Abraham's prayer) |
| **The notables of the people** | Those who speak in verse 23 | They are the real agents of the sentence; idols do nothing, the people who put them forward do |

The parallel at Ibrāhīm 14/36 supports the first reading markedly: there too a prophet, addressing his Lord, names the idols as "leading astray". The two prayers are in almost the same construction.

The two readings come to the same place in practice. As the Qurʾān says openly elsewhere, idols have no power of their own (*"they cannot even help themselves"*, al-Aʿrāf 7/192); the act of "leading astray" is ascribed to them **with respect to outcome** — because they were the occasion of the straying.

**كَثِيرًا** — "many". No number is given. The indefiniteness here conveys magnification.

### وَلَا تَزِدِ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا ضَلَالًا — "do not increase them in anything but their straying"

The **third "increase"** sentence of the sūra, and the first time it comes in the form of a **prayer**.

The construction is the same as that of verse 6: *lā yazidhum … illā*. There it was a statement ("my call served only to increase their flight"); here it is a prayer. That is, Noah **turns what he has observed into a request.** He asks for the continuation of what is in fact happening.

This observation lightens the moral weight of the verse and is important: Noah is not inventing a new punishment; he is asking for the completion of a working he has seen for years.

### Can a prophet ask for straying to increase?

This is a genuine difficulty the verse produces and it must not be brushed aside.

**The first matter: what does "ḍalāl" mean here?** In the classical commentaries expressions of this kind are generally explained in **two ways**:

- **Straying as punishment.** In the Qurʾān, leading astray most often comes as the result of a person's own choice: *"When they deviated, God made their hearts deviate"* (al-Ṣaff 61/5); *"By it God leads astray none but the transgressors"* (al-Baqara 2/26). That is, divine leading astray is not a beginning but a **response.** What Noah asks for is that this response be given.
- **Being abandoned.** Some exegetes explain *iḍlāl* as "withdrawing help, leaving to oneself".

Both explanations are in the classical sources; I do not present one as definitely correct. What they have in common is this: what is requested is not **the making bad of a good person.**

**The second matter: when was this prayer made?** This will be taken up in detail below at verse 26. Briefly: elsewhere the Qurʾān records that Noah **was informed** that *"none of your people will believe except those who have already believed"* (Hūd 11/36). That is, Noah's prayer comes after a piece of knowledge; not out of anger.

**The third matter: is this a prayer to be taken as a model?** No — and the Qurʾān itself shows this. When the Prophet wished to pass judgement on those who opposed him, this was said to him: *"Nothing of this matter is yours"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/128). That is, not every prayer of every messenger produces a general rule. Noah's prayer concerns his own people and his own process.

**ظالمين** — note the choice of word: not "disbelievers" but **"wrongdoers"**. The root ظ-ل-م is to put a thing outside the place where it should be. In his prayer Noah names them not by an attribute of belief but by **an attribute of act**. The same word will be repeated in verse 28; the last sentence of the sūra too is about "the wrongdoers".

## 71/25 — مِّمَّا خَطِيئَاتِهِمْ أُغْرِقُوا فَأُدْخِلُوا نَارًا فَلَمْ يَجِدُوا لَهُم مِّن دُونِ اللَّهِ أَنصَارًا

*Mimmā khaṭīʾātihim ughriqū fa-udkhilū nāran fa-lam yajidū lahum min dūni'llāhi anṣārā*

"Because of their errors they were drowned, then they were put into a fire, and they found for themselves no helpers against God."

### The narrator returns

From the beginning of the sūra it was Noah who was speaking. Here, for a single verse, the voice changes: it is **the narrator** who delivers the verdict.

This is the sūra's most important structural choice. Noah made a prayer in verse 24; he will make two more in verses 26-28. In between, **the sentence stating that the prayer was granted does not come from Noah's mouth.**

The messenger asks; the verdict belongs to another. The sūra says this not by content but by **a change of narrator**.

### مِّمَّا خَطِيئَاتِهِمْ — a knot in the grammar

The expression at the head of the sentence is a construction the lexicographers have dwelt on.

Its normal form would be *min khaṭīʾātihim* (because of their errors). A **مَّا** has come in between.

The explanation the classical grammarians give: this *mā* is **redundant** — that is, it adds no separate sense to the sentence, it has come for **emphasis**. There are other examples in Arabic of a *mā* coming between *min* and its governed noun to convey reinforcement.

The *min* here is **causal**: "because of, by reason of". So the sentence means "**because of** their errors", and the intervening *mā* stresses this causal link: from nothing else, from exactly this.

### خَطِيئَات — errors

**Root خ-ط-أ**: to miss the target, to fail to hit. To shoot an arrow and not hit the mark.

Arabic's words for sin look at it from different places and the distinctions must be known:

| Word | The root's image | Its emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| **khaṭīʾa** | Missing the target | Going to the wrong target |
| **dhanb** | *Dhanab* — a tail | The consequence that follows behind; the trace attached to it |
| **ithm** | To weigh down, to hold back | What loads, what makes heavy |
| **jurm** | To cut off, to sever | The act that breaks the bond |

The distinguishing side of *khaṭīʾa* is that **there is no intent** at the origin of the word — the one who misses was trying to hit the target. But the Qurʾān uses this word for deliberate sin as well, and the lexicographers record that *khaṭīʾa* in time acquired the sense of "deliberate sin" too. The use here, given the sūra as a whole, is not an unintended slip.

That the word comes in the **plural** is meaningful: not a single crime but an accumulation spread over centuries.

Variant readings are transmitted for the pronunciation of the word (such as *khaṭīʾātihim* / *khaṭāyāhum*). These differences do not change the sense; both are plural. I do not give the names of particular imāms of the readings, because I am not certain of them.

### أُغْرِقُوا — "they were drowned"

**Root غ-ر-ق**: to sink in water, to drown. The verb is in the **passive**: *ughriqū* — "they were drowned".

The non-naming of the agent is a widespread choice in the Qurʾān's accounts of punishment. One reason for it is that the punishment is kept closed to any personalising: who delivers the penalty is not made a matter of discussion, only that it has been delivered is stated.

**And now note the sūra's proportion.** This single word is the whole of the event of the flood, to which the Qurʾān elsewhere devotes dozens of verses. Sūrat al-Qamar narrates the flood in seven verses (54/9-16); Sūrat Hūd sets up a scene from the building of the ark to its coming to rest on land. In this sūra all of it is **one verb.**

The sūra is concerned not with the event but with the process. The outcome is merely entered in the record.

**And the Qurʾān narrates the same event from the other side too.** In Sūrat al-Ḥāqqa the flood is given from the front of those who were **carried**, not of those who were drowned: *"When the water overflowed, We carried you in the running vessel"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/11). There the verb is active and its subject is evident (*ḥamalnākum* — We carried); here the verb is passive and its subject is not stated (*ughriqū*).

Two records of the same event, in two different voices: **the rescue is openly owned, the destruction is left in the passive.** I record this as an observation.

### فَأُدْخِلُوا نَارًا — "then they were put into a fire"

The **فَ** (*fāʾ*) at the head conveys in Arabic "immediately after". It leaves no interval.

This is the most striking expression in the verse: **from the water straight into the fire.** Two opposite substances are set one after the other and no waiting in between is described.

In the classical commentaries this expression has been used as evidence in the discussion of **the punishment of the grave / the *barzakh***: that there is a punishment between the drowning and the resurrection is drawn from the verse's *fāʾ*. This is an inference widely treated in the classical literature of theology and exegesis.

The contrary view says that the *fāʾ* here conveys not temporal closeness but **sequence and certainty**: "in the end they were put into a fire" — how much time passed in between is not stated.

Another verse on the subject is counted as support for the first reading: of the house of Pharaoh, *"They are presented to the fire morning and evening; and on the day the Hour comes it is said: put the house of Pharaoh into the severest punishment"* (al-Ghāfir 40/46). Here it is clear that a punishment **before** the resurrection is spoken of.

I make no definite preference; neither reading runs against the text, and this is a matter falling within the field of theology.

**نَارًا comes indefinite** — not "al-nār" (that fire) but "a fire". This is one of the elements that feeds the dispute over whether the verse speaks of hell or of some other punishment.

### فَلَمْ يَجِدُوا … أَنصَارًا — "they found no helpers"

The last part of the verse closes the argument the sūra has been building from the start.

**Root ن-ص-ر**: to help — in particular to help **one in difficulty, coming from outside**. From the same root come *naṣr* (victory), *nāṣir* (helper), *anṣār*.

**مِّن دُونِ اللَّهِ** — "other than God", that is, "against God, apart from Him".

Now look back: in verse 21 it was said that the people followed the man whose **wealth and children were many**. Wealth and children are a human being's most concrete sources of "help" — one buys, the other stands behind you. Verse 25 states that those sources did not work.

That is, the sūra closes this: **what is relied on is not there at the moment it is relied on.** This is a recurring verdict of the Qurʾān (*"His wealth and what he earned did not avail him"*, al-Masad 111/2; *"His wealth will not avail him when he falls"*, al-Layl 92/11).

## 71/26-27 — Noah's imprecation

**26.** وَقَالَ نُوحٌ رَّبِّ لَا تَذَرْ عَلَى الْأَرْضِ مِنَ الْكَافِرِينَ دَيَّارًا
*Wa qāla Nūḥun Rabbi lā tadhar ʿalā'l-arḍi mina'l-kāfirīna dayyārā*
"Noah said: My Lord! Do not leave a single one of the disbelievers on the earth."

**27.** إِنَّكَ إِن تَذَرْهُمْ يُضِلُّوا عِبَادَكَ وَلَا يَلِدُوا إِلَّا فَاجِرًا كَفَّارًا
*Innaka in tadharhum yuḍillū ʿibādaka wa lā yalidū illā fājiran kaffārā*
"For if You leave them they will lead Your servants astray and will beget none but the depraved, the utterly ungrateful."

### The two heaviest verses of the sūra

These two verses must be taken up without softening. A prophet is asking for the destruction of the whole of his own people. There is little like it in the Qurʾān.

The order of treatment will be this: first the words, then the ground of the verse, then its moral frame.

### دَيَّارًا — "a single one"

A word that occurs in the Qurʾān **only here**.

**Root د-و-ر**: to turn, to go round, to encircle. From the same root come *dār* (a house — a place enclosed round about), *dawr* (a turn), *dāʾira*, *dawarān*.

**Dayyār** is on the *fayʿāl* pattern (originally *daywār*, becoming *dayyār* by a sound change). Its meaning: **one who dwells in a house, who moves about there.** That is, not "a human being" but "one who is settled, who has made the place his home".

The most defining property of the word is that in Arabic it is used **only in negative sentences**: *mā fi'l-dāri dayyār* — "there is not a soul in the house". Like the English "not a living soul", it is an idiomatic pattern for conveying total emptiness.

This makes the choice of the word meaningful. When Noah says "leave no one", he uses **the most settled word Arabic has for conveying emptiness**. And the sense of "house" at the root of the word adds an image to the sentence: let no one remain dwelling in his house on the earth.

An observation: in the last verse of this same sūra Noah will pray for **"whoever entered my house as a believer"** (*man dakhala baytī muʾminan*). Between the two verses "house" occurs twice — one to be emptied, the other to be protected. This is an observation resting not on unity of root but on unity of concept.

### عَلَى الْأَرْضِ — "on the earth"

The scope of the prayer is determined by these two words, and a question arises here: does Noah mean the whole earth, or his own region?

The word **ارض** means in Arabic both "the earth" and "soil, land, region". Both uses occur in the Qurʾān. The wording of the verse is therefore **open to both readings**:

- The whole of the earth → the flood is universal.
- The land where Noah's people lived → the flood is regional.

This distinction is the grammatical leg of the dispute about the flood touched on at the beginning of the sūra, and the Qurʾān does not close the question. I make no preference.

**One further note:** in making this prayer Noah says *"of the disbelievers"*. That is, the object of the prayer is not "human beings" but those carrying a particular attribute. Those of his own people who believed are outside this sentence — in verse 28 he will pray for them separately.

### Verse 27: the ground of the prayer

Noah does not leave his prayer without a ground. Verse 27 offers a ground with a **conditional sentence**: *"if You leave them…"*

Two consequences are enumerated:

**1. يُضِلُّوا عِبَادَكَ — "they will lead Your servants astray."** That is, the matter is not their own condition; it is **their effect on others.** In verse 24 this was recorded as something already done (*"they have led many astray"*). Here it is said that it will continue.

Note: it says "Your servants". That is, the party to be protected is those who believe.

**2. وَلَا يَلِدُوا إِلَّا فَاجِرًا كَفَّارًا — "they will beget none but the depraved and the ungrateful."**

This is the heaviest sentence in the verse and it **must be read very carefully.**

**فاجر**, root ف-ج-ر: to split, to overflow, to tear through a limit. The concrete sense of the root is water's cleaving its bed and overflowing; from the same root comes *fajr* (the dawn's cleaving of the darkness). The analysis of this root was made in Sūrat ʿAbasa (80/42, *al-kafaratu'l-fajara*); I rely on that. *Fujūr* is the tearing of the moral limit.

**كفار**, an intensive on the *faʿʿāl* pattern: utterly ungrateful, persistent in denial. (The same pattern as *ghaffār* in verse 10 — one an excess in forgiving, the other an excess in denying.)

### Is this sentence a verdict on lineage?

No — and this is something that must be said openly.

**First: this sentence is not God's verdict but Noah's ground.** The verse occurs inside Noah's prayer. The Qurʾān is not laying down a rule here; it is relating an assessment a prophet offered to his Lord.

**Second: the Qurʾān's general principle is the opposite of this.** *"No bearer of a burden bears the burden of another"* (al-Anʿām 6/164; al-Isrāʾ 17/15; Fāṭir 35/18; al-Zumar 39/7; al-Najm 53/38). This principle is among the rulings the Qurʾān repeats most. No one is under sentence because of the family he was born into.

**Third: the Qurʾān's own example within the story of Noah refutes it.** Noah's son did not believe (Hūd 11/42-43) — that is, a believing father has an unbelieving son. An example in the reverse direction is within the same story too. Lineage does not bequeath faith; nor does it bequeath denial.

**What, then, does Noah's sentence say?** There are two readings and both fit the text:

- **An observation resting on knowledge.** Noah is expressing an outcome that has been made known to him. The Qurʾān records that Noah was told: *"None of your people will believe now except those who have already believed"* (Hūd 11/36). This notice shows that Noah had **certain knowledge** in his hands. His prayer comes after that knowledge.
- **An inference resting on long observation.** Noah is a preacher who has seen the same outcome across generations. His sentence is the expression of that observation: this order reproduces itself.

The second reading takes the sentence out of being a genetic claim and turns it into **a social observation**: what determines what a child will become is not his lineage but the order he is born into and in which no alternative remains. Unless this order is broken, the outcome does not change.

In my judgement the second reading fits the text and the Qurʾān as a whole better; but this preference is not binding.

### The moral frame of the prayer

Now let us come to the real question: how is a prophet's making such a prayer to be assessed?

Four elements have to be set side by side.

**1. The length of the span.** The Qurʾān says Noah stayed among his people *"a thousand years less fifty"* (al-ʿAnkabūt 29/14). That figure is not in this sūra; but the variation of method the sūra describes (night and day, open and secret, offer and proof) is the mark of a long process. The prayer does not come after the first refusal.

**2. Knowledge.** Mentioned above: at Hūd 11/36 Noah **was told** that no one would believe any longer. That is, the prayer was made after the possibility had closed. Such a prayer was not made while the guidance of a single person was still possible.

**3. Noah's own position.** The Qurʾān records his state in another place too: *"He called upon his Lord: I am overcome, so help me"* (al-Qamar 54/10). That is, this is not an imprecation made from a position of power; it is the cry of **a defeated man**. The difference is morally decisive.

**4. And most important: Noah's prayers are not automatically counted correct.** The Qurʾān shows this openly within the same story. When Noah said of his drowning son *"My Lord! My son is of my family; and Your promise is the truth"*, the answer is: *"O Noah! He is not of your family; what he did was not right. Do not ask Me for what you have no knowledge of; I counsel you not to be among the ignorant"* (Hūd 11/45-46). And Noah's answer is: *"My Lord! I take refuge with You from asking You for what I have no knowledge of"* (Hūd 11/47).

**This is the most important balance the Qurʾān sets up within itself.** One prayer of Noah's was granted (in this sūra), and one prayer was openly refused and he himself was warned (in Hūd). That is, no rule such as "a prophet prayed, therefore it is right" is set up by the Qurʾān.

In reading these two verses one must remember that the same prophet received the warning *"do not ask Me for what you have no knowledge of"* about another of his prayers. The prayer Noah makes here, by contrast, is made upon **something he knew** — that is where the difference lies.

Note: the root ع-و-ذ and Noah's sentence of taking refuge at Hūd 11/47 were treated in the discussion of Sūrat al-Falaq (113/1); I rely on that.

### What this says to the present

There is no "licence to imprecate" that can be drawn from these two verses, and none should be. The conditions the sūra itself sets up — an effort spread over centuries, every method exhausted, and certain knowledge of the outcome — do not come about for any human being. The third above all: **no one has certain knowledge of who will believe.**

The Qurʾān's general command on this matter is clear and runs in the opposite direction: *"Call to the way of your Lord with wisdom and beautiful admonition; and dispute with them in the best manner"* (al-Naḥl 16/125). And what was said to the Prophet when he wished to pass judgement on those who opposed him is: *"Nothing of this matter is yours"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/128).

What these verses give the reader is not a permission but a **sense of scale**: for a human being to give up hope of another, he would need to have what Noah had in his hands — a divine notice. No one has that notice.

## 71/28 — رَّبِّ اغْفِرْ لِي وَلِوَالِدَيَّ وَلِمَن دَخَلَ بَيْتِيَ مُؤْمِنًا وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ وَلَا تَزِدِ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا تَبَارًا

*Rabbi'ghfir lī wa li-wālidayya wa li-man dakhala baytiya muʾminan wa li'l-muʾminīna wa'l-muʾmināt; wa lā tazidi'l-ẓālimīna illā tabārā*

"My Lord! Forgive me, and my parents, and whoever entered my house as a believer, and all believing men and believing women. And do not increase the wrongdoers in anything but ruin."

### The closing prayer of the sūra

After the weight of verses 26-27 the sūra closes with **a prayer for forgiveness**. And the structure of this prayer is carefully built.

### The rings of the prayer

The prayer draws four rings widening outward from the centre:

| Ring | Who | Its scope |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | *lī* — me | Himself |
| **2** | *li-wālidayya* — my parents | The tie of blood |
| **3** | *li-man dakhala baytiya muʾminan* — whoever entered my house as a believer | The near circle, conditional |
| **4** | *li'l-muʾminīna wa'l-muʾmināt* — all believing men and women | No limit of time or place |

**The order itself gives a measure.** Noah asks first for himself.

At first sight this may look strange — like selfishness. It is not, and it is an order repeated in the Qurʾān (Abraham's prayer: *"Our Lord! Forgive me, and my parents, and the believers, on the day the reckoning is held"*, Ibrāhīm 14/41). The ground of the order is this: a person who has not settled his own account is in no position to ask for another. To begin with oneself is not a priority but an **admission**: I am on this list too.

And note: Noah is a prophet who preached for nine hundred and fifty years and whose people were destroyed. In the last verse of the sūra the first thing he asks for is **his own forgiveness.** The principle treated in Sūrat al-Naṣr (110/3) holds here too: the act performed when the work is finished is asking forgiveness — "giving up the claim that what was done was done without shortfall."

### وَلِوَالِدَيَّ — "my parents"

**The word is dual**: *wālidayya* — the two parents.

Root و-ل-د: to beget. *Wālid* (father), *wālida* (mother), *walad* (child). The wide analysis of this root was made in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ (112/3).

Noah's asking forgiveness for his parents has been made the ground of an inference in the classical commentaries: **that they were believers.** The ground is this: the Qurʾān openly forbids asking forgiveness for those who have died as *mushrikūn* and treats this by the example of Abraham's father (al-Tawba 9/113-114). Since a prophet cannot be supposed to do what is forbidden, Noah's parents must have been believers.

This is an inference, not an open statement of the Qurʾān. There is no other information whatever about Noah's parents in the Qurʾān.

### وَلِمَن دَخَلَ بَيْتِيَ مُؤْمِنًا — "whoever entered my house as a believer"

The third ring, one of the most disputed expressions in the sūra.

**بيتي — what does "my house" mean?** Three readings are transmitted in the classical commentaries:

| Reading | Its ground |
|---|---|
| **Noah's house** — his dwelling | The first and clearest sense of the word |
| **The ark** | In the flood, "to enter the house" is to board the ark; the Qurʾān mentions those who boarded separately (Hūd 11/40) |
| **A mosque / place of worship** | The Qurʾān uses the word "bayt" for a place of worship too (al-Baqara 2/125) |

The first reading is the most widespread and stands closest to the wording of the word. The second is reasonable too and fits the plot of the story. I make no definite preference.

**مُؤْمِنًا — the real point is here.**

The word is a **circumstantial accusative**: "he entered — being a believer". That is, the verse does not set two conditions; it sets **a single condition**, and that condition is not "to enter" but **"to enter as a believer."**

This is a fine distinction and consistent with the whole sūra. Not everyone who entered Noah's house is within the scope of the prayer. Physical nearness produces no status.

**And this has an extremely sharp counterpart within the Qurʾān:** Noah's wife. The Qurʾān mentions the wives of Noah and of Lot together and gives this verdict: *"Both were under two righteous servants of Ours; but they betrayed them. Their husbands availed them nothing against God"* (al-Taḥrīm 66/10).

That is, "entering the house" — and as the prophet's wife at that — is not enough. The note *muʾminan* in Noah's prayer preserves exactly this distinction. Even while praying, the sūra does not loosen the measure.

I record that I make this link between the sūra and al-Taḥrīm 66/10 as my own reading.

### وَلِلْمُؤْمِنِينَ وَالْمُؤْمِنَاتِ — all the believers

The fourth and widest ring. And here the prayer goes beyond Noah's people.

In this expression there is **no note of time.** It does not say "those who believed on that day". Most classical commentaries say that this expression covers **all believers until the resurrection**.

If this reading is correct, the sūra closes thus: a prophet rejected for centuries prays in his last sentence for everyone who will come after him.

**And there is this too:** *muʾminīn* and *muʾmināt* — men and women are named separately. This is the **only place** in the sūra where women are mentioned. This paired construction is a method the Qurʾān uses to state openly that a ruling covers both sides (its widest example: al-Aḥzāb 33/35).

### وَلَا تَزِدِ الظَّالِمِينَ إِلَّا تَبَارًا — the last sentence of the sūra

The **fourth and last "increase"** sentence of the sūra. It is the same as the sentence in verse 24, only the last word changed: there *ḍalāl* (straying), here *tabār*.

**تبار**, root ب-و-ر: **to lie idle, to become unworkable, to fall to ruin.**

The concrete sense of the root comes from agriculture: *arḍun bāʾir*, land unsown, left empty, giving no yield. From here arises the sense "to become useless, to rot".

Its derivatives:
- **bār / būr** — coming to nothing, useless. In the Qurʾān: *"You are a people gone to ruin"* (al-Fatḥ 48/12, *qawman būrā*).
- **yabūr** — it comes to nothing. *"The snare of those who plot evil comes to nothing"* (Fāṭir 35/10).
- **tabār** — ruin, total destruction.

**The choice of the word closes the sūra.** These were a people who had been promised **gardens and rivers** in verse 12 — that is, sown, watered, fertile land. The name of what they receive in return for what they rejected is a word chosen from the same field: **land left empty, giving no yield.**

I record this as my own reading; the agricultural sense of the root, on the other hand, is information given in the classical lexicons.

### Two prayers, one verse

The most striking side of verse 28 is that it joins **two opposite prayers in a single sentence**: the request for forgiveness and the request for ruin. Between them there is only a *wāw*.

This juxtaposition is uncomfortable and it should be. The sūra does not put mercy and verdict into separate verses and hold them apart; it says them in the same breath.

This is the Qurʾān's general method in any case, and it was recorded in other parts of this text as well: glad tidings and warning stand side by side, neither cancelling the other. Here the same thing appears in a prophet's prayer.

## The sound texture of the sūra

The verse-endings of Sūrat Nūḥ set up an order strikingly consistent with the content of the sūra. In order:

**1-4:** *alīm — mubīn — aṭīʿūn — taʿlamūn*
**5-14:** *nahārā — firārā — istikbārā — jihārā — isrārā — ghaffārā — midrārā — anhārā — waqārā — aṭwārā*
**15-20:** *ṭibāqā — sirājā — nabātā — ikhrājā — bisāṭā — fijājā*
**21-28:** *khasārā — kubbārā — nasrā — ḍalālā — anṣārā — dayyārā — kaffārā — tabārā*

There are four clusters, and **the clusters coincide with the sūra's sections of content:**

- **1-4** (introduction and first address): the verse-endings are variable, no weave has been set up yet.
- **5-14** (report and offer): **ten verses one after another** end in the long *-ārā*. This is a monotony rarely met with even in short sūras.
- **15-20** (the cosmological proofs): the sound **changes**. The long *ā* is kept but the final consonant changes: *q, j, t, j, ṭ, j*. A separate texture across six verses.
- **21-28** (conclusion and prayers): *-ārā* returns (apart from *ḍalālā* in verse 24).

That is, **the proof section of the sūra carries its own identity of sound.** One sound while Noah relates and prays, another while he points to the universe.

I record this coincidence as an observation; I do not know of a classification of this kind being made in the classical sources. The verse-endings themselves, on the other hand, are a datum of the text and are not disputed.

One further note: **the verse-ending of verse 24** (*ḍalālā*) breaks the *-ārā* series around it. And verse 24 is exactly the verse where the sūra passes from report to prayer. I make no claim as to whether this coincidence is deliberate; I record it.

## Links within the sūra

The sūra contains a striking number of internal echoes in twenty-eight verses. Seeing them together shows how tightly the sūra is woven.

| Root / concept | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **ز-ي-د** (to increase) | 6, 21, 24, 28 | The backbone of the sūra: everything increases, in the wrong direction |
| **د-ع-و** (to call) | 5, 6, 7, 8 | The call to the people becomes prayer to the Lord |
| **غ-ف-ر** (to forgive) | 7, 10, 28 | Noah's intention → a command to the people → Noah's own request |
| **و-ذ-ر** (to leave) | 23, 26, 27 | "Do not leave your gods" ↔ "do not leave a single one" |
| **و-ق-ر** (weight) | 7 (*waqr*, in the ear), 13 (*waqār*) | Those who did not give the weight to God put it into their own ears |
| **Wealth and offspring** | 12, 21 | What is promised ↔ what turns to loss |
| **Cognate accusative** | 7, 9, 17, 18, 22 | The sūra's emphasis on force and certainty |
| **"House"** | 26 (*dayyār*, one dwelling in a house), 28 (*baytī*, my house) | What is to be emptied ↔ what is to be protected |
| **Garden/water ↔ barren** | 12 (*jannāt*, *anhār*), 28 (*tabār*) | Fertile land ↔ land left empty |
| **Noah's name** | 1, 21, 26 | The section headings |

## The sūra as a whole: what a report of preaching says

Let us read the sūra once more from beginning to end, this time with a single question: **what was this report given in order to prove?**

The answer stands in the sūra's own structure. Noah enumerates the following:

1. **The task was received** (1-2) — I did not begin of my own accord.
2. **The message was fully delivered** (3-4) — I left nothing out.
3. **The time was used** (5) — night and day.
4. **The outcome was recorded** (6-7) — they fled, they closed themselves.
5. **The method was varied** (8-9) — open, secret, both at once.
6. **Encouragement was tried** (10-12) — I promised abundance.
7. **Reasoning was tried** (13-20) — I brought proofs.
8. **The outcome was reported** (21-24) — they opposed, they followed, they laid a snare.

This is not a defence; it is an **account**. And the place where the account is given is a place where the outcome is not asked for — because it is presented to the Lord.

**The sūra's quietest observation is here.** Nowhere in his report does Noah say *"how many believed"*. He gives no number. He states no outcome. The only thing he states is **what he did.**

The limit of a messenger's responsibility is spread across the whole sūra, and the Qurʾān formulates it openly elsewhere: *"Yours is only the delivering of the message"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/20; al-Raʿd 13/40; al-Naḥl 16/82; al-Shūrā 42/48). This sūra is that sentence in its applied form.

## What this says to the present

**First: the measure of the effort is not the outcome.**

This is the sūra's clearest and hardest teaching. Everything enumerated in Noah's report was done; and the outcome is a handful of people. The sūra does not present this as a failure.

Its present-day counterpart is wide: the teacher, the parent, the physician, the counsellor — everyone doing work whose outcome is not in his own hands. The measure the sūra gives is not to make the outcome unimportant; it is **to stop the outcome being the mark-book of the effort.** The difference between the two determines whether a person can carry on.

**Second: varying the method is a responsibility.**

Verses 8-9 show that persisting in a single method is not defensible. Noah tried the open, tried the secret, tried both at once; he tried encouragement, he tried reasoning. To repeat the same thing in the same way and expect the outcome to change is outside the model the sūra presents.

**Third: the four defences still work.**

The series in verse 7 — finger, covering, persistence, pride — is a complete inventory of closing oneself to a piece of knowledge, and though its technical form has changed its structure has not. And that pride is placed at the end of the series shows where the diagnosis lies: the other three are consequences.

**Fourth: wealth makes its owner an authority.**

Verse 21 describes what a society follows and on what basis: visible gain. This mechanism is faster still today, because gain is more visible. The question the verse asks is plain and still worth asking: how does the account of the person you follow stand?

**Fifth: *shirk* comes as a reflex of conservation.**

Verse 23 says that "the greatest snare" is a slogan: *do not leave.* What the Qurʾān criticises here is not attachment to tradition but **putting tradition in the place of discussion.** The truth of a thing is not measured by how old it is — just as it is not measured by how new it is.

**Sixth: the loss of memory is the carrier of deviation.**

The mechanism described by the report about the five idols — a monument is set up, a generation passes, why it was set up is forgotten, respect turns into worship — is not peculiar to religion. Every institution, every tradition, every practice carries this risk: when the reason for which it was done is forgotten, the practice becomes its own reason.

**Seventh: abundance is not a formula.**

Verses 10-12 tie asking forgiveness to worldly plenty; the sūra itself records in verse 21 that this plenty can turn to loss. Unless the two are read together the verses are open to exploitation. The link the Qurʾān makes is real but it is **social, conditional and not guaranteed.**

**Eighth: the messenger's last word is asking forgiveness for himself.**

The sūra closes, at the end of a task of nine hundred and fifty years, with the man who performed the task asking for his own forgiveness. The principle in Sūrat al-Naṣr holds here too: no work has been done as well as it is claimed to have been done.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

At several points through the text definite language was deliberately not used; the reader needs to know these:

- No ruling was issued on **the geographical extent of the flood** (universal or regional). The Qurʾān does not close the question; the word *arḍ* in verse 26 is open to both readings.
- No thesis was defended about **the relation between the Mesopotamian flood texts and the Qurʾānic account**; only the structural difference was recorded.
- Three readings were given of the function of **the *min* in the expression *min dhunūbikum* at 71/4**; no preference was adopted.
- **The question of the two appointed terms** (71/4) is a theological dispute; the views were transmitted, no ruling was issued.
- The classical explanations of **the *istaghshaw thiyābahum* at 71/7** were transmitted; none was counted definitely correct.
- Two readings were given of **the order of the secret and open call at 71/9**; no preference was adopted.
- Three readings were given for **the *lā tarjūna* at 71/13**. An inclination towards one was stated and it was written that it is not binding.
- No preference was adopted as to whether **the number "seven" at 71/15** is a literal number or conveys multiplicity. Matching it with modern astronomy, on the other hand, was openly rejected.
- **The distinction between *nūr* and *sirāj* at 71/16** was given at the level of the lexical sense of the words; that the classical exegetes saw the difference was recorded. **It was not turned into a claim of scientific miracle** and the grounds for not turning it into one were written out.
- The classical explanations of the matter of **the *fīhinna* at 71/16** were transmitted; no preference was adopted.
- **The variant reading of *kubbār* at 71/22** was recorded in general terms; the names of the imāms of the readings were not given, because I am not certain of them.
- **On the five idols at 71/23:** what the Qurʾān says, what the transmitted report says and what the external sources say were given separately. It was stated that the report transmitted with an ascription to Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Bukhārī's section of exegesis is **mawqūf (the word of a Companion)**; it was recorded that the reliability of the body of detail around it falls.
- **The data concerning the South Arabian inscriptions** were given in broad outline; no claim was made about a particular inscription number, reading or dating.
- No ruling was issued on the question of **how these names passed from Noah's people to Arabia**; two readings were set side by side.
- On **the relation between the root و-د-د and the idol Wadd**, the note from the discussion of al-Burūj was preserved: the unity of the root is a datum of the language, and beyond that no ruling was issued.
- Two readings were given of the subject of the verb **at 71/24, *aḍallū*,** (the idols, or the notables); no preference was adopted.
- Two readings were given as to whether the expression **at 71/25, *fa-udkhilū nāran*,** is evidence for the punishment of the *barzakh*; no preference was adopted. This is a matter falling within the field of theology.
- **The variant readings at 71/25** were recorded in general terms; no imām's name was given.
- **The *lā yalidū illā fājiran kaffārā* at 71/27**: that this is not a verdict on lineage was grounded in the Qurʾān's own principles. Two readings were given, an inclination towards one was stated and it was written that it is not binding.
- No preference was adopted as to what the word **at 71/28, *baytī*,** is (a house / the ark / a place of worship).
- **That Noah's parents were believers** was transmitted as a classical inference; it was stated that this is not an open declaration of the Qurʾān.
- The places **marked in the text as "my own reading"** are inferences, not transmitted reports: the emphasis of word order at 71/6, the ordering of night and day at 71/5, the *waqr* / *waqār* link, the echo of the root و-ذ-ر between 23 and 26, the singular–plural difference between 12 and 21, the agricultural link between *jannāt* and *tabār*, the coincidence of the verse-ending clusters with the sections, and the link between 71/28 and al-Taḥrīm 66/10.
