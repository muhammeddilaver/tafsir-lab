# 82. Sūrat al-Infiṭār

Nineteen verses. It takes its name from the verb in the first verse: **انفطرت** — it was split open.

The sūra is built upon two movements, and the two are exact opposites of one another.

The first movement is at the opening: the sky is split, the stars are scattered, the seas are burst open and poured out, the graves are overturned. Four verses, four verbs, and all four recount **the coming apart of something that had been held together**.

The second movement begins at the seventh verse: *He created you, He proportioned you, He balanced you, He assembled you in whatever form He willed.* Four verbs again — and all four recount **the gathering together of what was scattered**.

The two verses in between are the bridge: one says that every soul will learn what it sent ahead and what it left behind (5), the other puts a single question to man (6). The whole weight of the sūra gathers in that question:

> **مَا غَرَّكَ بِرَبِّكَ الْكَرِيمِ** — "What has deceived you about your generous Lord?"

The real subtlety of this question lies not in its being asked but in **which of God's attributes is named inside it**. The verse does not say "about your mighty Lord" or "about your overpowering Lord". It says **"about your generous Lord"**. That is: what is shown as the cause of the deception is not the severity of the one deceived about, but His **generosity**. I shall return to this in a separate section; that is the heart of the sūra.

## The skeleton of the sūra

1. **Coming apart** (1-4): four scenes, four verbs with the doer unnamed.
2. **The outcome** (5): every soul learns what it did and what it left behind.
3. **The question** (6): direct address to man — what deceived you?
4. **The answer inside the question** (7-8): the one who made you was He; it is told with four verbs.
5. **The diagnosis shifts ground** (9): the matter is not deception but denial.
6. **The record** (10-12): there are recorders over you.
7. **Two groups** (13-16): the *abrār* and the *fujjār*; two sentences symmetrical to the letter.
8. **The pause** (17-18): "and what will make you know what the Day of Judgement is" — twice.
9. **The answer** (19): on that day no soul has power over anything for another soul.

There is a proportion worth noticing: the sūra gives four verses to the scene of the end, two verses to the making of man, three verses to the record. But the thing that takes up most room is **the non-transferability of the reckoning** — once in verse 5, once in verse 19, and everything that passes between the two binds those two sentences together.

## The name of the sūra

*Infiṭār* is the verbal noun of the verb in the first verse. In the sources the sūra is also called *"Idhā'l-samāʾu'nfaṭarat"*. In the early period sūras were referred to not by an official title but by a striking word inside them; this second name is a trace of that usage.

There is one further sūra name derived from the same root: **Fāṭir** (sūra 35). The two come from one root and stand at its two ends — one names **splitting something open into being**, the other **being split apart**. This is no coincidence; it is a doubleness that comes from the structure of the root itself, and it will be taken up in detail at the first verse.

## Meccan

On the sūra's being Meccan there is something close to unanimity in the sources. The content supports it as well: short rhymed verses, an opening with the scene of the end, the direct address "O man", the denial of the reckoning as the central matter, and the absence of any legal regulation.

The sūra stands inside a cluster of the Qurʾān that lies very close together: al-Takwīr (81), al-Infiṭār (82), al-Inshiqāq (84) — all three open with the pattern "**إذا** … when", all three enumerate a cosmic coming apart, and all three then pass to the reckoning of man. The comparison of these three sūras will be made in its place.

The word-by-word analysis of the basmala was carried out in the section on al-Fātiḥa; it is not repeated here. The verse numbers are counted without it.

## 82/1 — إِذَا ٱلسَّمَآءُ ٱنفَطَرَتْ

*Idhā'l-samāʾu'nfaṭarat*
"When the sky is split open."

### إِذَا — not a condition but a calendar

This use of the particle *idhā* was treated in the section on al-Zilzāl: in Arabic *idhā* is used for events counted as certain to occur, *in* for those in doubt. The sentence therefore does not say "if the sky splits", it says **"when the sky splits"**. Whether the event will happen is outside the discussion; what is spoken of is what will happen when it does.

There is a further point here. The sūra opens with **four** *idhā* clauses (1, 2, 3, 4) and the answer to all four stands in the fifth verse: *ʿalimat nafsun.* That is, four conditional clauses are attached to a single main clause. In Arabic this structure produces **waiting** by delaying the answer: the listener takes in four scenes one upon another, the sentence is still not complete, and at last it closes in the fifth verse.

One consequence of this delay is the following: **the four scenes are not a scene recounted for its own sake; they are the condition of a sentence.** The description of the end is not a subject here but a note of time. The sūra does not say "the end will be like this"; it says "when this happens, this will be known".

### ٱلسَّمَآء — the sky

**Root: س-م-و.** Height. *Samāʾ* means "that which is above"; from the same root come *ism* (which raises a thing above the others and marks it out), *sumuww* (loftiness), *asmāʾ*.

The most concrete use of the word in Arabic is striking: everything above a thing is that thing's *samāʾ*. The *samāʾ* of a room is its ceiling, the *samāʾ* of man is the sky. The word therefore names not a body but a **position**: what stands above.

This determines the picture the verse gives. What is split is not an object; it is **the covering over us**. In most places the Qurʾān speaks of the sky as a roof or a ceiling:

- *"And We made the sky a protected ceiling."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/32)
- *"Who made the sky a building and the earth a bed."* (al-Baqara 2/22 — this verse was treated in the section on al-Shams.)

The splitting of a ceiling is something other than the breaking of an object: **it is the end of a closed space's being closed.** The first sentence of the sūra recounts the opening of the enclosed volume man lives inside.

### ٱنفَطَرَتْ — *infaṭarat*

The word that gives the sūra its name, and the product of one of the densest roots in the Qurʾān.

**Root: ف-ط-ر.**

The root's basic sense in the lexicon is **to split, to crack lengthwise, to open**. But the Arabic dictionaries give two clusters of meaning under this root that look different from one another, and this doubleness carries the whole force of the verse:

**The first cluster — to split, to be split:**
- *faṭara* — he split, he cracked.
- *infaṭara* — it was split (reflexive/passive).
- *fuṭūr* — cracks, fissures.
- *tafaṭṭara* — it cracked and broke apart.

**The second cluster — to make for the first time, to begin out of nothing:**
- *faṭara'l-khalqa* — He began the creation, He brought it into being for the first time.
- *fāṭir* — the one who brings into being out of nothing, who creates first.
- *fiṭra* — the state of a thing at its first creation, its condition before any intervention.

**That these two clusters are found in one root is no coincidence, and there is a settled explanation of it among the lexicographers:** *faṭr* is the first opening of something closed and whole. To "create out of nothing" is to split the closedness of non-being and bring something out. That is, **creation too is a splitting.**

This is the common logic of several "splitting" roots the Qurʾān uses, and two of them were treated in earlier sections:

- **ف-ل-ق** (the section on al-Falaq): the splitting of the seed and the stone, the splitting of the morning. *Fāliqu'l-ḥabbi wa'l-nawā*, *fāliqu'l-iṣbāḥ.*
- **ف-ج-ر** (the section on al-Shams): the bursting forth of water, the splitting of the dawn, and in the moral sense the tearing of the bound (*fujūr*).

**ف-ط-ر** is added to these two as a third and has a place of its own. Its difference is this:

| Root | What is split | What comes out | Its principal field in the Qurʾān |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ف-ل-ق** | Seed, husk, night | Shoot, light | Birth, the opening of life |
| **ف-ج-ر** | Ground, rock, bound | Water, dawn — and in *fujūr*: overflowing | Bursting out, overflowing |
| **ف-ط-ر** | Non-being / wholeness | Existence itself | **First creation** and **final dissolution** |

*Falaq* and *fajr* recount **something coming out of something**. *Faṭr* recounts **a thing beginning to exist** or **ceasing to exist**. This is why *fāṭir* is an attribute of creating while *infiṭār* is a verb of dissolution — and both stand in one root.

This observation must not be confused with the claim of "the greater derivation". As was stated in the section on al-Falaq, the search for nearness of meaning among roots sharing their first two letters is a recognised topic in Arabic, but it is not a claim of unity of root. What is said here is simpler and beyond dispute: **ف-ط-ر is a single root, and the dictionaries give under this root both "to split" and "to bring into being for the first time".**

### The root's other derivatives

**فِطْرَة (*fiṭra*).** The structure of the word is worth dwelling on. The *fiʿla* pattern in Arabic conveys **the manner of doing a thing, its state**: *jilsa* (the manner of sitting), *rikba* (the manner of riding). So *fiṭra* is not "creation" but **"the manner of creation, the state acquired in the act of being created"**.

Its occurrence in the Qurʾān: *"So set your face to the religion as a ḥanīf. The fiṭra of God upon which He created people."* (al-Rūm 30/30). In the verse the noun (*fiṭra*) and the verb (*faṭara*) stand side by side.

**The idea of "the first splitting" in the root of the word is reflected in the concept of *fiṭra* as well:** the *fiṭra* is the first state, to which nothing has yet been added, which has undergone no intervention. This is a subject to be treated at length in later sections; here I record only the kinship of the root.

**إِفْطَار (*ifṭār*).** The breaking of the fast. The word familiar in English as *iftar* comes from this root, and the choice is meaningful: a fast is a closing, and *ifṭār* is the **opening** of that closedness. From the same root *faṭūr* — breakfast, the meal that opens the night's hunger. And *faṭīr* — unleavened bread, bread not left to stand, made at once; that is, bread that has stayed in its first state.

Read together, these derivatives make the idea beneath the root clear: **the opening of what is closed.** Creation is the opening of the closedness of non-being. The *fiṭra* is the state at the moment of opening. *Ifṭār* is the opening of the fast's closedness. And *infiṭār* is the opening of the sky's closedness.

### فَاطِر — the one who splits into being out of nothing

The Qurʾān names God with this root in six places. The occurrences I can give with certainty:

- *"Say: shall I take as a protector any other than God, the one who splits the heavens and the earth into being?"* (al-Anʿām 6/14)
- *"I have turned my face, as a ḥanīf, towards the one who split the heavens and the earth into being."* (al-Anʿām 6/79)
- *"Splitter of the heavens and the earth into being! You are my protector in this world and in the next."* (Yūsuf 12/101)
- *"Praise belongs to God, who split the heavens and the earth into being, who made the angels messengers."* (Fāṭir 35/1)

**About this name there is a report very widely transmitted in the tradition of exegesis.** It is told that Ibn ʿAbbās did not know what the expression *fāṭiru'l-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* meant, and then, hearing one of two bedouin quarrelling over a well say *"anā faṭartuhā"* — "I opened it, I am the one who first dug it" — he grasped the sense of the word. I cannot give the chain of this report with certainty; but it is repeated widely in the books of exegesis and its linguistic content is sound: **faṭr is to begin a thing for the first time** — the one who first digs the well is the one who opens a place no one had opened.

### The Qurʾān's interpretation by the Qurʾān — three separate places of the root

Setting three verses side by side explains the first sentence of the sūra all by itself.

**One — the present state: there is no crack in the sky.**

*"You will see no disharmony in the creation of the Most Merciful. Turn your gaze and look: do you see any fissure?"* (al-Mulk 67/3)

The word in the verse is **فُطُور** — the same root as the verb of Sūrat al-Infiṭār, the same field of meaning: **cracks, fissures.** Sūrat al-Mulk says that the eye that looks will find no fissure in the sky.

**Two — the source: it is He who first split the sky into being.**

*Fāṭiru'l-samāwāti wa'l-arḍ* — the one who splits the heavens and the earth into being.

**Three — the outcome: the sky will be split.**

*"When the sky is split open."* (al-Infiṭār 82/1). And: *"On that day the sky is split by it; His promise is carried out."* (al-Muzzammil 73/18).

The three are three sentences said with the same root, and they set up a line:

> **The sky was brought into being by a splitting (faṭara) → at present there is no fissure in it (fuṭūr) → in the end it will be split (infaṭarat).**

That is, the beginning and the end of the sky are the same verb. It came to be by one splitting, and it ends by another. And throughout the span between, the eye that looks can find no crack in it.

**I present this link not as an interpretation but as the data of the words themselves:** the same root occurs in all three verses and all three take the sky as their subject. The line drawn from that, however, is my own reading.

The root has one further usage, and it opens another door onto the sūra:

*"At this the heavens are near to cracking apart, the earth to splitting, the mountains to falling down in ruin."* (Maryam 19/90). The verse speaks of a child being ascribed to God and renders the response as a cosmic cracking. The verb here too is from the same root (*yatafaṭṭarna*).

This verse is important because **it carries the root into a moral context.** In al-Infiṭār the sky is split because its time has come; in Maryam it is on the point of splitting from the weight of a thing said. Read together, the two verses set up a link between cosmic dissolution and the word and act of man. That link is in the structure of Sūrat al-Infiṭār as well: after the graves are opened in the fourth verse, the reckoning comes in the fifth.

### The verb's coming in the *infiʿāl* form

**انفطر** — the *infaʿala* form. In Arabic this form conveys **muṭāwaʿa**: the object of a verb accepting that verb, undergoing it.

*Kasartuhu fa'nkasara* — "I broke it, and it broke."
*Faṭartuhu fa'nfaṭara* — "I split it, and it split."

So *infaṭarat* means "it was split", but inside it lies this information: **there is one who splits, and the sky has accepted his act.** The verb is not passive (had it been passive it would be *fuṭirat*); it is reflexive. The difference is fine but real: the passive form conceals the doer, the *muṭāwaʿa* form does not conceal the doer — it merely turns the focus of the account onto the object.

Its counterpart within the sūra is this: in all four of the first four verses **the doer is not named** — two are in the *muṭāwaʿa* form (*infaṭarat*, *intatharat*), two are passive (*fujjirat*, *buʿthirat*). In all four the name of the one who does it is withheld. The name of the doer comes in the seventh verse: **الذي خلقك** — "He who created you." The sūra sets up four scenes without saying who scatters, and then says that the same subject is the one who made you.

### What this says to the present

What is hardest for the reader of today in this verse is not the **magnitude** of the event it describes; it is its **certainty.**

The sky is the most unchanging thing in human experience. Cities fall, states end, people die — the sky stays where it is. If one were asked where man learned the notion of "constant", the first answer to be given is that: the stars standing in the same place every night, the sun rising from the same place every morning.

What the verse does is to remove the last fortress of constancy. And it does this not with a threat but with a **note of time**: *idhā* — "when that time comes".

A boundary must be drawn: to build a bridge from here to modern cosmology is a forcing of the kind forbidden in `USLUP.md`, and it will not be done. The verse does not state a proposition of physics; by using one word — *faṭr* — for both the beginning and the end, it says that **the two ends of existing are the same verb**. This is not a measurable claim; it is a way of looking.

The practical consequence of that way of looking is this: **that a thing has remained the same for a long time is not proof that it is permanent.** The human mind infers permanence from continuity; this is an inference that works but is invalid. In the sixth verse the sūra will ask man "what deceived you?"; this is exactly one of the sources of the deception.

## 82/2 — وَإِذَا ٱلْكَوَاكِبُ ٱنتَثَرَتْ

*Wa idhā'l-kawākibu'ntatharat*
"When the stars are scattered."

### ٱلْكَوَاكِب — the *kawākib*

**Singular: كَوْكَب.** The word names the bright bodies in the sky. In modern Arabic *kawkab* has come to mean "planet" and *najm* "star"; **but this distinction did not exist in classical Arabic, and it is not right to load it back onto the verse.** In classical usage a *kawkab* is a bright body visible in the sky — the distinction is not astronomical but visual.

Its uses in the Qurʾān show this:

- *"O my father! I saw eleven stars, and the sun and the moon."* (Yūsuf 12/4). Joseph's dream.
- *"When the night covered him he saw a star."* (al-Anʿām 6/76). The story of Abraham (Ibrāhīm).
- *"As though it were a star shining like a pearl."* (al-Nūr 24/35). The likeness of the lamp in the verse of light.
- *"We adorned the nearest sky with an adornment, the stars."* (al-Ṣāffāt 37/6).

**This last is directly meaningful for our verse.** Al-Ṣāffāt 37/6 describes the stars as *zīna* — an adornment. An adornment is by definition something **arranged**; objects scattered at random are not called an adornment. What makes a necklace an adornment is not the existence of the beads but the manner of their stringing.

And al-Infiṭār 82/2 recounts exactly the undoing of that stringing.

### ٱنتَثَرَتْ — they were scattered

**Root: ن-ث-ر.** Its concrete sense: **to scatter, to sprinkle, to disperse.** And the distinguishing feature of the word lies in the kind of thing scattered: *nathr* in Arabic is used especially for the scattering of **dry things in small grains** — pearls, seed, grain, beads.

Its derivatives:
- *nithār* — the thing scattered; the money or sweets scattered at a wedding.
- *manthūr* — scattered.
- *intathara* (the *iftiʿāl* form) — it was dispersed, it was scattered; the verb in the verse.

### نَثْر and نَظْم — an opposition Arabic makes for itself

The use of this word in Arabic literary terminology explains the verse all by itself.

In Arabic there are two kinds of speech:
- **نَظْم (*naẓm*)** — strung speech, that is, verse. The root sense: **to string a pearl on a thread.** *Niẓām* (order) is from this root too.
- **نَثْر (*nathr*)** — scattered speech, that is, prose. That which is "not strung on a thread", left loose.

Both terms are taken from the same image: **the pearl.** A pearl strung on a thread is *naẓm*, a pearl spilled is *nathr*. In both the pearl is the same pearl; the only thing that changes is the **bond** between them.

The verse applies this image to the stars. The stars do not cease to be; **their order is undone.** What was called an "adornment" in al-Ṣāffāt 37/6 was not the material of the adornment but its arrangement; and it is that arrangement that is removed here.

This carries on the same idea as the first verse of the sūra. The sky was split — the closedness went. The stars were scattered — the order went. In both, what ceases to be is **not an object but a relation**.

### The other usages in the Qurʾān

The root occurs rarely in the Qurʾān, but the places where it occurs are striking:

- *"We shall take in hand every deed they did and make it **scattered dust**."* (al-Furqān 25/23). *Habāʾan manthūrā* — dust flying in the air, not to be caught, not to be gathered together again. Here what is scattered is **deeds**.
- *"Immortal youths go round among them; seeing them, you would think them **scattered pearls**."* (al-Insān 76/19). *Luʾluʾan manthūrā.*

**These two verses together show that the word carries no verdict.** The same word recounts in one place the coming to nothing of deeds and in another the beauty of the garden. To be scattered is not in itself an evil; what is evil is the scattering of **something that ought to be in order**.

For the stars, being scattered is a corruption, because their function lay in their order: finding direction, reckoning time, knowing the season. The Qurʾān says this expressly: *"It is He who made the stars for you, that you might find your way by them through the darknesses of land and sea."* (al-Anʿām 6/97).

What the verse says takes concrete shape in this light: **the thing man looks at to find his direction no longer shows the direction.**

### A comparison with al-Takwīr

Sūrat al-Takwīr recounts the same scene with a different verb:

*"When the stars fall dulled."* (al-Takwīr 81/2) — **انكدرت** (*inkadarat*).

Root **ك-د-ر**: to grow turbid, to darken, to lose one's light. *Kadir* — turbid water. The verb is also explained in the sense of "to pour down from above" (from a verb used of a bird gliding down).

The two sūras say two different things:

| | al-Takwīr 81/2 | al-Infiṭār 82/2 |
|---|---|---|
| Word | **نجوم** (*nujūm*) | **كواكب** (*kawākib*) |
| Verb | **انكدرت** — grew dark / poured down | **انتثرت** — were scattered |
| What is lost | **Light** | **Order** |

Two separate faces of the same event. One recounts what happens in the star itself, the other what happens in the relation between the stars. I record this as an observation; I make no claim that the two sūras have made a deliberate division of labour.

## 82/3 — وَإِذَا ٱلْبِحَارُ فُجِّرَتْ

*Wa idhā'l-biḥāru fujjirat*
"When the seas are burst open and made to flow."

### ٱلْبِحَار — the seas

**Singular: بَحْر.** Root: ب-ح-ر. The basic sense the lexicographers give is **breadth and depth**; the verb *baḥara* also means "to split, to widen". The sea is the wide hollow split open in the earth.

The word's opposite is **بَرّ (*barr*)** — the dry land, and that too carries the sense of "breadth". The two are often named together in the Qurʾān: *"Through the darknesses of land and sea…"* (al-Anʿām 6/97), *"He knows whatever is on land and in the sea."* (al-Anʿām 6/59).

Here a note on the wording must be entered that looks forward in the sūra. In the thirteenth verse of this sūra the word **الأبرار** (*abrār*) will come, and its root is **ب-ر-ر**; in the section on al-Baqara it was recorded that the basic sense of this root is **breadth**, and that from the same root comes *barr* — the wide dry land that stands over against the sea. So *biḥār* in the third verse of the sūra and *abrār* in the thirteenth are relatives of two words that stand facing one another in Arabic's own lexicon.

**I record this as an observation about sound and association, not as a proof.** The roots are separate (ب-ح-ر / ب-ر-ر) and I set up no claim of derivation between them. I merely note that the two words stand over against one another in Arabic and that both are present in the sūra.

### فُجِّرَتْ — burst open and made to flow

**Root: ف-ج-ر.** This root was treated in detail in the section on al-Shams: its concrete sense is **to split, to split open and make flow, to burst and make gush**; *fajr* (the moment when the night is split and the light gushes out) and *fujūr* (the tearing of the bound that holds a person) come from the same root.

Here the **water** side of the root is at work. Its uses in the Qurʾān give this side very plainly:

- *"And We split the earth into springs; and the waters met for a matter already decreed."* (al-Qamar 54/12). Noah's flood: the waters gushing from the earth and the waters coming down from the sky meet.
- *"And We made a river gush forth between the two of them."* (al-Kahf 18/33).
- *"A spring from which the servants of God drink, making it gush and flow."* (al-Insān 76/6).
- *"We shall not believe you until you make a spring gush forth for us from the earth."* (al-Isrāʾ 17/90).

**The pattern: تفعيل (*tafʿīl*).** The verb is not *fajara* but *fajjara* — with the doubled consonant. As was treated in the section on al-Humaza, this form conveys **intensification** (*takthīr*): the multiplicity, the spread, the repetition of the act. So not "it was split at one point" but **"it was split at every point, over and over."**

And the verb is **passive**: *fujjirat* — not "it split" but **"it was split, it was made to flow"**, without the doer being named.

### The disagreement: what is happening to the seas?

The exegetes differ over the event the verse describes. The principal views:

| View | What it means | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **The barrier between them is removed** | The screen between the seas is lifted, and all become a single body of water | Al-Raḥmān 55/19-20 says that there is a *barzakh* between two seas and that they do not mingle. The lifting of that barrier is the end of the order |
| **They join with the waters gushing from the earth** | The earth is split at every point and water gushes out, and the seas overflow | The use of the root in al-Qamar 54/12: the scene of the flood. The end would then be described as a repetition of the flood on a universal scale |
| **The seas are split open and drained** | The sea bed is split, the waters withdraw | The "splitting" sense of the root; and further, its being read together with the verb *sujjirat* (set ablaze / filled up) of al-Takwīr 81/6 |

The first and second views do not exclude one another: both recount the removal of the bound that holds the water. In my judgement this is the real information the verse carries, and which mechanism is meant cannot be settled. **This is a preference; it is not binding.**

Al-Raḥmān 55/19-20 must be touched on separately, because it is directly connected with the logic of the sūra:

*"He has let loose the two seas; they meet. Between them is a barrier they do not cross."*

The verse speaks of an order of **bounds** at work in the world. And a few verses later the same sūra will speak of the balance being set down and of not overreaching in the balance (al-Raḥmān 55/7-9). So in Sūrat al-Raḥmān the bound of water and the bound of measure are named side by side. This link will become central in the next sūra — in al-Muṭaffifīn.

### A link within the sūra: فُجِّرَتْ and ٱلْفُجَّار

Now we come to one of the tightest internal links of the sūra.

**فُجِّرَتْ in the third verse and ٱلْفُجَّار in the fourteenth are from the same root: ف-ج-ر.**

As was recorded in the section on al-Shams, a *fājir* is "the one who splits the covering over him and comes out, who pierces the bound that holds him"; *fujūr* is not a lack but an **overflow** — like water that will not fit its vessel breaking the dyke.

The sūra builds this likeness with **real water**: in the third verse the seas break their dykes and overflow, and in the fourteenth those who split their bound and overflow are named. The same verb, one for water and one for man.

And there is one further layer: in the third verse the overflowing happens **on that day**; in the fourteenth the overflowing is an attribute already accomplished **in this world**. Man breaks his dyke first, the universe after.

**I present this link as my own reading.** The unity of the root is a fact standing in the text; the comparison I draw from it is an interpretation, and I do not ascribe it to any classical exegete.

## 82/4 — وَإِذَا ٱلْقُبُورُ بُعْثِرَتْ

*Wa idhā'l-qubūru buʿthirat*
"When the graves are overturned."

### ٱلْقُبُور — the graves

**Root: ق-ب-ر.** To bury, to inter. *Qabr* — the place in which one is buried. *Maqbara/maqbura* — a burial ground.

In the Qurʾān the word also occurs in a verse that says the burial of the dead is an **honour**: *"Then He caused him to die and put him in the grave."* (ʿAbasa 80/21). The context of the verse is a sequence in which the favours given to man are enumerated; and the grave is inside that sequence. So to be buried is a respect shown to the body of man — it is not to be left in the open.

This increases the weight of the fourth verse: **what was closed is being opened.** The first verse of the sūra had opened the closedness of the sky; the fourth verse opens the closedness of the earth. The uppermost covering and the lowest are lifted two verses apart.

### بُعْثِرَتْ — they were overturned

This word was treated in the section on al-ʿĀdiyāt. I do not repeat what was recorded there; in brief: **a quadriliteral root (ب-ع-ث-ر)**, its sense "to turn a thing upside down, to spill out what is inside it"; some of the lexicographers count it a compound of *baʿatha* (to raise up) and *athāra* (to stir up), but this is a disputed etymology; and the verb comes in the passive.

**The word occurs in only two places in the Qurʾān:** al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/9 and here.

But between the two occurrences there is a difference that deserves attention — and this difference had not been noted in the section on al-ʿĀdiyāt.

### The difference of object: the graves, or those in the graves?

Let us set the two verses side by side:

| | al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/9 | al-Infiṭār 82/4 |
|---|---|---|
| Wording | *buʿthira **mā fī'l-qubūr*** | *buʿthirati **l-qubūr*** |
| Subject of the passive | **What is in the graves** | **The graves themselves** |
| Sense | The content is spilled out | The vessel is overturned |

In al-ʿĀdiyāt what is overturned is **what is inside** the graves; in al-Infiṭār what is overturned is **the graves themselves**.

This difference is not trivial. When Sūrat al-ʿĀdiyāt sets up the scene of the reckoning it was looking at the content — for that sūra goes on to say *"and what is in the breasts is sifted out"*; the subject there is what is brought out. Al-Infiṭār, by contrast, behaves consistently with its other three verses: **the sky is split, the arrangement of the stars is undone, the dyke of the seas is broken, the graves are overturned.** In all four, what is overturned is **structure**, not content.

So the first four verses of al-Infiṭār make a consistent choice: in every verse **what holds a thing together** is removed.

### The common structure of the four

Now let us look at the four verses together.

| Verse | Subject | Verb | Pattern | What is removed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sky | ٱنفَطَرَتْ | *infiʿāl* (*muṭāwaʿa*) | Closedness |
| 2 | Stars | ٱنتَثَرَتْ | *iftiʿāl* (*muṭāwaʿa*) | Arrangement |
| 3 | Seas | فُجِّرَتْ | *tafʿīl* (passive) | Bound |
| 4 | Graves | بُعْثِرَتْ | quadriliteral (passive) | Lid |

Four different patterns, four different roots, and all four ending on the same sound: **-rat.** *Infaṭarat, intatharat, fujjirat, buʿthirat.* In all four the letter **ر** stands immediately before the close. The fifth verse will end on the same sound too: *akhkharat*. Five verses, a single sound.

And in all four **the doer is not named.** Two verbs are in the *muṭāwaʿa* form (that is, not "it was done" but "it happened"), two are passive (that is, "it was done", but without the doer being mentioned). The sūra sets up four scenes without using a subject.

**This gap will be filled in the seventh verse:** *ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَكَ* — "He who created you." The name of the one who scatters will be given as the one who made you.

### An observation about the structure: four undoings, four buildings

The architecture of the sūra becomes visible here, and this must be recorded separately, because it explains the whole of the text.

**In verses 1-4 there are four verbs, and all four recount the dissolution of a whole.**
**In verses 7-8 there are four verbs, and all four recount the building of a whole:** *khalaqa* (He created), *sawwā* (He proportioned), *ʿadala* (He balanced), *rakkaba* (He assembled).

The opposition is exact and arithmetical: four to four.

And between the two series there is not only a contrast but a difference of **direction**:
- The objects of the first four are **the universe** and the verbs have **no subject**.
- The object of the second four is **you**, and all the verbs end with the suffix **كَ** ("you"); their subject is stated openly.

So this is what the sūra does: it recounts the coming apart of the universe without a doer, and then recounts your being built with a doer. On the cosmic stage it is as though there were no one; on the personal stage the subject appears.

I shall cast this structure into a table in the section that treats the sūra as a whole. Here it is enough to state this much: **the enumeration of the things that come apart is there not to present a scene but to prepare the second four.**

## 82/5 — عَلِمَتْ نَفْسٌ مَّا قَدَّمَتْ وَأَخَّرَتْ

*ʿAlimat nafsun mā qaddamat wa akhkharat*
"Every soul knows what it has sent ahead and what it has left behind."

### One answer to four conditions

This verse is the answer to the four preceding *idhā* clauses. In grammar this is called the *jawāb al-sharṭ*, and its position says this: **the four scenes were set up for this sentence.**

To see the weight of that, it is enough to take the verse away and look. The sky is split, the stars are scattered, the seas overflow, the graves are overturned — and as the outcome of all this, what happens? No scene of the end is completed. **A piece of knowledge comes to light.**

The sūra ties the dissolution of the universe to an act of knowing. The "answer" to four verses of cosmic destruction is a person's learning something about himself.

### عَلِمَتْ — it knew

**Root: ع-ل-م.** To know. *ʿIlm*, *ʿālim*, *maʿlūm*, *ʿalāma* (the sign that makes a thing known).

The verb is in the **perfect**: "it knew". The expected tense would have been the imperfect — "it knows / it will know". Its coming in the perfect is the usage treated in the section on al-Humaza: **something certain to occur in the future being recounted in the past tense as though it had happened.** Its function is to convey certainty.

But here there is one more detail. In conditional sentences the answer's coming in the perfect is an ordinary structure in Arabic and may carry no emphasis by itself. So the conclusion to be drawn from the perfect tense should not be overstated. What is certain is this: the verb is not "it learns" but "it knows" — that is, what comes to light is a **piece of knowledge**, not a punishment.

**This distinction matters for the tone of the sūra.** The verse does not say "every soul sees its punishment"; it says "every soul knows". The punishment will be spoken of separately in verses 14-16. What is said here comes earlier: first it is known, then the recompense is given.

### نَفْسٌ — its coming indefinite

The word is **indefinite**: "a soul". But the sense is "every soul".

In Arabic this is a recognised usage. If the context conveys generality, an indefinite noun covers the whole of the class. Here the context makes it necessary: since the four cosmic events encompass all of them at once, the expression "a soul" cannot mean a particular person.

Then why was "*kullu nafsin*" (every soul) not said?

The explanation given in the classical sources is this: **the indefiniteness sets up the scene one by one.** "Every soul knows" states a collective judgement; "a soul knows" takes a single person into view. Not the crowd but the individual. And that individual is the person reading the sentence.

The sūra as a whole supports this choice: in the sixth verse the address will be **singular** (*yā ayyuhā'l-insān*, *gharraka*), and in the nineteenth a single soul will again be spoken of (*lā tamliku nafsun li-nafsin*). That is, the sūra chooses to disperse the crowd and take one person before it.

**The nearest instance of the same pattern is in Sūrat al-Takwīr:** *"Every soul knows what it has made ready."* (al-Takwīr 81/14) — *ʿalimat nafsun mā aḥḍarat.* The structure of the sentence is identical to the letter; only the last verb changes: instead of *aḥḍarat* (what it has made ready), here *qaddamat wa akhkharat*.

Two sūras set up the same sentence at the end of two ten-verse cosmic series. I record this as one of the clearest proofs of the kinship of the two sūras.

### مَا قَدَّمَتْ وَأَخَّرَتْ — what it has sent ahead and what it has left behind

The most disputed expression of the sūra.

**قَدَّمَ** — root ق-د-م. *Qadam* — the foot. *Qudūm* — to come, to arrive. *Taqdīm* — to bring forward, to present, to send on ahead. *Qadīm* — that which comes before.

The concrete sense of the root is **to put forward**, and the usage here rests on an image of trade or of travel: to send a thing on ahead of oneself. A man sends his load before he goes himself; when he arrives he finds it there.

**أَخَّرَ** — root أ-خ-ر. *Ākhir* — the last. *Ākhira* — the abode that comes after. *Taʾkhīr* — to leave behind, to postpone. *Ukhrā* — the other.

The two are a perfect pair of opposites in Arabic: *muqaddam / muʾakhkhar*, that which is brought forward / that which is left behind.

**But what does it mean in this verse?** The exegetes differ, and the views are these:

| View | *Qaddamat* | *Akhkharat* | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Done / not done** | The deeds it performed | What it should have done and did not, what it abandoned | The most widespread view. That the reckoning looks at both act and omission agrees with the general line of the Qurʾān (in al-Māʿūn 107/3 "not urging" was counted as a charge) |
| **Done earlier / later** | What it did at the beginning of its life | What it did at the end | In sense this amounts to "all of it"; the expression is counted a formula of inclusion |
| **Its own deed / the trace it left** | What it performed itself | The effect that goes on after it: the custom it set up, the path it opened, the word it wrote | The view with the strongest support from within the Qurʾān — see below |
| **In the context of property** | What it gave in its lifetime | The property it left for after its death | A narrow reading; it restricts the wording |

**The third view has direct support from within the Qurʾān:**

*"It is We who bring the dead to life; and We write down **what they have sent ahead and the traces they have left**."* (Yāsīn 36/12)

In the verse *mā qaddamū* and *āthārahum* (their traces, their works) are set side by side. That is, what enters a man's reckoning is not limited to the works he did; **what goes on walking behind the work he did** is written down as well.

Another support:

*"On that day man is informed of **what he has sent ahead and what he has left behind**."* (al-Qiyāma 75/13)

This verse uses the identical expression. So the formula is the Qurʾān's own, and in both places it is in the context of the reckoning.

**The preference and its ground.** The views do not exclude one another; the expression appears to have been left deliberately wide. In my judgement the wording of the text supports the first and third views most: the reckoning looks at both **what was done** and **what was not done**, at both **the act** and **the continuation of the act**. Both fall within the scope of "what is left behind", because *taʾkhīr* is open to both "I did not do it, I put it off" and "I left it behind me". **This preference is not binding.**

### What is not said: to whom the knowledge belongs

There is one further striking side to the verse, and it is generally passed over.

In the sentence **the one who knows is the soul itself.** It does not say "it is made known to him"; it says "he knows". The subject of the verb is the person.

This is a structure the Qurʾān often uses in setting up the scene of the reckoning:

*"We have fastened every man's deed about his neck. On the Day of Rising We shall bring out for him a book he will find laid open. **Read your book! Today your own soul suffices as a reckoner against you.**"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/13-14)

In the verse the one who reads the reckoning and the one who sees it are both the person himself. The same idea was in al-Zilzāl 99/6-8 too: *"that their deeds may be shown to them"* and *"whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it."* This was treated in detail in the section on al-Zilzāl.

**The conclusion that follows is this: the reckoning is not a verdict announced from outside; it is the completion of a person's knowledge about himself.**

It is not hard to see why this is a heavy thing. There are things a man does not know about himself, and most of them are bearable precisely because they are not known. It is not possible for a man to see all the consequences of the work he did, all the ears his word reached, all the lives his decision altered. The verse recounts the opening of that view.

### What this says to the present

**The column headed "what he left behind" is wider today than it has ever been.**

What the verse calls *akhkharat* — the part of your work that goes on walking after you have stopped — was limited in traditional life. What a man left behind him were his children, the order he set up, the path he opened, the words he said and that were passed on. All of these were things that spread slowly and generally died out within a generation or two.

Today a sentence does not stay where it was written; it is copied, quoted, taken out of its context, and goes on circulating long after the one who wrote it has forgotten it. A piece of software goes on producing decisions years after its author has left the job. A structure that has been set up goes on working after the one who set it up has died.

The distinction the verse records is useful here: **what you did and the walking of what you did are two separate columns**, and the second is usually larger than the first. A person knows the first; the second he does not.

It is more correct to read this not as a threat but as a measure. When Yāsīn 36/12 says "the traces", it covers the good trace as well as the bad. The question the measure asks is this: *what am I leaving that will go on working after I have stopped?*

## 82/6 — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ مَا غَرَّكَ بِرَبِّكَ ٱلْكَرِيمِ

*Yā ayyuhā'l-insānu mā gharraka bi-Rabbika'l-Karīm*
"O man! What has deceived you about your generous Lord?"

The heart of the sūra.

### The change of address

For five verses there had been third-person narration: the sky, the stars, the seas, the graves, a soul. In the sixth verse the narration breaks off and **direct address** begins: *yā ayyuhā'l-insān.*

This is an instance of the style of **iltifāt** (a shift in the direction of address) treated in the section on al-Humaza. But its use here is harder: there a question was put and the narration was resumed; here the address opens and the speaking to the one addressed continues to the end of the sūra (verses 7-12 are all directed to him).

That the address opens with **the vocative particle** (*yā*) is also worth recording. In Arabic the vocative is for calling one who is far off; for addressing one who is near, a particle is generally not used. In the classical books of rhetoric the function of such uses is explained thus: **the heedlessness of the one addressed requires that he be called as though he were far away.** So the particle conveys not the physical distance of the one addressed but the distance of his attention.

### ٱلْإِنسَٰن — man

The dispute over the root of this word was treated in the section on al-ʿAṣr: the disagreement between *a-n-s* (familiarity, habit) and *n-s-y* (to forget).

The form chosen here is important: the verse does not say "O you who believe", it does not say "O disbelievers", it does not even say "O men" (in the plural). **"O man"** — the name of the species, singular.

This has two consequences:

**One. The one addressed is not chosen according to his identity.** The question is directed not at a group but at the quality of being human. Whoever is human is the one addressed.

**Two. It is a question no one can stay outside of.** Had it said "the disbelievers", the reader could have held himself outside. When it says "man", that door closes.

In the Qurʾān the address *al-insān* generally comes together with the noting of a fault or a weakness: *"Man is indeed in loss"* (al-ʿAṣr 103/2), *"Man was created in hardship"* (al-Balad 90/4), *"Man is most ungrateful to his Lord"* (al-ʿĀdiyāt 100/6). These were treated in those sections. The address in al-Infiṭār is on the same line — but its difference is that it is not a **statement** but a **question**.

### مَا غَرَّكَ — a question, or a rebuke?

In form the sentence is a question: *mā* (what) + verb + the pronoun of the one addressed.

But no answer to this question is expected. In Arabic questions of this kind are called *istifhām inkārī* or *istifhām tawbīkhī*: a question asked not to seek information but **to call the one addressed to give an account.**

English has an exact counterpart: "What got into you, to do such a thing?" The one who asks is not curious about the answer; the question itself is a verdict.

But there is a fine point here that classical exegesis has dwelt on: **this question is asked not because it has no answer, but because its answer cannot be given.** Every answer that could be given to "what deceived you?" amounts to a confession of the deception itself. Say "my wealth deceived me", and you have admitted that your wealth cannot protect you. Say "my youth deceived me", and you have admitted that your youth is over. The question invalidates every answer in advance.

Another subtlety: the question is framed not as **"why did you do it"** but as **"what deceived you"**. That is, the subject of the verb is not man but the thing that deceives. Man here is the **object** — the party deceived.

This determines the tone of the verse. The sentence does not accuse man directly; it **assumes that man has been taken in** and asks who took him in. This is a more effective approach than a harsh rebuke: instead of putting the person on the defensive, it gives him the chance to look at his own situation from outside.

And then in the ninth verse this softness will be removed: *kallā bal tukadhdhibūna bi'l-dīn* — "No! Rather, you deny the reckoning." So the sūra first opens the possibility of deception, then closes it. I shall take this up at verse 9.

### The root غ-ر-ر

**Root: غ-ر-ر.** The frame of the concrete sense: **the difference between the visible face of a thing and its reality.**

Derivatives:

- **غَرَّ (*gharra*)** — he deceived, he misled. He made a thing appear other than it is.
- **غُرُور (*ghurūr*)** — being deceived; and at the same time **the thing that deceives**. The word names both the act and the doer.
- **الْغَرُور (*al-gharūr*)** — the intensive form: the one who deceives greatly. In the Qurʾān it is used as a name for Satan.
- **غِرّ (*ghirr*)** — inexperienced, artless, a youth who does not know the world. One open to being deceived.
- **غَرَّة (*ghirra*)** — the moment of heedlessness, the unguarded moment. *ʿAlā'l-ghirra* — to catch someone unawares.
- **غَرَر (*gharar*)** — that whose outcome is uncertain, whose result is unknown. In jurisprudence it is a technical term.

**The idea at the centre of the word is not "to tell a lie" but "to appear wrongly".** The thing that deceives is under no necessity of lying; it is enough that it stand as it is and be misunderstood. This distinction is decisive for the verse, and it will be returned to below.

**غَرَر and jurisprudence.** The term names the uncertainty forbidden in contracts in Islamic law: not knowing the quality of the thing sold, or its quantity, or whether it can be delivered. Reports are transmitted in the sources that *bayʿ al-gharar* (a sale containing uncertainty) was forbidden; I do not give the wording of the report or its source with certainty here.

The logic of the concept throws light on the verse: *gharar* is **one party's making an agreement about something he does not know.** This is why gambling and transactions of uncertain term are treated under this heading. So the root names not only "being taken in" but **deciding on incomplete information**.

The verse's question becomes clear in this light: man has played at a game of chance. He has wagered on something he does not know. The question is what persuaded him into that wager.

### غرور in the Qurʾān

The word's uses in the Qurʾān give an orderly picture:

- *"The life of this world is nothing but the goods of delusion."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/185). *Matāʿu'l-ghurūr* — the material of being deceived.
- *"So let not the life of this world deceive you, and let not the great deceiver deceive you about God."* (Luqmān 31/33; similarly Fāṭir 35/5). Two deceivers are named in the verse: **the life of this world** and **al-gharūr** (Satan). And Satan's method of deceiving is given as *"bi'llāh"* — "by means of God".

**Luqmān 31/33 (and Fāṭir 35/5) is the closest relative of al-Infiṭār 82/6, and this link must be dwelt on.**

In both verses there is the same root, the same structure and the same particle:

| | Luqmān 31/33 | al-Infiṭār 82/6 |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | *yaghurrannakum* | *gharraka* |
| Particle + construct | **بِٱللَّهِ** (by God) | **بِرَبِّكَ** (by your Lord) |

In both verses the object of the act of deceiving is man, and the **instrument** is God. That is, not "to be deceived about God" — this too is a possible reading; but the principal sense the particle carries is **"to be deceived by means of God"**: an attribute of God is made the material of the deception.

**Over the function of the particle *bāʾ* here there is disagreement:**

| Reading | Sense |
|---|---|
| *Bāʾ* as "concerning" (of relation) | "What deceived you concerning your Lord?" — that is, how did you come to a wrong opinion about Him |
| *Bāʾ* as "against" | "What emboldened you against your Lord?" |
| *Bāʾ* as "by / by means of" | "What deceived you by using your Lord's (attribute of generosity)?" |

The third reading is the one that best matches the use of *bi'llāh* in Luqmān 31/33, and it agrees with the rest of the sūra as well. **I state this as a preference; it is not binding, and the three readings do not exclude one another.**

### Why ٱلْكَرِيم?

Now we come to the finest point of the sūra.

The verse names God with an attribute, and the attribute chosen is surprising.

In a sentence of threat the expected attributes are known: *al-Qahhār* (the overwhelming), *al-Jabbār*, *Shadīdu'l-ʿiqāb* (severe in punishment), *al-Muntaqim*. The Qurʾān uses these names and places them in contexts of threat.

The verse chooses none of them. It says **الْكَرِيم**: generous, bestowing, of worth.

**This choice puts the answer to the question inside the question.**

As was recorded in the section on al-ʿAlaq, *karīm* means generous and honourable and of worth all at once; the Qurʾān gives this attribute to provision, to speech, to the Book, to the Throne and to the messenger. At the centre of the root **giving without return** and **worth** stand together. *Karam* is to give a thing though it has not been earned; *karāma* is the worth a thing carries in itself.

Now let us read the question again: *"What has deceived you about your generous Lord?"*

The sentence implies this: **the source of the deception is the generosity of the one deceived about.**

### How generosity deceives

This is the real idea of the sūra, and it must be built up step by step.

**First step: a delay in punishment is read as permission.**

A man does something and nothing happens. Then he does it again, and again nothing happens. The third time he no longer asks the question.

This is an error of reasoning, but an entirely natural one. The mind learns events from their consequences. If the consequence does not come, the mind enters the record "this act is harmless". For the mind, a delay in punishment is the same thing as **the absence of punishment**.

**The Qurʾān describes this mechanism openly:**

*"If God were to take people to task at once for what they have earned, He would not leave a single creature on the earth. But He defers them to an appointed term."* (Fāṭir 35/45)

*"Your Lord is the forgiving, the possessor of mercy. Were He to hold them responsible for what they have earned, He would hasten the punishment for them. But there is an appointed term for them."* (al-Kahf 18/58)

Both verses say the same thing: **the delay is the consequence of an attribute.** In Fāṭir the deferral is stated directly; in al-Kahf the names *ghafūr* and *raḥma* are named as the cause of the deferral.

That is, the punishment's not coming is not from indifference or from there being no judgement; it is from **generosity**.

**Second step: the generosity itself is used as evidence.**

The one deceived is not unthinking. He performs a piece of reasoning, and his reasoning is this: *so much has been given to me, therefore there is approval; so much time has passed and nothing has happened, therefore there is no reckoning.*

The premises of this reasoning are true — it really was given, nothing really did happen — but its conclusion is false. **The thing that deceives is not lying; a true datum is being tied to a false conclusion.**

This is the exact counterpart of the distinction recorded at the head of the root: *ghurūr* is not lying but appearing wrongly. Generosity does not lie; it is merely misunderstood.

**Third step: the Qurʾān warns against this misunderstanding separately.**

*"Let not those who disbelieve suppose that Our giving them respite is good for them. We give them respite only that they may increase in sin."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/178)

The verse takes up the respite (*imlāʾ*) directly and says that it is not an approval. The same idea occurs in several other places.

**Fourth step: the sūra's own answer is in the seventh verse.**

The sixth verse does not ask its question and leave it. The seventh verse fills in the content of the attribute *al-Karīm*: *"He who created you, proportioned you, balanced you."*

That is, what the verse does is this: immediately after showing generosity as the cause of the deception, it enumerates what that generosity is. And the things it enumerates are not wealth, position, health or length of life; they are **the body of the one addressed**.

This is an extremely effective move. A person may or may not look around him to think about the things given to him; but he cannot escape his own existence. The verse shows the proof of generosity not outside man but **in man himself**.

### An interpretation in classical exegesis

There is an interpretation widely circulated in the literature of exegesis about this verse, and it is worth recording:

The naming of the attribute *al-Karīm* here is the **prompting** of the answer to be given by the one addressed. That is, the one who asks the question is also whispering the answer: "Your generosity deceived me." When a person gives this answer he has in fact confessed a fault but has at the same time taken refuge in mercy.

It is stated that this interpretation is ascribed to some early scholars. **Because I cannot give the soundness of the ascription with certainty, I give no name**; the interpretation itself is widespread in the tradition of exegesis and agrees with the language of the text.

There is a reason to look at this interpretation with caution: in the ninth verse the sūra will say *kallā* and cut off the excuse. That is, the text does not say that this answer is an acceptable excuse. But it is also true that the question is asked **in a merciful tone**; the two observations do not conflict.

### What this says to the present

The mechanism this verse describes has no need of a religious context; it works everywhere.

**One. Our intuition does not work against anything whose harm is delayed.**

The human mind sets up the link between act and consequence over short intervals. As the interval lengthens the link weakens; past a certain point it breaks entirely. Smoking, debt, neglected maintenance, a relationship not kept up, a check-up put off — all have the same structure. The harm is there, but today it is invisible; and every day it stays invisible refreshes the record "no harm".

The only defence developed against this is not **seeing** the consequence but **knowing** it. And this is what the sūra does: for nineteen verses it describes a consequence not yet visible.

**Two. Generosity produces in the person treated generously a sense of entitlement.**

This is an observable piece of human behaviour and can be stated without any moral charge: when a thing is given regularly, after a while the one who receives it begins to perceive it as a **right**. Its continuing to be given becomes normal, and its not being given comes to look like an injustice.

The same mechanism works in a person treated with indulgence. Being forgiven is read as a guarantee that one will go on being forgiven. Where no boundary is set, it is assumed there is no boundary.

The verse describes this as a moral fault, but it delivers the diagnosis not as a declaration of fault but as a **question**. And the answer to the question is left to the person.

**Three. Against deception the sūra sets not a piece of information but a way of looking.**

The seventh verse does not say "consider this proof"; it says "look at yourself". The answer is shown not outside but in the structure of the one addressed. This is the function of the two verses that follow.

## 82/7 — ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَكَ فَسَوَّىٰكَ فَعَدَلَكَ

*Alladhī khalaqaka fa-sawwāka fa-ʿadalak*
"He who created you, then set you in proportion, then balanced you."

### Three verbs, two فَ

The sentence is a qualifier of *Rabbika'l-Karīm* in the preceding verse: "that generous Lord of yours, who…"

Three verbs, joined by two **فَ**. In Arabic the letter *fāʾ* conveys **succession and joining without an interval** (treated in the section on al-Māʿūn). So there is no gap between the three verbs: when creating ends, proportioning begins; when proportioning ends, balancing.

This is the repetition of a pattern recorded in the section on al-Shams: in recounting the creation of man the Qurʾān follows the order **first creation, then proportioning, then the giving of a faculty** (al-Sajda 32/9, al-Ḥijr 15/29, Ṣād 38/72, al-Aʿlā 87/2). That is, *taswiya* is the stage at which the raw material is brought into working condition.

Here the third step of the series is different: instead of the breathing of the spirit or the giving of hearing and sight, **عَدَلَ** comes.

### خَلَقَ — He created

**Root: خ-ل-ق.** The concrete sense of the word is worth dwelling on: *khalaqa* in Arabic was used for **measuring and cutting a thing to shape**, especially for cutting and shaping leather. *Khulq* — character, the nature that comes with creation. *Khilqa* — build, constitution.

So the idea beneath the root is not "to bring into being from nothing" but **"to shape according to a measure"**. The idea of bringing into being from nothing is more marked in the root of the sūra's first verse (ف-ط-ر). The difference between the two is recorded in the classical sources:

| Root | Its emphasis |
|---|---|
| **ف-ط-ر** | For the first time, on no model, to begin out of nothing |
| **خ-ل-ق** | To measure and cut, to make by determining |
| **ب-ر-أ** | To separate, to bring a thing cleanly out of another |

The three come side by side in al-Ḥashr 59/24: *"He is God, the creator (al-Khāliq), the maker out of nothing (al-Bāriʾ), the fashioner (al-Muṣawwir)."*

**This verse is directly meaningful for al-Infiṭār 82/7-8**, because al-Infiṭār sets up the same order: *khalaqa* → … → *fī ayyi ṣūratin* (concerning form). The three names in al-Ḥashr and the series of verbs in al-Infiṭār recount the same process in different words.

### سَوَّىٰ — He set in proportion

This verb was treated in detail in the section on al-Shams. In brief: **the root س-و-ي, the idea beneath it equality and equivalence**; in Form II (*sawwā*) the verb means "to bring the parts of a thing into correspondence with one another, to get its proportions right, to bring it to a complete and faultless state". The Turkish borrowing *tesviye etmek*, "to level, to grade", comes from this root.

As was stated there, al-Shams 91/7 and al-Infiṭār 82/7 use the same root; in al-Shams what is proportioned is the **soul**, here it is the **body**.

One addition must be made. In al-Shams *sawwāhā* had come absolutely: what was brought into correspondence with what was not said. Here the verb is **attached to the one addressed**: *sawwā-ka* — "you". So what is meant is not an abstract ordering but the structure of the very person standing before it.

### عَدَلَ — He balanced

**Root: ع-د-ل.** The concrete sense of this root is decisive for understanding the verse.

*ʿAdl* in Arabic is first a word of the **scales**. **عِدْل (*ʿidl*)** means the counterweight loaded on one side of a camel; two *ʿidl*s balance the animal's two flanks. So at the centre of the word stands **the two sides being equal to one another**.

From this three senses issue, and all three are used in the Qurʾān:

1. **Equivalence, balance** — the equalising of two sides.
2. **Justice** — the equal distribution of what is due. The name of justice is "equivalence".
3. **Ransom, substitute** — the equivalent thing given in exchange for something.

The third sense occurred in the section on al-Baqara: al-Baqara 2/48 says *"no ransom (ʿadl) shall be taken from it"*, and as was recorded there **the word means not "justice" but "that which is equivalent".**

This is the first link of a connection that will be made towards the end of the sūra; it will be returned to at verse 19.

**Over the sense of the verb in the verse there are two views, and they depend on a difference of variant reading:**

| Reading | Sense | Its ground |
|---|---|---|
| **فَعَدَلَكَ** (undoubled) — "He made you equal" | He made your limbs equal to one another, symmetrical, proportioned | The basic sense of the root; it continues on the same line as *sawwā* |
| **فَعَدَلَكَ** (undoubled) — "He turned you" | *ʿAdala ʿan* — to turn from something; "He turned you into whatever form He willed" | It joins directly to the next verse: *fī ayyi ṣūratin mā shāʾa rakkabak* |
| **فَعَدَّلَكَ** (doubled) — "He balanced you thoroughly" | The *tafʿīl* form, intensification: He set the balance up fully and densely | The pattern conveys that the work was done with care and repeatedly |

Both readings are transmitted among the imams of the variant readings. **Because I cannot give with certainty which reading belongs to which imam, I give no name.**

Both senses work in the text and do not exclude one another: He made you balanced, and He directed you into whatever form He willed. The advantage of the second reading is that the link it makes with verse 8 is tighter.

### What does "being made equal" mean?

The first reading is worth dwelling on, because it rests on a concrete observation.

The human body is **doubly axed**: two eyes, two ears, two hands, two feet, two lungs, two kidneys. And these pairs are symmetrical — each is the measure of the other's mirror. This symmetry is not merely a matter of appearance; it is the condition of standing, of walking, of seeing and of hearing. The position of the two eyes gives the perception of depth, the position of the two ears the direction of a sound.

*ʿAdala* names exactly this structure: **one side's being equal to the other.**

And this sets up a contrast with the first four verses of the sūra. There the undoing of balances was recounted: the arrangement of the stars is scattered, the dyke of the seas is broken. Here the setting up of a balance is recounted. **The same sūra shows first a balance undone and then a balance built** — and the second is the body of the one addressed.

In the section on al-Balad it was recorded that when the Qurʾān enumerates what has been given to man it mentions the paired organs separately: *"Have We not given him two eyes, a tongue and two lips?"* (al-Balad 90/8-9). There the meaning of the count's being made in the form "two, one, two" was dwelt on. Al-Infiṭār 82/7 compresses the same fact into a single verb: *ʿadala.*

### What this says to the present

The proof the verse gives is the body of the one addressed; and this proof has one property: **it is invisible.**

A man does not notice his own structure, because he has never lost it. Balance is not a datum until it is lost. Illness is needed for health to be noticed, dizziness for balance, a fracture for the hand.

The link with the question of the sixth verse is here: deception develops most of all against **what is continuous**. When a thing is given without interruption, its being given is forgotten and it is perceived as a ground. And a ground produces no gratitude.

The two favours named in the hadith transmitted in the section on al-Takāthur — health and free time — are exactly of this kind: favours that cannot be counted, cannot be stored, and are not noticed as they are being used up.

The seventh verse of the sūra brings to the front what is most used and least looked at.

## 82/8 — فِىٓ أَىِّ صُورَةٍ مَّا شَآءَ رَكَّبَكَ

*Fī ayyi ṣūratin mā shāʾa rakkabak*
"In whatever form He willed, so He assembled you."

### Word order — the fronting of the phrase

The ordinary order of the sentence would have been *rakkabaka fī ayyi ṣūratin mā shāʾa*. The verse pulls the phrase *fī ayyi ṣūratin* **in front of** the verb.

In Arabic an element pulled forward from its normal place gains **emphasis and restriction**. So the centre of gravity of the sentence is not "He assembled" but **"in whatever form"**.

What this means is that the verse emphasises not the act of assembling but **the form's having been chosen**. Not that you were made, but **that you were made like this.**

### صُورَة — form

**Root: ص-و-ر.** Shape, figure, appearance. *Taṣwīr* — to give shape. *Muṣawwir* — the one who gives shape; it is among the names of God (al-Ḥashr 59/24).

Its uses in the Qurʾān:

- *"He fashioned you and made your forms good."* (al-Taghābun 64/3; similarly al-Muʾmin 40/64).
- *"He assembled you in whatever form He willed."* — this verse.

**One further sense of the word is recorded:** *ṣūra* — a horn, a trumpet blown into. *Ṣūr* (that which will be blown at the end) is a relative of this word. But I do not set this kinship up as a link of meaning; I content myself with recording it.

### مَّا شَآءَ — "whatever He willed"

*Mā* here may be redundant (*zāʾid*) or a relative pronoun (*mawṣūla*). There are two readings:

- **Redundant:** "in whatever form He willed" — *mā* merely increases the indefiniteness.
- **Relative:** "whatever form He wills is the form" — *mā shāʾa* becomes the explanation of *ayyi*.

The difference in sense between the two readings is small. The common outcome is this: **the form has been chosen, and the one who chose is God.**

Here we must stop and close off a misunderstanding. The verse does not present the bodily differences of people as a measure of superiority or of inferiority; it does exactly the opposite. That the form has been chosen means that no one has chosen his own form. Height, face, colour, build — none of them is the person's achievement or his fault.

This is a line the Qurʾān sets up openly in other places: *"We have made you peoples and tribes so that you might come to know one another. The most honoured of you in God's sight is the one most mindful of Him."* (al-Ḥujurāt 49/13 — this verse was cited as a measure in the section on al-Baqara.)

### رَكَّبَ — He assembled

One of the words that carries the structure of the sūra.

**Root: ر-ك-ب.** Its concrete sense: **to climb onto a thing, to mount.** *Rākib* — a rider. *Rikāb* — riding beasts. *Markab* — that which is ridden.

From this a second sense is born: **to set one thing upon another, to build a whole by placing the parts one upon the other.**

- **تَرْكِيب (*tarkīb*)** — assembling, bringing parts together. In Arabic grammar the construction of a sentence is *tarkīb*; in chemistry a compound is *murakkab*.
- **مُرَكَّب (*murakkab*)** — compound, made up of more than one part. Its opposite is *basīṭ* — simple, of one piece.

**The pattern: تفعيل (*tafʿīl*).** The verb is not *rakiba* but *rakkaba*. As was treated in the sections on al-Humaza and al-Shams, this form conveys both transitivity and **intensification**: the multiplicity of the parts and the repetition of the operation.

So *rakkabaka* is not "He made you" but **"He built you part by part, setting one upon another"**.

### The roof of the sūra: scattering and assembling

Now the structure noted at the end of verse 4 is completed.

| | **Verses 1-4** | **Verses 7-8** |
|---|---|---|
| Number of verbs | Four | Four |
| The verbs | *infaṭarat, intatharat, fujjirat, buʿthirat* | *khalaqa, sawwā, ʿadala, rakkaba* |
| Direction | **Dissolution** | **Construction** |
| Object | Sky, stars, seas, graves | **You** (the suffix *-ka* in all four) |
| Doer | Not named | *Alladhī* — your Lord |
| Pattern | *Muṭāwaʿa* and passive | Active, transitive to the one addressed |

**The opposition is not only in the four verbs; it is in the words themselves.**

*Intithār* (scattering) and *tarkīb* (assembling) are exact opposites in Arabic: one disperses what is together, the other brings together what is dispersed. In the same way *infiṭār* (splitting) and *taswiya* (bringing the parts into correspondence) work in opposite directions.

So the sūra shows two directions of the same operation one after the other: **the universe is coming apart, you have been put together.** And the doer of both is the same.

**The conclusion that follows from this observation is the following, and I write it as my own reading:** the sūra does not set up the scene of the end as a scene of terror. It sets it up in order to compare it with the structure of man himself. The argument works like this:

> The one who can scatter things that stand together is the one who gathers together what is scattered. It is He who gathered you together. Then it is possible for Him to gather you together again.

This is the abbreviated form of an argument the Qurʾān frequently uses for **the possibility of the resurrection**, and it is stated openly in other places: *"As He began the first creation, so He will repeat it."* (al-Anbiyāʾ 21/104 in sense; also al-Rūm 30/27, Yāsīn 36/79).

And one more layer: in the fourth verse the **graves** were overturned. The grave is where the man whose assembly has been undone lies. The eighth verse says who set that assembly up.

### The ordering of the verbs

The order of the four verbs is meaningful too, and resembles a process of manufacture:

1. **خلق** — to measure and cut. The determining of the raw material.
2. **سوّى** — to bring the parts into correspondence. The getting right of the proportions.
3. **عدل** — to balance. The two sides being equal to one another.
4. **ركّب** — to assemble. The bringing together of the parts.

But there is a question of order here: logically, assembling (*tarkīb*) is expected not at the end but before the balancing — first the parts join, then the balance is set.

The explanations given for this in the classical sources are these: (a) the letter *fāʾ* does not always convey temporal order, sometimes it conveys *tartīb dhikrī* (order of mention); (b) the eighth verse is not joined by a *fāʾ* — it has no connective — and therefore is not the continuation of the preceding three but their explanation; (c) *rakkaba* here means not "He built" but "He placed into a form".

The second explanation appears best suited to the wording: the eighth verse comes without a connective and is a sentence that gathers up the preceding series of verbs. **This is a preference; it is not binding.**

### The sound turning back to the one addressed

Something striking happens here in the sound structure of the sūra.

Verses 1-5 ended on the sound **-rat**. Verse 6 ended with **-īm** (*al-Karīm*). Verses 7 and 8 end with these sounds: **فَعَدَلَكَ** and **رَكَّبَكَ** — both with **-ka**.

So for two verses the rhyme of the sūra becomes **"you"**. And those two verses are precisely the two that recount the making of the one addressed.

Moreover, the same sound occurs twice inside verse 6 as well: *gharra-**ka** bi-Rabbi-**ka*** and in what follows *khalaqa-**ka**, sawwā-**ka**, ʿadala-**ka**, rakkaba-**ka**.* In a stretch of three verses the pronoun of the one addressed is repeated six times, and in two of them it holds the end of the verse.

**I record this not as a claim of a miracle but as an agreement of form with content:** that two verses whose subject is "you" should have "you" for their sound as well is a consistency often seen in the short Meccan sūras. An observation of the same kind was made in the section on al-Humaza.

And in the ninth verse the sound will change again — because the one addressed will change too: from singular to plural.

## 82/9 — كَلَّا بَلْ تُكَذِّبُونَ بِٱلدِّينِ

*Kallā bal tukadhdhibūna bi'l-dīn*
"No! Rather, you deny the reckoning."

### The turning point of the sūra

The sixth verse had asked a question: *what deceived you?* The seventh and eighth verses had opened out the attribute inside that question — *al-Karīm*.

Now the ninth verse removes the frame of the question.

*Kallā* — no. And after it *bal* — rather, in truth. Two particles come one upon the other, and the function of each is different.

### كَلَّا — what does it cut off?

The function of the particle *kallā* was treated in the section on al-Takāthur: it conveys both **rejection** (*radd*, the erasing of a judgement) and **restraint** (*zajr*).

What is cut off here? There are two possibilities, and both are mentioned in the classical sources:

| View | What *kallā* cuts off |
|---|---|
| **The excuse** | The expected answer to the question of the sixth verse. Every sentence that would begin "such-and-such deceived me" is invalidated before it is even framed |
| **The deception itself** | The person supposes he has been deceived; but he is not deceived — he knows and refuses. *Kallā* means "the matter is not deception" |

**The second reading matches the rest of the sūra better**, because the *bal* that comes immediately after brings a correction: the matter is not that, but **this**. That is, the diagnosis is being changed.

There is a third view as well: that *kallā* here is a particle of confirmation in the sense of "truly, indeed" (*ḥaqqan*). This view is defended by some of the lexicographers, but the presence of *bal* here weakens it: since *bal* already conveys *iḍrāb* (a change of direction), it is more consistent for a rejection to stand before it.

### بَلْ — *iḍrāb*

**بل** is the particle of *iḍrāb* in Arabic: leaving what has been said and moving on to something else. It has two kinds — that which entirely annuls what came before (*iḍrāb ibṭālī*) and that which leaves what came before and moves on to something more important (*iḍrāb intiqālī*).

Here the second appears to hold: the matter of the deception is not annulled, **the real cause behind it** is brought forward.

So the logic of the verses is this:

> The question: What deceived you? → While an answer is awaited: No, this is not deception. → The real matter: You deny the reckoning.

**This is the most important move of the sūra, and it deepens the diagnosis by one layer.**

To be deceived is a passive state: the person has been taken in, the responsibility is partly outside him. To deny is an active stance: the person has passed a judgement and is defending that judgement.

The sūra first shows man the merciful door (you may have been deceived), then says that door is not the real situation. **The deception is the covering of the denial.** The person says to himself "I was not aware"; the verse says "you were aware, you did not accept."

### The passage from singular to plural

The second striking side of the verse is the pronoun.

Verses 6-8 had used the **singular** address: *gharra**ka**, Rabbi**ka**, khalaqa**ka**, sawwā**ka**, ʿadala**ka**, rakkaba**ka**.*

Verse 9 goes over to the **plural**: *tukadhdhibū**na*** — "you deny". And this plurality will continue to the end of the sūra: *ʿalaykum* (10), *tafʿalūn* (12).

The explanations of this shift:

**One — the grammatical explanation.** *Al-insān* in the sixth verse is a generic noun; singular in wording, plural in sense. In Arabic such words may be referred back to with either a singular or a plural pronoun. The same case was recorded for the word *kull* in the section on al-Humaza.

**Two — the explanation from sense.** Creation happens one by one; denial happens collectively. A man's creation belongs to himself — no one has been created in his place. But denial is a stance learned from the surroundings and kept up with them.

**I present this second explanation as my own reading**, but the structure of the sūra supports it: in the nineteenth verse it will return to the singular and say precisely this: *"On that day no soul has power over anything for another soul."* That is, those who deny collectively will be called to account one by one.

This is why the sūra changes the pronoun twice: **you were created one by one → you deny collectively → you will give account one by one.**

### تُكَذِّبُونَ — you deny

The analysis of this verb was carried out in the section on al-Māʿūn. In brief: the root is **ك-ذ-ب**; in the *tafʿīl* form, *kadhdhaba* is "to call another's true word a lie" — it differs from *kadhaba* (to tell a lie). And the imperfect tense conveys continuity: not a stance taken once, but a position kept up.

What must further be recorded here is that the verb comes in the **plural and the imperfect**: the denial is described as both widespread and ongoing.

### ٱلدِّين — the reckoning

The root analysis of the word was carried out in the section on al-Māʿūn, and the reading "the day of reckoning" was preferred there. A few parallel verses were shown as its ground — and this verse was one of those grounds.

Now the ground can be seen in its own place: **immediately after "you deny the dīn" is said in verse 9, the recorders who write down deeds are named in verses 10-12; and in verse 15 *yawma'l-dīn* — "the day of the dīn" — is said openly.** So within the sūra itself the word is used in the sense of "reckoning".

This is a datum that confirms from the text the preference made in the section on al-Māʿūn; I do not repeat the analysis given there.

### How the sentence works

What the verse does must be gathered up once more, because the whole second half of the sūra is built on this sentence.

The sūra has gone forward like this:
1. The universe will come apart (1-4).
2. Everyone will know what he did (5).
3. What deceived you? (6)
4. He is the one who made you (7-8).
5. **In truth the matter is not deception: you are refusing the reckoning.** (9)

The fifth step binds the first four to a single point. The coming apart of the universe is the stage on which the reckoning will be set up; every soul's knowing is the working of the reckoning; creation is the proof that makes the reckoning possible. What is refused is all of these.

And the three verses that come after this verse will recount **how, technically, the thing refused works**: a record is being kept.

## 82/10-12 — وَإِنَّ عَلَيْكُمْ لَحَٰفِظِينَ • كِرَامًا كَٰتِبِينَ • يَعْلَمُونَ مَا تَفْعَلُونَ

*Wa inna ʿalaykum la-ḥāfiẓīn • kirāman kātibīn • yaʿlamūna mā tafʿalūn*
"Over you there are keepers-and-recorders; honoured writers; they know what you do."

The three verses are a single sentence and recount a single matter; I take them together.

### The layers of emphasis in the sentence

The sentence carries two emphases:

- **إِنَّ** — the strongest particle of emphasis in Arabic.
- **لَ** (*lām al-ibtidāʾ*) — the emphatic *lām* that comes at the head of the predicate.

And the sentence is a **nominal sentence**; there is no verb. In Arabic a nominal sentence conveys fixity and continuity. So not "recorders will be appointed" but **"there are recorders"** — as an uninterrupted state.

The use of three means of emphasis together shows that the sentence is an answer to **a claim being contested**. In Arabic emphasis is calibrated to the position of the one addressed: to one who accepts, without emphasis; to one who hesitates, with a single emphasis; to one who denies, with many. The preceding verse had already spoken of denial; the density of emphasis here is its counterpart.

### عَلَيْكُمْ — "over you" or "against you"?

The choice of particle is meaningful, and it carries both senses at once.

**عَلَى** in Arabic conveys basically **being above**. From this two secondary senses are born:
- **Oversight:** what is above sees what is below.
- **Being against:** a thing's being "upon" someone is its being to his detriment. In Arabic the distinction *lahu / ʿalayhi* (in his favour / against him) is set up with this particle.

The Qurʾān uses this distinction openly: *"What it has earned is in its favour, what it has committed is against it."* (al-Baqara 2/286).

The verse's saying *ʿalaykum* calls up both at once: the recorders are both **over you** (above, in the position of overseer) and, in respect of what they write, in a position to be evidence **against you**.

Moreover *ʿalaykum* has been brought before the predicate: the order of the sentence is *inna ʿalaykum la-ḥāfiẓīn*, not *inna ḥāfiẓīna ʿalaykum*. The element brought forward gains emphasis (the same structure was treated in the section on al-Humaza). So the emphasis is not on "there are recorders" but on **"over you"**.

### حَٰفِظِين — keepers, or recorders?

**Root: ح-ف-ظ.** And this root has two branches of meaning; Turkish has borrowed words from both:

1. **To guard, to preserve.** *Ḥifẓ* — guarding. The line of the borrowed Turkish words *muhafaza* (preservation), *muhafız* (guard), *himâye* (protection).
2. **To memorise, to keep in mind.** *Ḥāfiẓ* — one who has memorised the Qurʾān. The borrowed Turkish verb *hıfzetmek* — to take into memory.

The two join in a single idea: **not to let a thing be lost.** To guard property and to memorise a word are both the prevention of loss.

The Qurʾān uses both branches:

- *"It is We who sent down the Reminder, and it is We who are its keeper."* (al-Ḥijr 15/9). The sense of guarding.
- *"He is the absolute master over His servants, and He sends keepers over you."* (al-Anʿām 6/61). *Ḥafaẓa* — the same root, the same context.
- *"He has attendants before him and behind him; they guard him by God's command."* (al-Raʿd 13/11). *Yaḥfaẓūnahu* — here plainly in the sense of guarding.

**So which is it in al-Infiṭār 82/10?** The classical exegetes differ:

| View | Ground |
|---|---|
| **Recorders** | The next verse says *kātibīn* (writers); the qualifier explains what it qualifies |
| **Keepers** | The basic sense of the root is guarding; al-Raʿd 13/11 and al-Anʿām 6/61 are on this line |
| **Both together** | The same angels both guard and write; the word carries both senses |

The third view is often defended in the sources and is linguistically sound: the word bears both senses, and the following verse opens out only one of them — but its opening one does not annul the other.

**And this doubleness agrees surprisingly well with the sixth verse of the sūra.** The sixth verse had shown *karam* as the cause of the deception. The tenth verse says that those over us both **guard** and **record**. The same beings do two works: they protect you and they write you down. The same Lord both gives and keeps the account.

**I present this link as my own reading.** That the word has two senses is a datum of the language; the link I draw from it is an interpretation.

### كِرَامًا — why this attribute?

The angels are described as *kirām*: the plural of **الْكَرِيم**.

**This is one of the tightest internal links of the sūra.** In the sixth verse the Lord was named *al-Karīm*; in the eleventh verse the scribes are named *kirām*. The same root, six verses apart, to two different subjects.

What can this mean? The explanations given for the attribute in the classical sources:

**One — because they are honoured and of worth.** The sense of *karam* as "worth". The standing of the angels in God's sight is being conveyed.

**Two — because what they write can be trusted.** One who is honourable does not write falsely, does not write incompletely, bears no grudge. So the attribute conveys **the trustworthiness of the record**. The value of a document depends on the honesty of the one who keeps it.

**Three — because they behave generously.** An interpretation frequently repeated in the literature of exegesis says that the angels write down the good at once and hold back before writing the bad. **Because I cannot give with certainty the soundness of the reports this interpretation rests on, I make no ascription to a source**; the interpretation itself is present in the sources.

**Four — and this is my own reading:** the attribute lays down a judgement about **by whom the record is kept**. The recorders are *karīm* because they are the ones appointed by *al-Karīm*. The attribute flows from above downwards.

This reading has a consequence, and it will be taken up in the section on what this says to the present: **recording another's fault is not a work of worth in itself.** What makes the record worth something is the quality of the one who records.

### كَٰتِبِين — the writers

**Root: ك-ت-ب.** The analysis of this root was carried out at al-Baqara 2/2, and what was recorded there is decisive here: **the basic sense of the root is not "to write" but "to bring together, to join, to sew".** The Arabs used this verb for sewing pieces of leather to one another; and writing too is the sewing of letters side by side. *Katība* — a military unit not scattered but gathered.

This piece of information is useful in the verse. **To write is to bring together what is scattered.** A man's life consists of moments disconnected from one another: a word here, an act there, a decision years later. Writing gathers them into one place.

And it connects with the roof of the sūra: in the first four everything was coming apart, in the second four man was being assembled (*rakkaba*). In the eleventh verse **the record assembles**. The scribe is the one who gathers what is scattered.

**Why writing, why not memory?**

The question must be asked, because the verse could have said "they know" — and indeed it says so in the next verse. But first it puts the verb **to write**.

The difference between writing and memory is that writing makes what it carries **independent of its carrier**. What is remembered changes along with the mind of the one who remembers: it is forgotten, softened, reconstructed. What is written is fixed and **can be produced afterwards**.

The Qurʾān works this difference out openly in another place: the longest verse, al-Baqara 2/282, commands that a debt be written down. This detail was recorded in the section on al-ʿAlaq — the mention of the pen in the first texts of revelation and the devoting of the longest verse to the writing down of a debt were set side by side.

The function of writing is the same in both: **a record not left to the remembering of either party.**

And from here a link is made with the ninth verse of the sūra: what the one who denies the reckoning relies on is the assumption that the reckoning is **without documents**. The eleventh verse removes that assumption.

### يَعْلَمُونَ مَا تَفْعَلُونَ — "they know what you do"

The third verse completes the first two. And it carries two points.

**One. "They know" — they do not merely write.**

The verse does not say "they write what they write". A writer can write down something he does not understand; a scribe can be a mechanical functionary. The verse closes off this possibility: **the one who writes knows what he writes.**

The consequence is this: the record is not like an audio recording but a record of **understanding**. It holds not the outward appearance but what the thing was.

**Two. تَفْعَلُونَ — "what you do", not "your deeds".**

The verse says *tafʿalūn* (what you do) rather than *taʿmalūn* (what you perform as deeds). The distinction the lexicographers record between these two roots is this:

| | **عمل** | **فعل** |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Narrow | Wide |
| Its emphasis | Work done with intent and over time | Doing of every kind; intent not required |
| Usage | Generally of man | Used of the inanimate and of animals too |

**I transmit this distinction as the lexicographers record it**; the Qurʾān's usage does not confirm it sharply everywhere, and the two words can also stand in each other's place.

But if the distinction holds, the verse's choice is meaningful: the record has to do not with "your deeds" but with **"what you do"**. That is, behaviour that was not intended, not thought important, not reflected upon falls within the scope as well.

This is on the same line as the measure of al-Zilzāl 99/7-8: *"Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it; whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it."* There the smallness of the measure is emphasised, here the width of the scope.

### The Qurʾān's interpretation by the Qurʾān

The closest relatives of these three verses:

**Qāf 50/17-18:** *"The two receivers receive, seated on the right and on the left. He utters no word but there is a watcher by him, ready."*

The two verses together complete al-Infiṭār's description: position (right and left), act (receiving), and scope (speech). The words *raqīb* (watcher) and *ʿatīd* (standing ready) correspond to al-Infiṭār's *ḥāfiẓīn*.

**Al-Isrāʾ 17/13-14:** *"We have fastened every man's deed about his neck. On the Day of Rising We shall bring out for him a book he will find laid open. Read your book! Today your own soul suffices as a reckoner against you."*

This verse binds al-Infiṭār 82/5 to 82/10-12: a record is being kept (10-12) and that record is read by the person himself (5).

**ʿAbasa 80/13-16:** *"In honoured pages; exalted, purified; by the hands of envoys honoured and good."*

The last expression: **كِرَامٍ بَرَرَة** — *kirāmin barara.*

**This verse is especially striking for al-Infiṭār**, because it carries two separate words of the sūra together:
- **كرام** — *kirāman* in al-Infiṭār 82/11.
- **بررة** — the same root (ب-ر-ر) as *abrār* in al-Infiṭār 82/13.

So ʿAbasa joins in a single construct two words that occur separately in verses 11 and 13 of al-Infiṭār. I record this as an observation; I make no claim that there is a deliberate reference between the two sūras.

### The angelic record as a belief, and its function

The belief these verses describe is a concrete aspect of one of the six articles of faith in Islam (faith in the angels), and it has a practical function.

**The function is this: the removal of the situation in which behaviour is not seen.**

This was the matter treated in the section on al-Māʿūn: what a man really believes comes to light at the moment when what he does has no worldly return. The example of the orphan was chosen because there was no one behind the orphan to call for an account.

Al-Infiṭār 82/10-12 comes at the same matter from the other end: **there is no situation in which there is no one to call for an account.** There is no such thing as unseen behaviour.

The consequence of this for morals is not the production of a fear but a demand for **consistency**. To be the same person alone and in a crowd. The Qurʾān names this elsewhere as a concept: *"Whoever fears the Most Merciful though he does not see Him, and comes with a heart turned to God."* (Qāf 50/33).

### What this says to the present

**The record is the thing most familiar to the man of today and the thing he feels least.**

The situation these verses describe — a life in which everything you do is written down — was, in the period of its revelation, a piece of knowledge requiring imagination. Today it requires no imagination: the phone's location history, the call log, the message archive, the record of purchases, the camera footage. Modern man knows by experience what a recorded life means.

But for exactly that reason we know one more thing: **knowing that one is recorded does not by itself correct behaviour.** Man gets used to the record. He learns to behave ordinarily under a camera, to be himself in a conversation that is being recorded. Awareness, once it becomes continuous, ceases to be felt.

This brings back the link the verse makes with the sixth: **continuity is the most fertile ground for deception.** What is uninterrupted becomes invisible.

The only thing the verse sets against this is the quality of the record: the recorders are *kirām*. That is, the record kept is not an instrument of surveillance; it is **a document kept by a scribe who is of worth.** The difference lies in the purpose for which the record is kept.

And this contains a measure for people who keep records. Recording another's mistake — taking a note, saving a screenshot, storing things up for later use — is extremely easy today. The verse gives those who do this work an attribute: *kirām*. What makes a record legitimate is not its being true; it is **what the one who records is keeping it for.** The person described by Sūrat al-Humaza had come to be what he was because he collected other people's faults.

## 82/13-14 — إِنَّ ٱلْأَبْرَارَ لَفِى نَعِيمٍ • وَإِنَّ ٱلْفُجَّارَ لَفِى جَحِيمٍ

*Inna'l-abrāra la-fī naʿīm • wa inna'l-fujjāra la-fī jaḥīm*
"The good are in bliss; those who break their bounds are in the blazing fire."

### The word-for-word symmetry of the two verses

The two sentences are word for word in the same mould:

| | Verse 13 | Verse 14 |
|---|---|---|
| Emphasis | إِنَّ | وَإِنَّ |
| Subject | ٱلْأَبْرَارَ | ٱلْفُجَّارَ |
| Emphatic *lām* + particle | لَفِى | لَفِى |
| Place | نَعِيمٍ | جَحِيمٍ |

Of four elements two are the same and two are opposite. And the two opposed words are on the same pattern as well: *naʿīm* and *jaḥīm* — the **فَعِيل** pattern, the same sound, the same number of syllables.

**This complete symmetry is the reverse of the structure treated in Sūrat al-Qāriʿa.** There, in the recounting of two groups, the symmetry was deliberately broken (al-Qāriʿa 101/6-9); here it is not broken at all. The difference is meaningful: in al-Qāriʿa there was a balance — something measured — and the outcome of the measuring was recounted in different forms. Here it is not a measure but **two attributes** that are compared, and the attributes are exact opposites of one another.

### ٱلْأَبْرَار — the *abrār*

**Root: ب-ر-ر.** This root was treated at al-Baqara 2/44, and what was recorded there is the key here: **the basic sense of the root is breadth.** From the same root *barr* — the dry land, that is, the wide expanse over against the sea. *Birr* too is this breadth: goodness, in the logic of the word itself, is **the opposite of narrowness**.

*Abrār* is the plural of **بَرّ** or **بَارّ**: "one who is good, who has made goodness his habit."

The conclusion that follows from the concrete sense of the word is this: **the *abrār* are "the wide ones".** Goodness is named here not as a list of behaviours but as a **width of space**. As was recorded in the section on al-Baqara: miserliness narrows, goodness widens.

The root's other usages show this breadth:

- **بِرّ الوالدين** — goodness to one's parents. It is usually rendered "kindness to parents" in English; what the word carries is the widening of the space given to them.
- **حَجّ مَبْرُور** — an accepted, a "good" pilgrimage.
- **الْبَرّ** — one of the names of God (al-Ṭūr 52/28: *"Truly He is the doer of good, the compassionate."*).

And in one place the Qurʾān defines *birr* openly; this definition was given in the section on al-Baqara (al-Baqara 2/177): not to turn your face east or west, but faith + giving of property though you love it + prayer + almsgiving + keeping your word + patience in hardship.

**So the *abrār*, by the Qurʾān's own definition, are those whose belief has turned into behaviour.** This is directly connected with the ninth verse of the sūra: there, those who deny the reckoning were named; here, their opposite, those who reflect the reckoning in their behaviour.

The word generally occurs in this context in the Qurʾān: *"The good drink from a cup mixed with camphor."* (al-Insān 76/5), *"Truly the book of the good is in ʿIlliyyūn."* (al-Muṭaffifīn 83/18). In the next sūra the same word will occur twice at an interval of four verses.

### ٱلْفُجَّار — the *fujjār*

**Root: ف-ج-ر.** It was treated in the section on al-Shams: a *fājir* is the one who splits the covering over him and comes out, who **pierces** the bound that holds him; *fujūr* is not a lack but an **overflow**.

*Fujjār* is the plural of **فَاجِر**.

**And here the tightest word-link of the sūra is completed:** in the third verse the seas are *fujjirat* — split open, their dykes broken. In the fourteenth verse men are *fujjār* — those who break their dykes.

The same root, in the same sūra, one for the universe and one for man.

What this link says is the following, and I write it as my own reading: **the sūra names what will happen at the end with the same word as what happens in this world.** The sea's breaking its dyke is a catastrophe; man's breaking his own dyke is a choice. And the two are named with the same verb.

One more layer: on that day the seas will overflow, because the order that holds them will be removed. But man overflows **today**, and moreover while the order that holds him is still standing.

### Two groups — is there no third?

The verses divide people in two: *abrār* and *fujjār*. They leave no category in between.

This is a structure the Qurʾān often uses in its scenes of the hereafter, and it has come before us several times in earlier sections: in al-Qāriʿa those whose balance is heavy and those whose balance is light; in al-Balad the companions of the right and of the left; in al-Shams the one who purifies and the one who buries.

A boundary must be drawn about what these twofold divisions mean.

**The division is set up not to divide people into two boxes but to describe two directions.** The words themselves show this: *abrār* and *fujjār* are **adjectival** plurals, not nouns — they convey not an identity but an attribute. Whoever carries that attribute is included in it.

And the Qurʾān itself says in other places that there are degrees in between: *"They are of degrees with God."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/163); and further, in the next sūra a difference of rank will be set up between the *abrār* and the *muqarrabūn* (al-Muṭaffifīn 83/21, 28).

So the twofold division here is not a classification that counts and divides people; it is the naming of **two directions**. One that widens, one that overflows.

### نَعِيم and جَحِيم

**نَعِيم** — root **ن-ع-م**. The concrete sense of the root is **softness**: *nāʿima* — soft. From this *niʿma* (that which gives a person softness and ease), *naʿīm* (the abundance and continuity of favour), *anʿām* (soft-treading cattle).

That softness stands at the centre of the word is striking: favour is the removal of hardness. The state of life's not striking against a man.

**جَحِيم** — root **ج-ح-م**. *Jaḥmatu'l-nār* — the burning embers of the fire, the flame at its most intense. *Jaḥama* — the fire grew fierce.

The word is one of the names of the fire of hell. As was recorded in the section on al-Humaza, the Qurʾān uses several names for hell (*jahannam, nār, jaḥīm, saqar, laẓā, saʿīr, hāwiya, ḥuṭama*) and each name brings one aspect of the fire to the front. *Jaḥīm* brings to the front **the fierceness of the heat.**

**The opposition of the two words works at the level of their roots as well:** one softness, the other fierce heat. One the softening of contact, the other contact become unbearable.

### لَفِى — "in"

In both verses the particle of place is **فِى**, and the emphatic *lām* has come before it.

In the section on al-ʿAṣr the function of this particle was dwelt on: *it does not say "man has suffered loss", it says "man is in loss."* Loss is given not as an event but as an **environment**.

The same holds here: the *abrār* do not attain to bliss, they **are in** bliss; the *fujjār* are not thrown into the fire, they **are in** the fire. Both are described not as entering a place but as being in a place.

And both verses are **nominal sentences** — there is no verb. In Arabic a nominal sentence conveys fixity. So what is recounted is not an event but a **state**.

In the next verse a verb will come (*yaṣlawnahā* — they enter it and burn) and then movement will begin. But the two verses themselves are motionless; two statements of a state.

## 82/15-16 — يَصْلَوْنَهَا يَوْمَ ٱلدِّينِ • وَمَا هُمْ عَنْهَا بِغَآئِبِينَ

*Yaṣlawnahā yawma'l-dīn • wa mā hum ʿanhā bi-ghāʾibīn*
"They enter it and burn on the day of reckoning; and they will not be absent from it."

### The division of labour between the two sentences

The fourteenth verse had stated a state: *the fujjār are in the fire.* The fifteenth verse puts a time to that state: *the day of reckoning.* The sixteenth puts a duration to it: *they will not be parted from it.*

So the three verses give three separate pieces of information: **where** (14), **when** (15), **how long** (16).

And the sixteenth verse has one further function: it takes the fourteenth out of metaphor. The expression "they are in the fire" could have been read as the description of a state; the sixteenth verse gives it certainty and continuity.

### يَصْلَوْنَ — to enter the fire

**Root: ص-ل-ي.** Its concrete sense: **to enter the fire, to be roasted in the fire, to remain inside the flame.**

*Ṣalā'l-shāt* — he roasted the sheep in the fire. *Iṣlāʾ* — to cast into the fire. *Ṣalan* — the hot place of the fire.

**A caution is needed here:** this root must not be confused with **ص-ل-و**, the root of the word for the ritual prayer. The two roots part at the final letter (*yāʾ* / *wāw*), and in Arabic a root is determined by the identity of its three consonants — the same caution was given for *falaq* and *falāḥ* in the section on al-Falaq. There is no derivational link between *ṣalāt* (the prayer) and *iṣlāʾ* (to cast into the fire).

Its uses in the Qurʾān make the root's sense clear:

- *"I shall cast him into the scorching fire."* (al-Muddaththir 74/26). *Sa-uṣlīhi saqar.*
- *"He shall enter a fire of blazing flame."* (al-Masad 111/3). *Sa-yaṣlā nāran dhāta lahab.*
- *"They enter a scorching fire."* (al-Ghāshiya 88/4). *Taṣlā nāran ḥāmiya.*

The verb is in the **imperfect**: continuity and renewal. And the pronoun attached to the verb, **هَا** — "it" — returns to the *jaḥīm* of the preceding verse.

### يَوْمَ ٱلدِّين — the day of reckoning

The construct occurs here for the first time in the sūra in its full form. In the ninth verse there was only *al-dīn*; here it becomes *yawma'l-dīn*, and it will be repeated twice more in verses 17 and 18. So the expression occurs **three times** in the sūra and stands at the centre of the last four verses.

The analysis of the word was carried out in the section on al-Māʿūn: the root **د ي ن**, the idea at its centre **return**; *dayn* (a debt), *dāna lahu* (he submitted), *dānahu* (he called him to account), *dīn* (a way, a system). In all of them the same skeleton: something given and its coming back.

The best-known place of the construct in the Qurʾān is in al-Fātiḥa: **مَالِكِ يَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ** — "the master of the day of reckoning". In the section on al-Fātiḥa this verse and the *mālik / malik* difference of variant reading were treated.

**This link bears directly on the last verse of the sūra** and will come up again at verse 19: al-Fātiḥa says that He is the *mālik* of that day; al-Infiṭār 82/19 says that on that day no soul is *mālik* over anything. The same root, to two different subjects.

### غَآئِبِين — those who are absent

**Root: غ-ي-ب.** To disappear from sight, to become invisible, not to be present in a place.

*Ghayb* — what is unseen, what falls outside the range of the senses. *Ghība* — speaking behind someone's back; the word itself means "while the person is not there". *Ghāʾib* — one not present.

And the concrete origin of the word rests on an image: **الغَابَة (*ghāba*)** — a dense forest; whoever enters it becomes invisible. From the same root *ghayāba* — the bottom of the well in the story of Joseph: *"Cast him into the bottom of the well."* (Yūsuf 12/10, 15). A depth that is not seen.

The sentence is **negative** and a **nominal sentence**: *wa mā hum ʿanhā bi-ghāʾibīn.* Moreover a redundant **بِ** has been added to the predicate — a usage in Arabic that strengthens the negation. So not "they do not disappear" but **"they will never disappear."**

### The disagreement: who is absent from whom?

Over the direction of the expression there are two readings:

| Reading | Sense | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **They are not absent from the fire** | They are not taken out of it, not removed, given no interval | The conveying of continuity; the punishment's being uninterrupted |
| **The fire is not absent from them** | The fire does not leave their sight | The dimension of seeing and consciousness: not merely burning but continually seeing |

The wording suits the first reading better, because the subject is *hum* (they), and it is they who are absent. **This is a preference; it is not binding.**

The first reading has support within the Qurʾān: *"They neither die there nor live."* (al-Aʿlā 87/13); *"They are not to come out of it."* (expressions like al-Baqara 2/167; also al-Māʾida 5/37: *"They will wish to come out of the fire, but they are not to come out of it."*).

### The echo of the word: for those who deny the *ghayb*

Here there is an observation I shall present as my own reading.

The ninth verse of the sūra had said: *you deny the reckoning.* To deny the reckoning is by definition a refusal concerning **what is unseen**. What is denied is a day that has not yet come and is not seen. The Qurʾān names this domain: *al-ghayb.* And it sets it as the first item of the description of faith: *"those who believe in the unseen"* (al-Baqara 2/3 — treated in the section on al-Baqara).

The sixteenth verse sets up a sentence using the same root: **they will not be absent from it.**

That is, for the man who refuses what is unseen, nothing is left that will remain unseen. The recompense for denying the *ghayb* turns out to be the total disappearance of the *ghayb*.

**I present this link as an observation resting on unity of root; I do not ascribe it to any classical exegete.** That the root is the same in both places is a fact standing in the text.

And the next sūra will complete this idea from the reverse side: *"They are on that day veiled from their Lord."* (al-Muṭaffifīn 83/15). Those who here are not absent from the fire will there be veiled from their Lord. This link between the two sūras will be treated in the section on al-Muṭaffifīn.

## 82/17-18 — وَمَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا يَوْمُ ٱلدِّينِ • ثُمَّ مَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ مَا يَوْمُ ٱلدِّينِ

*Wa mā adrāka mā yawmu'l-dīn • thumma mā adrāka mā yawmu'l-dīn*
"And what will make you know what the day of reckoning is? Then, what will make you know what the day of reckoning is?"

### The pattern itself

The pattern *mā adrāka* was treated in the sections on al-Humaza and al-Qadr. In brief: the root **د-ر-ي** (*dirāya* — to know, to be aware); *adrā* (the *afʿala* form) — he made known. *Mā adrāka* is literally "what has made you know?" — its sense: "your knowledge cannot take hold of this; had you known it, you would have known it from one who made it known."

And there was a regularity recorded there:

| Pattern | What happens |
|---|---|
| **وما أدراك** (perfect) | An explanation follows |
| **وما يدريك** (imperfect) | No explanation follows; the knowledge stays hidden |

**Here the pattern is in the perfect and the rule holds:** in the nineteenth verse the explanation will come — *"on that day no soul has power over anything for another."*

### The repetition itself

That the pattern is repeated **one after the other, in identical wording**, is striking. *Mā adrāka* occurs in many places in the Qurʾān; but as a place where two verses repeat the same sentence word for word, this holds a special position.

In Sūrat al-Qāriʿa the pattern occurred twice (101/3 and 101/10), but there were seven verses in between and the object changed. In the next sūra it will again occur twice (83/8 and 83/19) but the objects will be different (*Sijjīn* and *ʿIlliyyūn*). Here the object is the same, the wording is the same, and the two verses stand side by side.

**What is the function of the repetition?**

In Arabic the repetition of the same wording (*takrīr*) is a recognised instrument of rhetoric, and its functions are these: emphasis, the producing of awe (*tahwīl*), and the fixing of attention.

It can be said that all three are at work here. But there is a fourth function as well, and it follows from the structure of the text:

**The question is asked twice because the answer is given once.** The seventeenth verse asks the question, the eighteenth asks the same question again without giving the answer, and only in the nineteenth does the answer come. That is, the answer has been delayed twofold.

This delay is the reverse of the structure at the beginning of the sūra. There were **four conditions** there and a single answer; here there are **two questions** and a single answer. In both places waiting is produced.

### ثُمَّ — order, or rank?

The second verse begins with **ثم**.

*Thumma* in Arabic conveys basically **succession with an interval**: "then, after a while has passed." This is its difference from *fāʾ* — *fāʾ* joins without an interval, *thumma* joins with one.

But *thumma* has one further usage, and it is the one that holds here: **the conveying of rank (*tarākhī fī'l-rutba*).** That is, not an interval of time but a **difference of degree**: "moreover, further, beyond even this."

An example: when in praising a person one says "he is learned, then he is generous", no order of time is meant; it is conveyed that the degree of the second is higher.

So here too: the second question is not asked **after** the first; it stands **further out** than the first. That is, "you do not know what that day is — and even your knowing that you do not know is not enough."

This usage is recorded in the classical sources. **I state this as a preference**; there are also those who say that *thumma* here conveys ordinary succession, and that reading is possible too.

### The one addressed by the question

A grammatical detail must be dwelt on.

*Adrā-**ka*** — the one addressed is singular. Yet in the verses immediately before, the plural was used: *ʿalaykum* (10), *tafʿalūn* (12), and *hum* (16).

So the sūra returns here to the singular — to the address of verses 6-8.

This is the same function as the **iltifāt** treated in the section on al-Humaza: the narration breaks off, and the one addressed is taken before it one by one.

And thus the movement of pronouns in the sūra is completed:

| Verse | Pronoun | What is recounted |
|---|---|---|
| 1-5 | Third person | The universe and "a soul" |
| 6-8 | **Singular address** | Your creation |
| 9-12 | **Plural address** | Your denial, the record over you |
| 13-16 | Third person | Two groups |
| 17-18 | **Singular address** | You do not know |
| 19 | Third person | No soul |

The sūra changes position six times. And in the last verse it returns to the expression "a soul" of the opening verses.

### The question's going unanswered

As was recorded in the section on al-Humaza, this pattern declares, before it gives any information, that **knowledge will not suffice**. The question is not a request for a definition; it is a declaration of an impossibility.

Its particular consequence here is this: the nineteenth verse will give an answer, but the answer it gives is **not a description**. The fire will not be recounted, the scene will not be drawn. The only piece of information to be given is **what will not work** on that day.

That is, the question is asked as "what kind of day is that day?" and the answer is "on that day no one can do anything for anyone." The sūra describes the day not by its qualities but by its **impossibilities**.

## 82/19 — يَوْمَ لَا تَمْلِكُ نَفْسٌ لِّنَفْسٍ شَيْـًٔا ۖ وَٱلْأَمْرُ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِّلَّهِ

*Yawma lā tamliku nafsun li-nafsin shayʾā • wa'l-amru yawmaʾidhin lillāh*
"The day on which no soul has power over anything for another soul. On that day the command belongs to God alone."

The last verse of the sūra, and it answers both questions at once.

### يَوْمَ — its place in the grammar

The word is in the **accusative**, and there are several views about what it attaches to:

| View | Attachment |
|---|---|
| An explanation (*badal*) of *yawmu'l-dīn* in the preceding verse | "The day of reckoning — that is, the day on which…" |
| An adverbial attached to an implied verb | "[The reckoning is held] on the day on which…" |
| Attached to the verb *yaṣlawnahā* (verse 15) | "On that day they enter it" |

The first view appears best suited to the context: two verses in succession had asked "do you know what the day of reckoning is?"; this verse describes that day. **This is a preference; it is not binding.**

### لَا تَمْلِكُ — not to have power

**Root: م-ل-ك.** This root was treated in the section on al-Fātiḥa (*māliki yawmi'l-dīn* and the *mālik / malik* difference of variant reading).

The idea at the centre of the root is **the power of disposal**. *Mulk* is not merely ownership but **the authority to act upon a thing**. In Arabic the verb *malaka* means "to have power over a thing, to have a say in it".

For this reason it is not enough to render the verse "no soul owns another". What is said is: **there is no operation any soul can perform for another.** No authority is left in its hand.

And the verb's coming in the **negative imperfect** conveys continuity: not a one-time lack of authority, but the structure of that day.

### نَفْسٌ لِّنَفْسٍ — two indefinites

The densest place in the sentence.

Both words are **indefinite**: "a soul", "for a soul". In Arabic an indefinite noun coming in a negative sentence conveys **generality** — it covers all without exception.

So the sentence does not say "such-and-such a person cannot do anything for such-and-such another"; it says that **no soul can do anything for any soul**. And there is a third indefinite as well: **شَيْـًٔا** — "a thing", again in a negative context, again general.

Three indefinites one upon another. Every door of the sentence is shut.

Further, the **repetition** of the word is meaningful. The verse does not say "no one for anyone"; it uses the same word twice: *nafsun li-nafsin.* This conveys that the two parties are **of the same kind**. That is, there is no difference of rank between the one who cannot help and the one who cannot be helped; both are the same thing, both are a soul.

**And this connects with the first half of the sūra.** The fifth verse ran: *ʿalimat **nafsun** mā qaddamat wa akhkharat.* The same word, in the same indefinite state.

The first soul of the sūra came to have knowledge about itself; the last soul is left without authority over another. **The two sentences make a pair: the knowledge is yours, the authority is not.**

### The link with al-Baqara 2/48

The closest relative of this verse is in Sūrat al-Baqara, and the two texts must be set side by side.

*"And guard yourselves against a day on which no soul shall pay anything on behalf of another soul; no intercession shall be accepted from it; no ransom shall be taken from it; and they shall not be helped."* (al-Baqara 2/48)

This verse was treated in the section on al-Baqara, and the four negations were given there in a table:

| al-Baqara 2/48 | The road that is closed |
|---|---|
| *lā tajzī nafsun ʿan nafsin* | No one can pay in another's place |
| *wa lā yuqbalu minhā shafāʿatun* | No mediation is accepted |
| *wa lā yuʾkhadhu minhā ʿadlun* | No ransom is taken |
| *wa lā hum yunṣarūn* | No help is given |

And the conclusion recorded there was this: **"All four together say a single thing: the reckoning cannot be transferred."**

Now let us compare with al-Infiṭār 82/19:

| | al-Baqara 2/48 | al-Infiṭār 82/19 |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Four separate negations | A single negation |
| Verb | **لا تجزي** — cannot pay, cannot answer for | **لا تملك** — has no power, no authority |
| Scope | Each of four roads is closed separately | All the roads are closed in a single sentence |
| Addition | — | **والأمر يومئذ لله** |

**The difference between them is that the two verses speak from different places.**

Al-Baqara 2/48 enumerates one by one **the roads that might be tried**: payment, mediation, ransom, help. Four separate doors, and all four shut. As was stated in the section on al-Baqara, the verse addresses the community that in the preceding verse was told "you were favoured", and declares that lineage, belonging and past privilege will not work on that day.

Al-Infiṭār 82/19 does not count the doors; it removes **the authority of the hand that would open a door**. *Lā tajzī* means "it will be of no use"; *lā tamliku* means "it is not in a position to do it". One refuses the outcome, the other the possibility.

**What emerges when the two are read together is this:** al-Baqara says that every road that might be tried is closed, al-Infiṭār says that the trying itself is not possible.

### A link of root: عدل

Here a connection made within this sūra, and pointed to above, is completed.

**The word occurring in the third item of al-Baqara 2/48 is عَدْل**, and it was stated openly in the section on al-Baqara: *"the word here means not 'justice' but ransom; the basic sense of the root is 'that which is equivalent'."*

**The same root had occurred in al-Infiṭār 82/7: فَعَدَلَكَ** — "He balanced you."

So the same root is used at the two ends of the sūra for two different works:

> **He made you equivalent (82/7) → on that day no equivalent (ransom) is of any use (the road closed by al-Baqara 2/48).**

Man is a being who has himself been balanced; but he can find no equivalent to put in his own place.

**I present this link as my own reading.** The unity of root stands in the text; the opposition set up between the two usages is an interpretation, and I do not ascribe it to any classical exegete.

### What the verse does not close: intercession

A boundary must be drawn, because this verse read on its own leads to a disputed heading in the history of theology.

This matter was treated in the section on al-Baqara and a balance was set up there: al-Baqara 2/48 on its own looks like an absolute rejection of intercession; but al-Baqara 2/255 (*"Who is there that can intercede with Him except by His leave?"*) qualifies it. The conclusion reached there was this: **"there is no intercession that stands of itself, as a right, that a person may count on as a guarantee; what is bound to leave is a separate matter."**

The same balance holds here. The verse refuses a person's doing anything for another **by his own authority**. And indeed the second half of the sentence says this openly: *wa'l-amru yawmaʾidhin lillāh* — where the authority lies is being declared.

In this commentary no side is taken on this matter; setting the two verses side by side is all that is done.

### وَٱلْأَمْرُ يَوْمَئِذٍ لِّلَّهِ

The last sentence of the sūra.

**ٱلْأَمْر** — root **أ-م-ر**. The word carries two senses in Arabic, and both are at work here:

1. **Command, order.** Its plural is *awāmir*.
2. **Affair, matter, situation.** Its plural is *umūr*.

That the same word means both "command" and "affair" shows the idea beneath the root: **the authority to decide how a matter shall go.** The one who commands is the one who determines the course of the affair.

The sentence is a **nominal sentence** and its predicate is a prepositional phrase: *lillāh.* The Arabic letter **ل** here conveys possession and exclusive attachment: His alone.

And in the middle of the sentence the adverb **يَوْمَئِذٍ** — "on that day" — has come between subject and predicate. This position gives it emphasis.

### The qualification "on that day": is it not so today?

A legitimate question arises here: is the command not God's today? Why is the qualification "on that day" laid down?

The answers given in the classical sources:

**One.** The command is His today as well; but today others too have authority **in appearance**. People rule, decide, command. On that day this appearance will be removed.

**Two.** Today a **field of disposal** has been given to people: they can choose, they can act, they can help one another. On that day that field will be taken back.

The Qurʾān sets up this distinction itself:

*"The command belongs to God, before and after."* (al-Rūm 30/4)

By saying "before and after" the verse widens the note of time. So al-Infiṭār's qualification "on that day" does not say that it belonged to another before it; it says that on that day **no claim of partnership will remain**.

And the closest parallel:

*"On that day they come forth; nothing of theirs is hidden from God. To whom belongs the kingdom today? To God, the One, the Overwhelming!"* (Ghāfir 40/16)

This verse asks a question and answers it itself. Al-Infiṭār 82/19 says the same thing without asking a question.

Further: *"Say: the command belongs entirely to God."* (Āl ʿImrān 3/154).

### The last word's breaking of the rhyme

Something happens here in the sound structure of the sūra, and it is worth recording.

From the ninth verse onward the verse-endings of the sūra had been built on a nasal sound: *al-dīn, ḥāfiẓīn, kātibīn, tafʿalūn, naʿīm, jaḥīm, al-dīn, ghāʾibīn, al-dīn, al-dīn.* A rhyme unbroken for ten verses.

The last verse ends with **لِلَّهِ**. Outside the rhyme.

So the sūra lets go, in its last word, of the sound it had kept up for ten verses — and that word is **the name of God**.

**I record this not as a claim of a miracle but as an observation of form.** That the pattern of the verse-endings changes in the last verse stands in the text; the meaning I draw from it is an interpretation. But the interpretation's agreement with the text is plain: as the sūra says "on that day the command belongs to God", the sentence itself steps outside its own order.

### The closing of the sūra

The links the last verse makes with the sūra must be gathered up:

- The word **نَفْس** occurs once in verse 5 and twice in verse 19. The sūra closes with this word the account it opened with it.
- The root **عدل** in verse 7 (*ʿadalaka*), and in al-Baqara 2/48 in the sense of ransom. The being that was balanced can find no equivalent for itself.
- The link of **مالك / يوم الدين** attaches to al-Fātiḥa: the master of that day is one.
- The word **ٱلْأَمْر** answers the absence of a doer in the first four verses of the sūra. By whose command was the sky split? By whose command did the seas overflow? At its beginning the sūra did not say; at its end it says.

This last completes the structure of the sūra. In the first four the doer was concealed; in the seventh verse it was partly opened with *alladhī khalaqaka*; in the last verse it is said in full: **al-amru yawmaʾidhin lillāh.**

## The sūra as a whole

### The sound structure

The verse-endings of the nineteen verses gather into three clusters:

**The first cluster (1-5): -rat.** *Infaṭarat, intatharat, fujjirat, buʿthirat, akhkharat.* All five of the five verses end on the same sound, and in all five the letter **ر** stands before the close. The opening of the sūra is a repeating, hard sound.

**The second cluster (6-8): -īm and -ka.** *Al-Karīm, ʿadalak, rakkabak.* The rhyme breaks, and for two verses **the pronoun of the one addressed** holds the end of the verse. The two verses whose subject is "you" have "you" for their sound as well.

**The third cluster (9-18): a nasal close.** *Al-dīn, ḥāfiẓīn, kātibīn, tafʿalūn, naʿīm, jaḥīm, al-dīn, ghāʾibīn, al-dīn, al-dīn.* For ten verses, a long vowel + *nūn* or *mīm*. This is the texture seen in Sūrat al-Māʿūn as well, and is a common feature of the short Meccan sūras.

**And the last verse (19): outside the rhyme.** *Lillāh.*

The boundaries of the three clusters **coincide exactly** with the sūra's divisions of sense: the cosmic coming apart (1-5), the turn to the one addressed (6-8), the reckoning and the recompense (9-18), the close (19). Sound and structure move together.

### Structure: four undoings, four buildings

The skeleton of the sūra was cast into a table at verse 8. Here it is once more, in its completed form:

| | **Dissolution** (1-4) | **Construction** (7-8) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ٱنفَطَرَتْ — it was split | خَلَقَكَ — He measured and cut you |
| 2 | ٱنتَثَرَتْ — they were scattered | سَوَّىٰكَ — He brought your parts into correspondence |
| 3 | فُجِّرَتْ — its dyke was broken | عَدَلَكَ — He balanced your two sides |
| 4 | بُعْثِرَتْ — it was overturned | رَكَّبَكَ — He assembled your parts |
| Object | The universe | **You** |
| Doer | Not named | Your Lord |

And the two verses between the two series (5-6) carry the whole weight of the sūra: **knowledge** (5) and **the question** (6).

After this the sūra sets up a second pair:

| | **The record** (10-12) | **The recompense** (13-16) |
|---|---|---|
| What is happening | What is done is being written | What is written is being repaid |
| Subjects | *ḥāfiẓīn, kirām, kātibīn* | *abrār, fujjār* |
| Time | Now | That day |

And the last three verses (17-19) are the close: two questions and one answer.

### The real idea of the sūra: the deceptiveness of generosity

I return to the question I noted at the beginning of the sūra: why *al-Karīm*?

The answer stands scattered across the whole of the sūra and must be gathered.

**First step: all the proofs the sūra shows are gifts.**

The seventh and eighth verses enumerate the body of the one addressed. But note that this is presented as a **proof**, not as a **favour**. That is, the verse does not say "I gave you these things, be grateful"; it says "it was I who built you".

There is a difference between the two. A favour asks for gratitude; a proof asks for a conclusion.

**Second step: the gift itself can become the material of the deception.**

When something is given to a man, two outcomes are possible: remembering the existence of the giver, or growing used to what is given and forgetting the giver.

The second outcome is the more widespread, and the reason is not psychological but structural: **what is given without interruption is perceived as ground.** And ground is not noticed. A man does not think about the place he stands on.

The question of the sūra is exactly at this point: *what deceived you about your generous Lord?* That is: how did what was given you without interruption take you away from the one who gave it?

**Third step: the delay of punishment is a gift too, and it deceives as well.**

Fāṭir 35/45 and al-Kahf 18/58 said that the deferral of the reckoning is a mercy. But deferral gives the impression that the thing deferred **does not exist**.

So both what is given and the punishment that is not given work in the same direction: both produce the message "there is no problem". And the source of both is the same attribute: **generosity.**

**Fourth step: the sūra does not count this deception an excuse.**

The ninth verse says *kallā*. That is, the deception may be real, but it is not an excuse. And the reason is stated at once: the matter is not deception but **denial**. It is not that the person does not know; he does not accept.

**Fifth step: the record is set down as the antidote to the deception.**

From the tenth verse on, the sūra takes aim at the assumption the deception rests upon: *there is no record of what I do.* Three verses remove that assumption. And what is striking is this: those who keep the record are **karīm** as well. That is, both the giver and the recorder are named with the same attribute.

**Sixth step: generosity is not the opposite of the reckoning.**

This is the last thing the sūra sets up, and it is generally missed. Generosity and the reckoning are not two things that cancel one another. They are two attributes of the same Lord: the one who gives and the one who asks. The deception is born of taking the first for the absence of the second.

The symmetry the sūra sets up with the recompense can be seen here too:

| What man does | Its counterpart |
|---|---|
| **He infers from generosity the absence of the reckoning** | *Yawma'l-dīn* is repeated three times |
| **He assumes that what he does is not recorded** | *Kirāman kātibīn* — and honoured scribes at that |
| **He breaks his own dyke** (*fujūr*) | The dyke of the seas is broken (*fujjirat*) |
| **He forgets that he was put together** (*rakkaba*) | The graves are overturned, the assembly is undone |
| **He hopes that someone can come between** | *Lā tamliku nafsun li-nafsin shayʾā* |

### Al-Takwīr, al-Infiṭār, al-Inshiqāq

In the arrangement of the codex sūras 81, 82 and 84 open with a similar pattern, and comparing them is instructive.

| | al-Takwīr 81 | al-Infiṭār 82 | al-Inshiqāq 84 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | *Idhā* + 12 scenes | *Idhā* + 4 scenes | *Idhā* + 3 scenes |
| Answer | *ʿAlimat nafsun mā aḥḍarat* (81/14) | *ʿAlimat nafsun mā qaddamat wa akhkharat* (82/5) | *Yā ayyuhā'l-insānu innaka kādiḥun ilā Rabbika* (84/6) |
| Sky | *kushiṭat* — stripped away and opened | *infaṭarat* — split | *inshaqqat* — split |
| Stars | *inkadarat* — grew dark | *intatharat* — were scattered | — |

All three sūras use the same pattern: a long series of *idhā*, then the turn to man. In all three the outcome is a piece of **knowledge** or an **encounter**.

**It must be stated that this is an observation about the arrangement of the codex; it is not a claim about the order of revelation.** The relation between sūras is a recognised subject in exegesis, but one must be cautious against forcing links of this kind.

### The link with al-Muṭaffifīn

Between al-Infiṭār and the next sūra there is a much closer overlap of vocabulary than would be expected for two sūras standing side by side in the arrangement of the codex. I shall treat this link in detail in the section on al-Muṭaffifīn; here I record the list:

| Word | al-Infiṭār | al-Muṭaffifīn |
|---|---|---|
| **ٱلْأَبْرَار** | 82/13 — *inna'l-abrāra la-fī naʿīm* | 83/18, 83/22 — *inna'l-abrāra la-fī naʿīm* |
| **ٱلْفُجَّار** | 82/14 | 83/7 |
| **نَعِيم** | 82/13 | 83/22, 83/24 |
| **جَحِيم** | 82/14 | 83/16 |
| **يَوْمُ ٱلدِّين** | 82/15, 17, 18 | 83/11 |
| **حَٰفِظِين** | 82/10 — *wa inna **ʿalaykum** la-ḥāfiẓīn* | 83/33 — *wa mā ursilū **ʿalayhim** ḥāfiẓīn* |
| **مَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ** | 82/17, 18 | 83/8, 19 |
| **كَلَّا** | 82/9 | 83/7, 14, 15, 18 |
| **تُكَذِّبُونَ** | 82/9 | 83/10-12, 17 |
| **مَا تَفْعَلُونَ** | 82/12 | 83/36 — *mā kānū yafʿalūn* |

**Two verses are identical to the letter:** al-Infiṭār 82/13 and al-Muṭaffifīn 83/22 — *inna'l-abrāra la-fī naʿīm.*

And two of them stand as though each were the answer to the other: al-Infiṭār says *"over you there are keepers-and-recorders"*; al-Muṭaffifīn says of those who mocked the believers *"they had not been sent as keepers over them"*. The same word, the same particle (*ʿalā*), two opposite judgements.

There may be an explanation for this overlap: the two sūras may be addressing a similar period and a similar audience. But I do not set this up as a claim about revelation; **the overlap of vocabulary is a datum standing in the text, while the historical conclusion to be drawn from it requires separate evidence.**

## What this says to the present

**First: "nothing has happened to me" is not a proof.**

The diagnosis of the sixth verse of the sūra can be framed in a non-religious language as well: the human mind errs systematically about behaviours whose consequences are delayed.

The reason for this is not moral but structural. Learning rests on the nearness between act and consequence. As the distance between them grows, the link cannot be made; past a certain point the mind classes the act as "without consequence". And every repetition without consequence reinforces that classification.

This is why the most dangerous side of delayed harms is not their magnitude but **the sense of security they produce over the time they are delayed.** A neglected medical check-up, an accumulating debt, a conversation avoided, a structure not inspected — all work in the same way. And in all of them the defensive sentence is the same: *nothing has happened so far.*

The question the verse asks is directed precisely at that sentence.

**Second: the one treated well counts good treatment a right.**

This is an observable pattern of behaviour and can be stated without accusation. Something given continuously comes after a while to be expected without its being noticed as given. In the same way, behaviour that is continually met with indulgence is read as meaning that there is no boundary.

For this reason generosity carries a risk for the one who is generous: it may produce a sense of entitlement on the other side. This observation is not a ground for being less generous; **it explains why it is vain to expect gratitude in return for generosity.**

And for the one the sūra addresses the measure is this: *do I see what is given me as a right or as a gift?* The difference between the two shows in behaviour — the holder of a right is angry when it is not given, the receiver of a gift is surprised when it is.

**Third: keeping a record depends on the quality of the one who records.**

The attribute *kirāman* in the eleventh verse takes on a particular meaning today.

Recording another person's mistake has never been easier. A screenshot, a recording, an archive — all instantaneous and costless. And this ease is beginning to make the record itself look like a merit: *I am documenting, therefore I am in the right.*

The verse enters a note against this: the attribute of the recorders is **being of worth**. That is, what makes a record legitimate is not its being true; it is **what the one who records is keeping it for.**

The person Sūrat al-Humaza describes was exactly a keeper of records — but what he stored up was other people's faults, and that storing up was what made him a *humaza*.

**Fourth: the non-transferability of the reckoning means that responsibility cannot be distributed.**

The nineteenth verse, while giving a piece of information about the hereafter, also lays down a measure for this world: **what one person does cannot be entered in another's account.**

This has practical counterparts. The most widespread is the distribution of responsibility to the crowd: "everyone was doing it", "there were orders", "the system is like that", "I was only a part of it". These sentences are attempts to hand an act over to a collective.

In the section on al-Shams this matter was treated by way of the story of Thamūd: the one who hamstrung the camel was a single person, but the punishment came upon the community — because the ground that produced the act was the community's. That is, responsibility was both individual and shared.

Al-Infiṭār 82/19 holds the other end of this matter: the crowd does not carry the individual. A shared ground may give birth to an act, but it cannot take on the reckoning.

**Fifth: "what you have left behind" is in the account too.**

The column of the fifth verse, *akhkharat* — what you leave behind you and what goes on walking after you — is larger today than it has ever been.

A sentence that is written does not stay where it was written. An order that is set up goes on working after the one who set it up. A piece of software that is written goes on deciding after its author has forgotten it. A word that is said goes to places the one who said it cannot reach.

The expression of Yāsīn 36/12, "and We write down the traces", names this column. And the measure works both ways: the trace left may be good or bad. The question itself is neutral: *what am I leaving that will go on working after I have stopped?*

**Sixth: the sūra describes an attribute, not an identity.**

The thirteenth and fourteenth verses divide people in two. The side of this division most easily abused is turning it into a **list**: who is of the *abrār*, who of the *fujjār*.

The words themselves prevent this. *Abrār* and *fujjār* are adjectival plurals — they convey not an identity but an attribute. The principle recorded in the section on al-Humaza holds here exactly: **the verse describes an attribute; whoever carries that attribute is included in it.**

And the one the sūra addresses is reading this division not in order to apply it to others but in order to put the question of the sixth verse to himself: *what deceived you?*

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

The points at which, throughout the text, a language of certainty was deliberately not used:

- **That the root ف-ط-ر carries the senses "to create" and "to be split" together** is the data of the lexicographers. But the line drawn from this — "the beginning and the end of the sky are the same verb" — is **my own reading**; it has not been ascribed to any classical exegete.
- **The report of "faṭartuhā" ascribed to Ibn ʿAbbās** is widely transmitted in the literature of exegesis; **because I cannot give its chain with certainty, it has been transmitted with a note of caution.**
- The nearness between *biḥār* (verse 3) and *abrār* (verse 13) has been recorded **as an observation about sound and association**; the roots of the two words are separate (ب-ح-ر / ب-ر-ر) and no claim of derivation has been set up.
- **The three views on what happens to the seas** were given in a table; a preference was stated and it was recorded that it is not binding.
- The unity of root between *fujjirat* (3) and *fujjār* (14) is the data of the text; the comparison drawn from it is **my own reading.**
- **The difference of variant reading in verse 7** (*fa-ʿadalaka / fa-ʿaddalaka*) and the two senses (to make equal / to turn) were recorded; **because I cannot give with certainty which reading belongs to which imam, no name was written.**
- **Three readings were given of the function of the particle *bāʾ* in verse 6**; a preference was stated, and it was recorded that it is not binding.
- **The interpretation that the attribute *al-Karīm* prompts the answer** is widespread in the tradition of exegesis; **it is stated that it is ascribed to some early scholars, but because I cannot give the soundness of the ascription, no name was written.**
- **The interpretation that the angels write the good at once and hold back before writing the bad** is present in the sources; **because I cannot give the soundness of the reports it rests on, no ascription to a source was made.**
- **The distinction between *fiʿl* and *ʿamal*** was transmitted as the lexicographers record it; it was stated that the Qurʾān's usage does not confirm this distinction sharply everywhere.
- **Two views were given on what the *kallā* of verse 9 cuts off**; a preference was stated.
- **Whether the *ḥāfiẓīn* of verse 10 are "keepers" or "recorders"** is disputed; three views were given in a table.
- **Two readings were given of the direction of the absence in verse 16**; a preference was stated, and it was recorded that it is not binding. The link set up between the root *ghayb* and the denial of verse 9 is **my own reading.**
- **The reading by which the *thumma* of verse 18 conveys rank** was stated as a preference; the reading of ordinary succession is possible too.
- **The link set up between *ʿadala* (82/7) and the *ʿadl* (ransom) of al-Baqara 2/48** is my own reading; the unity of root is the data of the text, the opposition is an interpretation.
- **No side was taken on the matter of intercession**; the balance set up in the section on al-Baqara was repeated.
- **The last verse's breaking of the rhyme** is an observation of form; the meaning drawn from it is an interpretation and has not been presented as a claim of a miracle.
- **The trio al-Takwīr–al-Infiṭār–al-Inshiqāq and the overlap between al-Infiṭār and al-Muṭaffifīn** are observations about the arrangement of the codex; they contain no claim about the order of revelation.
