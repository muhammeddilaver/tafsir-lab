# 80. Sūrat ʿAbasa

Forty-two verses. It is counted as belonging to the Meccan period, and it comes early in the lists of the order of revelation. It takes its name from its first word: **ʿabasa** — "he frowned".

No other sūra in the Qurʾān begins like this. Not with an oath, not with a command, not with a word of praise; with **the description of an expression on a face**. And the face described is the face of the man to whom this book was sent down.

## What the sūra does

The sūra consists of five parts, and at first sight the parts look loosely joined to one another. The join is in fact very tight; it will be shown under the sūra as a whole.

**First part (1-10).** An incident is related: someone came and was not received. The one who came could not see. The one who did not receive him was at that moment occupied with someone else. Then the address changes direction and the incident turns into a criticism.

**Second part (11-16).** The subject moves from the incident to the text: this is a reminder, whoever wills remembers it; it is on pages made honoured, raised high and purified, in the hands of noble scribes.

**Third part (17-23).** A heavy curse, and after it the short story of man: he was created from a drop, he was given measure, his path was made easy for him, he was made to die, he was put in a grave, and when it is willed he will be raised. The conclusion: he has still not done what he was commanded.

**Fourth part (24-32).** A picture of a table. Rain, split earth, grain, grapes, green fodder, olive, date palm, thick-treed gardens, fruit, pasture. And the close: "for your enjoyment and that of your livestock."

**Fifth part (33-42).** That day. The sound that deafens the ears; man's flight from his brother, from his mother and his father, from his wife and his children; then a picture of two kinds of face — shining faces and faces on which dust has settled.

The sūra has a single question: **by what do we know who matters?** The first part shows one wrong answer to it. The remaining four parts set out in turn where the answer is to be taken from: from the text, from man's own story, from his plate, and from his end.

## The names of the sūra

It is transmitted that in the sources it is referred to by the names **ʿAbasa** (the opening word), **Wa tawallā** and **al-Safara**. In the early period it was common for sūras to be referred to not by an official name but by some striking word inside them; this multiplicity is the trace of that.

That one of the names is *al-Safara* is worth noticing, because the hidden backbone of the sūra is precisely the root of that word — it will be shown under the sūra as a whole.

## The occasion of revelation

This sūra is one of the places where the link between the report of the occasion of revelation and the reading of the text is at its tightest. The report must therefore be given carefully and with its qualification.

**A summary of the report.** The Prophet was speaking with several of the leading men of Quraysh, calling them to Islam. While this was going on a blind Companion came and cut in, asking to be taught something. The Prophet did not like the breaking off of the conversation; he frowned and turned away from him. The sūra was sent down upon this.

**The standing of the report.** This report is transmitted with attribution to ʿĀʾisha and is found in sources among which is al-Tirmidhī. I do not quote its wording verbatim; there are differences among its variants and some of them are *mursal* (the chain does not reach back to an eyewitness). There is broad agreement in the sources on the *meaning* of the report; there is none on its details.

**The name of the blind Companion.** In the reports he appears as **ʿAbd Allāh b. Umm Maktūm**; in some transmissions his name is given as ʿAmr. That this person existed, and that he was a well-known Companion in Medina, is historically firm. It is also transmitted that when the Prophet saw him in a later period he said, "Welcome to the man on whose account my Lord admonished me" — the chain of this second report is disputed, and it is not presented here as an established transmission.

**The names on the other side.** The reports differ as to whom the Prophet was speaking with at that moment: names such as ʿUtba b. Rabīʿa, Shayba b. Rabīʿa, Abū Jahl, al-ʿAbbās b. ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, Umayya b. Khalaf and al-Walīd b. al-Mughīra occur in different transmissions in different combinations. **That the names do not agree with one another strengthens the possibility that they are answers given after the event to the question "who might they have been?"** We met the same situation in Sūrat al-Māʿūn; the measure applied there holds here too: the sūra does not give a name, it gives an attribute.

**And the real point to notice: the sūra itself gives no name at all.** It names neither the blind man, nor the leading men, nor the one who frowned. Whereas in Sūrat Tabbat a single person is named openly, here the incident is related and the person is not named. I shall dwell on this choice at the first verse.

## Who is addressed? — the minority view, and why it stayed a minority

In the history of tafsīr there is a view holding that the one addressed in the first verses of the sūra is not the Prophet, and that someone else present in the gathering at the time is meant (in some transmissions a man from the Banū Umayya). This view has remained a minority one, but it is a real view and it would not be right to conceal it.

| View | Ground | Its weak side |
|---|---|---|
| **The one addressed is the Prophet** (majority) | The address in verses 3-10 is in the second person singular and ascribes the act to the one addressed by saying *"you turn towards him"*, *"you turn away from him"*; and in the rest of the sūra the address comes back to the Prophet (*"It is only a reminder"*). The reports on the occasion of revelation run in this direction without exception. There are other places in the Qurʾān where a choice of the Prophet's is corrected (al-Tawba 9/43; al-Anfāl 8/67-68; al-Aḥzāb 33/37; al-Taḥrīm 66/1). | That the sūra begins in the third person looks at first sight as though it points to someone else. |
| **The one addressed is someone else** (minority) | That verses 1-2 are in the third person; that the verb *ʿabasa* stands far from the Qurʾān's own description of the Prophet (al-Qalam 68/4: *"You are upon a great character"*; al-Tawba 9/128: *"raʾūf and raḥīm towards the believers"*). | The text itself does not carry this reading: from verse 3 onward the address turns to the second person and is directed at **the one who did the act**. Had a separate person been meant, one would expect a single indication in the text that this person was present in the gathering; there is none. Moreover the rest of the sūra (*"kallā innahā tadhkira"*) addresses the Prophet beyond dispute; one would have to assume that the address changes owner in the middle. |

**Why is the majority in the first view?** For three reasons:

1. **The grammar of the text.** Verses 6 and 10 say *"fa-anta"* — "it is you". This is not description but ascription. The act is loaded onto the one addressed.
2. **The direction of the reports.** Though the reports on the occasion of revelation differ in detail, they do not differ in direction.
3. **And most importantly: the second view appears to have arisen in order to solve a problem.** The problem it tries to solve is the question "can the Prophet be criticised?" But this is not a problem drawn out of the text; it is a worry brought to the text from outside. The Qurʾān itself does not carry this worry — indeed it does the same thing openly in other places.

My preference is in the direction of the majority view, and its ground is given above. **It is not binding.**

## The text criticising its own prophet

This is the most-discussed aspect of the sūra and a place that must be handled carefully. I write it without exaggeration, as a matter of fact.

**The fact is this:** the Qurʾān criticises a piece of conduct on the part of the man who was delivering it — and does so immediately after the event, and irreversibly, by writing it into the text. And this is not the only instance:

- *al-Tawba 9/43*: *"May God pardon you; why did you give them leave before those who spoke the truth became clear to you?"*
- *al-Anfāl 8/67-68*: the questioning of the decision taken about the captives of Badr.
- *al-Aḥzāb 33/37*: *"You were hiding within yourself what God was to bring out, and you were wary of people; whereas God was the one you should have been wary of."*
- *al-Taḥrīm 66/1*: *"Why do you forbid yourself what God has made lawful for you?"*

The existence of these verses was noticed and discussed within the tradition as well. In a report stated to be found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, it is transmitted that ʿĀʾisha said of al-Aḥzāb 33/37 something to the effect that had Muhammad been going to conceal anything of the revelation, he would have concealed this verse. So this observation is not new; it was made in the early period too.

**The observation is this:** in a text written by someone who wants to establish his own authority, the recording of a mistake of his is not to be expected. Texts that establish authority tend to show the bearer of the authority as without blemish; this is a pattern observed in political and institutional texts as much as in religious ones. Sūrat ʿAbasa does the opposite: the criticism is made permanent, recited in prayer, memorised.

**And this qualification must be entered:** I do not advance this as a *proof*. A claim to proof requires saying "it cannot be explained otherwise"; whereas other explanations of a passage of this kind can also be conceived — it could be argued, for instance, that giving an impression of sincerity is itself a strategy. This objection must be taken seriously. What can be said against it is this: the **content** of the criticism is not of the kind one would choose as a strategy. The mistake chosen gains no credit; neglecting a weak man is not something that can be turned into a virtue in any reader's eyes.

In the end this is **an observation**: the text contains a passage that does not work in the interest of its bearer. The reader decides for himself where to place this observation in his own reasoning. This commentary does not decide.

**And what must not be forgotten:** this is not the sūra's own question. It is a by-product of the sūra. The sūra's question is the question of who is counted as important.

## The basmala

The word-by-word analysis of the basmala was made in Sūrat al-Fātiḥa; I do not repeat it here. A note particular to this sūra: the first sentence of a text that opens with the names al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm describes the frowning of a face. Mercy is a turning towards the one who brings no return; the first ten verses of the sūra show exactly a moment in which that faltered.

## 80/1-2 — عَبَسَ وَتَوَلَّىٰٓ ۝ أَن جَآءَهُ ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ

*ʿAbasa wa tawallā ۝ An jāʾahu'l-aʿmā*
"He frowned and turned away — because the blind man came to him."

### That it begins in the third person

The finest point of the sūra is here, and it needs to be seen carefully.

The sentence could have been built like this: **عَبَسْتَ وَتَوَلَّيْتَ** — "you frowned and turned away". Two letters would have changed, the metre would not have been broken, the rhyme would not have been broken either. It is not built that way.

Instead the third person is used: **"he frowned"**. The subject of the verb is not even stated; it is passed over with a concealed pronoun. There is no name in view, no "you". Only a verb and a face.

**What does this do?**

1. **It separates the act from the doer.** Conduct related in the third person is set one step away from the one it belongs to. The person addressed sees it first **from the outside**. We do the same thing in English: when we have hurt someone close to us, saying "something like this happened" instead of "you did this" carries the same information with less sting. The grammar of the sentence is regulating the dose of the criticism.

2. **It makes the reader a witness to the event.** Had it begun in the second person the reader would have stayed outside; the event would have been a matter passing between two people. Related in the third person, the reader sees the scene. And one who has seen the scene then feels more sharply the place where the address turns.

3. **And most importantly: the verdict is passed first on the act, then on the person.** Through two verses the thing being judged is a **piece of conduct**. Who did it is not yet the subject. When the address turns in the third verse, the wrongness of the conduct has already been established; all that remains is whose it was.

**The moment the address turns.** The third verse says *"wa mā yudrīka"* — "what do you know" — and the address passes to the second person singular. In Arabic this manner is called **iltifāt** (a shift in the direction of address); it was treated in the sections on al-Humaza and al-Qāriʿa.

But the function of the *iltifāt* here differs from the ones there. In al-Humaza the *iltifāt* drew the reader into the scene. Here the *iltifāt* **delivers up the owner of the criticism.** And the sequence needs to be seen:

| Verse | Person | What is done |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | Third person | The act is described, its owner is not stated |
| 3-4 | Second person, **a question** | The one addressed is fixed, but what comes is not an accusation, it is a question |
| 5-10 | Second person, **description** | The act is openly ascribed to the one addressed: *"it is you"* |
| 11-16 | Second person, the subject changes | The criticism ends, the text itself is taken up |
| 17- | Third person, **general** | The subject is now "man"; the one addressed is released |

**This is an architecture of courtesy.** The criticism enters in the softest form (third person), continues with a question, reaches its hardest point in the middle, lasts six verses, then ends and is never returned to. The remaining thirty-two verses of the sūra do not come back to this matter.

And the courtesy does not lighten the criticism. Verses 5-10 are extremely plain; there is no covering at all. What is softened is not the accusation itself but **the entry into the accusation**. This distinction matters: courtesy is not bending the truth, it is correcting the manner in which the truth is carried.

One more detail: the criticism ends, and **no apology is asked for, no repentance is commanded, no expiation is imposed.** It is said, and it is left. The rest of the sūra speaks of other things. This too is part of the architecture.

### عَبَسَ — to frown

**Root: ع-ب-س.** Its meaning: to screw up the face, to knit the brows, the hardening of the countenance. **عَبُوس (ʿabūs)** — sullen-faced, scowling.

The word describes something concrete and narrow: the movement of the muscles of the face. Not a word, not an attitude, not a decision — a **movement of muscle**. And the sūra takes a thing this small as its subject.

**The other uses of the root in the Qurʾān** show the weight of the word:

- *al-Muddaththir 74/22*: *"Then he knitted his brows and scowled"* (*thumma ʿabasa wa basar*) — in the description of someone trying to pass judgement on the revelation.
- *al-Insān 76/10*: *"We fear from our Lord a day sullen and severe"* (*yawman ʿabūsan qamṭarīrā*) — as an attribute of the day of resurrection.

So in the Qurʾān's own lexicon the root is not a light word. Once it is used of the face of a denier, and once of the day of resurrection itself.

I record this not as an accusation but as **a measure of the weight of the word**. What the sūra is doing is precisely to establish that weight: a movement that looks small is named not with a small word but with a heavy one.

**And this point must not be passed over: the blind man did not see it.**

The frown was a reaction the person opposite him could not perceive. Since the man was blind, he saw neither the knitted brow nor the hardened face. He was not even hurt — because to be hurt one must notice.

This is the most shaking side of the sūra: **a fault no one saw was written into a text that will be recited until the resurrection.** A wrong the injured party did not know of went on record. It is the exact reverse of the mechanism described in Sūrat al-Māʿūn: there, repelling the orphan went unchecked because it carried no worldly sanction; here, an expression on a face was recorded although it had no worldly visibility at all.

The two are two faces of the same principle: **what is not seen goes on record.**

### وَتَوَلَّىٰ — and he turned away

**Root: و-ل-ي.** At the centre of the root lie **nearness and adjacency**: *walī* (the one who is near, who protects), *mawlā*, *walāya* (the bond of mutual protection), *tawālī* (coming one after another, following in succession). *wālī* — the one who takes charge of an affair.

**تَوَلَّى** (Form V, *tafaʿʿul*), on the other hand, does two opposite jobs at once in Arabic:

- *tawallā'l-amra* — he **took the matter on**, took charge of it, took command of it.
- *tawallā ʿan* — he **turned his face away**, turned his back, withdrew.

The same form covers both taking on and abandoning. The reason is the logic of the root: both are a movement of **taking a direction**. To turn towards something is at the same time to turn from something. Whoever turns his face to one has given his back to the other.

And this is exactly what the verse relates. The Prophet had turned in one direction — towards the leading men of Quraysh. To turn in that direction meant giving his back to the other. **The choice had been made not as a rejection but as a turning towards.** The sixth verse of the sūra will say this openly: *"you turn towards him."*

This may be the most practical lesson of the sūra: neglect is most often not a decision to refuse. It is the withdrawal from one direction of the attention given to another. Nobody says "I do not think this man matters"; he simply looks at someone else.

### أَن جَآءَهُ — "because he came"

**Grammar.** The *an* here is for *taʿlīl* (the statement of cause); the *lām* at its head has been dropped: *li-an jāʾahu* — "by reason of his coming to him". So the verse does not merely set two verbs side by side; it **establishes a relation of cause.** The face frowned *because of* the blind man's coming.

Stating the cause openly makes the sentence heavier. Had it said "he frowned, and at that moment a blind man came", the events would have stood side by side. When it says "he frowned because he came", the reaction is bound to its cause.

**A note on the variant reading.** It is transmitted that in some variant readings the head of the verse is read with the interrogative *hamza*: **أَأَنْ جَاءَهُ** — "Because the blind man came?!" On this reading the sentence turns into a question and the tone of rebuke rises. Since I am not certain which of the imāms it belongs to, I give no name; the existence of the reading is recorded in the sources.

**The direction of the verb.** *jāʾa-hu* — "he came **to him**". The movement runs from the blind man towards the Prophet. In the eighth verse this will be repeated and widened: *"the one who came to you running"*. So the side that came is clear; the sūra records it twice. The other side — the leading men — did not come; they were **gone to** (*taṣaddā*). The difference of direction is part of the sūra's argument.

### ٱلْأَعْمَىٰ — the blind man

**Root: ع-م-ي.** The absence of the faculty of sight. *aʿmā* — an adjectival pattern resembling the elative (*afʿal*), the pattern of adjectives denoting colours and defects.

**The man is named not by his name but by his defect.** At first sight this looks harsh. But it must be remembered that the sūra names no one by name: neither the blind man, nor the leading men, nor the one who frowned. The sūra does not give a name, it gives **a position**.

And the position given is exactly the knot of the matter. Why could the man be written off? Because he was blind? No — **because he was blind he was easy to neglect.** He could not see; that is, he would not notice that he was being neglected. He was poor, he had no men behind him, he would add nothing to the weight of the gathering.

So the word "al-aʿmā" is not a label but **an explanation**: the answer to the question why this man was counted as skippable.

**The definite article (al-).** Not "a blind man" but "the blind man". A person the addressees knew, a known figure. At the same time it denotes the class: whoever stands in this position.

### The Qurʾān's exegesis by the Qurʾān: which blindness?

The Qurʾān uses this root on two levels and openly binds the two together:

> *"The truth is that eyes do not go blind; what goes blind is the hearts within the breasts."* (al-Ḥajj 22/46)

The verse takes the real seat of blindness away from the eye and carries it to the heart. Read together with this verse, the scene in ʿAbasa doubles:

- A man whose eyes do not see comes **in order to see**.
- A gathering whose eyes do see insists on not seeing.

The sūra does not say this; I am the one drawing it, and I record this as my own reading. But the Qurʾān's way of using this root supports the reading: *"They have eyes with which they do not see"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/179).

### Historical background: how a gathering is built

To understand the weight of the verse one must know what that gathering was.

In Mecca the delivery of the message ran not through an institutional structure but through **personal relations and the weight of tribes**. A tribal chief's acceptance of Islam was not the joining of a single person; it meant the change of stance of a whole group behind him, the coming under protection of the Muslims belonging to that group, the stopping of torture.

So the Prophet's calculation in that gathering was **not a personal preference but a strategic calculation** — and a reasonable one. The greatest obstacle in front of a cause is removed by winning over the strongest opponents of that cause. This is the calculation every movement, every campaign, every institution makes today as well.

This is where the sūra shakes: **what is criticised is not a wickedness but a reasonable strategy.** The verse does not say "you were selfish"; it says "at the point where you supposed you were calculating correctly, you lost the measure." And the way it shows this will be the question in the next verse: *what do you know?*

This takes the sūra out of being a lesson in morals and makes it **a discussion about measure**. The question is not intention but how it is reckoned who is how important.

## 80/3-4 — وَمَا يُدْرِيكَ لَعَلَّهُۥ يَزَّكَّىٰٓ ۝ أَوْ يَذَّكَّرُ فَتَنفَعَهُ ٱلذِّكْرَىٰ

*Wa mā yudrīka laʿallahu yazzakkā ۝ Aw yadhdhakkaru fa-tanfaʿahu'dh-dhikrā*
"What do you know — perhaps he will purify himself; or he will take the reminder, and the reminder will profit him."

### The question that has no answer

The whole force of this verse lies in **its answer not being given**.

In the section on al-Humaza the difference between two patterns in the Qurʾān was treated, and it comes before us here:

- **مَآ أَدْرَىٰكَ** (*mā adrāka* — "what has made you know") — an explanation follows it. *"And what has made you know what the Night of Decree is?"* and a three-verse answer.
- **مَا يُدْرِيكَ** (*mā yudrīka* — "what makes you know") — an explanation **does not** follow it. *"What makes you know, perhaps the Hour is near"* (al-Shūrā 42/17).

ʿAbasa 80/3 is the second. And this choice is not chance; it is the sūra's argument itself.

Because the answer to the question **cannot be given**. The question is: how do you know whether this man will purify himself or not? The answer: you cannot know. No one can know. **This knowledge is not knowledge that lies in human hands.**

And precisely for this reason the ranking in the gathering collapses. The Prophet had made a reasonable calculation: if the leading men are won, much will change; this man is a Muslim already, he can wait. The hidden assumption of the calculation was this: **we can predict who will bring what.**

The verse takes aim at exactly this assumption. You cannot predict. And if you cannot predict, your ranking, which rests on prediction, is invalid.

**The tense says this too.** *yudrī* is imperfect (*muḍāriʿ*): "it does not make known, it cannot make known". Not a thing that went unknown in the past; a thing that **cannot be known**.

### دِرَايَة — which knowledge?

**Root: د-ر-ي.** The lexicographers make a distinction between *dirāya* and *ʿilm*: *dirāya* is knowledge arrived at by setting out from marks, traces and signs — a kind of inference. *ʿilm* is more general.

The verse's choice of this root is meaningful: the person addressed had drawn a judgement from signs visible from outside. The man's clothing, his standing, the number of men behind him, his contribution to the gathering. These are real signs; they can be read. The verse says that **the signs are not enough** — *dirāya* has a limit.

### يَزَّكَّىٰ — he will purify himself

**Its original form** is *yatazakkā*; by the assimilation (*idghām*) of the *tāʾ* into the *zāy* it has become *yazzakkā*. **Form V**: to do a thing of oneself, by one's own effort.

**Root: ز-ك-و.** This root was treated in detail at al-Shams 91/9 (*qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā*); the finding there holds here too, I do not repeat it here. In summary: **two senses stand together** beneath the root — purification and growth. *tazkiya* is to make grow by taking away.

The real point here is this: **Sūrat al-Shams had described deliverance with exactly this word.** *"He who purified it has prospered."* And now ʿAbasa says of the man who came to the door: *perhaps he will purify himself.*

So the man who came, came in pursuit of the very thing al-Shams described as deliverance. Those in the gathering, on the other hand — as will be seen in the next verse — counted themselves **self-sufficient**; the state that al-Layl 92/8 binds to miserliness and to denial.

**The two men of the two sūras stand in the same room in this sūra.** This is a real link established between sūras; it is not forced — it arises from the repetition of the same words in the same positions.

**And this fine point:** the word *laʿalla* (perhaps) states a possibility, not a certainty. The verse does not say "he will purify himself"; it says "perhaps he will purify himself". So the verse does not claim either that the blind man will certainly gain. **What is claimed is the unknowability of the outcome.** And unknowability is enough to make the ranking invalid.

If this distinction is missed the sūra is read wrongly. The sūra does not say "prefer the poor man, leave the rich one" — that would be a repetition of the same mistake in the opposite direction. The sūra says **not to appraise anyone in advance**.

### يَذَّكَّرُ فَتَنفَعَهُ ٱلذِّكْرَىٰ — he will take the reminder and the reminder will profit him

**Root: ذ-ك-ر.** To remember, to mention, to call to mind. *dhikr* — mention, reminder; one of the Qurʾān's own names. *dhikrā* — a reminding, a lesson. *tadhkira* — a thing that reminds.

**This root is the sūra's second backbone.** It occurs four times between verses 4 and 12: *yadhdhakkaru*, *al-dhikrā*, *tadhkira*, *dhakara-hu*. At the centre of the sūra stands a verb of reminding.

A note on the concrete sense of the root: some of the lexicographers establish a link between *ذَكَر* (male) and *ذِكْر* (mention), and also with uses carrying the sense of "sharpness" (*sayfun dhakar* — a sharp sword). This derivation is disputed; I transmit it with a note of caution.

**The difference between "he will take the reminder" and "he will purify himself."** The two verbs come one after the other, and they are not the same thing:

| | **يَزَّكَّىٰ** | **يَذَّكَّرُ** |
|---|---|---|
| What happens | A change of state | A quickening of knowledge |
| Where | Character, conduct, the inner world | The mind, the memory |
| Its difficulty | High — a transformation | Low — a remembering |

The verse states the high one first, then lowers the threshold with *aw* (or): **even if there is no full transformation, there may be a remembering.** And that remembering may profit.

This is the same technique as Sūrat al-Māʿūn's — lowering the threshold step by step. There not even "feeding" was asked for, but "urging". Here "purifying oneself" is not laid down as a condition; even "remembering" is counted as enough. And even after the return the man who came might bring has been reduced to a possibility this small, the preference still comes out on his side.

**A grammatical nicety: فَتَنفَعَهُ.** The verb is read in the subjunctive (*manṣūb*). The reason is that the *fāʾ* at its head is **causal** (*sababiyya*): it states a result depending on the hope built with *laʿalla*. That is, "he remembers and *thereby* profits". The *fāʾ* does not simply put the two verbs in sequence; it makes the second the product of the first.

**النَّفْع — profit.** Root *n-f-ʿ*. The verse's ascribing the profit to the blind man is itself a displacement. The calculation in the gathering was the calculation "who brings us profit". The verse turns the verb round: "it profits him". The measure ceases to be the gain of the cause and becomes the man's own gain.

This is the sūra's quietest but most radical intervention: **a human being is counted not as a means but as a gain in himself.**

## 80/5-7 — أَمَّا مَنِ ٱسْتَغْنَىٰ ۝ فَأَنتَ لَهُۥ تَصَدَّىٰ ۝ وَمَا عَلَيْكَ أَلَّا يَزَّكَّىٰ

*Ammā mani'staghnā ۝ Fa-anta lahu taṣaddā ۝ Wa mā ʿalayka allā yazzakkā*
"As for the one who counts himself self-sufficient — you turn towards him. Yet his not purifying himself is not your responsibility."

### Word order: a division into two arms

Verses 5-10 are built on Arabic's best-known pattern of division: **أَمَّا ... فَـ ... وَأَمَّا ... فَـ**

The function of *ammā* is twofold: it both separates the subject (*tafṣīl*) and adds emphasis. The lexicographers expound *ammā* as "mahmā yakun min shayʾin" (whatever the case may be); that is, "as for such a person, whatever the case may be, the situation is this". The *fāʾ* of the pattern is obligatory, and it glues the answer on.

When the two arms are set side by side, the symmetry comes out thus:

| | **The first arm (5-7)** | **The second arm (8-10)** |
|---|---|---|
| The person | *mani'staghnā* — the one who counts himself self-sufficient | *man jāʾaka yasʿā* — the one who came to you running |
| His state | (not stated) | *wa huwa yakhshā* — and he feels *khashya* |
| His place in relation to you | *lahu* — **towards him** | *ʿanhu* — **away from him** |
| The verb | *taṣaddā* — you turn towards | *talahhā* — you busy yourself elsewhere |

**The contrast is gathered into two letters: لَـ and عَنْ.**

- **لِـ** denotes turning towards, arrival, assignment.
- **عَنْ** denotes parting, moving away, leaving behind (*mujāwaza*).

The same logic of letters was dwelt on at al-Māʿūn 107/5: the difference between *ʿan ṣalātihim* (away from their prayer) and *ʿalā ṣalātihim* (upon their prayer). Here too the whole matter rests on two letters. **The main load of the sentences is not on the verbs but on the letters.**

One more nicety of word order: in both answers *anta* (you) is written out separately. In Arabic the verb already carries its subject; *taṣaddā* by itself means "you turn towards". The addition of the separate pronoun is for **assignment and reinforcement**: "it is you, precisely you". The same work as that done by the pronoun *hum* in al-Māʿūn. The act is nailed to its doer.

### ٱسْتَغْنَىٰ — to count oneself self-sufficient

**Root: غ-ن-ي.** This word was treated in detail at al-Layl 92/8 and al-ʿAlaq 96/7; the findings there hold here too, I do not repeat them here. In summary: Form X here carries the function of "counting/seeing" — the word does not say "he became rich", it says **"he did not see himself as needy"**. This is not a condition of wealth but a condition of **conviction**.

In al-ʿAlaq this conviction was bound to *ṭughyān* (overstepping the bound), in al-Layl to *bukhl* and *takdhīb*. **ʿAbasa binds it to a third thing: to receiving attention.**

And this is the most uncomfortable of the three. For what draws the attention is not the man himself; it is **the weight created** by the man's counting himself self-sufficient. A person who looks as though he needs no one appears as though everyone needs him. *Istighnāʾ* gains its owner a power of attraction.

The sūra's diagnosis lies exactly here: **the supposal of needing nothing does not corrupt only the one who holds it; it corrupts the measure of everyone around him too.** Because the man does not see himself as needy, the others count him valuable.

This mechanism can be named today as well: standing is most often gained by not appearing needy. And not appearing needy is not the same thing as not being needy.

**There is one further place in the sūra where this root occurs, and it will be tied back to here:** in the thirty-seventh verse it will be said that *"each of them will have a concern of his own that suffices him (يُغْنِيهِ)"*. The same root. This turn will be come back to under the sūra as a whole.

### تَصَدَّىٰ — to turn towards, to place oneself opposite

Its original form is *tataṣaddā*; Form V, with one *tāʾ* dropped. It is also transmitted that in some variant readings it is read **تَصَّدَّىٰ**, with the assimilation of the *tāʾ* into the *ṣād*; the sense does not change, the reinforcement increases.

**Root: ص-د-د / صدي.** The lexicographers relate the word to *صَدَد* (the side of a thing that faces you, its front): *"dārī ṣadada dārihi"* — my house stands opposite his house. *taṣaddā li-shayʾ* — to place oneself opposite a thing, to turn towards it, to concern oneself with it, to be occupied with it.

The picture in the word is this: **to place oneself opposite someone and give him one's face.** That is, the exact opposite of the *tawallā* of the first verse (the turning of the back). In one verse the back, six verses later the face. The sūra uses the same movement of the body twice, in two different directions.

And **the verb is imperfect**: continuity, repetition. Not a one-off courtesy; a turning-towards that has become a habit. What the sūra criticises is not a moment but a tendency.

### وَمَا عَلَيْكَ أَلَّا يَزَّكَّىٰ — "his not purifying himself is not upon you"

This verse is the real blow of the first arm, and it can be read in two ways.

| Reading | Its meaning | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **"There is no responsibility on you"** | His not purifying himself is not written to your account; you delivered the message, the rest is not your affair | The widespread reading. It agrees with other places in the Qurʾān: *"Upon you is only the delivering of the message"* (al-Shūrā 42/48); *"You are not one who compels them"* (al-Ghāshiya 88/22) |
| **"What is it to you?"** — a reproach | What is his not purifying himself to you? Why do you labour over it so much? | The pattern carries this sense in Arabic as well; it agrees with the context |

The two readings do not exclude one another, and both arrive at the same place: **producing the outcome is not your affair.**

And this shows the source of the mistake in the sūra's opening verses. The mistake arose not from ill intent but from **taking ownership of the outcome**. The moment a person counts himself responsible for the outcome, he begins to rank people according to their contribution to it. The ranking becomes unavoidable; for the time in his hands is limited, and he will want to put it where it is most productive.

The verse cuts the chain at its root: **the outcome is not yours.** If the outcome is not yours, there is no calculation of productivity either. If there is no calculation of productivity, there is no ranking either.

This is a word said about the delivery of the message, but it settles into a far wider place. A teacher, a physician, an administrator — anyone whose work deals with people — begins to sift people the moment he starts writing the outcome to his own account. And the first victim of the sifting is always the person **whose return is uncertain**.

## 80/8-10 — وَأَمَّا مَن جَآءَكَ يَسْعَىٰ ۝ وَهُوَ يَخْشَىٰ ۝ فَأَنتَ عَنْهُ تَلَهَّىٰ

*Wa ammā man jāʾaka yasʿā ۝ Wa huwa yakhshā ۝ Fa-anta ʿanhu talahhā*
"As for the one who came to you running — and he is in *khashya* — you turn from him and busy yourself elsewhere."

### The man's three qualities

In these three verses three things are said about the blind man, and all three are **his own acts**:

1. **جَآءَكَ** — he came. No one brought him; he came himself.
2. **يَسْعَىٰ** — running. Not walking.
3. **يَخْشَىٰ** — feeling *khashya*. His inner state.

So the sūra introduces the man not as someone to be pitied but as **someone in motion**. His blindness was mentioned only in the first verse, as the reason for his being neglected; here it does not occur even once. The man is no longer "the blind man"; he is the one **who comes, who runs, who fears**.

This is the most elegant thing the verse does. The answer to defining a person by his defect is to define him by his acts.

### يَسْعَىٰ — to run

**Root: س-ع-ي.** To walk fast, to run; and from there: to strive, to exert oneself, to labour at a task. *Saʿy* — effort; the *saʿy* between Ṣafā and Marwa is this same word.

The uses in the Qurʾān show the two faces of the root:
- Physical running: *"And from the far end of the city a man came running"* (Yā Sīn 36/20).
- Moral effort: *"Man shall have only what he strove for (mā saʿā)"* (al-Najm 53/39).
- And as a command: *"When the call to prayer is made on the day of congregation, hasten to the remembrance of God"* (al-Jumuʿa 62/9).

**The verb stands as a circumstantial qualifier** (it states how the act of *jāʾaka* took place): "he came in a state of running".

**And this picture must not be missed: the man who sees is standing still, the man who does not see is running.** For someone who cannot see, running means taking a risk on ground he does not know. Running is many times harder for a blind man than for a sighted one, and it is by itself a declaration of wanting.

The verse does not say this; I see it, and I record this as my own reading. But the choice of the word sets up the picture.

### وَهُوَ يَخْشَىٰ — and he feels *khashya*

**Root: خ-ش-ي.** The difference between *khashya* and *khawf* was treated in a table at al-Bayyina 98/8; I do not repeat it here. In summary: **khawf comes from the harm itself, khashya from knowing the greatness of what is feared.** Fāṭir 35/28 binds *khashya* to knowledge openly: *"Among His servants it is only those who know who feel khashya of God."*

The consequence of this here is heavy. **Sūrat al-Bayyina, after its description of the garden, said this at the very end: *"That is for the one who feels khashya of his Lord."*** That is, the highest recompense was bound to precisely this attribute.

And ʿAbasa introduces the man kept waiting at the door with this very attribute.

Nothing further need be said to show how far the ranking in the gathering is turned upside down. By the Qurʾān's own measure the weightiest person in the room was the man standing at the door.

**The structure of the sentence carries this too.** *Wa huwa yakhshā* is a **circumstantial clause** (*ḥāl*); it is built as a nominal sentence (*huwa* + verb). In Arabic the nominal sentence denotes fixity and continuity. That is, not "he feared at that moment"; **khashya is his state.**

### فَأَنتَ عَنْهُ تَلَهَّىٰ — you busy yourself away from him

**Root: ل-ه-و.** This root was treated in detail at al-Takāthur 102/1 (*alhākumu't-takāthur*); I do not repeat it here. In summary: *lahw* is something other than what the English word "amusement" carries — **an occupation that holds a person back from his real business, that takes his attention from there and fastens it elsewhere.** At the centre of the word lies not cheerfulness but **displacement**.

**But here the pattern is different, and the difference matters.**

| | **al-Takāthur 102/1** | **ʿAbasa 80/10** |
|---|---|---|
| The verb | **أَلْهَىٰ** (*alhā*) | **تَلَهَّىٰ** (*talahhā*) |
| Form | Form IV — transitive | Form V — reflexive |
| Its meaning | "It distracted" — something distracted you | "He busied himself" — you distracted yourself |
| The human being's position | **Passive**: the one distracted | **Active**: the one who takes the distraction upon himself |

In al-Takāthur the human being was the victim of something: the race for more had distracted him. Here there is no such excuse. Form V denotes the subject's taking the act upon himself — the person is distracting himself.

**And one more thing:** one of the functions of Form V is **takalluf**, that is, doing a thing with effort. On this reading the verse grows heavier still: being distracted is here not something that happens of itself but something that is kept up.

**The sūra sets these two verbs — تَصَدَّىٰ and تَلَهَّىٰ — side by side in the same form, the same pattern, the same rhyme.** Both are Form V, both end with the sound *-addā / -ahhā*. The ear hears them as two directions of the same movement. And that is exactly what they are: to turn in one direction is to turn from the other.

### What this says to the present

These six verses are a description of how people rank one another, and the description has not grown old.

**One. The ranking is done in secret.** Nobody says "I find you unimportant". The ranking is done by the distribution of attention: how many minutes are given to whom, how long it takes for whose message to be answered, whose sentence can be cut short. What the sūra criticises is not a sentence but **an expression on a face and a turning**. The measure is that fine.

**Two. The ground of the ranking is always reasonable.** "My time is limited, I must put it where it will bring the most benefit." This sentence is true, and it is uttered every day. The verse breaks not the sentence but **the sentence's hidden assumption**: you do not know who will bring what.

**Three. Attention flows towards the one who does not appear needy.** *Istighnāʾ* gains its owner attraction. And the reverse is true too: the person who states his need openly is devalued for precisely that reason. The one who knocks at the door is taken less seriously than the one who does not. The sūra has given this perversity its name.

**Four. Taking ownership of the outcome leads to sifting.** This is the vein the seventh verse cuts. Whoever writes the outcome of a work to his own account is compelled to separate people according to their contribution to it. To know the limit of responsibility is not a comfort but **a condition of justice.**

## 80/11-12 — كَلَّآ إِنَّهَا تَذْكِرَةٌ ۝ فَمَن شَآءَ ذَكَرَهُۥ

*Kallā innahā tadhkira ۝ Fa-man shāʾa dhakarah*
"No! It is a reminder. So whoever wills remembers it."

### كَلَّا — the cut

How *kallā* works was treated in the sections on al-Takāthur and al-ʿAlaq; I do not repeat it here. In summary: *kallā* is a "no" said most often not to a sentence but **to a piece of conduct**; it cuts off a claim that has been made not in words but in action.

The claim cut off here is this: **this business runs according to who is listening.** That was the hidden assumption of the logic of the gathering. *Kallā* rejects the assumption, and after it gives the ground of the rejection: this is a reminder.

*Kallā* is at the same time the sūra's **first hinge**. The incident ends, the subject changes. In the twenty-third verse a second *kallā* will come, and there too the subject will change. The skeleton of the sūra is divided into three parts by these two words.

### تَذْكِرَة — a reminder

**The pattern.** A verbal noun / noun on the *tafʿila* pattern: the thing that reminds, the means of reminding. The Qurʾān uses this word of itself in several places (al-Muzzammil 73/19; al-Muddaththir 74/54; al-Ḥāqqa 69/48).

**What does the pronoun refer back to?** *Innahā* — a feminine pronoun. The views: (a) to the verses / to the sūra; (b) to the Qurʾān (because *al-sūra* or *al-āyāt* is feminine); (c) directly to "this admonition". There is no difference in respect of meaning; all of them point to the text itself.

**What does the choice of the word say?**

*Tadhkira* is not "instruction", not "informing", but **reminding**. And reminding is the waking of something already present in the one addressed. To one who does not know, knowledge is given; to one who knows, a reminder.

This is one of the ways the Qurʾān defines itself, and it has consequences: if the text is a reminder, then there is something inside the one addressed that answers to it. At al-Shams 91/8 it was said, *"and He inspired it with its fujūr and its taqwā"*; the inspiration there and the reminding here look to the same place.

**And its function in the context:** reminding depends not on the quality of the one who reminds but **on the readiness of the one who remembers**. The weight of a gathering reminds no one of anything. For this reason who is listening does not change how the text works.

### فَمَن شَآءَ ذَكَرَهُۥ — whoever wills remembers

**A grammatical nicety.** In the preceding verse the pronoun was feminine (*innahā*), here it is masculine (*dhakarahu*). The reason: the pronoun now refers back not to *tadhkira* but to **dhikr / the Qurʾān** (masculine); or else the word *tadhkira* is taken in the sense of "admonition, counsel" (*al-waʿẓ*, masculine). A small detail, but it shows the scrupulousness of Arabic about pronouns.

**مَن شَآءَ — whoever wills.** One of the sūra's most loaded expressions, and it says something that needs saying once more: **the door is open; whoever passes through, passes through.**

The sharpness of this in the context needs to be seen. The Prophet was occupied with forcing a door — the door of the leading men. The verse says: forcing the door is not your affair. Whoever wills, enters.

And the exact counterpart of this is found at **al-Takwīr 81/28-29**: *"For whoever among you wills to go straight"* and immediately after it: *"And you do not will unless God, the Lord of all the worlds, wills."* The two sūras take the same two steps: first man's willing, then the place on which the willing rests. ʿAbasa stops at the first step; al-Takwīr adds the second. The two should be read together — this link will be treated separately in the section on al-Takwīr.

**A caution.** If this verse is read as "whoever wants to, believes; whoever does not want to, does not; and that is all there is to it", the meaning of delivering the message is emptied out. The sūra itself does not permit this: for forty-two verses it keeps up the reminding, repeats the call, sets out the evidence. So the sentence "whoever wills remembers" does not lift the call; **it lifts the outcome of the call off the back of the one who makes it.** A continuation of what the seventh verse said.

## 80/13-16 — فِى صُحُفٍ مُّكَرَّمَةٍ ۝ مَّرْفُوعَةٍ مُّطَهَّرَةٍ ۝ بِأَيْدِى سَفَرَةٍ ۝ كِرَامٍۭ بَرَرَةٍ

*Fī ṣuḥufin mukarrama ۝ Marfūʿatin muṭahhara ۝ Bi-aydī safara ۝ Kirāmin barara*
"It is on pages made honoured; raised high, purified; in the hands of scribe-envoys — noble ones, given to goodness."

### Word order: what does it attach to?

*Fī ṣuḥufin* — "on pages". This phrase is an adjective or a circumstantial qualifier of the *tadhkira* of the eleventh verse: "it is a reminder, a reminder **that is on pages**". The majority of the classical grammarians run in this direction.

What draws notice in the word order is **the arrangement of the adjectives**: *mukarrama, marfūʿa, muṭahhara* — all three passive participles, all three **passive**, all three ending on the same rhyme. Then the pattern changes: *safara, kirām, barara* — these three are **active** formations (active participles, or the assimilate adjective).

So half the sentence relates the work done and half relates those who do it:

| | Who/what | Pattern | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| The pages | *mukarrama, marfūʿa, muṭahhara* | Passive participle | **Passive** — done to them |
| The bearers | *safara, kirām, barara* | Active participle / adjective | **Active** — what they themselves are |

The pages do not earn their own value, do not raise themselves, do not cleanse themselves. The bearers, on the other hand, **are** noble and good. This distinction was treated in the section on al-Bayyina 98/2; the finding there holds here too.

### صُحُف and مُطَهَّرَة — a comparison with al-Bayyina

These two words were treated in detail at al-Bayyina 98/2 (*ṣuḥufan muṭahhara*) and were compared there with these verses; I do not repeat it here. Briefly: the root **ص-ح-ف** is a flat surface written upon; *muṣḥaf*, the written codex, is from the same root. **ط-ه-ر** is cleanness; *muṭahhara* is a passive participle — not clean, but **cleansed**.

**The difference here, and the addition.** There is a real difference of word order between the two places, and it is worth recording:

| | **al-Bayyina 98/2** | **ʿAbasa 80/13-16** |
|---|---|---|
| The expression | *ṣuḥufan muṭahhara* | *ṣuḥufin mukarramatin marfūʿatin muṭahhara* |
| Definiteness | Indefinite, with tanwīn | Indefinite, but qualified by three adjectives |
| Number of adjectives | One | Three |
| Is a bearer mentioned | No — *an envoy who recites* is mentioned | Yes — *the hands of the scribes* |
| Context | What the evidence is | Where the text comes from |

In al-Bayyina the pages were **being recited**; the picture there was of an envoy reciting the text in front of people. In ʿAbasa the pages are **being held in the hand**; the picture here is the link of the chain before the text reaches man.

So the two sūras show two different ends of the chain: ʿAbasa the upper, al-Bayyina the lower. The same phrase, the same passive adjective, two different places.

**And the added sense *muṭahhara* gains here:** in the section on al-Bayyina it was recorded that the word does not say what the pages were purified of, and five explanations were given. The context of ʿAbasa supports the fifth explanation (raised high, held honoured), because the word here stands in the same list as *mukarrama* and *marfūʿa*. The company of the adjectives suggests that cleanness here is meant **in the sense of value and inviolability rather than of legal or bodily cleanness**. This is an inference; it is not binding.

### مُكَرَّمَة and مَرْفُوعَة

**مُكَرَّمَة — root ك-ر-م.** *Karam*: generosity, nobility, worth. *Karīm* — both generous and precious. *Ikrām* — the giving of honour.

A passive participle, from Form II (*tafʿīl*): **"made honoured".** Not precious of itself; its value has been given to it.

**And the same root comes again in the sixteenth verse: كِرَام.** The pages are *mukarrama* (made honoured, passive), the bearers are *kirām* (those who are honoured, active). The same root, two patterns, two positions. This is the tightest weave inside the four-verse passage.

**مَرْفُوعَة — root ر-ف-ع.** To raise, to lift. *Rifʿa* — height; *rafīʿ* — exalted.

Two readings: **(a) high in respect of place** — kept where men cannot reach it; **(b) exalted in respect of rank** — raised in worth. The Qurʾān has uses that support both: *"In houses which God has permitted to be raised"* (al-Nūr 24/36) — there *turfaʿa* is for rank.

The two do not exclude one another. And there is a logic of word order here: **mukarrama** (worth), **marfūʿa** (unreachability), **muṭahhara** (uncorruptedness). The three answer three separate questions that can be asked about a text: is it valuable, is it protected, has it been corrupted.

### سَفَرَة — the sūra's hidden root

The sūra's most loaded word is here, and it carries the whole of the sūra.

**Root: س-ف-ر.** Beneath this root there is a single idea: **to lift the covering, to bring to light.** All the derivatives come out of this centre, and their spread is astonishing:

- **سَفَرَ** — he uncovered, he lifted the covering. *Safarati'l-marʾatu ʿan wajhihā* — the woman uncovered her face. *Asfara'ṣ-ṣubḥu* — the morning broke, the light came out.
- **سَفَر** — **a journey.** The explanation the lexicographers transmit is this: travel was given this name because it **brings to light** people's characters. On the road no one can play a part; fatigue, hunger and necessity pour out a person's real condition.
- **سِفْر** (plural *asfār*) — a large book, a volume. Because it brings to light what is inside it. It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"Like the case of a donkey carrying books"* (al-Jumuʿa 62/5).
- **سَفِير** — an envoy who goes back and forth between two sides, a mediator. It is the word used today for an ambassador. Because he brings to light the matter standing between the two sides and clears the space between them.
- **سُفْرَة** — provisions for the road and the cloth they are laid on; hence, in Turkish, *sofra* — the cloth spread for a meal, and so the table itself.
- **مِسْفَرَة** — a broom. What opens the ground, what lifts off what lies on it.

**سَفَرَة** is the plural of *sāfir*, and within this network of the root it carries two senses at once: **scribes** (those who bring the text to light) and **envoys** (those who go back and forth between two sides).

And revelation is exactly these two things: something carried between two sides and brought to light. **The word is a description of the work it does.**

### The root that turns inside the sūra

Now look at the thirty-eighth verse: **وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ مُّسْفِرَةٌ** — "some faces on that day are *musfira*."

The same root. *Musfira*: a face uncovered, lit up, made to shine — like the breaking of the morning.

So the sūra uses this root **twice, at the two ends**:

| | 80/15 | 80/38 |
|---|---|---|
| The word | **سَفَرَة** | **مُسْفِرَة** |
| Who/what | Those who bear the text | The faces on that day |
| Its meaning | Those who bring to light | Those that have been brought to light |

The text comes to light in the hands of the *safara*; the faces of those who receive it become *musfira*. **The same act, once above and once below.**

This also explains why one of the names of the sūra is *al-Safara*, and it will be returned to under the sūra as a whole.

### Who are the سَفَرَة? — the disagreement

| View | Ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **The angels** | The expression *bi-aydī* (in their hands) points to a physical carrying; the qualities *kirām barara* are close to the expressions used of the angels in the Qurʾān (this verse of ʿAbasa may be compared with: *"noble scribes"*, al-Infiṭār 82/11). The context of the sūra relates the channel by which the revelation comes | The view of the majority; the context supports it |
| **The people who write and recite the Qurʾān** — scribes, memorisers, scholars | The "scribe" sense of *safara* is more natural for human beings; the qualities *kirām barara* are used of human beings too | A minority; but possible in respect of the language |
| **Both at once** | The word has been left undefined | It looks like an evasion, but it can be defended |

The majority is in the first view, and the context supports it too: the passage relates the **source** of the text, not its recipient. But the second view should not be excluded; the word itself is open to both.

### كِرَامٍ بَرَرَةٍ — noble, given to goodness

**كِرَام** — the plural of *karīm*; treated above.

**بَرَرَة — root ب-ر-ر.** The concrete centre of this root is **breadth**: *al-barr* — dry land (the opposite of the sea), open and wide ground. From here:

- **بِرّ (birr)** — comprehensive goodness, the broad reach of the good. Al-Baqara 2/177 defines *birr*, and the definition is kept wide: the articles of faith + giving of wealth + prayer + alms + keeping one's word + patience. This verse was dwelt on in the section on al-Māʿūn.
- **بَارّ / بَرّ** — one who does good, one who keeps faith. Their plurals: *barara* and *abrār*.
- **بِرّ الوالدين** — goodness towards one's parents.

*Barara* is on the **فَعَلَة** pattern. In Arabic this pattern is used for the plural of adjectives denoting a trade and a continuity (*kātib → kataba*, *sāḥir → saḥara*). That is, not "those who did a good deed once", but those who have made goodness their state.

**And now look at the last verse of the sūra:** *أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْكَفَرَةُ ٱلْفَجَرَةُ* — "those are the deniers, the transgressors."

**كَفَرَة** and **فَجَرَة** — the same pattern. The exact opposite of **بَرَرَة**, in the same form, on the same rhyme.

| | 80/16 | 80/42 |
|---|---|---|
| The expression | **كِرَامٍ بَرَرَةٍ** | **ٱلْكَفَرَةُ ٱلْفَجَرَةُ** |
| Pattern | *fiʿāl* + *faʿala* | *faʿala* + *faʿala* |
| Who | Those who bear the text above | Those who cover the text below |
| Rhyme | *-arah* | *-arah* |

**With the same sound and the same pattern, the sūra sets the highest and the lowest face to face.** This is a symmetry that stands in the text's own weave; it is not forced.

And the Qurʾān sets this pair openly side by side in another place: *"The good (al-abrār) are in bliss; and the transgressors are in hellfire"* (al-Infiṭār 82/13-14). The same two roots, the same opposition.

## 80/17 — قُتِلَ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ مَآ أَكْفَرَهُۥ

*Qutila'l-insānu mā akfarah*
"Perish man — how ungrateful he is!"

### قُتِلَ — the pattern of imprecation

One of the Qurʾān's heaviest expressions.

How this pattern works was treated in the section on Tabbat 111/1; I do not repeat it here. The finding there was this: **in form an imprecation, in content a verdict.** The sentence looks as though it frames a wish, but in fact it states something that has happened.

**The word itself.** *Qutila* — the passive of *qatala*: "he was killed". In Arabic this is a settled pattern of imprecation, answering to English expressions such as "damn him" or "may he drop dead". The lexicographers' explanation is this: since death is the last thing that can befall a man, it has been used as the furthest point of imprecation.

**Its other uses in the Qurʾān** show what kinds of situation the word is reserved for:

- *"Perish the liars"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/10) — *qutila'l-kharrāṣūn*.
- *"Perish the people of the trench"* (al-Burūj 85/4) — those who burned believers in a pit of fire.

In all three places the same pattern, and in all three an extremely heavy accusation. Here the one addressed is not a group; it is **al-insān** — man as a species.

**Why is this so harsh?** The context gives the answer. The four preceding verses related from what heights the text comes: honoured pages, raised high, purified, noble bearers. And now it turns to the one that text is addressed to. **What creates the harshness is distance:** the distance between the array above and the response below.

### ٱلْإِنسَٰن — man

**The definite article denotes the class:** not a particular person but man as a species. Here the sūra comes out of the single incident of the first ten verses and passes over to the general.

And this transition needs to be seen: the criticism is over. The one addressed has changed. In the rest of the sūra that incident will not be returned to. This is the last step of the architecture of courtesy recorded in the first section — **the one addressed is released.**

### مَآ أَكْفَرَهُۥ — three readings

| Reading | How it is understood | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **The pattern of wonder (*taʿajjub*)** | The *mā afʿalahu* pattern: "how ungrateful he is!" In Arabic wonder is framed with this pattern (*mā aḥsanahu* — how beautiful he is!) | The view of the majority. The pattern is directly this one |
| **A question (*istifhām*)** | "What made him a denier?" — what cause brought him to this state | Possible; there is a harmony of sound with the question *min ayyi shayʾin khalaqah* that follows |
| **Negation (*nafy*)** | *Mā* as the particle of negation: "it did not make him a denier" | Weak; it does not agree with the context and it strains the pattern |

The first reading is the strongest. But the second has a beauty of its own: read as a question, it forms a series of questions with the *"out of what did He create him?"* that comes immediately after, and an answer is given: out of a drop. That is, the cause of the ingratitude is asked for, and the material man came out of is shown as the answer.

### كُفْر — to cover

**Root: ك-ف-ر.** This root was treated in the section on al-Kāfirūn 109; I do not repeat it here. In summary: the concrete sense of the root is **to cover, to close over**. *Kufrān al-niʿma* — to cover over the favour that has been given.

**The pattern here is Form IV (*afʿala*)**, and read as the pattern of wonder it means "how much he covers".

**And in the weave of the sūra this root stands at the two ends:** *mā akfarah* in the seventeenth verse, *al-kafaratu* in the forty-second. The twenty-four verses that pass in between lay out for man an inventory of the things he covers over: his creation, his measure, his path, his death, his grave, his resurrection, his water, his soil, his grain, his grapes, his olive, his dates.

**So the sūra opens an accusation, sets out the evidence, then delivers the verdict.** The seventeenth verse is the indictment, 18-32 the evidence, the forty-second verse the decision. I shall gather this structure together again under the sūra as a whole.

## 80/18-20 — مِنْ أَىِّ شَىْءٍ خَلَقَهُۥ ۝ مِن نُّطْفَةٍ خَلَقَهُۥ فَقَدَّرَهُۥ ۝ ثُمَّ ٱلسَّبِيلَ يَسَّرَهُۥ

*Min ayyi shayʾin khalaqah ۝ Min nuṭfatin khalaqahu fa-qaddarah ۝ Thumma's-sabīla yassarah*
"Out of what did He create him? Out of a drop He created him and gave him measure. Then He made the path easy for him."

### A question asked and answered at once

In the third verse a question was asked and left **unanswered**. Here a question is asked and answered **at once**. The difference between the two questions shows the sūra's stance on knowledge:

- *"Perhaps he will purify himself — what do you know?"* → **No answer.** Because this is knowledge man cannot reach.
- *"Out of what did He create him?"* → **There is an answer, and in the same verse.** Because this is knowledge everyone has.

The sūra falls silent about what man does not know and speaks about what he does know. And the accusation comes out exactly from here: **man's ingratitude arises not from something he does not know but from his disregarding something he does know.**

**مِنْ أَىِّ شَىْءٍ — "out of what".** The indefiniteness of the word *shayʾ* and the interrogative sense of *ayy* carry here a note of **belittling (*taḥqīr*)**: "out of how insignificant a thing". The tone is close to the dismissive English "out of what, of all things?"

In the section on al-ʿAlaq the recurrence of this pattern in the Qurʾān was recorded: man, when he grows proud, is reminded of the material he came out of. *Al-Ṭāriq 86/5*: *"So let man look at what he was created from."* The same movement.

### نُطْفَة — a drop

**Root: ن-ط-ف.** Its concrete sense is **to drip, to seep**. *Naṭafa'l-māʾu* — the water dripped. *Nuṭfa* — a small quantity of water, a drop. Some of the lexicographers also give the word the sense of "clear water".

**Take note:** the word is not a technical term of reproduction. It means "drop"; and in choosing this word the Qurʾān is stating not a medical description but **a quantity**. The force of the accusation comes from here: out of a thing this small.

**The Qurʾān's sequence of creation.** This word occurs in many places in the Qurʾān as part of a sequence (al-Muʾminūn 23/12-14; al-Ḥajj 22/5); these sequences were gathered in the section on al-ʿAlaq and I do not repeat them here. Sūrat al-ʿAlaq did the same work with a different word: **علق** (that which hangs and clings). The two sūras choose two different stages; the point in common is that the stage chosen is **the least prestigious** one.

### فَقَدَّرَهُۥ — He gave him measure

**Root: ق-د-ر.** To measure, to determine a quantity, to have power over. *Qadr* — quantity, value, measure. *Taqdīr* — determination by measure. *Qadīr* — the one who has power. *Miqdār* — a measure.

**A difference of variant reading.** Two readings are transmitted: **فَقَدَّرَهُ** (with *shadda*) and **فَقَدَرَهُ** (without it). In both the sense comes out as "He determined by measure"; the reading with *shadda* denotes repetition and detail. I give no imām's name.

**What was measured?** In the classical explanations: the place and proportion of the limbs, the term, the sex, the lifespan, the provision, the appointed end. The verse gives no detail.

**But the word order says something: the letter *fāʾ*.** *Khalaqahu **fa**-qaddarah* — no interval has been left between the creating and the measuring; the *fāʾ* denotes succession and adjacency. Creation and measure are two faces of the same operation.

And the meaning of this is: a drop by itself is nothing at all. What makes it something is the measure placed in it. **The material is worthless, the ordering is what has value.** And the logic of the accusation comes out of this too: man cannot boast of his material, and the ordering is not his own.

### ثُمَّ ٱلسَّبِيلَ يَسَّرَهُۥ — He made the path easy

**A nicety of word order.** The normal order of the sentence would have been *thumma yassara-hu's-sabīl*. The object (*al-sabīl*) has been brought in front of the verb. In Arabic this is **taqdīm**, and it does two things: **ikhtiṣāṣ** (that thing precisely) and **ihtimām** (drawing further attention to that thing). It also preserves the harmony of the verse-endings (*yassarah* ends on the same sound as *qaddarah* and *khalaqah*).

So the sentence says: **the path, yes the path itself, He made easy for him.**

**The particle ثُمَّ.** Unlike the *fāʾ*, *thumma* denotes **tarākhī**: time comes in between. Creation and measure were adjacent; between the measure and the path there is an interval. The measure in the womb, the path in the world.

**ٱلسَّبِيل — which path?**

| View | Ground | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **The way out of the womb — birth** | The context is a biological sequence: drop → measure → coming out → death → grave → resurrection. Birth is the missing link of the sequence | Strong in respect of the completeness of the sequence. And the "making easy" of birth is a real fact |
| **The path of guidance and of straying; moral choice** | *Al-Insān 76/3*: *"We showed him the path (innā hadaynāhu's-sabīl); whether he is grateful or ungrateful."* Almost the same expression. And *al-Balad 90/10*: *"And We showed him the two heights"* | The strongest view. The parallel inside the Qurʾān is direct |
| **The path of livelihood and of life** | The making easy of provision, of work, of the means | Possible; it does not exclude the others |

**Preference:** the second view looks the stronger, and its ground is that al-Insān 76/3 uses almost exactly the same expression and that there the continuation is openly bound to the dilemma of **gratitude / ingratitude** — and this passage of ʿAbasa too had opened with *mā akfarah* (how ungrateful he is). The two texts build the same chain: the path was shown, and man chose ingratitude.

This preference is not binding. And it does not exclude the first view: the making easy of birth can be read as the first step of the making easy of the path of life.

**يَسَّرَ — to make easy.** Root **ي-س-ر**. *Yusr* — ease; its opposite *ʿusr* — hardship. This pair was treated at al-Inshirāḥ 94/5-6; I do not repeat it here.

**The choice of the word says something.** The verse does not say "He showed the path" (*hadā*), it says "He made the path easy" (*yassara*). The difference is real: to show is to give knowledge; to make easy is to make the going possible. That is, man has not merely been told which path is the right one; he has also been given **the equipment to walk** it.

And this makes the accusation heavier. The excuse "I did not know" is closed off by *hadā*; the excuse "I could not do it" is closed off by *yassara*. The verse closes off the second excuse.

## 80/21-22 — ثُمَّ أَمَاتَهُۥ فَأَقْبَرَهُۥ ۝ ثُمَّ إِذَا شَآءَ أَنشَرَهُۥ

*Thumma amātahu fa-aqbarah ۝ Thumma idhā shāʾa ansharah*
"Then He made him die and had him put in a grave. Then when He wills He will raise him."

### أَقْبَرَ — to make one possessed of a grave

**Root: ق-ب-ر.** This root was treated in the section on al-Takāthur 102/2 (*al-maqābir*), and this verse was pointed to there; the finding there is opened out here.

**The distinction the lexicographers make is critical:**

- **قَبَرَهُ** (*qabarahu*) — he buried him. The doer of the act is the person who digs the earth and lays the body in it.
- **أَقْبَرَهُ** (*aqbarahu*) — he gave him a grave, provided for his burial, ordered his burial. Form IV here has the function of "making one possessed of a thing / giving the means for it".

The verse uses the second. That is, it is not said that God buried him; it is said that He **established the order of burial**. The difference looks small but it changes the verse entirely: the work of burying is done by people, but that there should be such a thing as burial is not man's invention.

**And this is mentioned as an honour done to him.**

Look at the other items of the sequence: he was created, he was given measure, his path was made easy, he was made to die, **he was put in a grave**, he will be raised. The list is a list of the operations performed on man. And being put in a grave stands in this list among the things counted as favours.

**Why a favour?** Because the alternative is plain to see: a living creature that dies is left in the open, rots, becomes food for beasts of prey. The human species is the only one that has made the giving of its dead to the earth a rule. This is less an operation performed on the dead than **a declaration that the dead continues to count as a human being.** The grave is not the place where the body is hidden but the place where the identity is kept.

**The Qurʾān relates this in a scene.** After the first murder the killer does not know what to do with his brother's body:

> *"Then God sent a crow scratching at the ground, to show him how to bury his brother's body."* (al-Māʾida 5/31)

**That is, the first burial had to be taught.** This is exactly what the story records: man did not know of himself what to do with his dead. That the verb *aqbara* comes in Form IV agrees with this picture.

**A connection with the social sciences.** In archaeology, deliberate burial is counted among the strongest indicators that a community has developed symbolic behaviour — because burying produces no practical benefit; it costs labour, it has no return, and it is done only if the dead is believed to go on meaning something. I write this not as a claim of "miracle" but as a record that the place the verse gives to the grave agrees with observation.

### أَنشَرَ — to raise, to spread out

**Root: ن-ش-ر.** Its concrete sense: **to open out what has been folded, to spread, to lay out.** *Nashara'th-thawba* — he unfolded the cloth. Its opposite is *ṭayy* (folding).

And from this root the sense of raising the dead comes out: *nashara'l-mawtā* — he raised the dead. *Nushūr* — resurrection: *"And to Him is the rising"* (al-Mulk 67/15: *ilayhi'n-nushūr*).

**How do the two senses join?** A dead man is a folded thing — closed, gathered up, wrapped in a shroud. To raise him is to **unfold** him. This is the picture in the word.

**And this root establishes a direct link with Sūrat al-Takwīr:**

> **وَإِذَا ٱلصُّحُفُ نُشِرَتْ** — *"And when the pages are spread open"* (al-Takwīr 81/10).

The same root. In ʿAbasa **man** is unfolded, in al-Takwīr **the register**. Both are the same verb. And this is the most concrete connection between the two sūras: resurrection and the opening of the file are, in the Qurʾān's lexicon, a single movement.

The Qurʾān establishes this pairing in another place too: *"And on the day of resurrection We shall bring forth for him a book which he will find spread open"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/13) — there too *manshūrā*, the same root.

### إِذَا شَآءَ — "when He wills"

All the items of the sequence were without qualification: He created, He gave measure, He made easy, He made him die, He had him put in a grave. **Only the last comes with a qualification: when He wills.**

Why is this so?

Because the first five items are **the working of an established order**. They happen in every man, every time, in the same sequence. The sixth is not the working of an order but **a decision** — its time fixed but not announced.

And this distinction carries a hidden answer of the sūra's as well. When man denies the resurrection his ground is generally "no such thing has been seen". The verse builds the first five items of the sequence out of things that are seen and sets the sixth on top of them: **the work of the same hand, only its time is with Him.**

**And there is this too:** in the first ten verses man's own prediction was declared invalid (*"what do you know"*). Here as well the owner of the knowledge of the time is stated. The sūra says the same thing in two different places: **knowledge has a limit, and what lies beyond the limit belongs to another.**

## 80/23 — كَلَّا لَمَّا يَقْضِ مَآ أَمَرَهُۥ

*Kallā lammā yaqḍi mā amarah*
"No! He has still not carried out what He commanded him."

### The second كَلَّا

The sūra's second hinge. The first (verse 11) had passed from the incident to the text; this second one passes from man's story to the verdict.

And *kallā* here rejects something: the assumption that the favours counted out in the preceding six verses have met with a return. The list is finished, the reckoning has been made, the result: no.

### لَمَّا — "not yet"

The finest point of this verse, and it is generally lost in translation.

In Arabic there are two close particles of negation, and the difference between them is decisive here:

| | **لَمْ (lam)** | **لَمَّا (lammā)** |
|---|---|---|
| Its meaning | He did not do it | He has **not yet** done it |
| Expectation | None; the door is closed | **There is one**; the thing is still expected to happen |
| Time | The whole of the past | Up to now; what comes after is open |

The term the lexicographers use for *lammā* is **tawaqquʿ**: expectation. To say *lammā yaqḍi* is not to say "he did not do it and is not expected to do it"; it is to say **"up to now he has not done it"**. What comes after is open.

**The meaning of this is large.** The verse delivers the sūra's heaviest verdict — the return for all the favours counted out has not been given — and it delivers it **with a particle that does not close the door**. A single letter takes the verdict out of being final.

Read together with the imprecation of the seventeenth verse, the picture is completed: *"Perish man"* — an imprecation; *"he has still not done it"* — but the matter is not finished. The sūra both says the harshest word and leaves the word open to a return.

**A grammatical note.** The verb *yaqḍi* is in the jussive (*majzūm*) because of *lammā*, and its final letter (*yāʾ*) has dropped: *yaqḍī → yaqḍi*. In the muṣḥaf too it is written without the *yāʾ*.

### قَضَىٰ — to carry out

**Root: ق-ض-ي.** Its meaning is wide, and all of it turns around **taking a matter through to its end**: to judge (*qāḍī* — the one who gives judgement), to complete, to carry out, to finish. *Qaḍāʾ* — both a judgement and the later performance of what was not done at its proper time.

The choice of the word is meaningful: not "he did not do it" (*faʿala*) but **"he did not complete it"** (*qaḍā*). That is, the matter is not that he never began; it is that he has **not finished**. Man has done some things; but he has not taken the matter through to its end.

### مَآ أَمَرَهُۥ — what he was commanded

Two readings:

- **مَا** as a relative pronoun: "**the thing** He commanded him". That is, the content of the command.
- **مَا** as *maṣdariyya*: "**His commanding** him", that is, the carrying out of the command.

In respect of meaning the difference is small. In both, what is lacking is the same: that the command given has not been taken through to its end.

**What is the content of the command?** The verse does not say. And its not saying keeps the scope open, as the leaving of the word *māʿūn* undefined does in al-Māʿūn. Judging by what the sūra has counted out up to here, the core of the content is plain: **to acknowledge what has been given and to give its return.**

## 80/24 — فَلْيَنظُرِ ٱلْإِنسَٰنُ إِلَىٰ طَعَامِهِۦ

*Fa'l-yanẓuri'l-insānu ilā ṭaʿāmih*
"So let man look at his food."

### The drop in scale

The sūra's boldest move is here, and it needs to be seen architecturally.

Through twenty-three verses what was set out was this: pages raised high, noble scribes, creation, measure, death, the grave, resurrection. The scale as large as it could be. And now the verse says: **look at your plate.**

This is the same movement as the architecture of Sūrat al-Māʿūn. There the sūra began from the day of reckoning and came down to a bucket; the descent here is steeper still: from resurrection to a single meal.

**Why?** Because the dispute is a dispute about evidence, and the strongest evidence is that which is not disputed. Resurrection can be denied — it has not been seen. Revelation can be denied — its source is not visible. **But the food on the plate cannot be denied.** It is standing there, everyone eats it, and where it came from can be traced.

The sūra binds its largest claim to the smallest and nearest object. And this agrees with the sūra's first ten verses too: there as well it was related in how small a thing the measure shows itself — in an expression on a face.

### فَلْيَنظُرْ — the command

A command in the third person, framed with the **lām al-amr** (the lām of command): "let him look". Not a direct command (*unẓur* — look), but a command directed at a third person.

This agrees with the context: since the seventeenth verse the one addressed is "al-insān", not a single person. The command is framed accordingly.

**نَظَر — to look.** Root **ن-ظ-ر**. In the Qurʾān this verb is used in two ways: to look with the eye, and to examine by thinking. English keeps the same doubling: to *look at* a thing and to *look into* it are not the same act.

**The particle إِلَىٰ** brings the first to the front. *Naẓara ilā* — to turn one's eye towards a thing. Had it been *naẓara fī*, "to think it over" would have been the more dominant sense. So the verse asks first for **an actual looking**: for really looking at the plate.

But the eight verses that follow show where the looking must lead — to the rain, to the soil, to the splitting. That is, the verse asks for a looking with the eye and carries it over into thought. **The look is set down as the starting point of thought, not as something that takes its place.**

**طَعَام — food.** Root **ط-ع-م**: to taste, to eat. This root was treated at al-Māʿūn 107/3 (*ṭaʿāmi'l-miskīn*); the note there holds here too: the word can mean both "food" (the substance) and "feeding" (the act). Here the first sense is plain.

**Another reading.** It is transmitted that in the sources "ṭaʿām" is read here figuratively in the sense of **the knowledge or teaching a person takes in**, and understood as "let man look at what he takes and from whom". Since I am not certain to whom this reading belongs, I give no name. The context looks as though it does not permit it — the eight verses that follow are entirely about agriculture. But I record it as a horizon: for a man to ask after the source of what he takes into himself is not a question belonging to food alone.

## 80/25-32 — أَنَّا صَبَبْنَا ٱلْمَآءَ صَبًّا ۝ ثُمَّ شَقَقْنَا ٱلْأَرْضَ شَقًّا ۝ فَأَنۢبَتْنَا فِيهَا حَبًّا ۝ وَعِنَبًا وَقَضْبًا ۝ وَزَيْتُونًا وَنَخْلًا ۝ وَحَدَآئِقَ غُلْبًا ۝ وَفَٰكِهَةً وَأَبًّا ۝ مَّتَٰعًا لَّكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَٰمِكُمْ

*Annā ṣababna'l-māʾa ṣabbā ۝ Thumma shaqaqna'l-arḍa shaqqā ۝ Fa-anbatnā fīhā ḥabbā ۝ Wa ʿinaban wa qaḍbā ۝ Wa zaytūnan wa nakhlā ۝ Wa ḥadāʾiqa ghulbā ۝ Wa fākihatan wa abbā ۝ Matāʿan lakum wa li-anʿāmikum*

"We poured down the water in abundance; then We split the earth in clefts; and We made grain grow in it — and grapes and green fodder, olive and date palm, thick-treed gardens, fruit and pasture; for your enjoyment and that of your livestock."

### أَنَّا — a difference of variant reading

Two readings are transmitted, and they change the word order:

| Reading | How it attaches | Its meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **أَنَّا** (with *fatḥa*) | A substitute for the *ṭaʿāmih* of the preceding verse: "let him look at his food — **namely, that** We poured down the water…" | The thing to be looked at is directly this process |
| **إِنَّا** (with *kasra*) | A new sentence begins: "**Truly** We poured down the water…" | The process is given separately, as the ground of the command to look |

I give no imām's name. The difference of meaning is small but real: on the first reading what is to be looked at is not the plate but **the story of the plate.** That is, the command "look at your food" turns into "look at where your food came from".

### صَبَبْنَا ٱلْمَآءَ صَبًّا — We poured down the water

**Root: ص-ب-ب.** To pour, to make flow; to empty out from above downwards. *Ṣabb* — pouring. *Munṣabb* — that which is poured.

**The absolute object: صَبًّا.** The repetition of the verb's own verbal noun as its object. In Arabic the function of this is **reinforcement**: "we poured and poured, we poured in abundance".

And in this passage it is done **twice**: *ṣabban* and *shaqqan*. Two verbs, two reinforcements. The verbs that follow (*anbatnā* and the rest) are not reinforced. So the emphasis lies on the first two steps: **the pouring and the splitting.**

**It does not say "We sent down rain".** In other places the Qurʾān uses *anzalnā* (We sent down). Here it is *ṣababnā* — We poured. The difference lies in the concreteness of the picture: to pour is to empty out of a vessel. The sky is handled as though it were a vessel.

### شَقَقْنَا ٱلْأَرْضَ شَقًّا — We split the earth

**Root: ش-ق-ق.** To split, to divide in two, to crack. *Shiqq* — a half, a piece. *Mashaqqa* — hardship; the lexicographers bind this to the idea of "what splits a man in two". *Inshiqāq* — a splitting (al-Inshiqāq 84/1: *"When the sky is split open"*).

**The order is correct: first the water, then the splitting.** The verse does not say that the earth splits of itself; it says that it splits **after** the pouring of the water. And this is what is seen in the field: dry soil is hard and whole; when it takes water it loosens, the seed swells, and it comes out by cracking the surface. The cracking of the soil is the first sign that the shoot is coming.

I do not write this as a "scientific miracle"; the verse describes what the farmer sees every year. **Its value lies not in announcing a hidden piece of knowledge but in relating an open thing in the right order.**

### Three roots, one idea: شق / فلق / فلح

These three roots were treated in separate places in this commentary, and they can now be set side by side.

| Root | Its concrete sense | Where it was treated | Its typical use in the Qurʾān |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ف-ل-ق** | To split; the opening of what is closed and the coming out of something from inside it | `113-felak.md` | *Fāliqu'l-ḥabbi wa'n-nawā* (the splitter of the grain and the date-stone, al-Anʿām 6/95); *fāliqu'l-iṣbāḥ* (the splitter of the dawn, al-Anʿām 6/96) |
| **ف-ل-ح** | To split the soil; hence *fallāḥ* (the ploughman) and *falāḥ* (deliverance) | `001-fatiha.md`, `002-bakara.md`, `091-sems.md` | *Qad aflaḥa man zakkāhā* (al-Shams 91/9) |
| **ش-ق-ق** | To split, to divide in two | Here | *Shaqaqna'l-arḍa* (ʿAbasa 80/26); *inshaqqati's-samāʾ* (al-Inshiqāq 84/1) |

**The three are separate roots.** I establish no relation of derivation among them; the note of caution entered in the section on al-Falaq about the closeness between *falaq* and *falāḥ* holds here too. In Arabic it is a pattern that roots close in meaning share their first two letters, and this has been discussed in the linguistic tradition; but the discussion has not been brought to a settled conclusion, and I draw no ruling from it.

**What can be recorded is this:** in describing the coming forth of life the Qurʾān uses **the same picture** with three separate roots — the splitting of something closed and dark, and the coming out of something from within it. The seed, the morning, the sea, the sky, the soil. This is a pattern that really does repeat in nature, and that the language gathers it into a single picture is worth noticing.

And in the last verse of the sūra **a fourth root of splitting** will come: **ف-ج-ر**. But that will be a use of splitting in the opposite direction — it will be treated at the forty-second verse.

### The list: eight items

| Verse | Word | What it is | For whom |
|---|---|---|---|
| 27 | **حَبًّا** (ḥabb) | Grain, cereals — wheat, barley | Man (staple food) |
| 28 | **عِنَبًا** (ʿinab) | Grapes | Man |
| 28 | **قَضْبًا** (qaḍb) | Green fodder cut fresh and eaten, lucerne | **Livestock** |
| 29 | **زَيْتُونًا** (zaytūn) | The olive | Man (oil + fruit) |
| 29 | **نَخْلًا** (nakhl) | The date palm | Man |
| 30 | **حَدَآئِقَ غُلْبًا** (ḥadāʾiq ghulb) | Walled gardens of dense, large-trunked trees | Man |
| 31 | **فَاكِهَةً** (fākiha) | Fruit — eaten for pleasure | Man (over and above) |
| 31 | **أَبًّا** (abb) | Pasture, the herbage that is grazed | **Livestock** |

**The logic of the list: from the necessary to the pleasurable, with the livestock's share in between.**

The ordering begins from necessary food (*ḥabb* — grain, the thing that keeps a society on its feet) and arrives at the end at **fākiha** — by the lexicographers' distinction, *fākiha* is what is eaten not in order to stay alive but **in order to taste**. Root **ف-ك-ه**: to take pleasure, to enjoy; from the same root *fukāha* (pleasant talk, jesting).

So the list begins from need and rises to pleasure. **What is given is not in the quantity that suffices for living; there is more than that.** This is the evidence for the accusation *"how ungrateful he is"* in the seventeenth verse: the reckoning is not "enough was given and no thanks were offered" but **"more than enough was given"**.

**And the livestock's share is woven into the list, not appended to its end.** *Qaḍb* comes third, *abb* comes last. Both are things man does not eat and animals graze. The sūra does not put them in a separate list; it places them inside man's own table.

The thirty-second verse will say this openly: *"for your enjoyment and that of your livestock."*

### Notes on the words

**حَبّ** — root **ح-ب-ب**: grain, seed. From the same root come *ḥabīb* (beloved) and *ḥubb* (love); the lexicographers bind the link to the idea of "the seed, the core, of the heart". This derivation is disputed; I transmit it with caution.

**قَضْب** — root **ق-ض-ب**: to cut. *Qaḍīb* — a branch, a rod; *miqḍab* — a pruning knife. The word describes the plant that **grows again as it is cut**: it is sown once and mown many times. Lucerne and fodder plants like it are such.

This is the most technical word in the list, and the one that says the most: the existence of a plant that increases as it is cut is the condition on which the keeping of livestock is possible.

**نَخْل** — the date palm. A tree that occurs often in the Qurʾān; it is there in the scene of the childbirth of Mary (Maryam) as well (Maryam 19/25). In a desert economy the date palm is at once food, shade and building material.

**حَدَآئِق** — the plural of *ḥadīqa*. Root **ح-د-ق**: to encircle, to surround. *Ḥadaqa* — the pupil of the eye; because the eyelid encircles it. *Ḥadīqa* means **a garden with a wall around it** — that is, a garden that has a boundary, that has an owner, that is protected. Not an ordinary stand of trees.

**غُلْب** — root **غ-ل-ب**: to overcome, to be superior. *Aghlab* — thick-necked, large. *Ghulb*, said of trees, means "thick-trunked, tangled into one another, dense".

The choice of the word is fine: **the root of overcoming is used to relate the denseness of trees.** It is as though the trees in the garden were trying to overcome one another; growth itself is pictured as a race.

**أَبّ** — and here one must be honest.

What the word means is not certain. The widespread explanation: **pasture, the greenery on which animals graze**; on one view, "every plant that man does not eat and animals do". The word occurs only here in the Qurʾān, and it is rare in Arabic too.

Reports are transmitted that in the early period this word was asked about, and that the one who was asked gave an answer to the effect that "to go chasing after this is affectation / it is more correct to say that we do not know". The transmissions differ as to whom this report belongs, and there is dispute over its chain; for this reason I give no name and do not present it as an established transmission.

But the attitude the report conveys agrees with the method of this commentary: **to dress a word whose meaning is not certain in a certain meaning is not exegesis but addition.** The verse's function is plain in any case — to count what the animals graze on. Not knowing the exact botanical equivalent of the word does not mean not knowing what the verse says.

### مَّتَٰعًا لَّكُمْ وَلِأَنْعَٰمِكُمْ — the close

**مَتَاع — root م-ت-ع.** Its meaning: the thing enjoyed, the enjoyment of it; and inside the word there is **transience**. *Tamattuʿ* — to enjoy for a time. The Qurʾān's settled expression: *matāʿu'l-ḥayāti'd-dunyā* — the passing enjoyment of the life of this world (Āl ʿImrān 3/185; al-Raʿd 13/26).

**So the picture of the table closes with a word that carries the sense of "passing".**

And the next verse: *"When the sound that deafens the ears comes…"*

**The transition has been made inside the word.** The sūra does not change the subject; the last word it uses changes the subject of itself. Eight items of food were counted out, all of them were named *matāʿ*, and *matāʿ* means that which will come to an end. No separate sentence is needed in order to begin speaking of the resurrection.

**أَنْعَام — root ن-ع-م.** The collective name used for camels, cattle, sheep and goats. **And this root is the same root as the word "favour"**: *niʿma*, *naʿīm*, *anʿama* (he bestowed a favour), *nāʿim* (soft).

So the name given to a herd of animals means "favour". The word itself says what a herd meant in that society: capital, food, transport, clothing, standing — all of it in a single word.

**And the work the closing sentence does:** *"for you and for your livestock."* The food of man and the food of the animal come out of the same rain, the same splitting, the same soil. The sūra counts them out separately and then joins them in a single sentence.

And this speaks quietly with the sūra's first ten verses. There a man had seen himself as **self-sufficient**; he had supposed he needed no one. Here what he eats and what his animal eats are bound to the same source. The verse does not say this; I am the one drawing it, and I record this as my own reading. But the tension between the two ends of the sūra is real.

## 80/33-37 — فَإِذَا جَآءَتِ ٱلصَّآخَّةُ ۝ يَوْمَ يَفِرُّ ٱلْمَرْءُ مِنْ أَخِيهِ ۝ وَأُمِّهِۦ وَأَبِيهِ ۝ وَصَٰحِبَتِهِۦ وَبَنِيهِ ۝ لِكُلِّ ٱمْرِئٍ مِّنْهُمْ يَوْمَئِذٍ شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ

*Fa-idhā jāʾati'ṣ-ṣākhkha ۝ Yawma yafirru'l-marʾu min akhīh ۝ Wa ummihi wa abīh ۝ Wa ṣāḥibatihi wa banīh ۝ Li-kulli'mriʾin minhum yawmaʾidhin shaʾnun yughnīh*

"When the sound that deafens the ears comes — on that day a man flees from his brother, and from his mother and his father, and from his wife and his children. On that day each of them has a concern of his own that suffices him."

### ٱلصَّآخَّة — the deafening

**Root: ص-خ-خ.** A doubled root (*muḍāʿaf*). Its meaning: **a sound violent enough to deafen the ear; a noise that strikes and deafens.** *Ṣakhkha* — it struck, it deafened.

The word occurs **only here** in the Qurʾān.

The names of the resurrection, and what each name brings to the front, were given in a table in the section on al-Qāriʿa 101; this name is included there and was bound to hearing. I do not repeat it here.

**What is to be added here is this:** of all the names given to the resurrection, the one that brings **hearing** to the front is used precisely in this sūra.

The sūra had opened with a matter of **seeing**: a man who could not see came among those who could, and was not seen. And the sūra's scene of the resurrection opens with an event in which the distinction between seeing and not seeing loses its meaning: **a sound it is impossible not to hear.**

The sūra does not establish this link; I am the one establishing it, and I record this as my own reading. But the choice of word does not look like chance: with seven or eight names for the resurrection at the Qurʾān's disposal, its choosing the name of hearing in a sūra about seeing is worth recording.

**Why is sound so central?** Because sound cannot be escaped. The eye closes; the ear does not. One can refrain from looking where one should not look; a sound one should not hear cannot be left unheard. **The most direct way to relate that an event covers everyone equally is to describe it as a sound.**

**And *fa-idhā*.** The particle *idhā* denotes a time whose coming to pass is certain; this was treated in the section on al-Zilzāl. The *fāʾ* at the head binds the passage to the one before it: the picture of the table ended, the word *matāʿ* was said, **and then**. There is no break.

### The order of the flight — five people

This is one of the places in the sūra that most needs to be dwelt on.

**The order is this:** brother → mother → father → wife → children.

**The rhythm says something too:** the brother by himself (verse 34), then two pairs (35 and 36). 1 + 2 + 2. The brother is named alone, and he opens the door.

**On what basis is the ordering?**

| Order | Who | The kind of bond | The difficulty of breaking it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The brother** | Lateral — the same generation, not chosen | The easiest. Mutual and equal; neither owes the other |
| 2-3 | **Mother and father** | Upward — the source of your existence | Hard. You are in their debt; to flee is the plainest form of ingratitude |
| 4 | **The wife** | Horizontal but chosen — established by a contract | Harder. The one chosen bond; the closest partner in life |
| 5 | **The children** | Downward — entrusted to you, asked of you | **The hardest.** The last thing a man will let go of |

**So the ordering runs from the easiest to break to the hardest.** The sequence is a rising staircase, and its last step is **the children**.

Why this is the summit is plain: a person will do almost anything for his own child. The tendency of a parent to protect his child is one of the strongest and most widespread patterns of human behaviour; it is observed in every society, in every age. **The verse describes a moment in which that pattern is broken.** The measure of the terror is the last item of the list.

**The second logic of the ordering: completeness.**

Look at the five people. Lateral (the brother), upward (the parents), horizontal-and-chosen (the wife), downward (the children). **Every axis of kinship a person can belong to has been counted.** No one is left outside.

So this is not a list of examples but **a census**. The sūra does not say "he flees from his relatives"; it counts out one by one every kind of nearness and marks them all.

**The third logic: the chronology of a person's life.**

The brother — the companion of childhood. Mother and father — the beginning. The wife — the bond established in adulthood. The children — the future. The list passes in order through the bonds a person forms over the course of his life.

**Why the mother before the father?** The Qurʾān generally uses collective expressions such as *wālidayn* or *abawayn*; here the two are named separately, and **the mother first**. This agrees with the precedence the Qurʾān and the body of hadith give to the mother in the matter of goodness towards parents (*"His mother bore him in weakness upon weakness…"*, Luqmān 31/14 — there too the mother is named particularly).

Its function here may be this — as my own reading: **the mother is the person one would least expect to be fled from.** As the staircase rises, she is the first step that shakes. For this reason she has been placed at the head of the pair.

### صَاحِبَة — the wife

The verse does not say *zawja*, it says **صَاحِبَة**.

**Root: ص-ح-ب.** To be together, to keep company, to accompany. *Ṣāḥib* — friend, companion; *ṣuḥba* — companionship; *aṣḥāb* — those who are together.

**The difference is real:** *zawja* relates a legal bond (the marriage contract); *ṣāḥiba* relates **companionship**. The verse brings to the front not the legal bond but **the living side by side**.

Why? Because what matters in the scene of flight is not the legal bond but habit and closeness. A man flees not from a contract but **from the person with whom he shared his life**.

**And a link: Sūrat al-Takwīr uses this root too, in a critical place.** *"Your companion (صَاحِبُكُم) is not possessed"* (al-Takwīr 81/22). There the word rests on the acquaintance built by having lived together for long years: you know him.

The two sūras use the same root in two different directions: in one, companionship is a proof (you know him); in the other, companionship dissolves (he flees from her). I record this parallel as my own reading.

### يَفِرُّ — to flee

**Root: ف-ر-ر.** To flee, to desert. Turkish borrowed the word from this root as *firar*, "desertion". *Mafarr* — the place one flees to: *"On that day man will say: where is the place of escape?"* (al-Qiyāma 75/10).

**The word does not mean "to part" but "to flee".** Parting is neutral; fleeing presupposes **a threat**. The one who flees is expecting harm from what he flees.

What, then, is the threat? Two explanations are given, and both agree with the text:

1. **Fear of being called to account.** The person knows he has a debt towards these people. To meet them is to be reminded of the reckoning.
2. **The weight of his own concern.** The thirty-seventh verse gives this explanation openly.

The second explanation is the closer to the text, because the verse itself says it.

**الْمَرْء — a person.** Root **م-ر-أ**. *Imruʾ* — a person, a man; *imraʾa* — a woman; *murūʾa* — humanity, manliness, virtue. From the same root.

In relating the moment at which man's most human bonds dissolve, the sūra uses a word akin to the root of *murūʾa*. I record this as a nicety; I do not claim that there is this much intention in the choice of the word — *marʾ* is the ordinary word in Arabic in this context.

### شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ — a concern that suffices him

The sūra's finest turn of a word is here.

**شَأْن — root ش-أ-ن.** State, business, condition, a matter of importance. The Turkish words *şan* and *şe'niyet* are borrowings from this root. In the Qurʾān: *"Every day He is upon some business"* (al-Raḥmān 55/29: *kulla yawmin huwa fī shaʾn*).

That the word comes indefinite is for **magnification**: "such a concern that…". It is not defined, its boundary is not drawn.

**يُغْنِيهِ — root غ-ن-ي.** And here is the sūra's turn.

In the fifth verse there was this: **مَنِ ٱسْتَغْنَىٰ** — the one who sees himself as self-sufficient, who counts himself free of need.

In the thirty-seventh verse there is this: **شَأْنٌ يُغْنِيهِ** — a concern that makes him self-sufficient, that suffices him.

**The same root, at the two ends of the sūra.**

The meaning of the verb: *aghnāhu'sh-shayʾu* — that thing sufficed him, took him out of needing anyone else. So the verse says: **each will have such a concern that he will have no room left to think of anyone else.**

And the irony here lies in the word itself:

In this world a man had supposed himself **self-sufficient**. He had thought he needed no one, and for precisely this reason he drew everyone's attention. On that day the thing he wanted will be given him — **he really will need no one.** But by then it is no longer a privilege; it is the description of having no one.

The same technique was recorded in the section on al-Layl: there too the sūra gave the man who supposed himself *ghanī* his own word back, by telling him that his wealth would not avail him. ʿAbasa does the same work: **istighnāʾ is given to the one who wanted istighnāʾ.**

This is an inference, and I record it as my own reading. But that the root occurs at exactly the two ends of the sūra, in exactly these two positions, suggests that the words were not chosen at random.

**And there is this:** in its first ten verses the sūra had spoken of a matter of the distribution of attention — who is turned towards, who is turned away from. The thirty-seventh verse describes a day on which attention **comes to an end altogether**. That is, the sūra traces an arc: how you distribute your attention today is measured in the light of a day on which no one will be able to attend to anyone.

## 80/38-42 — وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ مُّسْفِرَةٌ ۝ ضَاحِكَةٌ مُّسْتَبْشِرَةٌ ۝ وَوُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ عَلَيْهَا غَبَرَةٌ ۝ تَرْهَقُهَا قَتَرَةٌ ۝ أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْكَفَرَةُ ٱلْفَجَرَةُ

*Wujūhun yawmaʾidhin musfira ۝ Ḍāḥikatun mustabshira ۝ Wa wujūhun yawmaʾidhin ʿalayhā ghabara ۝ Tarhaquhā qatara ۝ Ulāʾika humu'l-kafaratu'l-fajara*

"On that day some faces are shining; laughing, filled with good news. And on that day dust covers other faces; a darkness overspreads them. Those are the deniers, the transgressors."

### The sūra opened with one face; it closes with faces

To see this is to see the whole of the sūra.

**The first word: عَبَسَ** — an expression on a face.
**The last five verses: وُجُوهٌ ... وَوُجُوهٌ** — faces.

Forty-two verses open with one face and close on faces. Everything related in between — the text, creation, the table, the flight — stands between two faces.

And the sūra's claim is built by this frame: **what a face does today has to do with what will become of it on that day.** The sūra nowhere says this openly; it says it by structure.

### مُسْفِرَة — uncovered, shining

**Root: س-ف-ر.** It occurred in the fifteenth verse as *safara*; the whole network of the root was opened out there, I do not repeat it here.

**أَسْفَرَ** — Form IV: it came to light, it shone, it grew bright. *Asfara'ṣ-ṣubḥu* — the morning broke. **مُسْفِرَة** is an active participle: **shining, uncovered, lit up**.

**That is, the face is likened to the morning.** The moment when the darkness withdraws, the covering lifts, the light appears. This is the concrete sense of the word.

**And the two ends of the sūra join:**

| | Above | Below |
|---|---|---|
| Verse | 80/15 | 80/38 |
| The word | **سَفَرَة** | **مُسْفِرَة** |
| Who | Those who bear the text | The faces of those who receive the text |
| The act | To bring to light | To have been brought to light |

The text comes to light in the hand of the *safara*; the face of the one who receives it becomes *musfira*. **The same root, the same work, on two storeys.**

And this is why one of the sūra's names being *al-Safara* is apt: the root is the sūra's real backbone.

**Parallel descriptions in the Qurʾān:**
- *"On that day some faces are radiant (nāḍira), looking towards their Lord"* (al-Qiyāma 75/22-23).
- *"You will know in their faces the brightness of bliss"* (al-Muṭaffifīn 83/24).

In all three the same thing is done: the inner state is **read in the face.**

### ضَاحِكَةٌ مُّسْتَبْشِرَة — laughing, filled with good news

**ضَاحِكَة — root ض-ح-ك.** To laugh. One of the places where the Qurʾān uses laughter for those who are delivered.

And note: this is not a smile. *Ḍaḥk* is laughter with sound. In another place the Qurʾān gives this within an opposition: *"On that day the believers laugh at the deniers"* (al-Muṭaffifīn 83/34), and earlier the same sūra had related that in this world the deniers laughed at the believers (83/29).

**مُّسْتَبْشِرَة — root ب-ش-ر.** And the real sense of this root was treated in the section on al-Baqara 2/25: **the centre of the root is *bashara*, that is, the outer skin of the face.** Good news was given this name because good news **shows on the skin of the face**; from the same root comes *bashar* (man) — the being whose skin is visible.

**Now put this piece of knowledge here.**

The verse is speaking of **faces**. And the word chosen for those faces is the word whose root is **"the skin of the face"**. That is, *mustabshira* here is not a metaphor; the concrete sense of the word **is realised to the letter**: the good news is named at exactly the place where it shows — on the skin.

This is one of the sūra's cleanest correspondences of wording and sense, and it does not become visible without the analysis of the root established in al-Baqara.

**The pattern: Form X (*istifʿāl*).** *Istibshār* — to receive good news, to rejoice at good news; and with the "seeking" function of Form X: to await good news, to be in hope.

**The order of the three adjectives.** *Musfira* (shining) → *ḍāḥika* (laughing) → *mustabshira* (filled with good news). It runs from the appearance towards the cause: first the light, then the movement, then the reason for it.

If the order is reversed the logical order comes out: the good news was received → there was laughter → the face lit up. The verse gives not the logical order but **the order of appearance**.

This is the same work the sūra has been doing from the beginning: **saying first what is visible from outside.** The sūra had begun with an expression on a face — that too was the outward sign of an inner state. Sūrat al-Māʿūn used the same method: the hand was shown as the evidence of belief. **The inner state is read from its trace on the outside.**

### عَلَيْهَا غَبَرَةٌ / تَرْهَقُهَا قَتَرَة — what settles upon them

**And there is a grammatical asymmetry here; it must not be missed.**

| | The first group (38-39) | The second group (40-41) |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | **Adjectives** — *musfira, ḍāḥika, mustabshira* | **Sentences** — *ʿalayhā ghabara*, *tarhaquhā qatara* |
| Pattern | Active participle | A nominal sentence + a verbal sentence |
| What it says | The faces **are** so | There is something **upon** the faces |
| Its source | From themselves | **From outside** |

The brightness of the first group **belongs to them**: the face is shining, it laughs, it is filled with good news. The darkness of the second group **has been laid upon them**: the dust is *upon* them, the darkness *overspreads* them.

It is worth comparing this asymmetry with the sequence of passive adjectives treated in the section on al-Bayyina 98/2; but the difference here works in the opposite direction. **The good is their attribute, the evil is a load upon them.**

As a possible reading — my own reading: light is a state, whereas darkness is **an accumulation**. A face does not darken of itself; things settle on top of it. And the sūra describes what settles in two layers.

**غَبَرَة — root غ-ب-ر.** Dust, the dust of the earth. *Ghubār* — dust. From the same root comes *ghābir*, and it is curiously one of the **aḍdād**: it means both "the one who passes away" and "the one who stays behind" (*"except an old woman among those who stayed behind"*, al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/171).

**قَتَرَة — root ق-ت-ر.** Darkening smoke, soot, blackness. *Qatar* — the smoke and smell of roasting meat. And from the same root comes **iqtār**: **miserliness, stinting, narrowing.** In the Qurʾān: *"When they spend they neither waste nor stint"* (al-Furqān 25/67: *wa lam yaqturū*).

This standing-together is worth recording: **soot and darkness on the one side, miserliness and narrowing on the other, in the same root.** The idea common to the root may be "narrowing, stinting, darkening". I record this as an observation about the language; I bind no ruling to the verse from it.

**Two layers: dust, then darkness.** First *ghabara* (dust) — coming from outside, settling. Then *qatara* (soot, darkness) — overspreading, covering. The lower layer is dust, the upper layer is the darkening.

**تَرْهَقُ — root ر-ه-ق.** To overspread, to cover, to catch up with and settle upon; and from there *rahaq* — distress, pressure, compulsion. *Murāhiq* — one approaching puberty (one who is catching up).

The verb carries a movement of **catching up and settling**: something comes from behind and mounts upon it.

**The Qurʾān builds the exact opposite in another place** and uses the two words together:

> *"For those who do good there is the finest reward, and more. Neither a darkness (**قَتَر**) nor a humiliation covers their faces."* (Yūnus 10/26)

The same word (*qatar*), the same family of verbs (*yarhaqu*), with the verdict reversed. The last two verses of ʿAbasa are the negative of Yūnus 10/26.

### أُو۟لَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلْكَفَرَةُ ٱلْفَجَرَةُ — the final verdict

**Grammar.** *Ulāʾika humu* — a demonstrative pronoun + the pronoun of separation + a definite predicate. In Arabic this trio denotes **restriction (*ḥaṣr*)**: "those, precisely, are they". The same structure was used at al-Bayyina 98/6-7 (*ulāʾika hum sharru'l-bariyya* / *khayru'l-bariyya*).

**ٱلْكَفَرَة — root ك-ف-ر:** those who cover. Treated in the section on al-Kāfirūn.

**And now the finest close in the weave of the sūra:**

These people are called **those who cover**. And two verses earlier it was said that their faces were **covered in two layers**: dust and darkness.

The face of the one who covers is covered. The act's returning upon its doer is a symmetry established in the weave of the sūra — and it is another instance of the idea, treated in the section on al-Shams, that "the moral act returns upon its doer".

**ٱلْفَجَرَة — root ف-ج-ر.** And the sūra's fourth root of "splitting".

The concrete sense of the root is **to split, to make gush, to open**:
- *"And We made the earth gush forth with springs"* (al-Qamar 54/12: *wa fajjarna'l-arḍa ʿuyūnā*).
- *"Until you make a spring gush forth for us from the earth"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/90).
- **الْفَجْر** — the dawn. The splitting of the night.
- **الفُجُور** — sin; but what kind of sin? **To breach the boundary and pass beyond it, to burst outside the limit, to commit openly.**

At al-Shams 91/8 *fujūr* and *taqwā* were set face to face; that discussion was treated there.

**And now set the sūra's two uses of splitting side by side:**

| | 80/26 | 80/42 |
|---|---|---|
| Root | **ش-ق-ق** | **ف-ج-ر** |
| Who does it | God | Man |
| What is split | The soil | The boundary |
| Result | A shoot comes out, life comes | The limit is overstepped, a record is entered |

**The same movement, two directions.** Splitting the soil produces life; splitting the boundary produces ruin. Splitting is neither good nor bad; **what is split** is what decides.

In the picture of the table the sūra related one splitting, and in its last word it names another. This is a link standing in the text's own weave — and it explains why the sūra's last word is this word.

**And the forty-second verse is the counterpart of the sixteenth:**

*كِرَامٍ بَرَرَةٍ* (noble, given to goodness) ↔ *ٱلْكَفَرَةُ ٱلْفَجَرَةُ* (those who cover, those who overstep the boundary).

The same pattern, the same rhyme, exactly opposite content. Those who bear the text above, and those who cover it below. The sūra binds its own two ends with the same pattern of sound.

## The sūra as a whole

### The architecture

The sūra consists of five sections and two hinges:

| Verse | Section | Subject | Person |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-10 | **The incident** | A coming and a turning away | 3rd → 2nd person |
| **11** | *kallā* | **Hinge** | |
| 11-16 | **The text** | This is a reminder; where it comes from | 3rd person |
| 17-23 | **Man** | The imprecation and man's six-step story | 3rd person, general |
| **23** | *kallā* | **Hinge** | |
| 24-32 | **The table** | Water, soil, eight items of food | 1st person plural (We) |
| 33-42 | **That day** | The sound, the flight, two kinds of face | 3rd person |

**And the logic between the sections is this:**

The first section showed an error of measure: people were ranked according to the return they would bring. The remaining four sections show in turn where the right measure is to be taken from:

1. **From the text** (11-16) — the text is open to everyone; it does not change according to who is listening.
2. **From man's own story** (17-23) — everyone from the same drop, by the same measure, to the same grave.
3. **From the plate** (24-32) — everyone eats from the same rain, the same splitting of the soil.
4. **From the end** (33-42) — everyone hears the same sound, everyone is left alone.

**All four say the same thing: the differences between people are not fit to serve as a measure.** The ranking has no support, because nothing on which the ranking might rest comes outside the common ground.

### The roots that weave the sūra

**One. س-ف-ر — to bring to light.**

| Verse | Word | What |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | *safara* | Those who bring the text to light |
| 38 | *musfira* | Faces uncovered and lit up |

The sūra's hidden backbone, and one of its names. The network of the root — journey, book, ambassador, meal-cloth, broom — always relates a single act: **the lifting of the covering.**

**Two. ذ-ك-ر — to remind.**

| Verse | Word |
|---|---|
| 4 | *yadhdhakkaru* |
| 4 | *al-dhikrā* |
| 11 | *tadhkira* |
| 12 | *dhakarahu* |

Four times, within nine verses. The only thing the sūra says about the text: this is a reminder.

**Three. غ-ن-ي — needing nothing.**

| Verse | Word | Whose |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | *istaghnā* | The man who counts himself self-sufficient — a delusion |
| 37 | *yughnīh* | Everyone's, on that day — a reality |

The man had wanted *istighnāʾ*; it will be given.

**Four. ك-ف-ر — to cover.**

| Verse | Word | Its function |
|---|---|---|
| 17 | *mā akfarah* | The indictment |
| 42 | *al-kafaratu* | The decision |

The twenty-four verses that pass in between are the inventory of the evidence.

**Five. The roots of splitting.** *Shaqaqna'l-arḍa* (26) and *al-fajara* (42): the same movement, two directions. The splitting of the soil gives life; the splitting of the boundary is written to the account.

**Six. Symmetry of pattern.** *Kirām barara* (16) ↔ *al-kafaratu'l-fajara* (42): the same pattern, the same rhyme, opposite content.

### The structure of the sound

The sūra has one of the most marked sound-weaves among the short Meccan sūras, and the rhyme changes four times:

| Verse | Verse-ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1-10 | **-ā** (*alif maqṣūra*) | *tawallā, al-aʿmā, yazzakkā, al-dhikrā, istaghnā, taṣaddā, yazzakkā, yasʿā, yakhshā, talahhā* |
| 11-16 | **-arah** | *tadhkira, dhakarah, mukarrama, muṭahhara, safara, barara* |
| 17-23 | **-arah** | *akfarah, khalaqah, qaddarah, yassarah, aqbarah, ansharah, amarah* |
| 24-32 | **-ā / -abbā** | *ṭaʿāmih, ṣabbā, shaqqā, ḥabbā, qaḍbā, nakhlā, ghulbā, abbā, anʿāmikum* |
| 33-37 | **-īh** | *akhīh, abīh, banīh, yughnīh* |
| 38-42 | **-arah** | *musfira, mustabshira, ghabara, qatara, al-fajara* |

**And what should be recorded is this: the sound *-arah* comes round three times.** In 11-16 the text itself and its bearers, in 17-23 man's story, in 38-42 the last faces. Three sections, the same sound.

This binds two distant sections to one another by the ear. The opposition between *barara* and *al-fajara* is established by sound before the meaning is grasped.

### What it does not say: three wrong readings

**The first wrong reading: "The sūra condemns the rich and praises the poor."**

It does not say this. The fifth verse does not say *"to the one who is rich"*, it says *"to the one who counts himself self-sufficient"* — and this is not a condition of wealth but a condition of conviction. This distinction was treated in the sections on al-Layl and al-ʿAlaq.

Nor does the sūra declare the blind man superior in advance. The third verse says *"perhaps he will purify himself"*; the word *laʿalla* (perhaps) states a possibility, not a certainty. **The sūra does not build a ranking in the opposite direction; it makes ranking itself invalid.**

This distinction matters, because to read the sūra as "prefer the weak" is to repeat the same mistake in the opposite direction — measuring people in advance once again.

**The second wrong reading: using the sūra in order to argue about the Prophet's standing.**

The sūra's first ten verses take one moment of one person as their subject; its remaining thirty-two verses never come back to it. The text's own distribution of weight is plain. To put ten verses in the place of the whole sūra is to distort the text.

And the sūra's own stance prevents it as well: the criticism is said, it ends, it is not returned to, no apology is asked for. **The text closes the matter.** To keep open a matter that has been closed is to do the reverse of what the text does.

**The third wrong reading: using these verses in order to measure someone else.**

The sūra's third verse forbids exactly this: *what do you know?* If we cannot rank because we do not know where a person will arrive, then for the same reason we cannot judge either. To take the sūra in hand and say "that man too falls under ʿAbasa's description" is to re-join the vein the sūra cut.

Per the method (`USLUP.md`), a further point must be recorded: the sūra does not describe a group, a class, a section of society. It describes **attributes**.

### What this says to the present

**One. Attention is today the scarcest resource.**

What the sūra takes as its subject is neither money nor power: **attention.** Who is looked at, whose sentence can be cut short, who can be kept waiting.

And attention today is a scarcer resource than ever. Everyone can reach more people, but nobody has more attention. This makes ranking unavoidable — and spreads the mechanism the sūra criticises.

The sūra's question stands exactly here: **on what basis do you make your ranking, and where did you get that measure from?**

**Two. The calculation of return makes people invisible.**

The sentence "I must put my time where it is most productive" is true, and it is uttered every day. What the verse breaks is not the sentence but the sentence's hidden assumption: **the assumption that we can know who will bring what.**

And in practice this assumption turns out wrong again and again. Look at the history of an institution, a movement, a profession, and it is seen that the most decisive contributions have most often come from places that were never reckoned with. This is not a religious claim; it is an observation about the limit of prediction.

**Three. Courtesy is not the bending of the truth.**

What the sūra's first three verses establish is a rare balance: the criticism is made in full and made with the softest entry. It opens in the third person, continues with a question, then ascribes openly, then ends, and is never returned to.

Today criticism generally falls to one of two extremes: either it is not said (for fear of causing hurt), or in the saying of it the person is pulled down along with it. The sūra shows a third way — and the technique of the way is inside the grammar: **the act is first separated from its doer, the verdict is passed on the act, and then the doer is delivered up.**

**Four. Knowing the limit of responsibility is a condition of justice.**

The seventh verse — *"his not purifying himself is not upon you"* — looks like a sentence of relief. It is not. It is a sentence about **measure**.

Because whoever writes the outcome to his own account is compelled to sift people according to their contribution to it. And the first victim of the sifting is always the same: the one whose return is uncertain. To know where responsibility ends takes people out of being means.

**Five. Tracing the food.**

The twenty-fourth verse is a command **harder** to carry out today than on the day it was first sent down. In those days where what was on the plate came from could be seen: the field was there, the rain had been seen, the grain had passed through a known hand. Today food comes from the end of a chain too long for its source to be traced.

What the verse asks for is not a technical knowledge but **a way of looking**: to notice that what you eat has a story, and that the story begins from a part you had no hand in. A meal is the last link of a chain that begins with rain and continues with the cracking of the soil.

For a person who notices this, *"counting oneself self-sufficient"* grows harder. This may be why the sūra sets up the picture of the table precisely against the man of the fifth verse.

**Six. The fault no one saw.**

The sūra's most shaking fact is this: **the blind man did not see the frown.** He was not even hurt. And that movement was written into a text that will be recited until the resurrection.

What this says to the present is direct: the measure of a piece of conduct is not whether the other party noticed it. A slight no one saw is the same thing as a slight that was seen. And in practice, what we do where no one sees says more than what we do where someone does — because there the only thing holding us is the measure itself.

In the section on al-Māʿūn the exact reverse of this was recorded: repelling the orphan went unchecked because it carried no worldly sanction. ʿAbasa is the other face of the same principle: **an expression on a face was recorded although it had no worldly visibility at all.**

**Seven. The list of flight and the loneliness of the present.**

The five people between the thirty-fourth and thirty-sixth verses — brother, mother, father, wife, children — count out every bond a person can belong to. On that day all of them dissolve; and the reason is given too: each has a concern of his own that suffices him.

This is as much a diagnosis as a threat. For there is no need to wait for the resurrection for bonds to dissolve; **a man whose own concern is enough to fill him cannot attend to anyone today either.** The state the verse describes is a state that can be lived in this world too — and the sūra's first ten verses were exactly a small instance of it: in a gathering, a man occupied with a piece of business could not see the man at the door.

### Where this section does not speak with certainty

Per the method (`USLUP.md`), I list openly the places throughout the text where definite language was deliberately not used:

- **The report of the occasion of revelation.** There is broad agreement in the sources on the meaning of the report; there is none on its details. I stated that it is attributed to ʿĀʾisha and that it is found in sources among which is al-Tirmidhī; I did not quote its wording verbatim. There are differences among its variants.
- **The name of the blind Companion.** In the reports he appears as ʿAbd Allāh b. Umm Maktūm; in some transmissions his name is ʿAmr. I recorded this difference.
- The report **"Welcome to the man on whose account my Lord admonished me"**. Its chain is disputed; I did not present it as an established transmission.
- **The names of the leading men of Quraysh on the other side.** The reports do not agree with one another; I counted the names out, but I stated this inconsistency openly and accepted none of them as certain.
- **The disagreement over who is addressed.** The minority view was given in a table, without being concealed, together with its ground. I preferred the majority view and wrote its ground; **it is not binding.**
- **The text's criticism of its own prophet.** This was presented as **an observation**, not as a proof. The counter-objection (that the impression of sincerity might itself be a strategy) was recorded openly, and the answer to it was given not as a claim to certainty but as a piece of reasoning.
- **ʿĀʾisha's words about al-Aḥzāb 33/37.** It was transmitted with the statement that it is found in al-Bukhārī and Muslim; its wording was not quoted verbatim, its sense was given.
- **The أَأَنْ جَاءَهُ variant reading.** Its existence is recorded in the sources; since I was not certain which of the imāms it belongs to, I gave no name.
- **The فَقَدَّرَهُ / فَقَدَرَهُ variant reading.** In the same way no imām's name was given.
- **The أَنَّا / إِنَّا variant reading.** In the same way no imām's name was given.
- **What ٱلسَّبِيل is.** Three views were given in a table; I preferred the second (the path of guidance) and wrote its ground (the parallel of al-Insān 76/3). It is not binding, and it does not exclude the first view.
- **Whether the سَفَرَة are angels or human beings.** Three views were given; I preferred the view of the majority, but I stated that the second view is possible in respect of the language.
- **The reading of مَآ أَكْفَرَهُ.** Three readings were given in a table; I preferred the first, and I recorded the beauty of the second in the context as well.
- **The meaning of the word أَبّ.** It is not certain, and I wrote this openly. I transmitted the early report without a name, with the note that its chain is disputed.
- **The reading of "ṭaʿām" in the sense of knowledge or teaching.** I stated that it is transmitted in the sources; since I was not certain to whom it belongs I gave no name, and I wrote that the context does not permit it.
- **The links of derivation between حَبّ and حُبّ, and between ذَكَر and ذِكْر.** They were attributed to some of the lexicographers and the note "it is disputed" was entered.
- **The trio شق / فلق / فلح.** It was stated openly that the three are separate roots; no relation of derivation was established among them. The common picture was recorded as an observation, not as a ruling.
- **The grave and archaeology.** That deliberate burial is counted as an indicator of symbolic behaviour was transmitted as general knowledge; no claim of "miracle" was bound to the verse.
- **My own inferences.** The following are my reading, are not transmitted reports, and are not binding: the table of the "architecture of courtesy"; the conclusion drawn from the blind man's not having seen the frown; the link between *al-aʿmā* and al-Ḥajj 22/46; the link between *al-ṣākhkha* being a matter of hearing and the sūra's matter of seeing; the turn between *istaghnā* and *yughnīh*; the three logics of the order of flight (the difficulty of breaking, completeness, chronology); the parallel between *ṣāḥiba* and the *ṣāḥibukum* of al-Takwīr 81/22; the interpretation of the grammatical asymmetry between the two groups of faces; the two-directional reading of splitting between *shaqaqnā* and *al-fajara*; and the whole of the section "What this says to the present". These distinctions were stated within the text.
