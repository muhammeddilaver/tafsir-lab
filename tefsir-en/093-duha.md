# 93. Sūrat al-Ḍuḥā

Eleven verses. There is agreement close to unanimity that it is Meccan. It takes its name from its first word.

Few texts in the Qurʾān speak as directly as this to the personal situation of a prophet. The sūra opens no debate about doctrine, tells no story, lays down no ruling. To one addressee, at one moment, it says one thing: **you have not been left.**

But the manner of the saying is striking. The sūra does not say "do not grieve", does not say "be patient", does not say "everything will be put right". None of these is present. Instead it swears two oaths, rejects the claim in a single sentence, gives a promise of two sentences, then asks **three questions** and issues **three commands**. That is: the consolation is built not upon feeling but upon evidence; and it closes with a demand for conduct.

## The skeleton of the sūra

| Section | Verses | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Oath** | 1-2 | An oath by two states of stillness |
| **Negation** | 3 | The rejection of the claim: He has not forsaken, He has not turned away |
| **Promise** | 4-5 | What comes after is better; He will give, and you will be content |
| **Evidence** | 6-8 | Three past favours, in the form of three questions |
| **Command** | 9-11 | The turning of three favours into three commands |

These five sections are more orderly than one would expect in a text of consolation. The relation between the last two in particular is the backbone of the sūra and will be treated in detail below: **every favour received turns into a command given.**

## The structure of the sound

The first eight verses of the sūra end on the same sound — the *alif maqṣūra* closing in a long *ā*: *ḍuḥā, sajā, qalā, ūlā, tarḍā, āwā, hadā, aghnā.* An open sound, drawn out, flowing.

At the ninth verse this sound is cut off: *taqhar, tanhar, ḥaddith.* All three are short exits ending in closed syllables, on a *sukūn*.

The sūra thus divides in two within itself, and the division falls exactly on the border between consolation and command. The consolation flows; the command cuts. I record this as an observation about the sound; it is not a theological claim, but it is something heard in the reading.

The basmala was treated in earlier sūras and is not taken up again here; the verse numbers are counted without it.

## 93/1 — وَٱلضُّحَىٰ

*Waʾḍ-ḍuḥā*
"By the forenoon."

### The oath

The **وَ** at the head is the particle of oath; its function was treated in detail in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr. In brief: an oath does not convey information, it reinforces; and between the thing sworn by and the thing said a relation of evidence is most often established. In this sūra that relation is especially tight, and it does not become visible unless the two verses are read together.

### The root ضحى

The concrete sense of the root **ض-ح-و/ي** is the sun's rising above the horizon and the spreading of its light over the earth. The root describes not the sun itself but **the work the sun does**: the opening out of light, the withdrawing of shadows, the coming into view of what was covered.

The derivatives confirm this frame:

- **ضُحَى (*ḍuḥā*)** — the forenoon. The stretch of time when the sun has climbed some way after rising and the light has spread fully, but the heat of noon has not yet pressed down.
- **ضَحْوَة (*ḍaḥwa*)** — the earlier part of the same time.
- **أَضْحَى (*aḍḥā*)** — to enter upon the forenoon. In Arabic this verb came in time to be used also as a defective verb in the sense "to become, to come into that state" — the same development as *aṣbaḥa* (to enter upon the morning → to become).
- **ٱلْأَضْحَى / أُضْحِيَّة** — the Feast of Sacrifice and the sacrificial animal. The naming comes from the time: the sacrifice is slaughtered in the forenoon.
- **ضَاحِيَة (*ḍāḥiya*)** — the outward-facing, sun-catching edge of a settlement. The modern use of the word for a suburb comes from here.
- **ضَحِيَ (*ḍaḥiya*)** — to be exposed to the sun, to be left without shade. It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"There you will not thirst, nor be scorched by the sun"* (Ṭāhā 20/119).

The last item matters, because it shows the root's dimension of "being out in the open, being left uncovered". *Ḍuḥā* is not only a slot of hours; it is the state in which everything is seen and no shadow is left to hide in.

### *Ḍuḥā* in the Qurʾān

The word occurs in several places in the Qurʾān, and each time it is a moment of **openness and visibility**:

- *"Have the people of the towns felt secure that Our punishment might come upon them in the forenoon, while they are at play?"* (al-Aʿrāf 7/98). The forenoon is the time when a person is most heedless — the day has begun, the danger is supposed to be past.
- *"and let the people be gathered in the forenoon"* (Ṭāhā 20/59). The time Moses (Mūsā) chose for the meeting with the magicians. The reason is plain: the hour when everything is best seen and trickery is hardest.
- *"On the day they see it, it will be as though they had tarried only an evening, or its forenoon"* (al-Nāziʿāt 79/46). The shortness of a lifetime is measured by the two ends of a day.
- *"By the sun and its forenoon"* (al-Shams 91/1). Here the sun and the *ḍuḥā* are named separately — that is, *ḍuḥā* is not the sun itself but the state the sun produces.

### Why "the forenoon" and not "the sun"?

The oath could have been sworn by the sun directly; the Qurʾān does so elsewhere (al-Shams 91/1). What is chosen here is not the name of the body but **the name of the time**.

The consequence is this: the oath is sworn not by an object but by a condition. And the condition chosen is the most balanced moment of the day. The half-light of morning is past and the light is full; but the burning of noon is not yet there. There is neither indistinctness nor violence. Light, in a settled state.

Joined with the second verse, this observation brings out the sūra's first move.

## 93/2 — وَٱلَّيْلِ إِذَا سَجَىٰ

*Waʾl-layli idhā sajā*
"And by the night when it grows still."

### The root سجى

The root **س-ج-و** has two main senses, and the two come out at the same place:

**1. To come to rest, to settle, to grow calm.** The most typical example the dictionaries give is the sea: *baḥrun sājin* — a sea whose waves have died down and gone flat. In the same way *ṭarfun sājin*, a gaze grown calm and soft. Beneath the root lies the stopping of movement, the ending of turbulence.

**2. To cover, to overspread, to close over.** In Arabic, drawing a cloth over a dead body is called *tasjiya*; this usage is settled. Covering here is not concealing but closing over in order to quieten — like covering a child.

The two senses are not cut off from one another. The night quietens by covering. A cover stills what lies beneath it.

This root does not occur anywhere else in the Qurʾān; it is found in this verse alone. (This finding rests on memory, not on a full search.)

### إِذَا — "when it grows still"

The oath is sworn not by the night but by **a state** of the night. *Idhā* denotes a time whose occurrence is certain: "whenever it grows still — then."

The whole of the night, then, is not what is meant. At the beginning of the night movement continues — people return, doors are shut, sounds go on. The growing still is an advanced hour of the night: the moment when everything has settled into place and movement has ended.

### The Qurʾān's oaths by the night — which state is chosen

The Qurʾān swears by the night in many places, but each time together with a different verb. The verb chosen determines the subject of that sūra:

| Place | Expression | The state chosen |
|---|---|---|
| al-Layl 92/1 | *idhā yaghshā* | When it enshrouds — covering, overspreading |
| al-Muddaththir 74/33 | *idh adbara* | When it turns its back — withdrawal |
| al-Takwīr 81/17 | *idhā ʿasʿasa* | When it draws on / when it withdraws |
| al-Fajr 89/4 | *idhā yasri* | When it journeys on — flowing away |
| al-Shams 91/4 | *idhā yaghshāhā* | When it enshrouds the sun |
| al-Inshiqāq 84/17 | *wa mā wasaqa* | Together with what it gathers |
| **al-Ḍuḥā 93/2** | ***idhā sajā*** | **When it grows still — settling into calm** |

What the table shows is this: when the Qurʾān names the night it names not its darkness but **the work the night does**. And the work chosen in this sūra is the bringing to stillness.

### What the two oaths have in common: not light and darkness, but two stillnesses

This is the place in the sūra most easily missed.

At first glance the two oaths look like an opposition: day and night, light and darkness. In the Qurʾān that opposition really is set up — the first two verses of Sūrat al-Layl are exactly that: *"By the night when it enshrouds, and by the day when it shines forth"* (al-Layl 92/1-2). There one covers and the other uncovers; the contrast is clear.

Here there is no contrast.

Because the words chosen do not set up a contrast. **Ḍuḥā** is light in its settled, quietened state — neither the indistinctness of dawn nor the violence of noon. **The *sājī* night** is darkness in its settled, quietened state — neither the bustle of evening nor the shiver at the middle of the night.

Both oaths, then, are sworn by **the same thing**: by stillness. One a lit stillness, the other a dark stillness.

Its relation to the subject of the sūra is direct. The situation the addressee is in is a **silence**: the revelation has stopped, no voice is coming. And silence is the emptiness a person fills fastest with the worst interpretation. By swearing, the sūra does this: **it redefines the silence itself.**

The darkness of the night is not the cancelling of the day. The cutting off of light does not mean that light has ended. Night is not an absence but a part of an order; and within that order the night has a stillness of its own. This is the bond between the thing sworn by and the thing said under the oath: **both states are God's, and both are repose.**

This reading rests on the view that the oath establishes a "relation of evidence" (the grounds for that view were taken up in Sūrat al-ʿAṣr). It is not a binding preference; but it is the reading that works most consistently with the rest of the sūra.

One further note: the oath begins with *ḍuḥā* and continues with *the night* — that is, the order runs from light to darkness. This is also the order the addressee lived: first the revelation came, then it was cut off. The sūra follows the order of the addressee's own experience.

## 93/3 — مَا وَدَّعَكَ رَبُّكَ وَمَا قَلَىٰ

*Mā waddaʿaka Rabbuka wa mā qalā*
"Your Lord has not forsaken you, nor has He turned against you."

The answer to the oath. That is, this is the statement sworn to.

### وَدَّعَ — "He has not taken leave"

**Root: و-د-ع.** Its original sense is to leave, to abandon. From the same root:

- **وَدَاع (*wadāʿ*)** — the word said at parting. *Ḥajjat al-Wadāʿ* (the Farewell Pilgrimage) comes from here.
- **وَدِيعَة (*wadīʿa*)** — a thing left in trust. Something set down somewhere.
- **دَعْ (*daʿ*)** — the imperative "leave". It occurs in the Qurʾān: *"Do not obey the deniers and the hypocrites, disregard their harm, and rely upon God"* (al-Aḥzāb 33/48).

The two ends of the root are interesting: a thing set down is either **abandoned** or **entrusted**. The same verb carries two opposite outcomes — what determines the meaning of the leaving is the intention of the one who leaves.

**A point about the form.** The verse says *mā waddaʿaka* — the verb is in **Form II**, with the doubled middle radical. The plain form would have been *wadaʿaka* ("he left you"). Form II adds two things here: deliberateness and formality. To *waddaʿa* is not to leave in passing; it is **to end the relationship knowingly and openly.** One does not go back to a person one has taken leave of.

This choice of form appears to have been made for a reason: the claim being rejected was exactly this. The words "his Lord has forsaken him" do not mean "it no longer comes to him"; they mean "He is finished with him." The verse chooses the verb that matches the full weight of the claim in use, and negates it.

### قَلَىٰ — "nor has He turned against"

**Root: ق-ل-ي/و.** Intense loathing, hatred, a cooling so complete that one turns one's face from another.

The same root is also used in Arabic in this sense: **to fry in a pan.** *Miqlāt* means a frying pan; *qaliyya* is fried food. The classical dictionaries set these two senses side by side under the same entry.

The link between the two senses is the kind of relation the lexicographers establish, and it cannot be presented as a settled etymological ruling. But it is worth recording: rancour is a thing that burns on the inside. Loathing scorches the one who holds it. English carries the same intuition in expressions such as "burning with hatred" and "a hatred that consumes".

The root is rare in the Qurʾān. The other place it occurs is in what Lot (Lūṭ) said to his people: *"I am truly one of those who detest what you do"* (al-Shuʿarāʾ 26/168). The weight of the word is understood from this — it is not a slight hurt.

### Why both verbs?

"He has not forsaken" might have seemed enough. Why is there a second?

Because the fear of being abandoned has two distinct layers, and neither closes off the other:

- **Wadāʿ** is an **act**: the severing of the relationship. "He no longer comes."
- **Qalā** is a **state**: the reversal of feeling. "He no longer loves."

A person may grow cold towards another without leaving him; or may leave him without growing cold. The verse closes off both possibilities separately. The silence had two possible explanations — has the attention stopped, or has the affection ended — and both are rejected.

### The object left unsaid

Here is the grammatical point most dwelt upon in this verse: the first verb has an object (*waddaʿa-**ka***, "he forsook you"), the second has **none** (*mā qalā*, "he did not turn against" — against whom is not said).

*Mā qalāka* could have been said. It was not. Several explanations for this are given in the classical works of tafsīr, and they do not exclude one another:

**1. For the sake of the verse-ending.** Had it said *qalāka*, the verse would have ended in *-ka* and the *-ā* sound that runs through eight verses of the sūra would have been broken. This is the most concrete and least disputed reason. What must be said is this: an omission made for the sake of the verse-ending is not meaningless — while preserving the verse-ending, the Qurʾān also chooses which word it will drop.

**2. Propriety and courtesy.** This explanation is often stressed in the exegetical tradition: not naming the addressee as the object of a verb as heavy as "to loathe" places a distance between the verb and the addressee. Even while saying the thing it rejects, the sentence does not stick that thing to the addressee. The same can be built in English: "He has not forsaken you, nor has He turned against" — by not saying "you" in the second clause.

**3. Becoming absolute.** When the object is not said, the verb is left unattached and becomes general. Not "He did not turn against you", but simply "He did not turn against" — no loathing occurred at all.

All three explanations are reasonable, and the second and the third support one another. I adopt no definite preference; but I must state that the ground of the verse-ending is the soundest from the point of view of the language, because the other two remain at the level of interpretation.

### مَا — the choice of the negative particle

How the *lam* + imperfect (*muḍāriʿ*) construction works was treated in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ: it states a never-having-been, from the beginning, as a matter of kind.

Here **mā** + the perfect (*māḍī*) is used. This construction is closer to the rejection of an event claimed to have taken place within a particular stretch of time. And the context requires it: there is no general claim in play; there is an abandonment said to have occurred **in a particular period**. The verse takes aim at that period.

The difference is small but apt: the matter is not "does God never forsake His prophets" but "did He forsake you in these past days". The answer is given to that question.

### رَبُّكَ — "your Lord"

The verse does not say "God has not forsaken you"; it says **"your Lord"**. And this word occurs three times in the sūra: in verses 3, 5 and 11 — that is, within the negation, the promise and the final command.

The analysis of the word *Rabb* was made in the section on al-Fātiḥa. In brief: the one who nurtures, who raises stage by stage, who has disposal over him, who takes him as his own. That is, a name that **states a relationship**.

That the name which says he has not been forsaken should be exactly the name that looks after and brings up is no coincidence. The sentence does this: while rejecting the claim, it puts the ground of the rejection inside the name. One who raises you does not leave what he has raised out in the open. In the verses that follow, what this name does — sheltering, guiding, making free of need — will be counted out one by one.

## Historical background: the interruption of revelation

The event transmitted as the occasion of revelation of this sūra is one of the most often cited reports in the literature of tafsīr. The report must be conveyed as it stands and its state of soundness stated separately.

### The report

In a report transmitted from Jundub b. ʿAbdallāh in al-Bukhārī and Muslim it runs thus: the Prophet fell ill and for a night or two could not rise for prayer. A woman came and said, *"Muḥammad, I see that your satan has forsaken you"* (in another transmission, in the form "he no longer comes to you"). Thereupon *"Waʾḍ-ḍuḥā…"* was sent down.

There is no doubt that this report is found in the two Ṣaḥīḥs. But several qualifications must be entered:

- **The woman's name does not appear in the report.** The information identifying her as Umm Jamīl, the wife of Abū Lahab, stands not in the text of the sound report but in later sources. It is not a settled finding.
- The report does not say for how long the revelation was interrupted. Figures such as three days, twelve days, fifteen days, twenty-five days and forty days circulate in the sources. **None of these is certain, and they contradict one another.** Fixing the length does not appear possible.
- The report says that the Prophet "fell ill" and "could not rise at night"; the expression "the revelation was interrupted" has been added as interpretation in some transmissions of the report. What the sound text records, then, is the utterance of the mocking words and the descent of the sūra.

### How many interruptions?

In the biographical literature more than one period is spoken of under the heading *fatrat al-waḥy* (the interruption of revelation):

**The first:** the pause after the first revelation (the opening of Sūrat al-ʿAlaq); the resumption of revelation with Sūrat al-Muddaththir. The existence of this pause is widely accepted.

**The second:** a pause undergone in a later period and brought to an end by Sūrat al-Ḍuḥā.

Some scholars count these two as the same event and place al-Ḍuḥā at the end of the first interruption; some separate the two. **This question has not been settled definitively**, and the lists of the order of revelation are themselves matters of independent judgement — there is no ordering fixed by mass transmission. In most of these lists al-Ḍuḥā and al-Inshirāḥ are shown in early positions and one after the other.

### The Qurʾān's own record

What is interesting is this: the Qurʾān speaks of the interruption of revelation in another place too.

*"We do not descend save by the command of your Lord. To Him belongs what is before us, what is behind us, and what is between the two. And your Lord is not forgetful"* (Maryam 19/64).

This verse is a sentence spoken on behalf of the angels, and it is transmitted that it came upon a delay. It is reported that it came down when Gabriel was told, "if only you visited us more often"; this report is found in al-Bukhārī. The last sentence of the verse — *"And your Lord is not forgetful"* — does the same work as the third verse of al-Ḍuḥā: it separates an interruption from neglect.

### The realism of the words

Whatever the state of the debate over the report's soundness, the **kind of utterance** it describes is entirely realistic. The sentence "they have dropped him" is the cheapest and most effective way of demolishing a claim: without entering into the content of the claim at all, to say that its source has been cut off.

And the target these words select is the right one. The point at which a person is weakest is not doubting that he is in the right; it is **thinking that no one stands behind him**. The claim takes aim at exactly that.

The sūra's answer therefore does not change the subject; it stays in the same place: if the matter is who stands behind you, let us count who stands behind you.

## 93/4 — وَلَلْءَاخِرَةُ خَيْرٌ لَّكَ مِنَ ٱلْأُولَىٰ

*Wa la'l-ākhiratu khayrun laka mina'l-ūlā*
"And what comes after is better for you than what came first."

### Emphasis

The sentence begins with **وَلَ**: *wāw* + the *lām* of inception. This *lām* comes at the head of a nominal sentence and reinforces it. The sentence, then, is not a bare statement but an underlined one.

The third verse was negative — it rejected a claim. The fourth verse moves to the affirmative and carries the time forward. The consolation is built in three steps: there was no severance in the past (3), what lies ahead is better (4), and you will be given (5).

### ٱلْأُولَىٰ — why not "*dunyā*"?

In the Qurʾān this contrast is generally built with another pair of words: **al-dunyā** and **al-ākhira**. That pair occurs in dozens of places.

Here **al-ūlā** is chosen instead of *dunyā*. The difference matters:

- **دُنْيَا (*dunyā*)** — from the root *d-n-w*: the near, the low. The word states a **value**. "The nearest", that is, what is close to hand, what stands low.
- **أُولَىٰ (*ūlā*)** — from the root *ʾ-w-l*: first, the one that comes before. The word states an **order**.

The verse, then, does not **disparage** the life the addressee is in; it merely puts it in order. It does not say "this is worthless, look to the other"; it says "this one is first, the other comes after, and the one that comes after is better".

This choice suits the context. The addressee is at this moment someone struggling in the first phase. To tell a person who is struggling "what you are in was worthless anyway" is not consolation but belittlement. The verse does not do this.

### Two readings

*Al-ākhira* and *al-ūlā* in the verse are two adjectives, and what they qualify is not stated. The after of what, the before of what? Two readings are recorded in the classical works of tafsīr:

| Reading | Meaning | Ground | Weak point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The abode of the hereafter** | The next world is better than this world | The settled usage in the Qurʾān; the word *al-ākhira* mostly comes in this sense | Why should a statement this general be limited by the personal qualification *"for you"*? |
| **The end of the matter** | The end of your situation will be better than its beginning | The context: the sūra is speaking to the state of one person at a particular moment; the next verse says "He will give"; verses 6-8 describe the deprivation at the beginning | The use of the word *al-ākhira* in this sense is rarer |

The second reading is strong in point of context and explains the qualification **"for you" (*laka*)**. The first reading rests on the settled sense of the word.

The two do not exclude one another. The end of a life being better than its beginning is a particular case of the hereafter being better than this world; the second reading contains the first as well. I state no definite preference.

### خَيْرٌ لَّكَ — "for you"

The sentence does not say "the hereafter is better"; it says **"it is better for you"**. This qualification looks small, but it takes the statement out of being a piece of general information and binds it to one addressee.

This is the difference between stating a general truth and telling someone something about himself. From beginning to end the sūra does the second: it speaks to one addressee, with the data of his own life.

**A note on the language:** *khayr* here is an elative — "better". In Arabic this pattern normally comes on the *afʿal* form (*akbar*, *aḥsan*), but the words *khayr* and *sharr* are used without being put into that form, with their hamzas dropped. One does not say *akhyar*; one says *khayr*, and it means "better". This has to be pointed out, because in the English rendering it is not visible where the comparative force of "better" comes from.

## 93/5 — وَلَسَوْفَ يُعْطِيكَ رَبُّكَ فَتَرْضَىٰ

*Wa la-sawfa yuʿṭīka Rabbuka fa-tarḍā*
"And your Lord will give to you, and you will be content."

### وَلَسَوْفَ — a threefold emphasis

Three elements are stacked one upon another: the *wāw*, the *lām* of inception, and *sawfa*.

**سَوْفَ (*sawfa*)** is the particle of the future. Arabic has two instruments for stating the future: **سَ (*sa-*)**, attached to the front of the verb, and **سَوْفَ**, which comes as a separate word. The general opinion of the lexicographers is that *sawfa* states a wider and more distant future. That is, not "presently" but "in time to come".

The *lām* and *sawfa* coming together is rare in Arabic. Their combination means this: **certainly, but the time is not now.**

In point of context this is an entirely honest expression. The addressee's expectation was "now"; that is exactly why the silence weighed. The verse does not pull the expectation into the present — it says "in time to come". But by setting an emphasis before that "in time to come", it separates the delay from uncertainty.

### يُعْطِيكَ — "He will give"

**Root: ع-ط-و/ي**, in Form IV **aʿṭā**: to give. The properties of this verb were treated in the section on Sūrat al-Kawthar (*innā aʿṭaynāka'l-kawthar*). In brief: some of the lexicographers draw a distinction between *aʿṭā* and *ītāʾ* and characterise *aʿṭā* as a giving of the kind that is a gift, without return. The distinction is not absolute, but it is recorded.

**And here, exactly as in al-Kawthar, what will be given is not said.**

*Yuʿṭīka* — "He will give to you". Give what? The sentence has no object. This performs **the reverse function** of the omission in the third verse: there the dropping of the object protected (it kept the verb of loathing away from the addressee); here the dropping of the object **opens out**. What is not named cannot be bounded.

In a promise, the amount not being stated can mean one of two things: either what will be given is small and it is not wished to be said, or it is so wide that no one name can close it off. The next sentence determines which.

### فَتَرْضَىٰ — "and you will be content"

The finest sentence in the sūra.

**فَ** is the *fāʾ* of cause: He will give, and **as a result of that** you will be content.

**Root: ر-ض-و/ي.** To be content, to be well pleased, to accept. Its derivatives: *riḍā*, *marḍāt* (good pleasure), *rāḍī* (the one who is content), *marḍī/marḍiyya* (the one with whom one is content).

**The real matter is the measure.** The measure of a promise is normally **the object**: "I will give this much", "I will give this". An amount or a kind is stated, and the addressee weighs it accordingly.

Here the measure is not the object but **the state of the subject**. How much will be given is not said; what is said is **as much as will content you**.

In practice this difference is large. A word given in terms of an amount leaves open the question whether the amount will be enough. A person may receive a great deal and still not be content — this is something observed, the rule rather than the exception. The verse closes this gap: the promise is tied not to the amount of what is given but to **the satisfaction of the one who receives**.

### The mutuality of *riḍā*

In the Qurʾān *riḍā* is rarely one-sided. In the places where the word occurs most densely it works both ways:

- *"God is well pleased with them, and they are well pleased with Him"* (al-Bayyina 98/8; also al-Māʾida 5/119, al-Tawba 9/100, al-Mujādala 58/22).
- *"O soul at rest! Return to your Lord, well pleased and well pleasing"* (al-Fajr 89/27-28).

The last example matters particularly, because it is one of the sūras of the same period and it describes *riḍā* as a **point of arrival**. Al-Ḍuḥā 5 promises that point of arrival to one person.

### What is the promise about? — the disagreement

Various explanations of the content of this promise are given in the classical works of tafsīr: his station in the hereafter, al-Kawthar, intercession, the forgiveness of his community, the establishment of the religion upon the earth.

In one report it is transmitted that the Prophet said he would not be content as long as even one person of his community remained in the Fire. This report is frequently cited in the books of tafsīr in connection with this verse, but **its soundness is disputed** and it is not found with this wording in the sound collections of ḥadīth. Its being transmitted in the language of certainty must be approached with caution.

The text itself does not state the content. And its not stating it follows from what was said above: what is named is bounded.

## 93/6 — أَلَمْ يَجِدْكَ يَتِيمًا فَـَٔاوَىٰ

*A-lam yajidka yatīman fa-āwā*
"Did He not find you an orphan and give you shelter?"

Here the sūra changes direction. The first five verses gave **statements**; with the sixth verse the **questions** begin.

### أَلَمْ — not a question but an acknowledgement

**A-lam** = the hamza (interrogative) + *lam* (negation). In Arabic this compound mostly performs the function of **taqrīr**: a question asked not to obtain information but to make the addressee say "yes" himself.

To see why this is so powerful a method, consider the same sentence as a statement: *"He found you an orphan and gave you shelter."* The meaning is the same. But in that sentence the addressee is a listener; he may agree with what is said or he may not.

When it is said, *"Did He not find you an orphan and give you shelter?"*, the addressee's position changes. He is no longer a listener but a **witness**. He will give the answer out of his own life. And he cannot object to the answer he gives out of his own life.

This is not a debating technique but a choice about the form of the consolation. A word said from outside is of no use when a person does not believe it about himself. That is why the sūra does not tell him; it puts the question to him.

### Three questions, one particle

In the sixth verse there is *a-lam yajidka*. In the seventh and eighth verses the interrogative particle is **not repeated**: *wa wajadaka… wa wajadaka…* Only the verb "he found" repeats.

This shows that the three questions are built in one breath, as a single question. The force of the interrogative particle continues but is not marked. Not three separate interrogations, but one reminder in three items.

### وَجَدَ — to find

**Root: و-ج-د.** Its derivatives spread over a wide field:

- **وُجُود (*wujūd*)** — existence. To be found.
- **وَجْد (*wajd*)** — rapture, the state of finding something intensely.
- **وُجْد (*wujd*)** — power, means. In the Qurʾān: *"Lodge them where you dwell, according to your means"* (al-Ṭalāq 65/6).

**A grammatical point:** *wajada* here is a verb that takes two objects — "He found you an orphan". That is, not merely "to find" but "to find in a state". The second object of the verb (*yatīman*, *ḍāllan*, *ʿāʾilan*) states the **condition** of the one found.

This has a consequence: the verb describes not searching and coming across, but **the determining of a condition**. "To find" here is not a discovery; it is a state's being known.

### يَتِيم — orphan

**Root: ي-ت-م.** The original sense the dictionaries give is singleness and being separated off. The idea lying beneath the root is having broken away from a whole, having been left without a fellow or a support.

The clearest indication of this is the word's use outside the human case: **دُرَّة يَتِيمَة (*durra yatīma*)** — a pearl with no fellow and no like, a single pearl. There is no mourning or pain here; there is only singleness.

Used of a human being, *yatīm* means **a child who has lost his father**. (A distinction the lexicographers record: in the human case orphanhood is on the father's side, in animals on the mother's. The reason appears to have to do with which side livelihood and protection come from.)

At the core of the word, then, there is not pain but **the lack of support**. An orphan means one who has no one to stand behind him.

This is directly related to the context of the sūra. The mockery directed at the addressee was exactly this: no one is left behind you. The sūra recalls that the same claim held at the beginning of his life too — and what happened then.

### Its historical counterpart

According to what the biographical sources transmit, the Prophet's father ʿAbdallāh died before he was born. He lost his mother Āmina at about six, his grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib at about eight. Thereafter he grew up under the protection of his uncle Abū Ṭālib.

These details are transmitted from the historical sources and there are small differences in their dates. But the general line of the picture matters: **the sheltering is not a single event but several changes of hand, one after another.** The verse's saying "*fa-āwā*" gathers this whole chain into one verb.

### آوَىٰ — He gave shelter

**Root: أ-و-ي.** To take refuge, to be sheltered; in Form IV (*āwā*), to shelter, to give a place of refuge.

Derivatives:

- **مَأْوَى (*maʾwā*)** — a shelter, the place taken refuge in. In the Qurʾān it is used both for the Garden and for the Fire: *"jannat al-maʾwā"* (al-Najm 53/15) and *"maʾwāhum al-nār"* (Āl ʿImrān 3/151). That is, *maʾwā* states no value; it means only "the place finally arrived at".

Its other uses in the Qurʾān illuminate this verse:

- Noah's (Nūḥ's) son, fleeing the flood: *"I shall take refuge on a mountain that will protect me from the water"* (Hūd 11/43). The answer is this: *"Today there is nothing that protects from God's command."* Here the refuge is one **the person chose himself**, and it does not hold.
- The youths of the cave: *"Take refuge in the cave"* (al-Kahf 18/16).
- And the closest parallel: *"Remember when you were few on the earth, seen as weak, fearing that people would snatch you away; then He sheltered you"* (al-Anfāl 8/26). The same verb (*fa-āwākum*), the same structure, but the addressee is a community.

The last verse says to a community what al-Ḍuḥā 6 says to one person. This repetition between the two texts shows that the principle is not particular to one person.

### فَ — the adjacency of the consequence

In all three verses the same letter is present: *fa-āwā*, *fa-hadā*, *fa-aghnā*.

**فَ** in Arabic states *taʿqīb* (coming immediately after) and causation. That is: He found — and immediately upon His finding, as a result of His finding, He sheltered. There is no gap between.

In all three verses this letter builds the same pattern: **a state of lack → a direct response.** The structure of the sūra becomes visible here.

### To shelter is not to remove orphanhood

This is a point I must record as my own reading.

The verse does not say "He found you an orphan and gave your father back". The orphanhood was not abolished. What was lost was not brought back. What was done is something else: **sheltering.**

This distinction determines the sūra's understanding of consolation. The response is not the making good of the lack; it is being held despite the lack. What a person has lost does not come back — but he may not have been left out in the open in his state of loss.

Seeing the difference between the two is necessary in order to understand what the sūra promises and what it does not. The verse does not say that the past will be erased.

## 93/7 — وَوَجَدَكَ ضَآلًّا فَهَدَىٰ

*Wa wajadaka ḍāllan fa-hadā*
"And did He not find you not knowing the way, and guide you?"

The most disputed verse of the sūra. Because of the load the word carries, the root has to be opened up first and the disagreement then given in a table.

### The root ضلّ — not moral but spatial

**Root: ض-ل-ل.** The usual English renderings of this root ("error", "straying", "misguidance") carry a heavy moral load. The Arabic root itself does not carry that load.

The original sense of the root: **to be lost inside something and become invisible, to be unable to find the way, to lose one's track.**

There are three firm proofs of this from within the Qurʾān:

**1.** *"They say: when we have been lost in the earth, shall we really be in a new creation?"* (al-Sajda 32/10). Here *ḍalalnā* means "when we have mingled with the soil and disappeared". Neither sin nor straying is in play — only vanishing and becoming invisible.

**2.** *"My Lord neither errs nor forgets"* (Ṭāhā 20/52). Moses' answer to Pharaoh. Here *lā yaḍillu* is used of God, and its sense is "He does not go wrong in knowledge". The word negates not a moral defect but a lack of knowledge.

**3.** *"By God, you are still in your old bewilderment"* (Yūsuf 12/95). The words said to Jacob (Yaʿqūb) by those around him. Here *ḍalāl* is used of a prophet, and the speakers are not accusing him of denial — they find it unreasonable that he still believes Joseph (Yūsuf) to be alive. The same root occurs at Yūsuf 12/8 as well, in what the brothers say about their father.

These three usages show that the root carries no verdict about belief, and that it is the context that establishes the verdict.

### The difference from the "*ḍāllīn*" of al-Fātiḥa

The same root occurs in the last verse of al-Fātiḥa: *wa lāʾḍ-ḍāllīn*. As was treated there, "*ḍāllīn*" in that verse is a group set opposite "those who have incurred anger": **those who have the intention but not the knowledge** — those who are seeking, but who arrive at the wrong place because they do not know the way.

The use in al-Ḍuḥā 7 differs from this and must not be confused with it:

| | **al-Fātiḥa 1/7** | **al-Ḍuḥā 93/7** |
|---|---|---|
| The function of the word | A lasting **attribute** — the description of a group | A temporary **state** — an initial condition |
| Time | Ongoing; a plural active participle, stating continuity | Finished; *fa-hadā* comes immediately after |
| The kind of sentence | A prayer — "let us not fall into that state" | A statement — "you were brought out of that state" |
| Its opposite | *al-munʿam ʿalayhim* — the path of those favoured | *hadā* — the way was shown, the state was closed |
| Its load | In the end, a negative position | The middle of a list of favours |

The most decisive difference is the last. Al-Ḍuḥā 7 stands in the middle of a three-item **list of favours**. In al-Fātiḥa, by contrast, what is described is a position one asks to be spared.

That the two usages share the same word confirms that the word itself carries no moral judgement: the judgement is established by the context, not by the root.

### What does "*ḍāllan*" mean here? — the disagreement

| View | What it means | Ground | Weak point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Being unaware of revelation** (the most widespread) | In a state of not knowing what the Book is and what the sacred law is | The Qurʾān's own expression: *"You did not know what the Book is, nor what faith is"* (al-Shūrā 42/52). This verse is almost a sentence of tafsīr | — |
| **A state of seeking and bewilderment** | Seeking the truth but not knowing how to find it | The reports of his withdrawing to Ḥirāʾ before prophethood; the root's sense of "being unable to find the way" | The text speaks of no seeking; it remains at the level of inference |
| **Literally getting lost** | His actually losing his way in childhood or on a journey | It fits the most concrete sense of the root; reports to this effect are transmitted | The reports do not agree with one another and their chains are held to be weak; moreover a personal mishap does not appear large enough to enter a list of "favours" |
| **Having gone unappreciated** | His worth not understood among his people, like a treasure gone missing | Taking the word in the sense of a passive participle | It is forced in point of language; *ḍāll* is an active participle |

**The reading the exegetical tradition excludes unanimously** is the sense of "straying/denial". There are two grounds for this:

**The first is theological.** On the question of the *ʿiṣma* (protectedness) of the prophets there are differences of detail among the schools of Islam — which faults they were protected from, and in which period, has been debated. But on the point that **they were protected from associating partners with God even before prophethood** there is no division among the schools.

**The second, and the stronger, is textual:** the three verses are in the same pattern.

### The structure is itself a proof

This is the soundest way of understanding the verse, and the point I want to underline.

The three verses have exactly the same skeleton: *wajadaka* + [state] + *fa*- + [response].

- To be an **orphan** is not a sin. It is a deprivation.
- To be **short of means** is not a sin. It is a deprivation.

The middle one cannot be a sin either — because it stands inside the same list, the same pattern, the same breath. All three are **states of deprivation**:

| Verse | Deprivation | The lack of what |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | يَتِيمًا — orphan | **Belonging** — there is no one holding you |
| 7 | ضَآلًّا — not knowing the way | **Direction** — you do not know where to go |
| 8 | عَآئِلًا — short of means | **Livelihood** — what you are to live on is not settled |

These are the three basic layers of a human life: who holds you, where you are going, what you live on. The sūra counts all three, and says that in all three the response was given.

This table is an inference, but it stands in the text at the level of grammar and structure; while stating that it is my own reading, I think it is the strongest evidence within the text that we have for resolving the disagreement. It is not binding.

### هَدَىٰ — He guided

**Root: ه-د-ي.** To show the way, to bring to the right direction. The analysis of the word was made in the section on al-Fātiḥa.

A note belonging here: the concrete idea lying beneath the root is not only "to point out" but **to hold out gently**. From the same root come *hadiyya* (a thing presented, a gift) and *hady* (the offering driven to the Kaʿba). *Hidāya* is to show the way and to conduct along that way; it is not to describe it from a distance.

### The response verbs have no object

A small but consistent observation: *fa-āwā*, *fa-hadā*, *fa-aghnā* — in none of the three is there a "you". In the verbs of finding, by contrast, it is there: *wajada**ka***.

That is, the addressee is named explicitly at the moment of **being found**; he is not named at the moment of **response**.

The primary reason for this is the verse-ending: had it said *āwāka*, *hadāka*, *aghnāka*, the eight-verse fabric of sound in the sūra would have been broken. This must be stated, because otherwise we would be inventing an explanation based on meaning.

Yet the outcome is meaningful all the same: the response verbs are left unattached, in an absolute state. Sheltering, showing the way and making free of need stand not as a transaction registered to one person but as work that has been done.

## 93/8 — وَوَجَدَكَ عَآئِلًا فَأَغْنَىٰ

*Wa wajadaka ʿāʾilan fa-aghnā*
"And did He not find you short of means, and make you free of need?"

### عَآئِل — *ʿāʾil*

**Root: ع-ي-ل.** Poverty, straitness of livelihood. *ʿĀla yaʿīlu* — he fell into poverty, his means grew narrow.

The same root in the Qurʾān: *"And if you fear poverty…"* (al-Tawba 9/28).

**There is a neighbouring root, and the two must not be confused:** the root **ع-و-ل**. *ʿĀla yaʿūlu* — to be under obligation to maintain, to provide for. The words *ʿāʾila* (family) and *ʿiyāl* (those a person supports) come from this field. The same root is also discussed in the Qurʾān in the sense "to deviate from justice": *"That is more likely that you will not do injustice"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/3) — the meaning of the verb *taʿūlū* here is disputed among the exegetes.

The lexicographers keep these two roots apart. But that their fields of meaning should be neighbours is no coincidence: there is a direct relation between a household to be provided for and straitness of livelihood.

**Why not "*faqīran*"?** For poverty the Qurʾān generally uses the word **فقير (*faqīr*)**. The concrete sense of the root *faqr* has to do with the breaking of the spine, or with the vertebra (*faqār*) — that is, the bending of the back, becoming unable to carry.

Here *ʿāʾil* is chosen. The difference is fine and not suited to a definite ruling; but *ʿāʾil* stands closer to "one who struggles to make his living come out even" than to "one who has no property". The verse-ending may also have played a part.

### أَغْنَىٰ — He made free of need

**Root: غ-ن-ي.** It is usually rendered in English as "wealth", but the original sense of the root is **freedom from need**. *Istighnāʾ* means not standing in need of a thing.

**Al-Ghanī** is one of the names of God and carries exactly this sense: *"O people! You are the ones in need of God; and God is al-Ghanī, al-Ḥamīd"* (Fāṭir 35/15). This connection was made when the word *al-Ṣamad* was treated in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ: the one who is not in need, the one who is needed.

The root has a second use as well, and it occurs often in the Qurʾān: **aghnā ʿan** — "to ward something off from someone, to be of avail to him". *"His wealth and what he earned did not avail him"* (al-Masad 111/2); *"My wealth has not availed me"* (al-Ḥāqqa 69/28). That is, *ghinā* is not the amount of what is in hand but **the usefulness of what is in hand**.

### What is this "wealth"?

According to the historical sources, the Prophet's material situation changed through his marriage to Khadīja and through trade. But care is needed here: **the Prophet was never among the wealthy of Mecca**, and he spent the greater part of his life plainly. Many of the reports transmitted about the Medinan period describe that no fire was lit in his house for long stretches and that he kept to a plain living.

Reading *aghnā* solely as an accumulation of property therefore puts the verse into conflict with history.

A second reading resolves this difficulty: **ghinā al-nafs** — contentment of heart, the one who wants coming to want no longer. This reading is supported by a ḥadīth: that the Prophet said *"Wealth is not an abundance of goods; true wealth is the wealth of the soul"* is transmitted from Abū Hurayra in al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

And this reading is confirmed within the sūra itself. The fifth verse had said "you will be content". **Riḍā and ghinā look to the same place:** both are measured not by amount but by the state of the one who wants.

The root's sense of "freedom from need" already contains this. A person becomes free of need in one of two ways: either what he has in hand increases, or his wanting decreases. The word is wide enough to cover both, and the verse does not narrow it.

---

## On the three questions

### The direction of the evidence

The fourth and fifth verses concerned **the future**: what comes after will be better, you will be given. The verses from the sixth to the eighth turn to **the past**.

This is not an accidental order. A promise about the future has no verifiable side to it — it has not yet happened. That is why the sūra does not leave the promise hanging in the air; it draws its guarantee from the past: **look at what was done before.**

This is the Qurʾān's characteristic method of evidence. In speaking to a person about his future, it reminds him of his past. And what is recalled is not a general history but **the addressee's own life**.

### The common structure of the verbs

In all three verses there is the same order:

**A condition is determined** (*wajadaka* + deprivation) → **a response is given** (*fa* + verb).

And what the three response verbs have in common is this: none of them erases the deprivation retroactively. The orphanhood was not removed; a shelter was given. The not-knowing was not turned back into knowledge; the way was shown. The poverty was not taken out of history; freedom from need was given.

In all three verses, then, what is described is **not the making good of the lack, but the not being left in the state of lack**. This is the character of the sūra's consolation, and it is realistic: the past is not erased, but it is shown that in the past you were not left out in the open.

### Who is counting the favours?

One further observation: in these three verses the one counting the favours is **God**. The addressee only confirms.

In the eleventh verse the same work will be asked of the addressee: *"Speak of the favour of your Lord."* That is, the sūra first does itself what it commands. The text sets an example by counting; then it says "now you count".

This is a correspondence set up between form and content. This connection will be returned to below, at verse 11.

## 93/9 — فَأَمَّا ٱلْيَتِيمَ فَلَا تَقْهَرْ

*Fa-ammāʾl-yatīma fa-lā taqhar*
"So as for the orphan, do not crush him."

The sūra enters its third and last section. The sound changes, the kind of sentence changes, the direction changes.

### فَ — the letter of consequence

The **فَ** at the head of the ninth verse binds a whole section to what precedes it: since it is so, since these things were done for you — **then.**

This single letter carries out the sūra's most important move. The consolation is tied to a demand for conduct. The text does not comfort the addressee and leave him; from the place where it comforts him it draws out an obligation.

### The أَمَّا … فَ pattern

**أَمَّا (*ammā*)** is a particle in Arabic that states *tafṣīl* (setting out in detail) and condition. Its sense is roughly this: "whatever else may be the case, when it comes to this…"

The pattern has two functions:

**1. Fronting and emphasis.** The element that comes after *ammā* is brought to the head of the sentence and underlined.

**2. Generality and certainty.** Because the particle carries the sense of a condition, the ruling is tied not to a particular case but to every instance of that case. Not "this orphan", but "whenever it is a matter of an orphan, every time".

**A point about word order:** the sentence could have been built in the form *fa-lā taqhariʾl-yatīma* ("do not crush the orphan"). It was not. The object (*al-yatīma*) has been drawn in front of the verb, to the very head of the sentence. In Arabic this states particular concern and emphasis: what is at issue is the orphan.

The word's coming in the **accusative** (*al-yatīm**a***) confirms this — though fronted, it is still the object of the verb that follows.

### The root قهر

**Root: ق-ه-ر.** To overcome and force into submission, to break the will, to crush.

The word describes not only the use of force but **a difference of force**. *Qahr* does not occur between equals; it is possible only where one side is in a position to crush the other.

This root occurs in the Qurʾān among the names of God:

- **ٱلْقَهَّار (*al-Qahhār*)** — Yūsuf 12/39, al-Raʿd 13/16, Ṣād 38/65, al-Zumar 39/4, Ghāfir 40/16.
- **ٱلْقَاهِر (*al-Qāhir*)** — *"And He is the Subduer over His servants"* (al-Anʿām 6/18, 61).

**And the point that comes out of this is one of the sharpest places in the sūra:** God, who is al-Qahhār, forbids His servant to crush.

The reason is clear once it is thought through: *qahr* is the act of the one who holds the power. Since no one holds power in the face of God, His being *al-Qahhār* is not an injustice but a description of position. But among human beings the distribution of power is unequal — and **the orphan is the lowest end of that distribution.**

The verse forbids the place where power can be used most easily and most unpunished.

### The orphan in the Qurʾān

The orphan is one of the Qurʾān's most insistent subjects, and in almost every place he occurs he is set up as a test:

- *"Those who consume the property of orphans wrongfully are only filling their bellies with fire"* (al-Nisāʾ 4/10).
- *"That is the one who repels the orphan"* (al-Māʿūn 107/2). In Sūrat al-Māʿūn this act is counted as the first sign of denying the religion.
- *"No! Rather, you do not honour the orphan"* (al-Fajr 89/17).
- *"An orphan of near kinship…"* (al-Balad 90/15).
- *"They give food, though they themselves need it, to the needy, the orphan and the captive"* (al-Insān 76/8).

What these verses have in common is that they use the treatment of the orphan as **a measure**. How one behaves towards someone who cannot defend himself shows what a person really believes — because there is no expectation of return there.

What distinguishes al-Ḍuḥā 9 is that it ties this measure to **a personal memory**. The other verses set the orphan up as a test; al-Ḍuḥā says to the addressee, "you were he".

## 93/10 — وَأَمَّا ٱلسَّآئِلَ فَلَا تَنْهَرْ

*Wa ammāʾs-sāʾila fa-lā tanhar*
"And as for the one who asks, do not rebuff him."

### سَآئِل — two senses at once

**Root: س-أ-ل.** In Arabic this root does two jobs at once, and both are expressed by the same verb:

- **To ask a question** — to request information.
- **To ask, to beg** — to request goods or help.

English runs with Arabic on the verb here: the single verb "to ask" reaches both. But the noun splits — *questioner* on the one side, *beggar* or *petitioner* on the other — whereas *sāʾil* is one word for both. The Qurʾān uses it in both senses:

- Asking for information: *"They ask you about the spirit"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/85), *"Ask the people of remembrance, if you do not know"* (al-Naḥl 16/43).
- Asking for help: *"In their wealth there is a due for the one who asks and the one who is deprived"* (al-Dhāriyāt 51/19; also al-Maʿārij 70/24-25).

In the classical works of tafsīr both senses are given for the *sāʾil* of this verse and no preference is imposed. The reason is the word itself: there is no narrowing indication in the verse.

As will be seen below, in point of the sūra's structure this width is not a vagueness but a gain.

### The root نهر

**Root: ن-ه-ر.** To snap at harshly, to scold by shouting, to drive off by advancing on someone.

From the same root come **نَهْر (*nahr*, river)** and **نَهَار (*nahār* — daytime)**. Some of the lexicographers set up the connection thus: a river is water that flows by cutting open its bed; *nahār* is light that spreads and widens; *intahara* is to advance on someone with the voice. The common idea is advancing by force and cutting through.

This connection is a relation established by the lexicographers, and I do not present it as a settled etymological ruling.

The act the word describes is not a physical harm; it is **a driving away done with the voice**. Not striking but shouting. Not shutting the door in his face but scolding him at the door.

### The other place the same verb occurs

In the Qurʾān this verb occurs once more in the form of a prohibition, and its addressee is surprising:

*"Your Lord has decreed that you serve none but Him, and that you be good to parents. If one or both of them reach old age with you, do not say to them so much as 'ugh', **and do not rebuff them**, and speak to them a gracious word"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/23).

The Qurʾān's prohibition of rebuffing, then, has been set in two places: on **parents** and on **the one who asks**. This parallel is striking, because the two groups look very far apart — one the nearest, the other most often unknown. What they have in common is that both are asking something of the person before them and are not in a position to answer back.

### And a completing verse

A few verses later Sūrat al-Isrāʾ takes up the same subject once more and makes clear what this verse forbids:

*"And if you must turn away from them, awaiting a mercy from your Lord which you hope for, then speak to them a gentle word"* (al-Isrāʾ 17/28).

This verse says directly: **you may have nothing to give.** In that case what must be done is not to announce in a harsh tone that your hand is empty; it is to speak a gentle word.

What is forbidden, then, is not the withholding but **the rebuffing**. Read together, the two verses make the boundary of al-Ḍuḥā 10 clear.

### What the two commands have in common: where the threshold lies

This is the most practical place in the sūra, and it goes unnoticed.

The ninth and tenth verses are **not commands to give.**

"Feed the orphan" is not said. "Give to the one who asks" is not said. What is said is **"do not crush"** and **"do not rebuff"**.

This has two consequences, and the two work in opposite directions:

**It lowers the threshold.** These commands can be carried out even by someone who has nothing in hand. One with nothing to give can still manage not to crush and not to rebuff. The obligation has not been tied to means.

**It raises the threshold.** Because being a giver does not exempt one from these commands. One can crush while giving; one can rebuff while giving. Indeed this is one of the most widespread forms of help: alongside the thing given, a belittlement is added.

The verse regulates not the amount but **the treatment**. The Qurʾān formulates this openly elsewhere: *"A kind word and forgiveness are better than a charity followed by injury"* (al-Baqara 2/263).

### The sound

*Taqhar* and *tanhar*: between the two verbs there is a difference of a single letter (ق / ن). Two words on the same pattern, ending in the same closed syllable, carrying the same guttural sound.

The result is that the two commands stand in the ear as a single pair. And the exit of both words is hard — their utterance resembles the act they forbid. I record this as an observation about the sound.

## 93/11 — وَأَمَّا بِنِعْمَةِ رَبِّكَ فَحَدِّثْ

*Wa ammā bi-niʿmati Rabbika fa-ḥaddith*
"And as for the favour of your Lord, speak of it."

### The third differs from the others

The first two commands were **prohibitions**: *lā taqhar*, *lā tanhar*. The third is **an imperative**: *ḥaddith*.

And the word order changes too: in the first two, the element brought to the front was the object of the verb (*al-yatīma*, *al-sāʾila*, both in the accusative); here what is brought to the front is a **prepositional phrase** (*bi-niʿmati Rabbika*).

That is, after two "do nots" the sūra closes with a "do". And unlike the first two, the last command turns not to the addressee himself but to the source.

### The root حدّث

**Root: ح-د-ث.** Its original sense is **to be new, to come to be afterwards**.

The derivatives confirm this:

- **حَادِث (*ḥādith*)** — what comes to be afterwards, what newly appears. In the terminology of theology it is the opposite of *qadīm* (that which has no beginning): *ḥādith* means what was non-existent before it existed.
- **حَدَث (*ḥadath*)** — an event that occurs.
- **حَدِيث (*ḥadīth*)** — both "new" and "speech, report". The Qurʾān uses it of itself as well: *"Then in what speech after this will they believe?"* (al-Mursalāt 77/50).

**The link between the two ends of the root matters:** giving news and being new are expressed by the same word. Because news is what is new for the one addressed. To tell a thing is to bring it into being again in the listener.

**A point about the form:** the verb is in **Form II** (*ḥaddatha*). This form states repetition and continuity. That is, *ḥaddith* is not "say it once"; it is **keep on telling.**

The verse could have said *qul* (say). It did not.

### The letter بِ

Not *niʿmata Rabbika fa-ḥaddith*, but *bi-niʿmati Rabbika fa-ḥaddith*. A *bāʾ* has come in between.

In the classical grammatical analyses two explanations are given for this *bāʾ*: either it is a connecting particle that lets the verb reach its object (*ṣila*), or it carries the sense of "about". In the second case the sentence means "speak **about** the favour of your Lord".

The difference is small, but the second reading widens the subject of the telling: not merely counting the favour itself, but speaking upon the favour.

### نِعْمَة — favour

**Root: ن-ع-م.** The concrete sense of the root is **softness, smoothness**. *Nāʿim* is what is soft to the touch.

Derivatives:

- **نَعِيم (*naʿīm*)** — abundance, ease. It is used of the Garden.
- **أَنْعَام (*anʿām*)** — livestock, herd animals. It occurs as the name of a sūra as well. The naming comes from their being the source of wealth and ease in that society.
- **نَعَم (*naʿam*)** — "yes". The word of acceptance and affirmation.

That is, **a favour is what softens a life.** What takes the hardness away, what removes the roughness. This concrete sense stands underneath the abstract use of the word in English — "blessing", "favour" — and makes it more intelligible: a favour is not a possession but an easing.

### The construct رَبِّكَ — the limit on boasting

The verse does not say "speak of your favour". It says **"the favour of your Lord"**.

This construct is what separates the command from boasting, and it balances the whole of the command.

A person's telling of what he possesses can be two different jobs: **counting what he has in hand**, or **saying from whom he received it.** The first turns back to himself, the second to the source. The verse commands the second, and it places the qualification not on the verb but on the object: what is to be told is not your achievement but what was given to you.

If this qualification is lifted, the command turns into its exact opposite. This is why the construct is not incidental but the condition of the command.

### Concealing the favour

The word used in the Qurʾān for ingratitude comes from the root **kufr**, and the original sense of that root is **to cover** (a farmer's being called *kāfir* comes from his covering the seed with earth).

The picture that comes out of this sits properly: to speak of a favour is to uncover; ingratitude is to cover. The verse sets telling opposite covering.

And the Qurʾān sets up a link between telling and increase: *"If you give thanks, I shall surely increase you; and if you are ungrateful, My punishment is severe"* (Ibrāhīm 14/7).

In the tradition thanksgiving is treated at three levels, and this classification is widespread: **with the heart** (knowing from whom the favour comes), **with the tongue** (telling of it), **with the limbs** (using the favour in its right place). This verse commands the second; verses 9 and 10 are an example of the third.

### The sūra commanding itself

This was touched on above; it can be completed here.

In the three verses from the sixth to the eighth, **God counted** the favours. In the eleventh verse He asks the same work **of the servant.**

That is, the sūra first performs the act it commands. The text gives an example, then says "you do the same". Form and content turn into one another here.

### And the real answer to the claim

At the beginning of the sūra there was a claim: "his Lord has forsaken him".

The third verse rejected it directly. But the sūra does not end there, and the last verse contains the real answer given to the claim: **tell of it.**

The reason is this: silence is what makes room for the claim. The words "he has been forsaken" hold only if there is an unspoken emptiness standing there. Telling closes that emptiness.

The last command, then, is not merely an instruction in thanksgiving; it is the closing of the matter the sūra opened. The telling of the favour is the becoming visible of the not having been forsaken.

---

## The backbone of the sūra: three favours, three commands

The whole structure of the sūra gathers here, and unless this symmetry is set up the text has not been fully read.

| Received in the past (6-8) | Commanded now (9-11) |
|---|---|
| He found you an **orphan**, and **sheltered** you | Do not crush **the orphan** |
| He found you **not knowing the way**, and **showed** you the way | Do not rebuff **the one who asks** |
| He found you **short of means**, and **made you free of need** | Speak of **the favour** |

The logic of all three links is the same: **what you received turns into what you must give.**

### The first link — plain

The word is exactly the same: *yatīman* (6) → *al-yatīma* (9). The sūra uses the same word twice; in the first the addressee stands in the position of subject, in the second in the position of object.

This is the least disputable proof of the symmetry. The repetition of the word is not an association but the text's own sign.

### The second link — the two senses of *sāʾil*

In the seventh verse the not knowing of the way was remedied. In the tenth verse the *sāʾil* is not rebuffed.

When *sāʾil* is taken in the sense of "the one who asks a question", the link closes completely: **you were taught when you did not know; do not turn away the one who wants to learn.** The intolerance of one who has come late to knowledge towards one who does not know is a widespread behaviour — and the verse turns out to be placed exactly there.

When *sāʾil* is taken in the sense of "the one who asks for something, who begs", the link attaches instead to the eighth verse: **you were made free of need when you were in need; do not rebuff the one who asks.**

The two senses of the word, then, make two different alignments of the symmetry possible:

| Alignment | 7 → | 8 → |
|---|---|---|
| *sāʾil* = the one who asks a question | 10 (do not rebuff) | 11 (speak of it) |
| *sāʾil* = the one who asks for something | 11 (speak of it) | 10 (do not rebuff) |

Both work consistently. This is not a vagueness; the width of the word permits the structure to be built in two directions at once. Whichever alignment is taken, the symmetry does not break.

I present this table as my own reading. The establishing of the favour-command link is widely treated in the classical works of tafsīr; the detailed alignment here, however, is a classification I have drawn from the structure of the text, and not a transmitted report.

### The third link — a change of kind

This is the most striking. In the first two links the counterpart is **of the same kind**: you were sheltered as an orphan, do not crush the orphan. In the third the kind changes.

"You were made rich, so give" could have been said. It is not. What is said is **"tell"**.

This change appears to have to do with what it was that was given. The *ghinā* of the eighth verse, as was seen above, strains against history when read as an accumulation of property; it sits properly when read as freedom from need and contentment of heart. And the counterpart of contentment of heart is not distributing but **saying**. What is told is not property but the source of the favour.

### Why is this symmetry set up?

Here the sūra rejects an assumption, and the assumption is a widespread one: **one who has suffered does not make others suffer.**

This is not true. Observation shows the opposite as well: it is known that those who have been broken can be the group most inclined to break. Deprivation does not automatically produce mercy; sometimes it produces a search for compensation, sometimes the justification "I suffered too".

The sūra does not leave this transition **to itself**; it builds it **by command**. Having been found an orphan and sheltered does not guarantee behaving well towards an orphan — which is why it is commanded separately.

And the command is given by being tied to a memory. That is, the ground is not a principle from outside: not "the orphan must not be crushed" but "you were an orphan and you were not crushed". Morality is drawn not from an abstract rule but from the data of the person's own life.

## The sūra as a whole

### The flow of the argument

The sūra begins like a text of consolation but proceeds like a piece of reasoning:

1. **Two oaths** — redefining what the silence means (stillness, not absence).
2. **Negation** — rejecting the claim at two distinct layers (act and feeling).
3. **Promise** — concerning the future, but its measure is contentment, not amount.
4. **Evidence** — the guarantee of the promise is drawn from the past, from the addressee's own life.
5. **Command** — the favours counted as evidence are turned into an obligation.

None of the five steps can be skipped. Without the oath the silence would be left uninterpreted. Without the evidence the promise would hang in the air. Without the command the consolation would amount to no more than a relief.

### What is not said

What the sūra does not say is as striking as what it says:

- **It does not say "do not grieve".** The feeling is not touched at all. It is not said that the feeling is wrong, but neither is the feeling dealt with.
- **It does not say "be patient".** This command, which occurs very often in the Qurʾān, is absent here.
- **It does not say "they are liars".** The mockers are not spoken of at all; the maker of the claim is not even addressed.
- **It does not explain the reason for the silence.** Why the revelation was cut off is not said. Maryam 19/64 takes this up in another place; here the matter is not opened at all.

The last item matters particularly. The sūra does not answer the question "why"; it answers the question "what does it mean". And the two are different questions — the first most often stays unanswered, the second may not.

### Its relation to Sūrat al-Kawthar

The two sūras share the same structure and deserve to be read together.

**Al-Kawthar** is an answer to an accusation of deficiency: "your line is cut off" was said, and the sūra said "much has been given to you".
**Al-Ḍuḥā** is an answer to an accusation of abandonment: "his Lord has left him" was said, and the sūra said "He has not left him, and He gave these things".

In both the method is the same: **it does not argue with the accusation; it changes the measure.** Al-Kawthar takes the count of offspring out of being the subject; al-Ḍuḥā takes silence out of being abandonment.

And in both the consolation closes with a command — in al-Kawthar "pray and sacrifice", in al-Ḍuḥā three commands. Neither sūra comforts the addressee and leaves him; from the place where it comforts him it draws out a task.

### Its relation to Sūrat al-Māʿūn

Al-Ḍuḥā 9 and al-Māʿūn 107/2 take hold of the same subject from two different directions.

**Al-Māʿūn** describes the one who repels the orphan and counts this as the sign of "denying the religion". It makes a diagnosis.
**Al-Ḍuḥā** forbids the same act and draws its ground from the addressee's own life. It issues a command.

Set side by side, the two yield this: the treatment of the orphan is a unit of measure in the Qurʾān. What it measures is how a person behaves where he expects nothing in return.

### The practice of the takbīr

There is a practice recorded in the literature of the variant readings, and it concerns this sūra: in some schools of recitation — particularly in the transmission that goes back to the people of Mecca — the takbīr is pronounced at the end of every sūra from Sūrat al-Ḍuḥā to the end of Sūrat al-Nās.

As the ground of this practice, a report is transmitted that the takbīr was pronounced upon the revelation's having been cut off and having begun again with al-Ḍuḥā. That the practice is recorded in the books of the variant readings is undisputed; the soundness of the report it rests on, however, is disputed, and a great many scholars have counted this report weak.

I find it worth recording, because whatever the practice may be it shows one thing: in the first generations this sūra was understood as the moment when revelation **began again**, and that moment was marked like a feast.

## What this says to the present

**First: how the silence is filled.**

The problem the sūra resolves is not a lack of information. The addressee does not hold wrong information; **he holds no information at all.** The voice has stopped, and the emptiness stands there.

The relation of the human mind to that emptiness is well known: it fills it. And in filling it, it shows a tendency to pick the most negative possibility. An unanswered message, a piece of news that is late, a decision left unexplained — all of them set the same mechanism working. The emptiness does not stay neutral.

The sūra's first move is made exactly here: with two oaths, a meaning is given to the silence itself. Night is not the cancelling of day. This is not saying "be optimistic"; it is knowing that **an emptiness left uninterpreted will automatically be interpreted badly**, and putting an interpretation in between.

**Second: the form of the consolation.**

Today consolation is mostly built upon feeling: the confirming of one's feelings, being told that one is not alone, being reminded that it will pass. These have a function.

The sūra does something else: **it shows data.** It asks three questions and asks the addressee to answer out of his own life.

This method has one advantage: a word said from outside does not hold when a person does not believe it about himself. What he himself remembers does hold. The sūra's choosing the interrogative form appears to have to do with this — instead of telling, putting the question.

**Third: memory as the material of hope.**

The sūra gives a promise about the future (4-5) and draws the guarantee of that promise from the past (6-8). That is, hope is built not as a guess about the unknown but as an inference resting on the known.

Its concrete counterpart is this: a person's being able to count the moments in his own life when he was "sheltered", "shown the way", "given room", changes his assessment of the future. And these moments generally stand forgotten — because they are past, and when a problem is resolved the moment of resolution is not remembered; only the problem itself is remembered.

In a moment of hardship, the most deficient datum a person holds is his own past.

**Fourth: the wound turning into a command.**

The sūra's most original move is the symmetry set out in detail above. And its bearing on the present is direct.

The sentence "I suffered too" leads to two different conclusions. The first: *because I suffered, I know; let no one suffer what I suffered.* The second: *I suffered, so let him suffer too.* The second conclusion is at least as widespread as the first, and it is observed everywhere from institutional life to family relations — seniority turning into a licence to oppress, the one who was crushed as an apprentice crushing others once he becomes a master.

The sūra closes off this second possibility. And the manner of the closing is striking: it does it not by laying down a principle but **by tying the memory to the command**. That is, not a general ruling such as "orphans must be treated well", but "you were an orphan, you were sheltered, now do not crush".

**Fifth: the form of help, not its amount.**

That the ninth and tenth verses are not commands to give holds with particular force today.

In a world where help has been institutionalised, the amount can be measured but the treatment cannot. How much an institution distributes is reported; how it behaves while distributing is not reported. And for the receiving side the second is most often more decisive than the first.

The measure the verse lays down is this: **what is given cannot be assessed apart from the manner of its giving.** Al-Baqara 2/263 says this openly — a charity given with injury stands lower than a kind word.

**Sixth: contentment as the measure.**

The fifth verse tied a promise not to amount but to satisfaction.

The side of this that looks to the present is economic. The modern understanding of prosperity is built entirely upon amount: income, growth, consumption. What is measured is always "how much". And that an increase in what is measured does not increase satisfaction in the same proportion — that beyond a certain threshold it increases it almost not at all — is a finding that research into well-being meets again and again.

What the verse does is not to reverse this relation. It is to change the unit of measure: the end point of the promise is not the amount given but the state of the one who receives.

I record this not as a claim of a "scientific miracle" but as a choice of measure. The verse does not construct a theory of prosperity; it shows how a promise is formulated.

**Seventh: the limit of telling.**

The eleventh verse commands that the favour be told, but with the construct ("the favour of your Lord") it also lays down its limit.

This limit is particularly visible today. Displaying what one possesses has technically never been so easy. And the difference between displaying and telling lies exactly where the verse places it: the difference between **the one who counts what he has in hand and the one who says from whom he received it**.

The verse separates the two, and it does not leave the separation to a matter of intention; it writes it into the object itself. What is to be told is not "my favour" but "the favour of my Lord".
